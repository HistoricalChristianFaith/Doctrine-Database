Thomas Aquinas on penance & confession

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives the definitive scholastic account of penance as a sacrament. He fixes its matter (the acts of the penitent — contrition, confession, satisfaction — with sin as the "remote matter"), its form (the indicative "I absolve thee," not a mere prayer), its three parts (contrition, confession, satisfaction), its minister (a priest alone, exercising the "key of ministry"), the necessity of confessing every mortal sin to a priest, and its effect (the remission of guilt and the debt of eternal punishment). This is the architecture the Council of Trent would define almost point for point three centuries later — and its judicial "I absolve thee" is the Latin form the Reformers would reject and the Greek East never adopted.

Claims & evidence

A true sacrament — the priest signifies God forgiving sin

Against the objection that penance uses no corporeal matter as the other sacraments do, Aquinas holds that it is a sacrament in the full sense, because in it "something holy is signified" by both parties — the penitent renouncing sin, and the priest signifying the divine forgiveness:

in Penance something is done so that something holy is signified both on the part of the penitent sinner, and on the part of the priest absolving, because the penitent sinner, by deed and word, shows his heart to have renounced sin, and in like manner the priest, by his deed and word with regard to the penitent, signifies the work of God Who forgives his sins. Therefore it is evident that Penance, as practiced in the Church, is a sacrament.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 84, a. 1.1

Its matter: the acts of the penitent, and sin the "remote matter"

Where baptism's matter is water, penance's is human action — the penitent's own acts — while the sins those acts are directed against are the remote matter, present "not for approval, but for detestation, and destruction":

the proximate matter of this sacrament consists in the acts of the penitent, the matter of which acts are the sins over which he grieves, which he confesses, and for which he satisfies. Hence it follows that sins are the remote matter of Penance, as a matter, not for approval, but for detestation, and destruction.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 84, a. 2. Penance regards "actual mortal sin properly and chiefly… since this sacrament was instituted chiefly for the blotting out of mortal sin."2

Its form: the indicative "I absolve thee"

This is the decisive Latin development, and Aquinas defends it directly against the older deprecatory (petition) form still used in the public absolutions of the Divine Office. Because the New-Law sacraments "accomplish what they signify," and because penance works by removing the matter of sin, its form must state that removal in the indicative — on the authority of Christ's grant of the keys to Peter:

this sacrament, namely the sacrament of Penance, consists not in the consecration of a matter, nor in the use of a hallowed matter, but rather in the removal of a certain matter, viz. sin… This removal is expressed by the priest saying: "I absolve thee"… Wherefore it is evident that this is the most fitting form of this sacrament: "I absolve thee."

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 84, a. 3. "This form is taken from Christ's very words which He addressed to Peter (Mat. 16:19): 'Whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth,' etc., and such is the form employed by the Church in sacramental absolution."3 This is exactly the point the Council of Trent would dogmatize — absolution "is not a bare ministry only… but is after the manner of a judicial act" — and exactly the point at which the Greek East diverges: Eastern absolution stays deprecative (see the judicial/instrumental reading).

Its parts: contrition, confession, satisfaction

Aquinas grounds the classic threefold division in what atonement to God requires — not merely the restoration of justice, but "the reconciliation of friendship" — which demands, in turn, the will to atone, submission to the priest's judgment, and the atoning act itself:

the first requisite on the part of the penitent is the will to atone, and this is done by contrition; the second is that he submit to the judgment of the priest standing in God's place, and this is done in confession; and the third is that he atone according to the decision of God's minister, and this is done in satisfaction: and so contrition, confession, and satisfaction are assigned…

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 90, a. 2.4

The power of the keys — the "key of ministry"

The whole sacrament rests on the keys of Matthew 16:19. Aquinas distinguishes three grades: God holds the key by "authority," Christ as man by "excellence" (through the merit of his Passion), and the church's ministers hold the "key of ministry" — a real power to open heaven, but a derived and instrumental one:

the efficacy of the Passion abides in the sacraments of the Church. Wherefore a certain power for the removal of the aforesaid obstacle is bestowed on the ministers of the Church, who are the dispensers of the sacraments, not by their own, but by a Divine power and by the Passion of Christ. This power is called metaphorically the Church's key, and is the key of "ministry."

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 17, a. 1. That power "extends to the remission of guilt": in penance, unlike the other sacraments, the whole sacramental virtue is in the minister, so "the power of the keys which is in the priest, stands in the same relation to the effect of Penance, as the virtue in the baptismal water does to the effect of Baptism."5

The minister a priest alone — and confession to him necessary for salvation

Because grace is conferred in the sacrament, its minister must be a priest — "he alone who exercises a ministry over Christ's true body… who can consecrate the Eucharist." And because the sacrament is the ordained remedy for post-baptismal mortal sin, confessing that sin to a priest is itself necessary for salvation:

just as Baptism, whereby original sin is blotted out, is necessary for salvation, so also is the sacrament of Penance. And just as a man through asking to be baptized, submits to the ministers of the Church… even so, by confessing his sin, a man submits to a minister of the Church, that, through the sacrament of Penance dispensed by him, he may receive the pardon of his sins… Wherefore confession is necessary for the salvation of a man who has fallen into a mortal actual sin.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 6, a. 1; the priest as sole minister, Suppl. q. 8, a. 1. On James 5:16 ("confess… one to another"), Aquinas reads the apostle as presupposing that God "had already been prescribed… to be made to a priest," in the grant of John 20:23.6

Notes

  • The scholastic architecture Trent would define. Every load-bearing element the Council of Trent (Session 14, 1551) dogmatized against the Reformers — penance a true sacrament for post-baptismal sin; its parts contrition, confession, and satisfaction; absolution a judicial act, not a bare declaration; the priest as judge holding the keys — is already fixed here, a generation and more before the controversy. Trent's canons read as a ratification of this treatise.
  • The two flashpoints of the Reformation. Aquinas's indicative "I absolve thee" is precisely what the Reformers denied (they kept confession but read absolution as an announcement of the gospel, not a priestly sentence): see the declarative reading against the judicial/instrumental reading. And his placement of forgiveness at the term, when sorrow is "quickened by grace," anchors the Thomist side of the later attrition–contrition dispute: see the contritionist reading.
  • Provenance. Aquinas wrote out the treatise on penance himself in the Tertia Pars of the Summa (qq. 84–90), but the Summa breaks off there — at q. 90, "the parts of penance in general" — left unfinished at his death in 1274. The material on the power of the keys, the necessity and minister of confession, and satisfaction quoted here (Suppl. qq. 1–28) was compiled after his death from his earlier Commentary on the Sentences and appended as the Supplement; it transmits his teaching. The translation quoted is that of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.
  • Aquinas appears on this wiki also as a witness on the anointing of the sick timeline (the parallel treatise on extreme unction, Suppl. qq. 29–33), where he demotes the healing anointing of James 5 to the last rite of the dying.
