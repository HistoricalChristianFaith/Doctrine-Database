Kaspars Ozoliņš on Who Killed Goliath?

Dates: 2022 ("Who killed Goliath? The puzzling text of 2 Samuel 21:19," Tyndale House) · Tyndale House
Summary: The Tyndale House linguist lays out three possibilities for the relation of the two texts and offers as "one plausible option" that Elhanan the Bethlehemite killed a Philistine giant who was the brother of Goliath and was himself left unnamed; on that option the giant Elhanan killed was not Goliath himself. He also writes that scribes "rarely made deliberate changes."

Claims & evidence

Three options for the relation of Samuel and Chronicles

"(1) 'Lahmi' really was the name of the Philistine giant, and therefore 'Bethlehemite' has later crept into the text of Samuel, (2) Elhanan really was a Bethlehemite, and therefore we have a scribal error at Chronicles (i.e., 'Lahmi' wasn't the name of the Philistine giant), or (3) both were true: Elhanan was a Bethlehemite, and 'Lahmi' really was the name of the giant he slew."

— Kaspars Ozoliņš, "Who killed Goliath?" (Tyndale House, 14 April 2022), source.

One plausible option: Elhanan killed the (unnamed) brother of Goliath

"Given the arguments outlined above, however, one plausible option is that Elhanan the Bethlehemite actually killed the brother of Goliath, who himself was left unnamed."

— Ozoliņš, "Who killed Goliath?", source. He does not claim certainty — "we are still left with uncertainty as to the identity of Goliath's killer" — and he stresses that "scribes copying the Hebrew Bible rarely made deliberate changes to their text," so that on his reading the divergence arose by accident rather than by deliberate editing.

Notes

  • Neither text as the original. Ozoliņš takes neither Chronicles (as Kennicott does) nor Samuel (as McCarter does) as the original. On this option both texts descend from a shared original in which Elhanan the Bethlehemite killed Goliath's unnamed brother — Samuel losing the "brother of" (ʾaḥî misread as the object-marker ʾet), while Chronicles' "Lahmi" is not a genuine name but a scribal accident, the place-name "the Bethlehemite" (ha-laḥmî) mistaken for a proper noun (laḥmî). The corruption lies in how each branch garbled the shared source, and of the three options he lays out he sets aside the one on which "Lahmi" really was the giant's name.
  • On motive. His remark on deliberate scribal change bears on the wider charge of deliberate alteration of the Hebrew text, traced on the Masoretic-vs.-Septuagint page.
  • Placement. Ozoliņš (2022) is the most recent entry on the timeline.
