How did 2 Samuel 21:19 and 1 Chronicles 20:5 come to differ?

Disputed reading · on Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19)

Related readings: Is "Elhanan" in 2 Samuel 21:19 another name for David?

The passage

"And there was again war with the Philistines at Gob, and Elhanan the son of Jaare-oregim, the Bethlehemite, struck down Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam."

— 2 Samuel 21:19 (English Standard Version), source.1

"And there was again war with the Philistines, and Elhanan the son of Jair struck down Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam."

— 1 Chronicles 20:5 (English Standard Version), source.2

The question

The two notices differ in a few Hebrew letters: "Jaare-oregim" against "Jair," the object-marker ʾet against ʾaḥî ("brother of"), and "the Bethlehemite" (bêt hallaḥmî) against "Lahmi" (laḥmî). The proponents named below all take the Masoretic text of 2 Samuel 21:19 to have suffered in copying, and differ on which way the change ran; the question bears on whether the giant Elhanan killed was Goliath or Goliath's brother. Driver dates the intruded "oregim" before the Septuagint, and Ozoliņš reports that the Dead Sea Samuel material has "a gap at exactly this verse."3 A separate reading, that Elhanan is David himself, is set out on its own page.

Chronicles keeps the original; 2 Samuel 21:19 was corrupted in copying

On this reading the giant Elhanan killed was Lahmi, Goliath's brother, and a copyist's slips turned Samuel's notice into "struck down Goliath."

Held by: Benjamin Kennicott (Oxford Hebraist, 1753); Keil & Delitzsch (Lutheran commentators, c. 1875); Michael S. Heiser (evangelical Hebrew scholar, 2014).4

Kennicott starts from the narrative of 1 Samuel 17: since "Goliath the Gittite was slain by David," the Samuel verse must carry "a Mistake of some Transcriber," while the Chronicles reading is "plain and consistent." He reconstructs the corruption letter by letter: Jair became Jaare; the first "Oregim" "most probably was inserted from the Line immediately under"; and the letters of ʾet laḥmî were read as bêt hallaḥmî, "the Bethlehemite," so that the name Lahmi disappeared. On his account, the differences that look large in English are, in the Hebrew consonants, a short chain of slips.5 Keil & Delitzsch observe that, once the stray ārgîm is set aside, the two verses "presuppose the same original text, so that the difference can only have arisen from an error in copying"; they report "the majority of the expositors" as treating Chronicles as the true text, and conclude that "we must regard the reading in the Chronicles as the correct one, and alter our text accordingly; since the assumption that there were two Goliaths is a very improbable one."6 Heiser presents the same direction as ordinary transmission: the Samuel verse is "a defective reading since it is the result of a scribe's sincere effort to cope with a problematic manuscript," and so "1 Chr 20:5 should be used to correct 2 Sam 21:19." On this account the brother of Goliath fell at Gob in David's later wars, and 1 Samuel 17 and 2 Samuel 21 describe two different combats against two different men.7

Samuel's text is the older; Chronicles harmonized it

On this reading 2 Samuel 21:19 preserves an early notice that Elhanan killed Goliath, and Chronicles' "the brother of Goliath" is a later adjustment to agree with 1 Samuel 17.

Held by: Julius Wellhausen (German critic, 1871); S. R. Driver (Oxford Regius Professor of Hebrew, 1890); Henry Preserved Smith (ICC, 1899); P. Kyle McCarter (Anchor Bible, 1984).8

Wellhausen locates the mechanical fault in Samuel's patronymic, where "oregim" intruded "from the line below," but treats the Chronicler as a reviser: "given his tendency to avoid archaisms," one "must beware of simply adopting his text also for 2 Samuel." Driver turns this into an argument from the harder reading. A scribe "with the knowledge of I 17 that he must have possessed" would not have turned a text like Chronicles into the statement that Elhanan "slew Goliath of Gath," whereas "a motive for the correction of the text of Samuel by the Chronicler—or even by a copyist of the Chronicles—is obvious." The change from Samuel to Chronicles, he adds, needs ʾet to become ʾaḥî and "the Beth-lehemite" to become "Lahmi," a sequence he reads as a deliberate correction.9 Smith states the result in one line: "Elhanan ben Jair the Bethlehemite slew Goliath the Gittite; the harmonistic purpose of the Chronicler in making the victim the brother of Goliath is evident."10 McCarter extends the reading from the text to the tradition. He calls Chronicles' "Laḥmi, the brother of Goliath" "a scribal error with the effect of harmonizing this notice with I Samuel 17," and holds that "deeds of obscure heroes tend to attach themselves to famous heroes," so that "the tradition attributing the slaying of Goliath to Elhanan is older than that which credits the deed to David"; on his account the champion of 1 Samuel 17 was originally anonymous.11 Baruch Halpern gives the earlier notice a named hero: "the only other Elhanan and the only other Bethlehemite in the lists of David's heroes is 'Elhanan ben-Dodo from Bethlehem' (2 Sam 23:24)," whom he takes to have been "probably originally listed as Goliath's killer."12

Both verses descend from a shared original in which Elhanan killed Goliath's unnamed brother

On this reading neither surviving verse is the original: each garbled a common text in which Elhanan the Bethlehemite killed Goliath's brother, whose own name was not given.

Held by: Kaspars Ozoliņš (Tyndale House, 2022).13

Ozoliņš sets out three options: "(1) 'Lahmi' really was the name of the Philistine giant, and therefore 'Bethlehemite' has later crept into the text of Samuel, (2) Elhanan really was a Bethlehemite, and therefore we have a scribal error at Chronicles (i.e., 'Lahmi' wasn't the name of the Philistine giant), or (3) both were true." He favours the second. Elhanan's Bethlehem origin is supported by the list of David's warriors in 2 Samuel 23, which names an Elhanan from Bethlehem, so "Lahmi" in Chronicles is best taken as a scribal reading of the second half of "the Bethlehemite" (ha-laḥmî) as a proper name. Samuel, for its part, lost "brother of" when ʾaḥî was read as the object-marker ʾet. Each branch thus preserves part of the original and garbles part of it. The resulting reconstruction is that "Elhanan the Bethlehemite actually killed the brother of Goliath, who himself was left unnamed." The mechanism is accident rather than design, since "scribes copying the Hebrew Bible rarely made deliberate changes to their text"; and Ozoliņš presents it as "one plausible historical reconstruction," with the identity of the killer still carrying some uncertainty.
