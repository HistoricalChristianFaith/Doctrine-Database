Theoteknos of Livias on the Assumption / Dormition of Mary

Dates: c. 600 (Theoteknos, bishop of Livias in Palestine, east of the Jordan; his episcopate is placed between 550 and 650, and the homily with it) · e-Clavis
Summary: Theoteknos preached one of the two oldest surviving Greek homilies for the feast, and it uses a different word for it. Where his contemporaries speak of Mary’s Koimēsis (“falling asleep”), Theoteknos calls it her Análēpsis — her “taking up” — and affirms that her body, though it truly died, was kept from corruption and lifted to heaven with her soul.

Claims & evidence

Mary was “taken up into heaven” — body with soul

“she who had become wider than the heavens and higher than the cherubim was to receive the palm of reward to which she had been called, and to be taken up into heaven.”

— Theoteknos of Livias, Encomium on the Assumption of the Holy Mother of God §1, trans. Brian E. Daley, On the Dormition of Mary: Early Patristic Homilies (St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1998), English text online.1 His title uses the “taking-up” word: this is the earliest surviving homily to frame the feast as an Assumption rather than a Dormition, and to state the bodily claim in these terms.

Her body tasted death, yet was kept incorrupt

“even though the God-bearing body of that holy one did taste death, it was not corrupted; for it was kept incorrupt and free of decay, and it was lifted up to heaven with her pure and spotless soul by the holy archangels and powers.”

— Encomium on the Assumption §4, trans. Daley.2 The formula joins a real death to a body preserved from decay and raised. The same pairing — death and incorruption — appears in the later Greek homilists on this timeline.

Notes

  • Theoteknos wrote about two centuries after Epiphanius of Salamis, who in c. 377 wrote that Scripture is silent and “no one knows” how Mary’s life ended. Theoteknos preaches a detailed affirmation at the feast; whether that affirmation carries an older tradition or grew from the apocryphal narratives is disputed (see the Transitus narratives and the tradition John of Damascus invokes).
  • The bishopric of Livias lay in the old territory of Peraea, east of the Jordan, in Palestine, where the 15 August Marian feast is first attested (see Emperor Maurice).
