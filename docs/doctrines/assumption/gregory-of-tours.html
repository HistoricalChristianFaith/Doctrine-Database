Gregory of Tours on the Assumption / Dormition of Mary

Dates: c. 590 (Gregory, bishop of Tours in Gaul, c. 538–594; the account stands in his Glory of the Martyrs, De gloria martyrum I.4, written 580–594) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory gives the earliest extant narrative affirmation of the bodily assumption in the Latin West. He retells, as a completed event, the story that had come to him through the apocryphal Transitus tradition: at the end of Mary’s life the apostles gathered, the Lord received her soul, and her body was carried in a cloud to paradise and there rejoined to her soul.

Claims & evidence

Mary’s body was taken up to paradise and rejoined to her soul

“The Lord … commanded the body of Mary be taken in a cloud into paradise; where now, rejoined to the soul, Mary dwells with the chosen ones.”

— Glory of the Martyrs 4, as translated online (cf. R. Van Dam, trans., Glory of the Martyrs, Liverpool University Press). In the fuller account Gregory describes the apostles miraculously assembled at Mary’s house as her earthly course closed; the Lord Jesus coming with his angels and giving her soul to the archangel Michael; the apostles laying her body in a tomb and keeping watch; and the Lord returning to bear the body away. It is the earliest extant Western text to state the bodily assumption as a completed event.

Notes

  • Gregory’s account follows the Transitus Mariae (“Passing of Mary”) narratives, a book of which is listed among the books “not to be received” in the Gelasian Decree. With him the narrative passes into episcopal teaching in the West, two centuries after Epiphanius wrote that no one knew Mary’s end. Whether the narratives are the belief’s source or witnesses to an older tradition is disputed; see the Transitus narratives.
  • Gregory’s account reads as an epitome of the “Bethlehem” family of Transitus narratives, whose fullest Latin representative is Pseudo-Melito’s Transitus Mariae (c. 500). That Latin text opens by disowning an earlier Transitus it brands heretical.
  • About two and a half centuries later Paschasius Radbertus (c. 860) warned against the apocryphal Transitus and declined to affirm the bodily assumption as certain.
