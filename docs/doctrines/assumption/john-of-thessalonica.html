John of Thessalonica on the Assumption / Dormition of Mary

Dates: c. 615 (John, archbishop of Thessalonica, r. c. 610–649, d. c. 630; the Homily on the Dormition was composed to introduce the feast to his church) · Wikipedia
Summary: John’s homily is the earliest surviving “official” episcopal retelling of Mary’s Dormition, and it speaks about its own sources. He wrote it to introduce a feast his city had not been keeping, and he explains why: on his account the narratives of Mary’s end had been so corrupted by heretics that his predecessors had kept their distance. His remedy was to edit the accounts, cutting what he judged false and preaching only what remained.

Claims & evidence

The feast had not been kept in Thessalonica

“Practically every place under heaven celebrates every year the memory of her going to her rest, with the exception of only a few, including the region around this divinely protected city of Thessalonica.”

— John of Thessalonica, Homily on the Dormition, trans. Brian E. Daley, On the Dormition of Mary (St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1998), English text online.1 On John’s report the feast was not yet kept everywhere in the early seventh century; he writes to establish it where it had been absent.

Why it had not been kept: his account of corrupted narratives

“Although those who were present then described her end truthfully, we are told, mischievous heretics later corrupted their accounts by adding words of their own, and for this reason our ancestors distanced themselves from these accounts as not in accord with the catholic Church.”

— Homily on the Dormition, trans. Daley.2 A promoter of the feast here reports that the accounts of Mary’s Dormition were held to be corrupted, and that the earlier church of Thessalonica had for that reason not commemorated it.

His method: setting aside what he calls fabrications

“We have gathered these testimonies together in love of truth and in fear of God, taking no account of fabricated stories, since they have been interpolated into the traditions by the malice of those who fabricated them.”

— Homily on the Dormition, trans. Daley.3 John claims to relay only “what truly happened,” corroborated where possible by the physical sites still shown in his day. The narratives he trims are the same Transitus Mariae cycle behind Gregory of Tours; the Latin Pseudo-Melito blames a corrupted version on one “Leucius,” a name that, Shoemaker writes, “became synonymous with the composition of heterodox apocrypha” in the early church.

Notes

  • John’s preface shows how the feast reached the liturgy of his city: by an editorial act, a bishop selecting from the accounts the portions he could vouch for and publishing them as the feast’s official story. He holds that the original accounts were truthful and later corrupted; whether the narratives are the belief’s source or witnesses to an older tradition is disputed (see the Transitus narratives and the tradition John of Damascus invokes).
  • Some two and a half centuries later Paschasius Radbertus warned his readers against the apocryphal Transitus.
