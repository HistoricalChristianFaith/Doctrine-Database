Germanus of Constantinople on the Assumption / Dormition of Mary

Dates: c. 720 (Germanus, Patriarch of Constantinople c. 715–730; c. 634–733/740) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his homilies for the feast Germanus preaches Mary’s genuine falling-asleep in death — and, in the first homily, that her body knew no decay: it was a “stranger to all dissolution,” impossible for the tomb to hold; her flesh did not see corruption but was taken up incorrupt. His second homily, though, opens with a qualification — her bones had “become hard and shrivelled in decay” in the earth before being re-clothed with incorruptible flesh — a two-stage account in which an interval of decay is overtaken by restoration. In both homilies the end he preaches is the incorrupt, assumed body; Pius XII quoted him by name in the 1950 constitution.

Claims & evidence

The first homily: her body “does not know earthly dissolution”

“Your virginal body is utterly holy, utterly pure, truly the dwelling-place of God, and because of this it endures and does not know earthly dissolution.”

— Germanus of Constantinople, First Homily on the Dormition (PG 98.345B), trans. Stephen J. Shoemaker, Ancient Traditions of the Virgin Mary’s Dormition and Assumption (Oxford, 2002).1 Brian Daley’s rendering of the same homily is to the same effect: her “flesh did not undergo the corruption of the tomb,” her body “changed, in its humanity, to the highest incorruptible life,” so that Germanus can declare it “impossible that you, the vessel which bore God, should be dissolved and decomposed into the dust of death.”2

A real death and an empty tomb

Germanus preaches a real death — a Koimēsis — but one death could not master: “he has taken you to himself in his incorruption,” he says, so that “your tomb was revealed now as empty of your flesh.”3 For Germanus the Dormition is not only a departure of the soul: the body itself, kept from decay, is carried up.

The second homily: bones “shrivelled in decay” before their restoration

The picture of a body that never decayed is Germanus’s first homily. His second opens with a different image: Mary’s body is glorified not by escaping decay altogether but by a decay that is undone.

“For when her human bones were once transformed, by the mercy of God, in the depths of the earth, the immaculate body of the Mother of God again put flesh on them, though they had become hard and shrivelled in decay — indeed, they grew ‘softer than oil’ (Ps 54:22 [LXX]) in their incorruptibility, because of the resurrection of him who was born of her.”

— Germanus of Constantinople, Second Homily on the Dormition (PG 98.361), trans. Brian E. Daley, On the Dormition of Mary (St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1998).4 The first homily reads differently: there her flesh “did not undergo the corruption of the tomb” and neither sepulchre “was a workshop of decay”; here her bones do decay — “hard and shrivelled” in the earth — and are only afterward re-fleshed and made incorrupt. In both homilies the body is raised and taken up; the second gives a different account of how the incorruption is reached — through decay overtaken by restoration, rather than a body that never decayed at all. In this the second homily differs from other Greek Dormition preachers on this timeline: Andrew of Crete has her tomb “allow no corruption,” and John of Damascus asks how “corruption [could] dare to assault that body once filled with life.” Germanus’s second homily thus lets the grave touch the body before its glorification.

Notes

  • Germanus is a witness to Mary’s incorrupt, assumed body, in the two forms shown above. When Pope Pius XII defined the Assumption in Munificentissimus Deus (1950), he cited Germanus among the patristic authorities for Mary’s incorrupt body taken into heaven.
  • Like Andrew of Crete, his near-contemporary, Germanus holds together the two claims the Eastern Dormition homilies join: that Mary truly died, and that her body was nonetheless kept from the corruption of the grave. The same pairing appears in Theoteknos of Livias a century earlier.
