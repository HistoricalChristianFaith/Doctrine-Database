Pseudo-Melito, Transitus Mariae (the Latin Passing of Mary)

Dates: c. 500 (Latin; commonly placed in the late fifth or sixth century) — the Transitus Mariae falsely ascribed to Melito, bishop of Sardis (who in fact died c. 180), so cited as “Pseudo-Melito”; the “B” recension is the most widely copied of the Latin narratives and was edited critically by Monika Haibach-Reinisch (1962) · e-clavis: Assumption of the Virgin, by Pseudo-Melito
Summary: Pseudo-Melito’s Transitus is the most influential Latin narrative of Mary’s bodily assumption — the same “Bethlehem”-family account that Gregory of Tours epitomizes a generation or so later. Its prologue presents the work as a correction of an earlier Transitus it calls heretical — the work of one “Leucius,” whose account of Mary’s assumption, it says, “would be impious not only to read … in the church of God, but even to hear.”

Claims & evidence

The prologue: a self-declared purge of a Transitus it calls heretical

“Melito, servant of Christ, bishop of the church of Sardis, to the venerable brethren in the Lord appointed at Laodicea, in peace greeting. I remember that I have often written of one Leucius, who, having along with ourselves associated with the apostles, turned aside through alienated feelings and a rash soul from the path of rectitude, and inserted very many things in his books about the acts of the apostles. … Nor did he think this to be enough; but he even vitiated, by his impious writing, the assumption of the blessed ever-virgin Mary, the mother of God, to such a degree that it would be impious not only to read it in the church of God, but even to hear it.”

— The Passing of Mary (Second Latin Form / Pseudo-Melito), prologue, trans. Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. VIII (cf. M. R. James, The Apocryphal New Testament, Oxford, 1924, pp. 209–16).1 The prologue then promises a clean text — “those things which we heard from the apostle John” — set down “believing, not the strange dogmas hatched by heretics, but the Father in the Son, the Son in the Father.” The prologue presents an earlier narrative of Mary’s end as corrupted and this one as its correction — a Latin counterpart to the Greek disavowal of the same corpus by John of Thessalonica (c. 615). (The prologue is written in the name of Melito, bishop of Sardis in the second century; see the note on date below.) Whether such prologues show the narratives being purged or show the belief growing up in the apocrypha is disputed; see the Transitus narratives.

The narrative: Mary’s body raised from the tomb and borne to paradise

“Arise, my beloved and my nearest relation; thou who hast not put on corruption by intercourse with man, suffer not destruction of the body in the sepulchre. And immediately Mary rose from the tomb, and blessed the Lord … the angels along with Him, carrying the blessed Mary into the paradise of God.”

— The Passing of Mary (Second Latin Form / Pseudo-Melito), §§16–17, trans. Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. VIII.2 As in the Syriac Six Books, the pattern is Dormition joined to Assumption: a real death and burial, then the soul restored to an incorrupt body which is carried bodily into paradise. Gregory of Tours (c. 590) tells the same story as a completed event in the Latin West.

Notes

  • Date and recension. The text is pseudonymous — ascribed to Melito of Sardis (d. c. 180) but composed centuries later, generally between the fifth and seventh centuries, with the late fifth century the earliest commonly proposed. The dominant “B” recension (surviving in scores of manuscripts) was given its critical edition by Monika Haibach-Reinisch, Ein neuer ‘Transitus Mariae’ des Pseudo-Melito (Rome, 1962); an older Latin form had been printed by Tischendorf.3
  • The conduit to the West. Pseudo-Melito’s Transitus is the fullest Latin representative of the “Bethlehem” narrative family, and Gregory of Tours’s account in the Glory of the Martyrs (c. 590) reads as an epitome of this kind of Latin Transitus; on that reading this text, or its immediate source, is a channel by which the bodily-assumption story passed into Western preaching.
  • The Gelasian list. A Transitus “Passing of holy Mary” is what the Gelasian Decree lists among the books “not to be received”; Paschasius Radbertus (c. 860) warns against “that apocryphal work on the passing of the virgin.” Pseudo-Melito himself opens by disowning a predecessor he calls heretical; both facts figure in the disputed question of whether the apocryphal Transitus narratives are the belief’s source or witnesses to an older tradition.
