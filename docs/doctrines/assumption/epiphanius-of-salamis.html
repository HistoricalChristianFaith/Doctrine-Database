Epiphanius of Salamis on the Assumption / Dormition of Mary

Dates: c. 377 (Epiphanius, bishop of Constantia — ancient Salamis — in Cyprus, one of the most learned churchmen of his age; his witness here is the treatise against the Arabian Antidicomarianites folded whole into Book III of his Panarion as chapter 78, finished about 377; he died in 403) · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius is the earliest extant writer to raise the question of how Mary’s earthly life ended, and he answers it with deliberate agnosticism: Scripture is silent, the possibilities are several, and no one knows. He neither affirms nor denies a bodily assumption — he refuses to decide, and grounds that refusal in the silence of the record.

Claims & evidence

Scripture does not record whether Mary died

“But if some think us mistaken, let them search the Scriptures. They will not find Mary’s death; they will not find whether she died or did not die.”

— Panarion 78 (Against the Antidicomarianites), c. 377, as quoted (Carol, Mariology II, 139–40). Epiphanius challenges his opponents to comb the sacred text for any account of Mary’s end and insists they will find none: on his statement, Scripture does not record whether Mary died.

Three possibilities, and a chosen silence

“For my own part, I do not dare to speak, but I keep my own thoughts and I practice silence.”

— Panarion 78.23, as quoted (Carol, Mariology II, 139–40). In the surrounding passage Epiphanius canvasses three fates for the Virgin without endorsing any: that she died and was honourably buried; that she was martyred (reading the “sword” of Luke 2:35 literally); or that she was left alive, “for God is not incapable of doing whatever he wills.” Having set them side by side he declines to choose, noting that Scripture is silent and that the apostle John, who took Mary into his care, nowhere records that he carried her with him or what befell her.

Notes

  • Epiphanius is cited on both sides of the modern debate. Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. III, §83) sets his indecision within “the entire silence of the apostles and the primitive church teachers respecting the departure of Mary”; William Webster (Protestant) cites his agnosticism as evidence of the early Church’s silence on Mary’s end; Tim Staples (Catholic) reads his comparison of Mary with Elijah in Panarion 79 as agreement that she was taken up without dying. Stephen Shoemaker (Ancient Traditions of the Virgin Mary’s Dormition and Assumption, 2002, pp. 13–14) holds that the passage does not show that “there were as of yet no developed traditions about the end of the Virgin’s life in circulation; it merely reveals that there was no authoritative or orthodox tradition (in his view) to which he could turn,” and that his indecision suggests “some difference of opinion had already arisen among Christians as to whether Mary actually died or remained immortal.” The readings are set out at Does the silence of the first centuries tell against an apostolic origin for the Assumption? and Did Mary die before she was assumed?
  • The same treatise, Panarion 78, is Epiphanius’s defence of Mary’s perpetual virginity against the Antidicomarianites — so the earliest witness to the silence about her end is also a chief witness for her ever-virginity. He appears on this wiki as a witness on several other timelines as well.
