Does the tradition John of Damascus invokes trace to the apostles, or into the apocrypha?

Disputed reading · on The Assumption / Dormition of Mary · witness: John of Damascus

Related readings: Are the apocryphal Transitus narratives the source of the belief, or witnesses to an older tradition? · Does the silence of the first centuries tell against an apostolic origin for the Assumption? · Did Mary die before she was assumed?

The passage

“Why do you seek in the tomb one who has been assumed to the heavenly courts?”

— John of Damascus, Second Homily on the Dormition of the Theotokos, c. 730, English translation.1

The question

John of Damascus preaches Mary’s bodily glorification in homilies that draw on scriptural figures, arguments from fittingness and an account of her empty tomb, and the excerpt from the Euthymiac History transmitted with his second homily carries that account back to the Council of Chalcedon (451). What is disputed is where the tradition behind his account leads: to a memory handed down from the apostles, or into the fifth- and sixth-century apocryphal narratives that are its earliest surviving written witnesses.

The excerpt is not part of John’s own text. Its interpolator names his source as “chapter 40 of the third book of the Euthymiac History,” and the excerpt is usually regarded as an interpolation, although every surviving copy of the sermon includes it. The Euthymiac History itself, a Chalcedonian ecclesiastical history, is dated between about 550 and 750 (Van Nuffelen and Van Hoof; Shoemaker), perhaps as early as the early sixth century (Lourié); Jugie (1926) proposed a date late in the ninth century, which Lourié and Annemarie Weyl Carr reject.2

A tradition handed down from the apostles

In brief: the account John of Damascus preaches is a memory handed down in the Church from the apostles who witnessed Mary’s end.

Held by: Frederick Holweck (Catholic), “The Feast of the Assumption,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1907);3 Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox), “Panagia” (c. 1949);4 John of Thessalonica, Homily on the Dormition (c. 615);5 John of Damascus, three homilies on the Dormition (c. 730), with the Euthymiac History excerpt they transmit.2,6

The tradition presents itself as apostolic from its first homiletic statements. John of Thessalonica, introducing the feast c. 615, holds that “those who were present then described her end truthfully, we are told,” and that later heretics corrupted those accounts.5 The Euthymiac History, as transmitted in John of Damascus’s homily, has Juvenal of Jerusalem tell the Emperor Marcian and Empress Pulcheria at Chalcedon that the apostles were gathered at Mary’s death, her body was laid in Gethsemane, and the tomb, opened three days later, was found empty, only the grave-clothes remaining.2 Holweck gives this as John of Damascus’s formulation of “the tradition of the Church of Jerusalem” and holds that “Catholic faith, however, has always derived our knowledge of the mystery from Apostolic Tradition.”3

On this reading the doctrine’s warrant was never a documentary pedigree but a received tradition. Staples describes it as “passed down in the oral tradition of the Church and developed over the centuries,” and holds that the narrative families, “written in nine languages,” “all agree on Mary’s Assumption and they presuppose that the story was already widely known.”7 Lossky holds that “The Mother of God was never a theme of the public preaching of the apostles”: while Christ was proclaimed “to the whole world, the mystery of his Mother was revealed only to those who were within the Church,” “a fruit of faith, ripened in tradition.”4 A chain traced through surviving documents shows where the written record leads; a tradition kept within the Church that left no earlier written trace is, on this reading, what one would expect that record to miss.

A tradition whose traceable chain runs into the apocrypha

In brief: followed back through the surviving documents, the tradition leads to the fifth- and sixth-century apocryphal narratives, not to an earlier apostolic report.

Held by: Stephen J. Shoemaker, historian, Ancient Traditions of the Virgin Mary’s Dormition and Assumption (2002);8 Martin Jugie (Catholic, 1944; grants the documentary chain, holds the doctrine on other grounds) ⚠ unverified;9 Panagiotis N. Trembelas (Orthodox, 1959; on the 1950 dogma’s documentary basis).10

On this reading the tradition’s traceable chain runs back into the Transitus literature. Shoemaker reports that Jugie, charged with “combing the earliest Christian centuries in search of historical evidence,” concluded that the apocrypha “are of no ‘historical’ value” and “in no way indicative of a tradition of Mary’s Dormition and Assumption that reaches back to the apostles”; Shoemaker adds that “the historical record has still yielded no clear witness to the Virgin’s Dormition and Assumption from the earliest church.”9 Trembelas holds that the 1950 dogma “is based on apocryphal renditions dating from the fourth century.”4 John’s statement (c. 730) inherits its formula, a genuine death, an incorrupt body, and a heaven-borne translation, from the Greek Dormition homilists before him (Theoteknos, John of Thessalonica, Andrew of Crete); in the Latin West the belief enters preaching when Gregory of Tours (c. 590) retells the Transitus account; and the Transitus Mariae cycle descends from the earliest recoverable narrative, the Palm-of-the-Tree-of-Life tradition behind the Ethiopic Liber Requiei.8 On this reading no link in the chain crosses an independent, earlier apostolic report; it ends in the fifth- and sixth-century narratives, behind which stands Epiphanius’s statement (c. 377) that no one knew Mary’s end.
