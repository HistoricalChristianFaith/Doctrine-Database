Did Mary die before she was assumed?

Disputed reading · on The Assumption / Dormition of Mary · witnesses: Epiphanius of Salamis, Theoteknos of Livias, Pius XII

Related readings: Does the silence of the first centuries tell against an apostolic origin for the Assumption? · Are the apocryphal Transitus narratives the source of the belief, or witnesses to an older tradition? · Does the tradition John of Damascus invokes trace to the apostles, or into the apocrypha?

The passage

“immortal to the present time through him who had his abode in her and who assumed and raised her above the higher regions.”

— the homily On Simeon and Anna ascribed to Timothy of Jerusalem (PG 86, 245–247), as translated in Michael O’Carroll, Theotokos.1

“even though the God-bearing body of that holy one did taste death, it was not corrupted; for it was kept incorrupt and free of decay, and it was lifted up to heaven.”

— Theoteknos of Livias, Encomium on the Assumption of the Holy Mother of God §4, trans. Brian E. Daley.2

The question

Both readings hold that Mary’s body was taken to heaven undecayed; what is disputed is whether she first died. The earliest extant writer on Mary’s end, Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377), names natural death, martyrdom, and survival without death as possibilities and declines to choose among them,3 and the 1950 definition affirms the assumption of Mary “having completed the course of her earthly life” without stating that she died.4

Taken up without dying

In brief: Mary did not die; like Elijah, she was taken up to heaven living.

Held by: Martin Jugie (Catholic), La Mort et l’Assomption de la Sainte Vierge (1944) ⚠ unverified;5 the homily On Simeon and Anna ascribed to Timothy of Jerusalem (date disputed);1,6 Epiphanius of Salamis, Panarion 79 (c. 377), on the reading of Tim Staples (Catholic, 2019).7,8

Read at face value, the “Timothy” homily says Mary never died: she is “immortal to the present time,” already “assumed and raised.” On Jugie’s dating, which rests on internal signs (no mention of the Jerusalem tomb legend, no anti-Nestorian polemic, parthenos rather than theotokos), it would be among the earliest datable statements of Mary’s end.6 The option is also present in the earliest witness. Epiphanius lists survival without death among his three possibilities, “for God is not incapable of doing whatever he wills,”3 and in Panarion 79 he writes that Mary “is like Elijah, who was virgin from his mother’s womb, always remained so, and was taken up, but has not seen death.” Staples reads this as Epiphanius’s “personal agreement with the idea that Mary was assumed into heaven without ever having died,” and concludes that “there was disagreement in the circulating stories of the Assumption of Mary as to whether she was taken up alive or after having died.”7

Its leading modern Catholic proponent, on Shoemaker’s account, was Jugie, who “after decades of researching the matter on the Vatican’s behalf, emerged as a modern champion of Mary’s immortality, a view which he laboured to promote in his publications as the earliest (and thus, true) view regarding the end of Mary’s life,” and who argued from the “Timothy” homily.5 A theological argument from the Immaculate Conception points the same way, as Shoemaker summarizes it: “if Mary was immaculately conceived and therefore did not possess original sin, then it was necessary that she was bodily assumed and did not taste death, since she lacked that which causes human beings to die.”5 LoBiondo reports that Jugie, Roschini and Gallus served on the committee that helped draft the 1950 constitution, whose definition affirms the assumption of Mary “having completed the course of her earthly life” without saying that she died.9,4 On this reading Mary, preserved from sin, was preserved also from death and, like the Elijah of Epiphanius’s comparison, taken up living.

A real death, then a bodily raising

In brief: Mary truly died, as her Son did, and her body was then raised and taken up ahead of the general resurrection.

Held by: Kallistos Ware (Orthodox), “The Sanctity and Glory of the Mother of God: Orthodox Approaches” (1984), with Sergei Bulgakov (Orthodox);10 John Paul II (Catholic), General Audience of 25 June 1997;11 Theoteknos of Livias (c. 600) and the Greek Dormition homilists after him.2,12

The Eastern name of the feast, the Dormition (“falling asleep,” Koimēsis), is itself a claim: one falls asleep in death. On this reading the Assumption does not bypass death but reverses it, Mary’s soul surviving death in the middle state traced on the intermediate-state timeline before her body is raised. Both of the earliest narrative families have her die and then be raised. In the Six Books her body is borne to Paradise, where Christ calls “Mary, rise,” and “straightway she was restored to life.”13 The Coptic Pseudo-Evodius dates the two events to separate days: her soul leaves her body on the 21st of Tobe, and after a seven-month interval the body is raised on the 16th of Mesore, her soul returned to it.14

The homilists who carry the feast pair a genuine death with an incorrupt, heaven-borne body: Theoteknos (“did taste death”), and after him Modestus of Jerusalem, Germanus of Constantinople, Andrew of Crete, and John of Damascus, whose tomb, incorruption kept in the tomb, and empty grave found later all presuppose that Mary died. The body of Munificentissimus Deus speaks of the faithful’s belief that “the great Mother of God … like her only begotten Son, had actually passed from this life,” her body never subject to “the corruption of the tomb”; the parallel is to Christ, who died and rose.15 Staples holds that “at the level of the Ordinary Magisterium it does teach that Mary died,” citing the constitution at §§17, 20, 21, 29, 35, 39, and 40.7

John Paul II, teaching in 1997, held that “Pius XII did not intend to deny the fact of her death” and that “a common tradition actually exists which sees Mary’s death as her entry into heavenly glory”: “since Christ died, it would be difficult to maintain the contrary for his Mother,” and “To share in Christ’s Resurrection, Mary had first to share in his death.”11 Among Orthodox theologians, Bulgakov writes that “dying a natural death, she was not subject to corruption, but, raised up by her Son, she lives in her glorified body at the right hand of Christ in the heavens,” and Ware, who agrees with him “wholeheartedly,” notes that Bulgakov, “following the normal orthodox view, considers that Mary underwent physical death in the usual way, and that her body was afterwards resurrected and united with her soul in heaven.”10 On this reading Mary died as her Son died, and her assumption is her resurrection ahead of the general resurrection.
