Does the silence of the first centuries tell against an apostolic origin for the Assumption?

Disputed reading · on The Assumption / Dormition of Mary · witness: Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: Are the apocryphal Transitus narratives the source of the belief, or witnesses to an older tradition? · Does the tradition John of Damascus invokes trace to the apostles, or into the apocrypha? · Did Mary die before she was assumed?

The passage

“But if some think us mistaken, let them search the Scriptures. They will not find Mary’s death; they will not find whether she died or did not die… For my own part, I do not dare to speak, but I keep my own thoughts and I practice silence.”

— Panarion 78 (Against the Antidicomarianites), c. 377, as quoted (Carol, Mariology II, 139–40).1

The question

No canonical text records the end of Mary’s life, and no extant Christian writer before Epiphanius (c. 377) discusses it; the bodily assumption is first narrated in the apocryphal Dormition texts. What is disputed is what that silence implies: whether it tells against an apostolic origin for the belief, whether it is consistent with an apostolic tradition handed on without early written record, or whether the belief’s warrant depends on a documentary chain at all.

A belief first attested centuries after the apostolic age

In brief: with no record of Mary’s end for some three centuries, the belief cannot be shown to come from the apostles.

Held by: George Salmon (Anglican; Regius Professor of Divinity, Dublin), The Infallibility of the Church (1888);2 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian), History of the Christian Church, vol. III, §83;3 Panagiotis N. Trembelas (Orthodox, 1959; on the 1950 dogma’s documentary basis);4 James White (Protestant), “The Bodily Assumption, Scripture and Tradition” (2000).5,6

Salmon asks “by what means are we to suppose that the Roman Church acquired a knowledge of historical facts concerning which there is no historical tradition?”, for “If there had been any historical tradition on these subjects, the Church would always have known it”; to affirm the Assumption, he writes, is “to assert a historical fact without a particle of evidence.”2 Schaff holds that “The entire silence of the apostles and the primitive church teachers respecting the departure of Mary stirred idle curiosity to all sorts of inventions, until a translation like Enoch’s and Elijah’s was attributed to her,” and notes that “Epiphanius will not decide whether she died and was buried, or not.”3 “For centuries in the early Church there is complete silence regarding Mary’s end,” Webster writes.7

The record, on this reading, bears the claim out. No second- or third-century writer, martyr-act, or liturgy mentions Mary’s end, and the earliest extant writer to raise the question, Epiphanius (c. 377), lays out three possibilities (natural death, martyrdom, or survival without death) and settles on none.1 The bodily-assumption belief then surfaces in the apocryphal Transitus Mariae narratives, a book of which the Gelasian Decree lists among those “not to be received,”8 and from there enters preaching: Gregory of Tours in the West (c. 590), the Greek Dormition homilists in the East. Trembelas holds that the 1950 dogma “is based on apocryphal renditions dating from the fourth century” and that “such sources cannot be used as a serious basis for dogma.”4 White writes that “Generations of Christians lived and died without ever once uttering a word about a dogma that is now defined infallibly by Rome.”5 The physical cult is also late: Holweck, who holds the apostolic-tradition reading below, states that “the first six centuries did not know of the tomb of Mary at Jerusalem.”9 This reading holds that a belief first attested some three centuries after the event, in documents listed as apocryphal, has no documented apostolic origin; its case turns on attestation, not on whether a miracle is possible, and applies whether the event is held to be natural or miraculous.

An apostolic tradition handed on without early written record

In brief: the apostles handed on what they knew of Mary’s end by word of mouth, so the earliest writings need not record it.

Held by: Frederick Holweck (Catholic), “The Feast of the Assumption,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 2 (1907);9 Tim Staples (Catholic), “The Assumption of Mary in History,” Catholic Answers (2019).10

This reading grants the silence and locates the doctrine’s warrant in tradition. Holweck writes that “regarding the day, year, and manner of Our Lady’s death, nothing certain is known,” and records that Epiphanius “acknowledged that he knew nothing definite about it”; yet “Catholic faith, however, has always derived our knowledge of the mystery from Apostolic Tradition.” He gives that tradition in John of Damascus’s formulation of “the tradition of the Church of Jerusalem”: Juvenal of Jerusalem told the imperial couple at Chalcedon (451) that Mary died in the presence of the apostles and that “her tomb, when opened, upon the request of St. Thomas, was found empty.”9 Staples likewise describes the doctrine as “passed down in the oral tradition of the Church and developed over the centuries.”

The silence itself, on this reading, has an explanation. Early Christian writing, Staples argues, “mostly dealt with problem areas in the Church that needed to be addressed,” and “there is no record of anyone disagreeing on the matter”; he compares the absence of early writing on Jesus’ celibacy. He adds the argument from relics: early Christians sought relics “with the greatest fervor,” and “Cities, and, later, religious orders, would fight over the bones of great saints. … Yet never was there a single relic of Mary’s body?” Citing Michael O’Carroll’s Theotokos, he reports Syriac fragments of the narratives “being put as far back as the third century by one commentator,” and he reads Epiphanius’s own Panarion 79, where Mary “is like Elijah, who was virgin from his mother’s womb, always remained so, and was taken up, but has not seen death,” as a witness to the belief in Epiphanius’s day.10 Proponents also draw on the early datings of the narratives: Shoemaker and Bauckham date the earliest family, the Six Books apocryphon, to the fourth century or earlier, van Esbroeck and Gribomont concur in a fourth-century origin, and Shoemaker reads Epiphanius’s polemic against the Kollyridians (Panarion 79) as evidence of a Marian cult already in his century.11

Implicit in the apostolic deposit, without need of a documentary chain

In brief: the doctrine is contained implicitly in what was revealed to the apostles, and its truth does not depend on early documents.

Held by: Edward A. Wuenschel (Catholic), “The Definability of the Assumption,” Proceedings of the Catholic Theological Society of America (1947);12 Pius XII, Munificentissimus Deus (1950).13

This reading grants that the documents are late and holds that the doctrine does not depend on them. Wuenschel, writing while the definition was under consideration, states that “there is no authentic, circumstantial history of the Assumption” and that “there is no explicit apostolic tradition of the simple fact of the Assumption, or at least there is no real proof of such a tradition.” Neither, on his account, is required: “A doctrine may be defined as a revealed truth if it be implicit in the apostolic deposit—contained there in such a way that we must say God intended to reveal it and bring it to the knowledge of the Church by way of a progressive development,” and so “It is not at all necessary to forge an unbroken chain of historical testimonies reaching back to the apostolic age.” He adds that “the silence of the early centuries has been exaggerated,” pointing to the feast of the koimesis, and he locates the doctrine “implicit in the concrete, historical notion of the Divine Maternity, more proximately in the notion of Mary as the Second Eve, indissolubly associated with Christ in His victory over sin, concupiscence, and death.”12

The 1950 constitution grounds the definition on the present agreement of the Church: the “concordant teaching of the Church’s ordinary doctrinal authority and the concordant faith of the Christian people” “by itself and in an entirely certain and infallible way, manifests this privilege as a truth revealed by God and contained in that divine deposit” (§12). It holds that the theologians’ proofs “are based upon the Sacred Writings as their ultimate foundation” (§38), and it argues from Mary as “the new Eve,” whose struggle with her Son “should be brought to a close by the glorification of her virginal body” (§39).13 On this reading the doctrine’s warrant is the Church’s consensus about a truth contained implicitly in the apostolic deposit, and the early silence bears on how that truth came to be stated, not on whether it was revealed.

A mystery kept in the Church’s inner tradition and worship

In brief: Mary’s glory was a mystery kept within the Church’s worship rather than publicly preached, so its absence from the early public record is what one would expect.

Held by: Kallistos Ware (Orthodox), “The Sanctity and Glory of the Mother of God: Orthodox Approaches” (1984); Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox), “Panagia” (c. 1949); Paul Evdokimov (Orthodox), La nouveauté de l’Esprit (1977).4

On this reading the early silence is what the nature of the belief would lead one to expect. Lossky writes: “The Mother of God was never a theme of the public preaching of the apostles; while Christ was preached on the housetops, and proclaimed for all to know in an initiatory teaching addressed to the whole world, the mystery of his Mother was revealed only to those who were within the Church.” The belief “is not so much an object of faith as a foundation of our hope, a fruit of faith, ripened in tradition.” Evdokimov calls the Assumption “not a dogma” but “a fact of liturgical tradition and piety.”4

Ware reports that “most Orthodox are firmly convinced of the reality of the bodily Assumption,” while they “do not consider that it is desirable or even possible to define it as a dogma,” since “for anything to be defined as a dogma, it must have a clear basis in scripture.” The belief is held in the Church’s worship: the feast is usually called koimisis, “falling-asleep,” and its icon is “the icon of her Dormition.” Connected with the basic truths of the faith, the Assumption “follows from them not so much by any process of strict logic as by virtue of an inner coherence apprehended intuitively through prayer.”4 On this reading the belief’s warrant is the Church’s liturgical tradition, received within the Church and not dependent on an early public record.
