Are the apocryphal Transitus narratives the source of belief in Mary’s bodily assumption, or witnesses to an older tradition?

Disputed reading · on The Assumption / Dormition of Mary · witnesses: Pseudo-Melito, John of Thessalonica, Gregory of Tours, Paschasius Radbertus

Related readings: Does the tradition John of Damascus invokes trace to the apostles, or into the apocrypha? · Does the silence of the first centuries tell against an apostolic origin for the Assumption? · Did Mary die before she was assumed?

The passage

“The book which is called the Passing (Assumption) of holy Mary — apocryphal.”

— the Decretum Gelasianum (Gelasian Decree), among the books “not to be received,” as edited by E. von Dobschütz.1 The Decree circulates under the name of Pope Gelasius; Shoemaker, following von Dobschütz, assigns it to “an otherwise unknown Christian of sixth-century Gaul,”2 and Staples, citing The Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Faith, reports that it was probably written in the sixth century in Italy or Gaul and was most likely not a papal work.3

The question

The earliest narratives of Mary’s death and bodily assumption are the apocryphal Dormition texts, the Transitus Mariae (“Passing of Mary”) cycle, surviving in manuscripts of the fifth and sixth centuries; the churches that came to keep the feast both drew on these texts and disowned versions of them. What is disputed is whether the narratives are the origin of the belief or the earliest surviving witnesses to a tradition already in circulation. An Orthodox treatment of this specific question was not located; Ware’s survey of Orthodox approaches is given under the early silence.

Witnesses to an older account, later corrupted and corrected

In brief: the Transitus texts preserve an older account of Mary’s end, which their editors purged of later corruptions.

Held by: Antoine Wenger (Catholic), L’Assomption de la T. S. Vierge dans la tradition byzantine (1955);2 Tim Staples (Catholic), “The Assumption of Mary in History” (2019);3 the Latin Transitus ascribed to Melito of Sardis (Pseudo-Melito, c. 500);4 John of Thessalonica, Homily on the Dormition (c. 615).5

The texts’ own editors read them this way. Pseudo-Melito’s prologue presents the work as a correction of one “Leucius,” who “vitiated, by his impious writing, the assumption of the blessed ever-virgin Mary … to such a degree that it would be impious not only to read it in the church of God, but even to hear it,” and offers in its place “those things which we heard from the apostle John.”4 John of Thessalonica, introducing the feast to his city, holds that “those who were present then described her end truthfully, we are told,” and that “mischievous heretics later corrupted their accounts by adding words of their own”; he preaches the story after setting aside what he calls “fabricated stories.”5 On this reading the condemnations and corrections show the narratives being purged, not invented. Wenger, who on Shoemaker’s account took “a more positive approach to these ancient legends,” describes them as “streams from murky waters, but occasionally they bear in their muddy waters flecks of gold that will never be tarnished.”2

Staples argues that the narratives survive in “two different ‘families’ of manuscripts written in nine languages” that “all agree on Mary’s Assumption and they presuppose that the story was already widely known.” He reads the Gelasian list as a judgement on books, not on a doctrine: it “does not condemn any doctrines in the books it lists at all; it declares the books themselves to be apocryphal.”3 The modern datings are drawn on as well: Shoemaker places the emergence of the belief before the surviving manuscripts, and Bauckham dates the Six Books cosmic tour “from the fourth century at the latest, but perhaps considerably earlier”; Shoemaker finds in the Six Books an advanced Marian piety in a broadly orthodox, monotheist setting.6 He also notes that the surviving traditions adopt “a largely irenic tone, emphasizing the Virgin’s catholicity.”7 On this reading the narratives are late copies of an account already widely known, which the churches that kept the feast received once it had been cleansed.

The documentary source of the teaching

In brief: no record of the belief is older than these narratives, and the belief in Mary’s bodily assumption derives from them.

Held by: Heinrich Ewald (Protestant theologian and orientalist, Göttingen), review of Wright’s edition (1865);8 Agnes Smith Lewis (Presbyterian Syriac scholar), Apocrypha Syriaca (1902);8 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian), History of the Christian Church, vol. III, §83;9 James White (Protestant), “The Bodily Assumption, Scripture and Tradition” (2000).10,11

Ewald, reviewing William Wright’s 1865 edition of the Syriac narrative, held that “The whole cultus of Mary in the Papal Church rests upon this book; we might search in vain for any other foundation to it: notwithstanding the fact that it was excluded once again in early times from the list of canonical books by the Decretum Gelasii.” Smith Lewis, who edited the oldest Syriac manuscripts of the narrative, quoted the review in 1902 and wrote: “It is hardly necessary to say that I endorse the opinion of Dr Ewald.”8 Schaff holds that the feast “rests, however, on a purely apocryphal foundation”: “The entire silence of the apostles and the primitive church teachers respecting the departure of Mary stirred idle curiosity to all sorts of inventions,” and the apocryphal writings de transitu Mariae, then pseudo-Dionysius and Gregory of Tours, “for the first time contain the legend.”9 Webster holds that “the Transitus literature is the real source of the teaching of the assumption of Mary,”12 and White places “the first historical reference to the concept” in “the Transitus literature of the late fifth century.”10

The narratives, over sixty in nine languages before the tenth century, “first emerge into historical view from an uncertain past during the fifth and sixth centuries,” in Shoemaker’s words; he places the oldest of them, the Palm-of-the-Tree-of-Life tradition behind the Ethiopic Liber Requiei, in contact with a gnostic milieu.7 Holweck, who derives the doctrine from Apostolic Tradition, states that “the belief in the corporeal assumption of Mary is founded on the apocryphal treatise De Obitu S. Dominae, bearing the name of St. John, which belongs however to the fourth or fifth century.”13 The churches that kept the feast, on this reading, treated the corpus as suspect at every turn: the Gelasian list places it among the apocrypha;1 John of Thessalonica reports that “our ancestors distanced themselves from these accounts as not in accord with the catholic Church”;5 and Paschasius Radbertus (c. 860) warns against “that apocryphal work on the passing of the virgin” lest readers accept “doubtful things as certain.”14 On this reading the narratives are the documentary source from which the belief entered preaching: its earliest extant Latin statement, Gregory of Tours’s Glory of the Martyrs (c. 590), retells the Transitus account.15
