The Seven Sacraments (how the number was counted)

How did the Latin church come to teach that there are seven sacraments, and how have other communions counted them? The Latin word sacramentum began as the soldier's oath, Greek writers spoke of the mysteria, and the fathers applied the terms to many holy signs; no extant text before the twelfth century gives a closed list of seven. This page records the witnesses in order of earliest extant attestation: Tertullian, Ambrose and Augustine, who use the word without a count; the sixfold catalogue of Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500); Hugh of St Victor and Peter Lombard, whose Sentences (c. 1150) give the list of seven; Thomas Aquinas's account of why the number is seven; the decree of the Council of Florence (1439); Luther's count of two (1520); and the canon of the Council of Trent (1547) against those who say the sacraments are "more, or less, than seven." What the late appearance of the list shows about the origin of the seven is a disputed reading.

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 203)

Tertullian of Carthage uses sacramentum both of the soldier's oath of allegiance — the Christian's "divine sacrament" stands against the soldier's "human sacrament," and baptism is the swearing-in to "the warfare of the living God" — and of rites such as the Eucharist, writing of "the sacrament of the Eucharist"; he gives no count.1 Pierre Pourrat (Catholic, 1910) writes that "Baptism, the Eucharist and Matrimony were called sacramenta from the time of Tertullian."2

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose's catecheses for the newly baptized expound "the sacraments" as a small, concrete cluster of initiation rites — the font, the "spiritual seal" (the anointing), and the eucharist — each made effective by the word of Christ. He expounds what the rites are and do, and gives no count of them.3

Augustine of Hippo (c. 410)

Augustine defines a sacrament as a "sacred sign" of an invisible reality, the word joined to the element making a "visible word." The definition is broad, so many holy signs can be called sacramenta. In his letter to Januarius he says the sacraments of the new dispensation are "in number very few," naming baptism and the eucharist, "and such other things as are prescribed in the canonical Scriptures."4

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500)

Pseudo-Dionysius, in The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, devotes a chapter each to six sacred mysteries — baptism, the eucharist, the consecration of the ointment (chrism), the ordination of clergy, the consecration of monks, and the rites over the dead. His list includes monastic consecration and funerals and does not include penance or marriage. How his six bear on the later seven is part of the disputed reading below.5

Hugh of St Victor (c. 1134)

Hugh of St Victor, in On the Sacraments of the Christian Faith, sharpens the definition — a sacrament must not merely signify but contain grace — and rejects the loosest sign-definition (a picture or a written text is a sacred sign, but no sacrament). His working set is a large, tiered array sorted into ranks, not a list closed at any number.6

Peter Lombard (c. 1150)

Peter Lombard, in Book IV of the Sentences — the theology textbook of the medieval West — joined the tightened definition (a sign that causes the grace it signifies) to a named enumeration of seven: "baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage."7 D. J. Kennedy (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that Lombard's definition and enumeration "were at once universally accepted." Who first gave the number is reported differently: Kennedy records that "According to some writers Otto of Bamberg (1139), the Apostle of Pomerania, was the first who clearly adopted the number seven," and writes that "Most probably this honour belongs to Peter Lombard"; David S. Schaff (1907) names "Rolandus (afterwards Alexander III.) in his Sentences and Otto of Bamberg in a sermon" as giving seven "about the time of Peter the Lombard."8

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Thomas Aquinas gives reasons for the number of the list whose order, he writes, "is commonly adopted by all." In the Summa Theologiae (III, q. 65) he argues that exactly seven is fitting: the sacraments answer, one to one, the stages of the life of the body — baptism (birth), confirmation (growth), eucharist (nourishment), penance (healing), extreme unction (convalescence), orders (governance), matrimony (propagation) — a scheme he reinforces by a second derivation (remedies for the defects of sin) and a third (a virtue for each). He orders the seven and ranks the Eucharist first, "the greatest of all the sacraments," to which the others are ordained as to their end.9

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence, in its decree of union with the Armenians (Exsultate Deo), named the same seven, in the same order, as Lombard's list: "There are seven sacraments of the new Law, namely baptism, confirmation, eucharist, penance, extreme unction, orders and matrimony," which — unlike the sacraments of the old law — "both contain grace and bestow it on those who worthily receive them." The decree gives an account of the count: five for the individual, two for the whole church.10

Martin Luther (1520)

In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, Luther rejected the count of seven. Applying his test — a sacrament needs both a sign divinely instituted and a promise of forgiveness — he denied there are seven, opened with three (baptism, penance, the bread), and closed "strictly speaking, but two… baptism and the bread." He noted too that the one ancient witness cited for seven "omits marriage and so has only six."11

The Council of Trent (1547)

Answering the Reformers, the seventh session of the Council of Trent defined the enumeration under anathema: whoever says the sacraments "are more, or less, than seven, to wit, Baptism, Confirmation, the Eucharist, Penance, Extreme Unction, Order, and Matrimony," or that any one of them is not truly a sacrament, "let him be anathema."12

Disputed readings

The sevenfold count

How the late appearance of the list of seven has been read.

What does the late appearance of a list of seven sacraments (Peter Lombard, c. 1150) show about the origin of the seven?

Read as a recent enumeration (Luther, Calvin, Schaff); as seven rites from Christ (Trent, Jeremias II, the Synod of Jerusalem, Pourrat); and as mysteries not bound to a fixed number (Meyendorff, Ware, the Orthodox Church in America).
