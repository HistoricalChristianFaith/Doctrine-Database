Who Were the Magi?

Matthew gives them twelve verses and almost no detail: “wise men from the east” — magoi — who followed a star to Jerusalem, asked after the newborn “King of the Jews,” worshipped the child at Bethlehem, and went home “another way.” He does not say how many they were, where exactly they came from, what their names were, or whether they were kings. Later sources supplied each: the three, and the twelve; the astrologer-priests of Persia, the star-watchers of Babylon, or the wise men of Arabia; the kings read from the Psalms; Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar named in the Latin West; the relics enshrined at Cologne. This page records what was interpreted and claimed about the magi, earliest extant source first — the Gospel notice, the early Fathers who placed and numbered them, the Syriac romance that made them twelve, the kings-and-names tradition of the medieval West, and modern scholarship. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret the magi. How many they were, whether they were kings, where they came from, whether they were Zoroastrian priests, and how Balaam’s oracle relates to their star are set out, each reading with its holders, under Disputed readings. The magi’s star also figures in the early church’s debate over astrology: Ignatius and Chrysostom held that it ended the astrologers’ art.

Timeline

The Gospel of Matthew (c. 80)

Matthew alone reports that “when Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judaea in the days of Herod the king, behold, there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him.”1 Herod, troubled, learns from the chief priests that the Messiah is to be born at Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), sends the magi on, and asks to be told what they find. The star goes before them to the house, where they “fell down, and worshipped him: and when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.”2 Warned in a dream, they return home “another way,” and Herod, mocked, slaughters the children of Bethlehem. Matthew supplies the star, the gifts, and the worship — but neither the number of the magi, nor their names, nor their rank, nor any homeland more precise than “the east.”3

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

Ignatius gives the earliest extant reading of the star, and the earliest datable comment on the magi after Matthew; it concerns what their star did rather than who they were. Writing to the Ephesians on his way to martyrdom, Ignatius says that when Christ was manifested “a star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars,” so novel that sun, moon, and stars “formed a chorus” to it and men were seized with “agitation … as to whence this new spectacle came.”4 Its appearing, he goes on, was the undoing of the whole magical order: “every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared… and the old kingdom abolished.”5 For Ignatius the star that led the star-watchers ended their art. Tertullian, Chrysostom, and Gregory the Great later read the star the same way; the reading belongs to the debate over star-divination and is set out on the astrology timeline.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin is the earliest extant source to give the magi a homeland. Where Matthew says only “from the east,” Justin states — and repeats at least six times across the Dialogue with Trypho — that they were “Magi who came from Arabia,” who “worshipped Him, coming first to Herod … whom the Scripture calls king of Assyria,” and who at Bethlehem “presented Him with gifts, gold and frankincense, and myrrh.”6 He draws the homeland from prophecy: Justin reads Psalm 72 (the “kings of Arabia and Seba” bringing gifts, and “the gold of Arabia”) and Isaiah 8:4 (the “power of Damascus and spoils of Samaria” carried off “before the king of Assyria”) as fulfilled in the magi — and since he places Damascus “in the region of Arabia” and casts Herod as the “king of Assyria,” the wise men on his reading came from Arabia. Tertullian and Epiphanius later also place the magi in Arabia.7 Where the magi came from is set out under Disputed readings.

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus is the earliest extant voice to say what the magi’s three gifts mean. Matthew names gold, frankincense, and myrrh but leaves them uninterpreted; running through the infancy narrative to show that the God of the prophets and the God of the Gospel are one, Irenaeus reads each gift as a confession of who the child is: the magi “showed, by these gifts which they offered, who it was that was worshipped; myrrh, because it was He who should die and be buried for the mortal human race; gold, because He was a King…; and frankincense, because He was God.”8 Death, kingship, and Godhead, read off the opened treasure, form a threefold creed that later writers repeated; Irenaeus draws no number of magi from the gifts. In the same breath he grounds the star itself in Balaam’s oracle, the earliest extant link on this timeline between the two: “There shall come a star out of Jacob.”9

Tertullian (c. 207)

Tertullian is the earliest extant writer to connect the magi with royalty. Arguing that Isaiah’s child who takes “the riches of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria” (Isaiah 8:4) is the infant Christ, not a warrior, he reads the magi’s gold and frankincense as that Eastern tribute — and, quoting David’s psalm of “the kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer… gifts” and “the gold of Arabia,” twice reports that “the East generally regarded the magi as kings.”10 He reports that the East so regarded them, without asserting it in his own voice; the Latin adverb (fere, “well-nigh, almost”) marks a common estimate. In the same passages he keeps Justin’s Arabia homeland, identifying the gifts with the “strength of Damascus” and noting that Damascus “was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.”11

Origen (c. 248)

In Against Celsus Origen addresses the star, the magi’s motive, and the Balaam oracle. Answering the pagan Celsus — who had written that “Chaldeans” came to worship the infant — Origen first corrects the term (“one who cannot distinguish between Magi and Chaldeans … has falsified the Gospel narrative”) and then argues that the star was a new body, “unlike any of the other well-known planetary bodies … partaking of the nature of those celestial bodies which appear at times, such as comets”12 — not a horoscope but a one-off sign. He gives a mechanism for why the magi came: they worked by “familiar terms with evil spirits,” whose power was “overthrown” at Christ’s birth by “the light of divinity,” so that their sorceries failed and they “sought to know the reason of their failure” — a fuller form of Ignatius’s reading that the star ended magic.13 Third, he turns Irenaeus’s equation of the star with Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” into a transmission-history: the magi “possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam … and finding among them the prophecy about the star” recognized what the sign meant.14 He carries forward, too, the threefold gift-symbolism — “gold, viz., as to a king; myrrh, as to one who was mortal; and incense, as to a God”15 — and Anders Hultgård, among others, names him the earliest patristic witness to the number three, in a homily, surviving only in Latin, where the three visitors of Genesis 26 “can bear the figure” of the magi.16

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom gives the fullest patristic portrait of the magi as people. Preaching on Matthew 2 at Antioch, he takes them for “barbarians” of Persia — men who read the sky — yet raises them above the word: they came to the child “not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them,” and their long journey and fearless speech before Herod show them, for him, sincere, so that “to me at least they seem to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.”17 Their coming, he insists, was “not the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul,” as He once moved Cyrus — for him grace, not astral fate, is the cause — and God chose a star to summon them out of “exceeding condescension” to men accustomed to the heavens, who would not have heeded a prophet, a voice, or an angel. The object itself, he argues at length, was “not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all,” but “some invisible power” that moved north-to-south, shone at mid-day, hid and reappeared, and came down to stand over one roof — the whole sign given “to reprove the Jews for their insensibility” and to open “the door to the Gentiles.”18 His reading of the star as the end of star-craft, with his point that the magi “reasoned backwards” from a star to a newborn king, is set out on his astrology detail page.

Leo the Great (c. 450)

Leo is the fullest fifth-century Latin voice on the Epiphany. Preaching at Rome, he speaks throughout of three wise men, sets out the threefold gift-creed (“the incense they offer to God, the myrrh to Man, the gold to the King”), and reads the whole episode as the calling of the Gentiles: the wise men are the promised star-multitude seed of Abraham, and “in the three Magi let all people worship the Author of the universe,” while carnal Israel, reading the prophecy it will not believe, is displaced.19 Under that theme the Epiphany was kept in the West as the feast of the nations (⚠ unverified).20 Leo does not call the magi kings: the kingship in his sermons is Christ’s, the gold paid to the King, and he calls the visitors “wise men” — as his NPNF editor notes, “S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings.” Caesarius of Arles, whom Robin Jensen cross-references on the magi’s royal status, likewise reads the gold in his Epiphany sermon as a sign of Christ’s royalty.21

The Revelation of the Magi (Syriac; c. 500)

The Syriac Revelation of the Magi is the longest ancient account of the wise men, and its account differs at nearly every point from the Western tradition. Told in the magi’s own voice, it makes them not three but twelve (with hints of a company far larger), not Persians or Arabians but mystics of the far-eastern land of Shir at the shore of the great Ocean, and not astrologers but descendants of Seth who “pray in silence” and keep a prophecy Adam gave Seth of a coming star; the star itself, on its telling, is Christ, who takes a star’s form and then a child’s.22 Preserved whole only in the eighth-century Chronicle of Zuqnin but summarized in the West by the fifth-century Latin Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum — the pseudo-Chrysostom commentary through which it reached the Golden Legend and the medieval three-kings literature — the text is dated by its editor Brent Landau to the late second or early third century, and is anchored here at the form of the tradition attested by c. 500.23

The naming tradition (c. 500)

Matthew names none of them. The earliest extant attestation of the Western names Caspar (Gaspar), Melchior, and Balthasar is about AD 500, when they surface — as Bithisarea, Melichior, and Gathaspa — in the Excerpta Latina Barbari, a rough Latin rendering of a lost Greek chronicle written at Alexandria, and, some decades later, inscribed above the magi in the Ravenna mosaic of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo (c. 561–565), which the Wikipedia entry on the basilica says “is thought to be the earliest example” of the names in Christian art, though the inscriptions may be a later insertion (⚠ unverified).24 The portrait of three kings of three ages and three complexions, one for each of the three known continents, appears later, in the eighth-century Collectanea et Flores long printed among Bede’s works, whence the medieval iconography of a white, an Asian, and a black magus.25 The ancient Eastern churches transmit different sets — Syriac Larvandad, Gushnasaph, Hormisdas; Armenian Kagpha, Badadakharida, Badadilma; Ethiopic Hor, Basanater, Karsudan — and more often counted the magi at twelve. Raymond Brown and Richard Trexler treat the names as post-biblical accretion.26

The medieval cult of the Three Kings (1164–1298)

By the high Middle Ages the Latin West venerated the magi as three crowned kings, named, with a shrine and a body of relics, and three landmarks mark the period. Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), one of the most widely read books of the later Middle Ages, gathers the lore: it names the trio (in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin), keeps the Balaam pedigree and the three gifts, places their origin “from the end of Persia, from the Chaldeans,” and records that their bodies “were brought to Milan… and now be at Cologne.”27 Those relics had reached Cologne in 1164, carried north as spoils of Frederick Barbarossa’s conquest of Milan by his chancellor Rainald of Dassel, archbishop-elect of Cologne, and enshrined in the great triple sarcophagus of Nicholas of Verdun (c. 1180–1225), the largest reliquary in the Western world and one of Europe’s foremost pilgrimages.28 Marco Polo (Travels, c. 1298) reports being shown a rival tomb of the magi in Persia, at Saveh, the corpses “still entire, with the hair and beard remaining” — a rival claim to Cologne’s, and a record of a local Persian memory of the magi.29

Modern scholarship (a survey)

Modern study of the magi returns to four themes. First, the word: Raymond Brown’s commentary and the standard lexica take magoi as eastern priest-astrologers — the Median/Persian magu- caste that Herodotus lists among the tribes of the Medes and reports as indispensable at a sacrifice, later widening to cover astrologers and (pejoratively) sorcerers — and Brown reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers from an unspecified eastern land.30 Second, Brown and Richard Trexler treat nearly everything the popular Nativity adds — the number three, the crowns, the names Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar, the three continents — as post-biblical accretion on a text that supplies none of it; Trexler’s Journey of the Magi is a history of that reshaping.31 Third, Anders Hultgård sets the episode against the religious world of pre-Islamic Iran — a sky-watching priesthood, a Zoroastrian saviour-expectation, and Jewish–Iranian contact — as establishing that a Persian priestly interest in a royal birth-portent is historically intelligible, a plausibility of milieu rather than proof of the visit.32 Fourth, on whether the visit happened, Brown reads Matthew 2 as theological narrative working the Balaam and Moses templates, the star’s motion marking a composed sign, while Andreas Köstenberger and Alexander Stewart hold a real eastern embassy plausible within Herodian–Parthian politics.33

Disputed readings

How Matthew’s notice and the early sources on the magi have been read on five questions.

  • How many magi came to Bethlehem (Matthew 2:1–12)? — three, one for each gift; twelve, in the Syriac Revelation of the Magi; or a number Matthew leaves open.
  • Were the magi kings? — on whether the royal title of Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60 describes the visitors’ rank or the prophecies through which the scene was read.
  • Where did the magi of Matthew 2 come from? — Arabia, Persia (Media), Babylon, or an eastern land Matthew leaves unnamed.
  • Were the magi Zoroastrian priests? — on what the word magoi tells about the visitors’ religion.
  • How is Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) related to the magi’s star? — as a prophecy the magi possessed, or as a text the evangelist had in view.
