Who Were the Magi?

Matthew gives them twelve verses and almost no detail: “wise men from the east” — magoi — who followed a star to Jerusalem, asked after the newborn “King of the Jews,” worshipped the child at Bethlehem, and went home “another way.” He does not say how many they were, where exactly they came from, what their names were, or whether they were kings. The centuries supplied all of it. Out of Matthew’s bare notice the church built one of its richest legends: the three, then the twelve; the astrologer-priests of Persia, or the star-watchers of Babylon, or the wise men of Arabia; the kings crowned from the Psalms; Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar named at last in the Latin West; the relics enshrined at Cologne. This page is a history of what was interpreted and claimed about the magi, set out earliest source first — the Gospel notice, the earliest Fathers who placed and numbered them, the Syriac romance that made them twelve, the kings-and-names tradition of the medieval West, and the modern scholarship that grades it all. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret the magi, and the contested questions — how many were they? were they kings? Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians? did the episode really happen? — are weighed on their own argument pages below rather than settled by assertion. The magi’s star also sits at the heart of the church’s quarrel with astrology: did it vindicate the astrologers’ art, or (as Ignatius and Chrysostom held) abolish it?

Timeline

The Gospel of Matthew (c. 80)

The whole later tradition grows from a single dozen verses. Matthew alone reports that “when Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judaea in the days of Herod the king, behold, there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him.”1 Herod, troubled, learns from the chief priests that the Messiah is to be born at Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), sends the magi on, and asks to be told what they find. The star goes before them to the house, where they “fell down, and worshipped him: and when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.”2 Warned in a dream, they return home “another way,” and Herod, mocked, slaughters the children of Bethlehem. Matthew supplies the star, the gifts, and the worship — but neither the number of the magi, nor their names, nor their rank, nor any homeland more precise than “the east.”3

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

The earliest reading of the star, and the earliest datable comment on the magi at all, tells us nothing about the magi as people and everything about what their star did. Writing to the Ephesians on his way to martyrdom, Ignatius says that when Christ was manifested “a star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars,” so novel that sun, moon, and stars “formed a chorus” to it and men were seized with “agitation … as to whence this new spectacle came.”4 Its appearing, he goes on, was the undoing of the whole magical order: “every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared… and the old kingdom abolished.”5 The one group Scripture shows a star guiding to Christ were astrologers — and for Ignatius the star that led the star-watchers is the star that broke their art. That theological reading is the seed of a long tradition (Tertullian, Chrysostom, Gregory the Great); because it bears on the church’s quarrel with star-divination rather than on the magi’s identity, it is weighed adversarially on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument rather than settled here.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin is the first source to give the magi a homeland. Where Matthew says only “from the east,” Justin states flatly — and repeats at least six times across the Dialogue with Trypho — that they were “Magi who came from Arabia,” who “worshipped Him, coming first to Herod … whom the Scripture calls king of Assyria,” and who at Bethlehem “presented Him with gifts, gold and frankincense, and myrrh.”6 The homeland is not a travel report but a conclusion drawn from prophecy: Justin reads Psalm 72 (the “kings of Arabia and Seba” bringing gifts, and “the gold of Arabia”) and Isaiah 8:4 (the “power of Damascus and spoils of Samaria” carried off “before the king of Assyria”) as fulfilled in the magi — and since he places Damascus “in the region of Arabia” and casts Herod as the “king of Assyria,” the wise men must, on his reading, have come from Arabia.7 This is the fountainhead of the patristic Arabia tradition — later echoed by Tertullian (“the East regards the Magi almost as kings”) and Epiphanius — a minority stream against the more common reading that magoi names the astrologer-priests of Persia or Babylon. Whether the magi were Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians is weighed on its own argument pages below — Justin’s Arabia among them.

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus is the earliest surviving voice to say what the magi’s three gifts mean. Matthew names gold, frankincense, and myrrh but leaves them uninterpreted; running through the infancy narrative to prove that the God of the prophets and the God of the Gospel are one, Irenaeus reads each gift as a confession of who the child is: the magi “showed, by these gifts which they offered, who it was that was worshipped; myrrh, because it was He who should die and be buried for the mortal human race; gold, because He was a King…; and frankincense, because He was God.”8 Death, kingship, and Godhead, read off the opened treasure — a threefold creed that became the standard gift-symbolism and, once fixed, quietly underwrote the later inference of three magi (a gift apiece) and eventually three kings. In the same breath he grounds the star itself in Balaam’s oracle, “There shall come a star out of Jacob,”9 an early form of the Balaam link Origen would later develop. Irenaeus draws no number from the gifts himself; whether the gift count settles the magi count, and whether the Balaam oracle explains the star, are weighed on their own questions below.

Tertullian (c. 207)

Tertullian is the earliest surviving writer to pull the magi toward royalty. Arguing that Isaiah’s child who takes “the riches of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria” (Isaiah 8:4) is the infant Christ, not a warrior, he reads the magi’s gold and frankincense as that Eastern tribute — and, quoting David’s psalm of “the kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer… gifts” and “the gold of Arabia,” twice reports that “the East generally regarded the magi as kings.”10 The wording is careful and load-bearing: he does not assert the magi were kings but reports that the East so regarded them — the Latin adverb (fere, “well-nigh, almost”) marking a common estimate, not his own verdict. Still, by grafting the wise men onto Psalm 72’s tribute-bearing kings he plants the proof-text from which the later “three kings” tradition — and eventually the crowned Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar — would grow. In the same passages he keeps Justin’s Arabia homeland, identifying the gifts with the “strength of Damascus” and noting that Damascus “was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.”11 Whether the magi were kings, and whether they were Arabians rather than Persians or Babylonians, are weighed on their own argument pages; Tertullian supplies the earliest patristic footing for both readings, each drawn from prophecy rather than from any report about the magi themselves.

Origen (c. 248)

Origen is the first writer to give the whole episode a worked-out explanation, and a triple anchor on this timeline. Answering the pagan Celsus — who had sneered that mere “Chaldeans” were duped into worshipping an infant — Origen first corrects the term (“one who cannot distinguish between Magi and Chaldeans … has falsified the Gospel narrative”) and then argues that the star was a genuine new body, “unlike any of the other well-known planetary bodies … partaking of the nature of those celestial bodies which appear at times, such as comets”12 — not a horoscope but a one-off sign. His distinctive move is a mechanism for why the magi came: they worked by “familiar terms with evil spirits,” whose power was “overthrown” at Christ’s birth by “the light of divinity,” so that their sorceries failed and they “sought to know the reason of their failure” — a fuller form of Ignatius’s “the star abolished magic,” weighed on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument.13 Third, he turns Irenaeus’s bare equation of the star with Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” into a transmission-history: the magi “possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam … and finding among them the prophecy about the star” recognized what the sign meant.14 He carries forward, too, the threefold gift-symbolism — “gold, viz., as to a king; myrrh, as to one who was mortal; and incense, as to a God”15 — and is the Father most often named as the earliest witness to the number three, though that rests on a typological reading of a lost-in-Greek homily rather than any plain count, and he never fixes the number dogmatically.16 Whether the Balaam oracle explains the star, whether the star abolished astrology, and how many the magi were are each weighed on their own questions.

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom gives the fullest patristic portrait of the magi as people. Preaching on Matthew 2 at Antioch, he takes them for “barbarians” of Persia — men who read the sky — yet raises them above the word: they came to the child “not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them,” and their long journey and fearless speech before Herod prove them sincere, so that “to me at least they seem to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.”17 Their coming, he insists, was “not the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul,” as He once moved Cyrus — grace, not astral fate, the true cause — and God chose a star to summon them out of “exceeding condescension” to men accustomed to the heavens, who would not have heeded a prophet, a voice, or an angel. The object itself, he argues at length, was “not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all,” but “some invisible power” that moved north-to-south, shone at mid-day, hid and reappeared, and came down to stand over one roof — the whole sign staged “to reprove the Jews for their insensibility” and to open “the door to the Gentiles.”18 His handling of that star as the ruin of star-craft — and his point that the magi “reasoned backwards” from a star to a newborn king, so practised no genethlialogy at all — belongs to the church’s quarrel with star-divination, and is weighed on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument rather than settled here.

Leo the Great (c. 450)

Leo is the fullest fifth-century Latin voice on the Epiphany, and the point at which the Western reading of the magi settles into much of the shape the Middle Ages inherited — but not the crown. Preaching at Rome, he speaks throughout of three wise men, fixes the threefold gift-creed (“the incense they offer to God, the myrrh to Man, the gold to the King”), and reads the whole episode as the calling of the Gentiles: the wise men are the promised star-multitude seed of Abraham, and “in the three Magi let all people worship the Author of the universe,” while carnal Israel, reading the prophecy it will not believe, is displaced.19 That Gentiles-calling theme is what made the Epiphany, in the West, the feast of the nations.20 What Leo pointedly does not do is crown the magi as kings: for all his talk of gold and tribute, the kingship in his sermons is always Christ’s, the gold paid to the King, and he calls the visitors only “wise men” — a reserve the tradition’s own editor flags (“S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings”). The explicit crowning — the “three kings” read from Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60 — is a later development entrenched by the Latin liturgy; even Caesarius of Arles, the sixth-century preacher usually named for it, in fact keeps Leo’s reserve, so whether the fulfilment typology licenses calling the magi kings is weighed on the “were the magi kings?” argument page.21

The Revelation of the Magi (Syriac; c. 500)

The Syriac Revelation of the Magi is the fullest ancient account of the wise men — and it overturns nearly every figure the West would later settle. Told in the magi’s own voice, it makes them not three but twelve (with hints of a company far larger), not Persians or Arabians but mystics of the far-eastern land of Shir at the shore of the great Ocean, and not astrologers but descendants of Seth who “pray in silence” and keep a prophecy Adam gave Seth of a coming star; the star itself, on its telling, is Christ, who takes a star’s form and then a child’s.22 Preserved whole only in the eighth-century Chronicle of Zuqnin but summarized in the West by the fifth-century Latin Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum — the pseudo-Chrysostom commentary that carried it into the Golden Legend and the medieval three-kings lore — the text is commonly dated to the late second or early third century, and is anchored here at the later, securely attested form of the tradition.23 Its twelve is the principal ancient counter-evidence to the three that Origen’s gift-typology fixed; whether the magi were three, twelve, or unnumbered is weighed on the “how many magi?” argument page.

The naming tradition (c. 500)

Matthew names none of them; the familiar Western trio is a late arrival. The names Caspar (Gaspar), Melchior, and Balthasar cannot be traced back of about AD 500, when they first surface — as Bithisarea, Melichior, and Gathaspa — in the Excerpta Latina Barbari, a rough Latin rendering of a lost Greek chronicle written at Alexandria, and, at almost the same moment, inscribed above the magi in the Ravenna mosaic of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo (c. 561–565), the earliest naming of them in art.24 The now-standard portrait — three kings of three ages and three complexions, one for each of the three known continents — is later and pseudonymous, crystallizing only in the eighth-century Collectanea et Flores wrongly ascribed to Bede, whence the medieval iconography of a white, an Asian, and a black magus.25 That the names are late and local is plain from the ancient Eastern churches, which transmit wholly different sets — Syriac Larvandad, Gushnasaph, Hormisdas; Armenian Kagpha, Badadakharida, Badadilma; Ethiopic Hor, Basanater, Karsudan — and more often counted the magi at twelve, not three. No two traditions agree and none is early; the naming is a devotional filling of Matthew’s silence, not a recovery of fact, and modern scholarship grants the Western names no historical standing.26

The medieval cult of the Three Kings (1164–1298)

By the high Middle Ages the West had hardened the magi into a single devotional figure — three crowned kings, named, with a shrine and a body of relics — and three landmarks fix the stage. Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), the book every late-medieval reader knew, gathers the lore: it names the trio (in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin), keeps the Balaam pedigree and the three gifts, places their origin “from the end of Persia, from the Chaldeans,” and records that their bodies “were brought to Milan… and now be at Cologne.”27 Those relics had reached Cologne in 1164, carried north as spoils of Frederick Barbarossa’s conquest of Milan by his chancellor Rainald of Dassel, archbishop-elect of Cologne, and enshrined in the great triple sarcophagus of Nicholas of Verdun (c. 1180–1225), the largest reliquary in the Western world and one of Europe’s foremost pilgrimages — the act that fixed the magi’s number and rank in the Western imagination.28 Yet the bodies were never uncontested: Marco Polo (Travels, c. 1298) reports being shown a rival tomb of the magi in Persia, at Saveh, the corpses “still entire, with the hair and beard remaining” — a claim that cannot both be true with Cologne’s, but that attests a live Persian memory of the magi, telling for the Persia-origin reading against Justin’s Arabia.29 As history the cult adds nothing the earlier tradition lacked — the three, the crowns, and the relics are all reception, not record — but its Persian tomb is a data point for the “Persia, Babylon, or Arabia?” question, telling for the Persia reading.

Modern scholarship (a survey)

Critical study grades the whole tradition, and four results recur. First, the lexical one, on which the field is effectively unanimous: magoi denotes eastern priest-astrologers — the Median/Persian magu- caste that Herodotus lists among the tribes of the Medes and reports as indispensable at a sacrifice, later widening to cover astrologers and (pejoratively) sorcerers — and in no register does it mean kings; Raymond Brown’s standard commentary reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers from an unspecified eastern land.30 Second, nearly everything the popular Nativity adds — the number three, the crowns, the names Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar, the three continents — is demonstrably later accretion on a text that supplies none of it; Richard Trexler’s Journey of the Magi is the fullest history of that reshaping.31 Third, a distinct strand (Anders Hultgård foremost) sets the episode against the real religious world of pre-Islamic Iran — a sky-watching priesthood, a Zoroastrian saviour-expectation marked by a star, and Jewish–Iranian contact — establishing that a Persian priestly interest in a royal birth-portent is historically intelligible (plausibility of milieu, not proof of the visit).32 Fourth, on whether it happened, scholarship runs a genuine spectrum — from Brown’s reserve (Matthew as theologian working the Balaam and Moses templates, the star’s impossible motion reading as a composed sign) to defenders who hold a real eastern embassy plausible within Herodian–Parthian politics. This survey opens the contested questions — how many? were they kings? Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians? Zoroastrian priests? did it happen? — but settles none of them; each is weighed on its own argument page, on the evidence rather than on any prior assumption.33

Arguments

Related arguments — the magi’s homeland

Matthew says only that the wise men came “from the east” and calls them magoi; three homelands have been proposed, each with its own proponents and its own kind of evidence. Persia (or Media) is reached by the word — magoi is the Greek name of the Iranian priestly caste — and is the earliest and majority reading. Babylon is reached by the star-science — Chaldean astronomy, and the Jewish exiles among whom Daniel was set over the Babylonian wise men. Arabia is reached by the gifts — Arabian gold, frankincense, and myrrh, and the prophecies of Sheba and Midian bringing tribute to Zion. Each rival reading is weighed on its own page.

Did the magi come from Persia (Media)? (assessment: plausible)

The Persia reading is the best-anchored of the three, because it rests on the one datum Matthew supplies: the word magoi, the Greek name (from Old Persian magu-) of the Median/Persian priestly caste that Herodotus lists among the Median tribes and reports as indispensable at every sacrifice. It is early and majority in the tradition — Clement of Alexandria and Chrysostom name them Persians — and the modern study of the Iranian background (Hultgård, Panaino, Yamauchi) makes a Persian priestly interest in a royal birth-star intelligible. What holds it at plausible rather than higher: Matthew names no country, only “the east,” and magos had widened by the first century to “astrologer” of any nation, so the word fixes the wise men’s class more surely than their homeland. The leading reading — a best guess, honestly labelled a guess. Plausible.34

Did the magi come from Babylon (Chaldea)? (assessment: plausible)

The Babylon reading shares the “eastern astrologer” strength of Persia and adds a mechanism the others lack. Babylonia was antiquity’s premier centre of star-science, so “we have seen his star in the east” sits naturally with a Chaldean sky-watcher; and the Jewish community exiled to Babylon — where Daniel was set “over all the wise men of Babylon” — is a credible channel for a Jewish star-prophecy to reach Gentile diviners. Held by a patristic minority (Maximus of Turin, Theodotus of Ancyra) and by modern astronomers who read the star as a Babylonian conjunction (Hughes, Parpola). But it rests on reconstruction, not the text: Matthew writes magoi, not “Chaldeans” — a distinction Origen presses against Celsus — the Daniel-to-magi transmission is nowhere attested, and the astronomical identifications of the star are contested even among their proponents. A strong reconstruction, short of proof. Plausible.35

Did the magi come from Arabia? (assessment: weak)

The Arabia reading is the earliest the church named — Justin, Tertullian, and Epiphanius — but it is driven by prophecy against the word. Its footing is the gifts (gold, frankincense, and myrrh are Arabian products) and the tribute-prophecies that name Arabian peoples (Sheba, Seba, Midian, Ephah in Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60:6); the Fathers deduce Arabia from those texts, not from any report. A modern minority (Dwight Longenecker) revives it as a Nabataean thesis — the strongest version, since Nabataea sat on Judaea’s south-eastern doorstep and controlled the incense trade — but it too must argue past Matthew’s magoi, which names no Arabian class, and the gifts, traded everywhere, fix no origin. First in the tradition, last on the evidence. Weak as history.36

How many magi were there? (assessment: weak)

The number of the magi is not given by Matthew, who names three kinds of gift — gold, frankincense, and myrrh — but no number of givers. The familiar three is an inference from those three gifts: Origen is the earliest datable witness to it (by a typological reading of Genesis 26:26, not a count in the Gospel), Leo the Great made it standard in the Latin West, and the medieval cult fixed it as three crowned, named kings. As a tradition three is early and near-universal; as history it does not hold — three kinds of gift do not fix three bearers, and the ancient counts run wide, from two, three, and four in the Roman catacombs to as many as eight on a vase and twelve in the Syriac East (the Revelation of the Magi). A number nobody remembered and everyone supplied. Weak as history.37

Were the magi kings? (assessment: weak)

Whether the magi were kings turns on a rank Matthew never gives: he calls them magoi — the eastern priest-astrologer caste that the oldest sources (Herodotus) set apart from, and beneath, kings — not monarchs. The crown is read into the scene from prophecy: Psalm 72:10–11 and Isaiah 60:3, 6, the tribute-bearing kings whose gold and incense match the gifts. It is prophecy-driven, not evidence-driven, and the chronology confirms it — no source calls the magi kings before the sixth century; Tertullian reports only that the East “well-nigh” regarded them so (c. 207), and Leo the Great, steeped in the tribute imagery, still declines the title, calling them “wise men” and paying the kingship to Christ. The explicit crowning arrives only later, with the Latin liturgy of the “three kings” (even Caesarius of Arles, usually named for it, keeps Leo’s reserve). As typology — the episode fulfilling the prophesied homage of the nations’ kings, the Gentiles’ calling — the reading is coherent and liturgically load-bearing; but that is what the scene signifies, not the visitors’ rank. Weak as history.38

Were the magi Zoroastrian priests? (assessment: plausible for the core; weak for the whole)

Whether the magi were Zoroastrian priests splits into two claims that deserve different verdicts. That they were eastern priest-astrologers of the Iranian magu- caste is plausible, even probable — it is the identity-side of the Persia reading, resting on the word magoi itself (the Greek name of that priesthood) and named early by Clement of Alexandria and Chrysostom. The fuller claim — that they were orthodox Zoroastrians who came because their own scriptures taught them to expect a saviour (the Saoshyant) heralded by a star — is weak: “Zoroastrian” is anachronistically tidy for the diverse first-century Magian religion, and the decisive Saoshyant-and-star mechanism is largely read back from the ninth-century Pahlavi books, its earliest star-heralded form surviving only in a medieval Christian chronicle. The Avestan saviour-hope is genuinely pre-Christian, but a Persian priesthood reading Jesus off its own star-prophecy is a modern harmonization the sources will not bear; and magos had by Matthew’s day widened to any eastern astrologer or sorcerer (Simon Magus, Bar-Jesus), so the word fixes a class loosely, not a creed. Plausible for the priest-astrologer core; weak for the Zoroastrian-Saoshyant reading.39

Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s “star out of Jacob”? (assessment: plausible for the literary allusion; weak for the historical transmission)

The Balaam reading answers the question the whole episode raises — why these Gentiles read this star as a Jewish king’s — and it splits into two claims that deserve different verdicts. The literary claim, that Matthew tells the star episode with Balaam’s oracle (“there shall come a Star out of Jacob,” Numbers 24:17) in view and casts the magi as Balaam’s successors, is plausible-to-probable: the verse was a leading messianic proof-text in first-century Judaism (Qumran’s 4Q175, the Damascus Document, and Rabbi Akiva’s hailing of “Bar Kokhba,” son of the star), Balaam is himself an Eastern star-reading seer “from … the mountains of the east,” and the Balaam–Balak template runs through the whole of Matthew 2 — a case endorsed by Brown and by Davies and Allison (“Matthew probably thought of the magi as Balaam’s successors”). The historical claim — that the actual magi possessed and preserved Balaam’s prophecy and journeyed because they had read it, worked up by Origen and stated as flat history by Eusebius — is weak: no channel of transmission is attested, the “book of Balaam” behind it is a lost conjecture, and the tradition reads as aetiology composed backward from the Gospel. The oracle very likely stands behind Matthew’s narrative; it does not follow that it stood in the magi’s hands. Plausible for the literary allusion, weak for the historical transmission.40

Is the visit of the magi historical? (assessment: contested)

Whether the episode happened is the question beneath all the others, and it is genuinely disputed. Matthew alone tells it — as event, in a dated reign, with named places and gifts — and the historical reading is not implausible in its world: an eastern priestly delegation honouring a client-king over a royal portent is documented in the very generation, in the magi-escorted homage of Tiridates of Armenia to Nero (AD 66), down even to the “returned another way” detail; the Iranian milieu makes the magi’s interest intelligible, and Herod’s alarm fits his character. But the reading is pressed hard from the other side: the episode rests on a single source that Luke and Josephus do not corroborate (Josephus, who dwells on Herod’s cruelties, records no Bethlehem massacre), it is densely shaped to fulfil prophecy and to re-tell the infancy of Moses (Raymond Brown), and the star it turns on behaves like no astronomical body and so anchors nothing outside the narrative. The wiki does not weigh against the account merely because it involves a miracle — the star and the warning-dream are granted as possible, not ruled out — but a milieu that permits an event is not evidence that it occurred. Strong by the historian’s lights, weak by the source-critic’s; the star’s own nature is weighed separately on the astrology timeline. Contested.41
