Who Were the Magi?

Matthew gives them twelve verses and almost no detail: “wise men from the east” — magoi — who followed a star to Jerusalem, asked after the newborn “King of the Jews,” worshipped the child at Bethlehem, and went home “another way.” He does not say how many they were, where exactly they came from, what their names were, or whether they were kings. The centuries supplied all of it. Out of Matthew’s bare notice the church built one of its richest legends: the three, then the twelve; the astrologer-priests of Persia, or the star-watchers of Babylon, or the wise men of Arabia; the kings crowned from the Psalms; Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar named at last in the Latin West; the relics enshrined at Cologne. This page is a history of what was interpreted and claimed about the magi, set out earliest source first — the Gospel notice, the earliest Fathers who placed and numbered them, the Syriac romance that made them twelve, the kings-and-names tradition of the medieval West, and the modern scholarship that grades it all. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret the magi, and the contested questions — how many were they? were they kings? Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians? did the episode really happen? — are weighed on their own argument pages below rather than settled by assertion. The magi’s star also sits at the heart of the church’s quarrel with astrology: did it vindicate the astrologers’ art, or (as Ignatius and Chrysostom held) abolish it?

Timeline

The Gospel of Matthew (c. 80)

The whole later tradition grows from a single dozen verses. Matthew alone reports that “when Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judaea in the days of Herod the king, behold, there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him.”1 Herod, troubled, learns from the chief priests that the Messiah is to be born at Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), sends the magi on, and asks to be told what they find. The star goes before them to the house, where they “fell down, and worshipped him: and when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.”2 Warned in a dream, they return home “another way,” and Herod, mocked, slaughters the children of Bethlehem. Matthew supplies the star, the gifts, and the worship — but neither the number of the magi, nor their names, nor their rank, nor any homeland more precise than “the east.”3

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

The earliest reading of the star, and the earliest datable comment on the magi at all, tells us nothing about the magi as people and everything about what their star did. Writing to the Ephesians on his way to martyrdom, Ignatius says that when Christ was manifested “a star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars,” so novel that sun, moon, and stars “formed a chorus” to it and men were seized with “agitation … as to whence this new spectacle came.”4 Its appearing, he goes on, was the undoing of the whole magical order: “every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared… and the old kingdom abolished.”5 The one group Scripture shows a star guiding to Christ were astrologers — and for Ignatius the star that led the star-watchers is the star that broke their art. That theological reading is the seed of a long tradition (Tertullian, Chrysostom, Gregory the Great); because it bears on the church’s quarrel with star-divination rather than on the magi’s identity, it is weighed adversarially on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument rather than settled here.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin is the first source to give the magi a homeland. Where Matthew says only “from the east,” Justin states flatly — and repeats at least six times across the Dialogue with Trypho — that they were “Magi who came from Arabia,” who “worshipped Him, coming first to Herod … whom the Scripture calls king of Assyria,” and who at Bethlehem “presented Him with gifts, gold and frankincense, and myrrh.”6 The homeland is not a travel report but a conclusion drawn from prophecy: Justin reads Psalm 72 (the “kings of Arabia and Seba” bringing gifts, and “the gold of Arabia”) and Isaiah 8:4 (the “power of Damascus and spoils of Samaria” carried off “before the king of Assyria”) as fulfilled in the magi — and since he places Damascus “in the region of Arabia” and casts Herod as the “king of Assyria,” the wise men must, on his reading, have come from Arabia.7 This is the fountainhead of the patristic Arabia tradition — later echoed by Tertullian (“the East regards the Magi almost as kings”) and Epiphanius — a minority stream against the more common reading that magoi names the astrologer-priests of Persia or Babylon. Whether the magi were Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians is weighed on its own argument page.

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus is the earliest surviving voice to say what the magi’s three gifts mean. Matthew names gold, frankincense, and myrrh but leaves them uninterpreted; running through the infancy narrative to prove that the God of the prophets and the God of the Gospel are one, Irenaeus reads each gift as a confession of who the child is: the magi “showed, by these gifts which they offered, who it was that was worshipped; myrrh, because it was He who should die and be buried for the mortal human race; gold, because He was a King…; and frankincense, because He was God.”8 Death, kingship, and Godhead, read off the opened treasure — a threefold creed that became the standard gift-symbolism and, once fixed, quietly underwrote the later inference of three magi (a gift apiece) and eventually three kings. In the same breath he grounds the star itself in Balaam’s oracle, “There shall come a star out of Jacob,”9 an early form of the Balaam link Origen would later develop. Irenaeus draws no number from the gifts himself; whether the gift count settles the magi count, and whether the Balaam oracle explains the star, are weighed on their own questions below.

Tertullian (c. 207)

Tertullian is the earliest surviving writer to pull the magi toward royalty. Arguing that Isaiah’s child who takes “the riches of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria” (Isaiah 8:4) is the infant Christ, not a warrior, he reads the magi’s gold and frankincense as that Eastern tribute — and, quoting David’s psalm of “the kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer… gifts” and “the gold of Arabia,” twice reports that “the East generally regarded the magi as kings.”10 The wording is careful and load-bearing: he does not assert the magi were kings but reports that the East so regarded them — the Latin adverb (fere, “well-nigh, almost”) marking a common estimate, not his own verdict. Still, by grafting the wise men onto Psalm 72’s tribute-bearing kings he plants the proof-text from which the later “three kings” tradition — and eventually the crowned Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar — would grow. In the same passages he keeps Justin’s Arabia homeland, identifying the gifts with the “strength of Damascus” and noting that Damascus “was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.”11 Whether the magi were kings, and whether they were Arabians rather than Persians or Babylonians, are weighed on their own argument pages; Tertullian supplies the earliest patristic footing for both readings, each drawn from prophecy rather than from any report about the magi themselves.

Origen (c. 248)

Origen is the first writer to give the whole episode a worked-out explanation, and a triple anchor on this timeline. Answering the pagan Celsus — who had sneered that mere “Chaldeans” were duped into worshipping an infant — Origen first corrects the term (“one who cannot distinguish between Magi and Chaldeans … has falsified the Gospel narrative”) and then argues that the star was a genuine new body, “unlike any of the other well-known planetary bodies … partaking of the nature of those celestial bodies which appear at times, such as comets”12 — not a horoscope but a one-off sign. His distinctive move is a mechanism for why the magi came: they worked by “familiar terms with evil spirits,” whose power was “overthrown” at Christ’s birth by “the light of divinity,” so that their sorceries failed and they “sought to know the reason of their failure” — a fuller form of Ignatius’s “the star abolished magic,” weighed on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument.13 Third, he turns Irenaeus’s bare equation of the star with Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” into a transmission-history: the magi “possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam … and finding among them the prophecy about the star” recognized what the sign meant.14 He carries forward, too, the threefold gift-symbolism — “gold, viz., as to a king; myrrh, as to one who was mortal; and incense, as to a God”15 — and is the Father most often named as the earliest witness to the number three, though that rests on a typological reading of a lost-in-Greek homily rather than any plain count, and he never fixes the number dogmatically.16 Whether the Balaam oracle explains the star, whether the star abolished astrology, and how many the magi were are each weighed on their own questions.

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom gives the fullest patristic portrait of the magi as people. Preaching on Matthew 2 at Antioch, he takes them for “barbarians” of Persia — men who read the sky — yet raises them above the word: they came to the child “not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them,” and their long journey and fearless speech before Herod prove them sincere, so that “to me at least they seem to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.”17 Their coming, he insists, was “not the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul,” as He once moved Cyrus — grace, not astral fate, the true cause — and God chose a star to summon them out of “exceeding condescension” to men accustomed to the heavens, who would not have heeded a prophet, a voice, or an angel. The object itself, he argues at length, was “not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all,” but “some invisible power” that moved north-to-south, shone at mid-day, hid and reappeared, and came down to stand over one roof — the whole sign staged “to reprove the Jews for their insensibility” and to open “the door to the Gentiles.”18 His handling of that star as the ruin of star-craft — and his point that the magi “reasoned backwards” from a star to a newborn king, so practised no genethlialogy at all — belongs to the church’s quarrel with star-divination, and is weighed on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument rather than settled here.
