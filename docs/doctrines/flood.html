The Historicity of Noah's Flood

Genesis 6–9 tells of a Flood that destroyed all humanity except Noah, his family, and the creatures in the ark. The New Testament refers to it as a past event (2 Pet 2:5; 3:5–6; 1 Pet 3:20; Heb 11:7) and as a type — of judgment and of baptism — and Jesus sets "the days of Noah" beside his own coming (Matt 24:37–39; Luke 17:26–27). This timeline records how Jewish and Christian writers have read the narrative, earliest extant attestation first: whether they read it as an account of events, with or without figurative senses drawn from it; whether they held that it destroyed all humanity outside the ark (anthropological universality) and that its waters covered the whole earth (geographic universality); and when writers began to read it as a local flood. The witnesses from the New Testament to Bede (c. 735) read the Flood as an event, several of them also drawing figures of the Church, the cross and baptism from it; Isaac La Peyrère (1655) argued for a local flood. How these witnesses have been read in modern scholarship is set out under Disputed readings.

Related doctrines: Primeval history (Genesis 6–9) — see also the sons of God and the Nephilim (the giants of Genesis 6, whose wickedness the Flood comes to judge; the identity of the "sons of God" divides the same witnesses — Chrysostom's Sethites against Jerome's angelic reading — on both pages) · the dating of Noah's Flood (when the Flood took place — the Masoretic, Septuagint, and Samaritan chronologies of Genesis 5 and 11).

Timeline

Witnesses are listed by the date of their earliest extant attestation. Questions about how the record has been read are set out below under Disputed readings.

The New Testament (c. AD 50–90)

The earliest witness on this timeline is the New Testament itself, which refers to the Flood in four strands of its writings, each using it within an argument about something else. Jesus makes "the days of Noe" the type of his own coming: as the pre-Flood generation "knew not until the flood came, and took them all away, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be" (Matt 24:37–39; Luke 17:26–27, where "the flood came, and destroyed them all"). Hebrews lists Noah in its roll-call of exemplars of faith — "warned of God of things not seen as yet," he "prepared an ark to the saving of his house; by the which he condemned the world" (Heb 11:7). 1 Peter sets out an ark-and-Flood typology of baptism — "eight souls were saved by water. The like figure whereunto even baptism doth also now save us" (1 Pet 3:20–21); Theophilus, Chrysostom, and Jerome also count eight. And 2 Peter argues from the Flood twice: God "saved Noah the eighth person… bringing in the flood upon the world of the ungodly" (2:5), and, against the scoffers "who willingly are ignorant," "the world that then was, being overflowed with water, perished" (3:5–6) — the past judgment by water serving as the premise of the argument that a judgment by fire is still to come.1

Josephus (c. AD 100)

Flavius Josephus, retelling Genesis for a Greco-Roman readership in the Antiquities of the Jews (Book I), narrates the Flood as dated history — the earliest non-canonical witness on this timeline, and a Jewish historian rather than a Christian exegete. He retells the biblical detail: the ark "four stories high, three hundred cubits long, fifty cubits broad, and thirty cubits high," stocked with "all sorts of living creatures, the male and his female, for the preservation of their kinds"; and "when God gave the signal, and it began to rain, the water poured down forty entire days, till it became fifteen cubits higher than the earth." Noah tests the receding waters by bird — the dove returning "covered with mud, and bringing an olive branch" — and after the ark "rested on the top of a certain mountain in Armenia" he offers sacrifice, God binding himself that "the water shall no more overspread the earth," sealed with the bow.2 He argues the Flood's historicity from pagan corroboration: "Now all the writers of barbarian histories make mention of this flood, and of this ark; among whom is Berosus the Chaldean" — naming Hieronymus the Egyptian, Mnaseas, and Nicolaus of Damascus beside him as further witnesses to the same tradition.3 And he closes with reports that the vessel's remains survived and could still be seen: from Berosus, "there is still some part of this ship in Armenia, at the mountain of the Cordyaeans," whose bitumen "some people carry off … [as] amulets"; from Nicolaus of Damascus, "a great mountain in Armenia, over Minyas, called Baris," where "one who was carried in an ark came on shore upon the top of it; and … the remains of the timber were a great while preserved" — "this," Josephus adds, "might be the man about whom Moses the legislator of the Jews wrote."4

Theophilus of Antioch (c. 185)

Theophilus of Antioch, writing to the pagan Autolycus, treats the Flood as a datable event inside a continuous Adam-to-Marcus-Aurelius chronology — "during Noah's life, in his 600th year, the flood came," "the total number of years, therefore, till the flood, was 2242."5 He sets the Mosaic account polemically against the Greek flood-legends: there was one flood only, which "never again would there be… as neither indeed has there been, nor ever shall be," and "eight human beings were preserved in the ark… prepared by God's direction, not by Deucalion, but by Noah" — Noah, his three sons, and their wives.6 Noah first "announced to the men then alive that there was a flood coming," calling them to repentance.7 Against Plato — who limited his deluge to "the plains," so that those who "fled to the highest hills saved themselves" — Theophilus holds that the waters covered the highest hills: "the water overtopped every high hill 15 cubits. And thus the race of all the men that then were was destroyed."8 He closes by appealing to the ark's reported remains "to this day… in the Arabian mountains" and labelling the whole "the history of the deluge." He is the earliest post-apostolic Christian witness on this timeline, and he gives the narrative no allegorical reading.9

Origen (c. 248)

Origen, whose Homilies on Genesis give the ark an extended spiritual reading, defends the narrative as history against the pagan Celsus. Answering Celsus, who dismissed the deluge and the "monstrous ark" as "falsifying and recklessly altering the story of Deucalion," he defends the ark as a real structure of stated dimensions: against the capacity objection he reads the cubits geometrically, so the vessel "resembled an extensive city," and marvels that "by the providential arrangement of God, the elements of all the races were brought into it, that the earth might receive again the seeds of all living things" — noting that Celsus, "not being able to say anything against the history of the deluge," could only call it "monstrous."10 He treats the Flood as a single event, not an astrologically recurring world-cycle but a judgment whose cause "we declare to be the extensive prevalence of wickedness," and he faults Celsus for the double standard of allegorizing the Greek theogonies to dignify them while branding the Mosaic narrative a fable "clumsily invented for infant children."11 And in the Homilies on Genesis, where he builds his most elaborate spiritual reading of the ark — its decks the soul's ascent, Noah a type of "the true Noah," Christ, the ark the Church — he first works through the construction of the ark in literal detail.12

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373)

Ephrem the Syrian, the Syriac poet and exegete, is the earliest Syriac witness on this timeline and reads the Flood as a literal history. In his Commentary on Genesis he takes the ark's construction as a real building project — pressing the practical questions of "where was Noah to get gopher wood? pitch? iron? or rope?" and dating the build across the hundred years of the generation's reprieve — and counts the flood's calendar to the day: "in the six hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, on the seventeenth day of the month, the springs of the great abyss burst forth and the flood gates of heaven were opened… The deluge came and God blotted out all flesh." He totals Noah's time in the ark at "three hundred sixty-five days," then turns the number into a chronological argument — that the solar year was reckoned by Noah's household and so cannot have been "invented" by the Chaldeans and Egyptians — an argument from chronology against pagan claims to priority, a kind of argument Theophilus also makes. He lands the ark on Mt. Qardu (the Peshitta reading, with Josephus and the Targums), not the Armenian "Ararat"; reads the "sons of God" as the line of Seth against the angelic reading; and takes the 120 years of Genesis 6:3 as a real space for repentance. His one sustained figure — the miraculous peace of predator and prey on the ark, "the wolves and the lambs entered together," read in his hymns as a restored pre-Fall state and a type of the Church — is drawn from a scene he narrates as event.13

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil of Caesarea, the Cappadocian bishop, never wrote on the Flood directly — his Hexaemeron closes with the sixth day and never reaches Noah — and his references to it are incidental: he refers to the deluge as a real, datable, worldwide event. In his letter to Optimus he reads the generations "from Cain to the flood" as a literal count and calls the deluge a real "punishment… brought on the whole earth, because sin was everywhere spread abroad," contrasting that bygone judgment with Christ, who "takes away the sin of the world."14 In On the Holy Spirit, arguing that the Son is not "later" than the Father, he cites the Flood and Sodom as examples of ordinary temporal order — "what happened in Noah's time is prior to what happened to the men of Sodom" — taking "the events of the history of the men of Sodom," and the Flood with them, as historical markers in a treatise on another subject.15 He does not argue for the Flood's historicity; he argues from it.

Ambrose of Milan (c. 380)

Ambrose of Milan, in On Noah and the Ark (De Noe), reads the Flood both according to the letter and allegorically. He expounds the ark as a figure of the human body — its 300×50×30 cubits read off the body's own proportions — and of the Church, and the Flood as "a type of the purging of our soul," and he reads the flood as a real, dated event: he carries the chronology of Genesis 7–8 down to the day — "in the six hundredth year, according to Hebrew truth, on the first month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month, the flood began" — adjudicates the Hebrew and Greek month-reckonings, and marks the seam in his own hand, saying a thing "according to the letter" and then drawing "a deeper meaning" from it.16 The ark's three hundred cubits he reads as the cross "by whose type the righteous man was delivered from the flood," and its side-door as both the Church's members and the Church itself — an ark = Church / Christ's-cross typology that Augustine also gives, in the City of God (c. 420).17

John Chrysostom (c. 388)

John Chrysostom, preaching the Homilies on Genesis at Antioch, reads the Flood in the Antiochene literal-historical manner and praises the precision of the narrative: "See the precision of Scripture, how it not only taught us the year of the deluge but also made clear the month and the day"; the "floodgates of heaven" he treats as considerate speech — Scripture "describing everything in a human manner," not literal sluice gates — while affirming that the waters "inundated the whole world."18 For Chrysostom the dated record belongs to a moral history: the 120 years of Gen 6:3 are a reprieve in which that generation might "erase their sins by conversion," and the 40 days of rain are the same mercy stretched out, since God could have "submerged everything in one downpour" but delayed "that at least some of them might come to their senses."19 "The Lord God shut the ark from the outside" he glosses as divine considerateness, sparing Noah the sight of the drowning world.20 The judgment is anthropologically total — "there was absolutely nothing left standing… everything was annihilated along with the human race," with the eight in the ark "alone… saved."21 He calls the angelic reading of the "sons of God" "fanciful" and reads them as the line of Seth, drawing figure and moral from the events of the narrative.22

Jerome (c. 392)

Jerome, the biblical translator and philologist, reads the Flood as real, dated history, and reads it against the Hebrew. In his Hebrew Questions on Genesis he rejects a reading he says "many" hold in error: the "hundred and twenty years" of Genesis 6:3 were not a shortening of the human lifespan — "human life, as many err, is not condensed into 120 years" — but a reprieve granted that generation for repentance, as he argues from the ages men still reached after the Flood ("after the flood, Abraham lived for 175 years, and others for over 200 and 300 years"), which God cut short by twenty years, bringing "about the flood in the 100th year designated for doing penance."23 He treats the ark's window, the pitched planks, the waters receding to "the hidden veins of the abyss," and the sending of raven and dove as a real sequence of real events, and weighs Noah's righteousness "in his generation" as relative to the men actually alive with him — and, like Ambrose and Augustine, also reads figures from it: the ark's three hundred cubits are the cross (the letter T, the sign for 300), its fifty the psalm of penance, its thirty the age of the baptized Christ, and the eight souls saved the "octave" in which "'its counterpart, the church,' says Peter, 'now saves you'" (1 Pet 3:20–21); and where the identity of the "sons of God" is disputed his philology inclines to the angelic reading (Aquila's "sons of gods," Symmachus's "sons of the powerful"), against Chrysostom's Sethites.24

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine of Hippo, in the City of God, reads the Flood as history and allegory together. He defends the narrative as real chronology — the antediluvians' 900-year lives were ordinary sun-measured years, "the year then equal to the year now," not, as some held, short cycles of which ten made one of ours25 — and builds the figure of the ark atop that history: the ark "is certainly a figure of the city of God… the church," and its side-door is "the wound which was made when the side of the Crucified was pierced with the spear."26 In 15.27 he lays the both/and down as a rule, rejecting alike those who would "study only the historical truth, apart from any allegorical meanings" and those who hold "they are only allegories, and that there were no such facts at all" — insisting "they did happen, and have a significance, and that this significance has a prophetic reference to the church."27 He names none of those who held the narratives "only allegories"; how far his rule describes the Christian writers of his time is taken up on a disputed-reading page.

John Cassian (c. 435)

John Cassian, the monastic teacher, does not allegorize the Flood; he uses it in a theodicy. In Conference 8 he reads the giant offspring of Seth's line and Cain's daughters as real men whose "wickedness increased to such a pitch that the world could only be purified by the flood and deluge," and then defends the justice of that judgment against those who "depreciate the God of the Old Testament" for drowning a world that had no written law: the antediluvians possessed the natural law "implanted in man at his first creation" and transgressed it, so "God visited them with a righteous judgment." He cites Noah's ability to "distinguish what animals were clean and what were unclean" (Gen 7:2), "before the law… and even before the flood," as evidence that the natural law was implanted in man at his creation. As in Basil, the Flood appears inside an argument about something else; and, with Chrysostom, he reads the "sons of God" as the line of Seth, against Jerome's inclination to the angelic reading.28

Bede (c. 735)

The Venerable Bede, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, is the last witness on this timeline before the seventeenth century. His Commentary on Genesis reads Genesis 6–8 as a real, dated event — the flood in Noah's six hundredth year, "the second… Jair, which we call May," on the seventeenth day, forty days of rain, real animals housed in real chambers — and gathers into one commentary figures found in earlier Latin writers: Jerome's hundred-and-twenty-years reprieve, the ark's three hundred cubits as the Greek T/cross, Ambrose's ark-as-human-body, and Augustine's side-door as "the door… opened in the side of the Crucified Lord Savior by the soldier's lance." He weighs Origen's "geometric cubit" solution to the ark's capacity and rejects it on the evidence of Josephus, and holds that the ark's preservation "was full of divine miracles." He reads the destruction as anthropologically total ("all flesh means all humans"), takes the Sethite reading of the "sons of God," and slots the Flood into his literal scheme of the world's ages.29

Isaac La Peyrère (c. 1655)

Isaac La Peyrère, a French Calvinist scholar and secretary to the Prince de Condé, is the earliest witness on this timeline to deny that the Flood was universal. In Prae-Adamitae (Amsterdam, 1655; English Men before Adam, 1656) he argued that men existed before Adam, Adam being "the first and father of the Jews, not of all men," and drew from it the consequence that the Deluge — a punishment of the Jews' sins — "overflow'd only Palestine, and the Land of the Jews," sparing the pre-Adamite Gentile nations "that dwelt in China, America, the South, or Greenland."30 He denied the Flood's universality, not its reality: a real Noah and a real, local Deluge remained. How his thesis stands to earlier writers who limited the Flood's reach (writers reported by Augustine, Cajetan, Tostatus) is a disputed reading. The book was condemned and publicly burned at Paris in 1656; La Peyrère was arrested, recanted before Pope Alexander VII, and converted to Catholicism (1657) — and the Wikipedia article "Isaac La Peyrère" reports that he influenced Spinoza and Richard Simon.31

Disputed readings

How the pre-modern reading of the Flood, and the earliest arguments for a local flood, have been described and dated.

The earliest denials of a universal Flood

  • Was Isaac La Peyrère's local flood (1655) the earliest denial of a universal Flood? — on La Peyrère and Vossius, the Renaissance debates before them, and older limits on the Flood's reach.32

Pre-modern readings of the Flood

What the pre-modern reading of the Flood included: whether all writers read it as history, and whether they held that its waters covered the whole earth or that it destroyed all humanity.

  • Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history? — on the Catholic Encyclopedia's claim of unanimity, Augustine's rejection of a figure-only reading, and the origins of modern flood geology.33
  • Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity? — Theophilus against Plato, the writers who limited the waters, and those who held that not all humanity perished.34
