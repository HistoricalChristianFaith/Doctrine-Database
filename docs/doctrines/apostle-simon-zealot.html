Simon the Zealot

Simon the Zealot stands eleventh in every New Testament list of the Twelve, just before Judas Iscariot, and the New Testament gives him only a surname: Matthew and Mark call him “the Cananaean,” Luke and Acts “the Zealot.” Klemens Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) and C. M. Kerr (International Standard Bible Encyclopedia) take the two as one zeal-epithet: Löffler’s “Hebrew qana (the Zealous),” Kerr’s kanna’i, “the Jealous (or Zealous) One,” given in Greek by Luke as zēlōtēs; whether it marks membership of the revolutionary party or devotion to the Law is disputed. Later sources supply a mission and a death and differ on both: the Greek tradition sends him to the Black Sea, Egypt, North Africa and Britain; the Latin Passio and the Golden Legend send him to Persia to die with Jude; Moses of Chorene and Georgian tradition place him in the Caucasus; and some apostle-lists identify him with Simeon of Jerusalem, the Lord’s kinsman. This timeline sets out what the sources report about him, earliest extant source first: the Gospel epithet, the apostle-lists, the Latin passion and the Golden Legend, and modern scholarship. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on him; three disputed questions (the meaning of “the Zealot,” where he preached and died, and whether he was Simeon son of Clopas) have their own pages under Disputed readings. For the sources common to all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names Simon four times, always in the same place — eleventh, in the third group of four, immediately before Judas Iscariot — and records no saying, deed, mission or death of his: only a surname that marks him off from Simon Peter. Matthew and Mark write “Simon the Cananaean” (ho Kananaios); Luke and Acts, in the same slot, write “Simon called Zelotes.” Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that “Both surnames have the same signification and are a translation of the Hebrew qana (the Zealous)”; he reports that Jerome and others took Cana as Simon’s native place, and that the Greeks, Copts and Ethiopians identify him with Nathanael of Cana. Whether the surname names Cana, and whether the “zeal” denotes a member of the revolutionary party, a man zealous for the Law, or a follower zealous for Christ, is disputed; see the interpretation page.1

The apostle-lists (uncertain date)

The late apostle-lists — Pseudo-Hippolytus On the Twelve Apostles, Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre, and their Byzantine and Latin kin — supply a mission and a death, and give differing accounts of both. One strand sends Simon north and west, to the Black Sea coast, Egypt and North Africa, and Britain; another keeps him in the East, in Persia or the Caucasus. Pseudo-Hippolytus writes that “Simon the Zealot, the son of Clopas, who is also called Jude, became bishop of Jerusalem after James the Just, and fell asleep and was buried there at the age of 120 years.” Parentage, office and age match what Hegesippus reports of Simeon son of Clopas, the second bishop of Jerusalem, martyred under Trajan at 120. Whether the list fuses two men or preserves an identity is disputed; see the interpretation page.2

The Latin Passio and the Golden Legend (c. 1260)

The Latin Passio Simonis et Iudae, retold in Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), pairs Simon with Jude/Thaddaeus and sends the two together into Persia — Simon “preached in Egypt,” Jude “in Mesopotamia,” “and from thence came they into Persia” — to confront the sorcerers Zaroes and Arphaxat and to die together in a pagan temple at Suanir, where the priests “ran upon the apostles and hewed them to death.” Löffler reports that “In the Western Church he is venerated together with Jude (Thaddaeus); in the East separately,” and that the West keeps the feast on 28 October. Western art gives Simon a saw, “since his body was said to have been sawed to pieces.” The Golden Legend also reports that “it is founden in divers places, of S. Simon, that he was nailed to the cross,” while “some say verily that it was not this Simon that suffered the martyrdom of the cross, but it was another.”3

Modern scholarship

On the epithet, John P. Meier (A Marginal Jew, vol. 3, 2001; ⚠ unverified) holds that the organized Zealot party, which Josephus dates to the war of 66–70, postdates the call of the Twelve, so that the surname means “zealous for the Law”; Löffler (1912) reads it the same way. C. M. Kerr (ISBE) reads “Cananaean” as “of political rather than of geographical significance,” and Martin Hengel’s study of a Jewish “freedom movement” reaching back to Herod (The Zealots, Eng. tr. 1989) dates zealot activity well before the war. On the travels, Löffler writes that “Where he actually preached the Gospel is uncertain,” and Sean McDowell’s survey of the martyrdom accounts (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015) rates the case for any particular death of Simon among the weakest of the Twelve. Kerr argues that Simon the Zealot was Nathanael, son of Cleopas, and brother of James son of Alphaeus.4

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on Simon’s surname, his identity, and his mission and death.

  • Does Simon’s surname “the Zealot” mark him a member of the revolutionary party? — the Gospel epithet beside Josephus’s dating of the Zealot faction.
  • Where did Simon the Zealot preach and die — Persia, Britain, or elsewhere? — the Greek, Latin and Caucasian traditions.
  • Was Simon the Zealot the same man as Simeon son of Clopas, the second bishop of Jerusalem? — Pseudo-Hippolytus beside Hegesippus.
