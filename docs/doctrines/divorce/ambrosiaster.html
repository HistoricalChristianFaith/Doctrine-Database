Ambrosiaster on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Commentary on 1 Corinthians, part of the earliest complete Latin commentary on the thirteen Pauline epistles; “Ambrosiaster” is the modern name for the anonymous Roman churchman who wrote it under Pope Damasus, c. 366–384, long transmitted under the name of Ambrose) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading Paul’s divorce charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) with close attention to its wording, Ambrosiaster distinguishes the husband’s case from the wife’s. He holds that an innocent man who divorces an adulterous wife may marry another, because Paul’s prohibition of remarriage is addressed only to the woman: “a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.” The wife who leaves even an adulterous or apostate husband, by contrast, may neither remarry nor return. He also treats a marriage broken by an unbelieving spouse’s desertion as no longer binding. David G. Hunter (2018) relates his unequal standard to Roman law and to his theology of male headship. Henri Crouzel (1970) counted him the only church writer of the first five centuries who clearly permits remarriage; Hunter holds that his permission reflects contemporary Western practice rather than departing from it.1

Claims & evidence

The innocent husband’s remarriage

Coming to the second half of Paul’s charge — “and that the husband should not divorce his wife” — Ambrosiaster notes that Paul, having told the woman who separates to “remain single,” adds no such words to the man. From that silence he draws a right for the husband that he has just denied the wife:

And a husband should not divorce his wife. He says that a husband should not divorce his wife. But the following words are to be understood: Except on account of fornication. He did not add the words that he wrote concerning the woman: But if she separates, she should remain as she is, because it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins. For a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:10–11), in David G. Hunter’s translation of the Latin.2 Ambrosiaster reads Matthew’s exception clause as allowing the man not only to separate but to marry again. He treats the wife’s adultery as breaking the bond and freeing the innocent husband, as Roman law did in Hunter’s account.1 Clement, Tertullian and the Council of Elvira treat a new marriage while the first spouse lives as adultery (see their pages).

The wife’s case

Turning first to the woman, Ambrosiaster had already ruled out for her what he goes on to grant the man. Even a wife who leaves an adulterous or apostate husband, on his reading, is barred from remarriage:

For it is not permitted for a woman to remarry, if she divorces her own husband on account of fornication or apostasy or if he seeks to use her under the compulsion of his lewd desires, since the woman, as the inferior party, does not enjoy the same rights as the man, who is the superior. But if her husband commits apostasy or desires to enjoy his wife in a perverted manner, the woman can neither marry another man nor return to her husband.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:11), Hunter’s translation.3 He gives a theological reason for the difference: “the woman, as the inferior party, does not enjoy the same rights as the man.” The same commentary, in Gerald Bray’s rendering, compresses the point — “A woman may not marry if she has left her husband because of fornication or apostasy… But if the husband turns away from the faith or desires to have extramarital sexual relations, the wife may neither marry another nor return to him. The husband should not divorce his wife, but one should add the clause ‘except for fornication.’”4 Ambrosiaster assumes throughout that a woman may divorce her husband for his sexual misconduct, but denies her any second marriage; the liberty to marry again is given to the man.

The marriage deserted by an unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15)

On Paul’s further case — the believer whose unbelieving spouse departs — Ambrosiaster treats the abandoned union as dissolved by the departure, since it was never a marriage “dedicated to God”:

A marriage contracted without devotion to God is not binding, and for that reason it is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God. But the unbelieving partner sins both against God and against the marriage, because he or she is unwilling to live in a marriage relationship dedicated to God. It is not right to go to court over this because the one who leaves the marriage is doing so out of hatred for God, and for this reason he or she is not to be considered worthy of such attention.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:15), Bray’s translation.5 The gloss on “is not under bondage” is set beside what canon law calls the Pauline privilege — the deserted believer’s freedom from a marriage broken by an unbeliever’s hostility to the faith — and its description as the earliest clear patristic statement of it is ⚠ unverified. Ambrosiaster does not state a right to a new marriage here in the terms he uses for the wronged husband above; he says that such a marriage “is not binding,” and that it “is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God.”

Reception and context

  • Roman law and the double standard. As David Hunter argues, Ambrosiaster’s asymmetry “fit well into a culture in which a married woman’s sexual transgressions drew more serious criticism than those of a married man.” In Hunter’s account, in Roman law adultery was a wife’s crime; a husband’s extramarital sex with a low-status woman “had no legal bearing at all,” and Constantine’s divorce legislation (Codex Theodosianus 3.16.1, a.d. 331) was harsher on wives who sought divorce than on husbands. Hunter notes Ambrosiaster’s interest in Roman law and his denial that women bear the image of God, which he grounds in their exclusion from legal roles. Hunter also argues against treating his teaching as merely “eccentric”: on other matters (nuptial blessings, clerical continence, the offices of bishop and presbyter) he reads Ambrosiaster as a witness to contemporary Roman church practice, and his permission of remarriage as evidence of a Western practice more varied than a single rule.1
  • Text-critical footing. The key clause survives in the manuscript recensions with only a small variant: the bracketed uxorem in “si dimiserit [uxorem] peccantem” was added in the later (gamma) recension; the sense — “it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins” — is stable across the tradition. Hunter also notes that the CSEL editor prints Ambrosiaster’s own paraphrase (uirum uxorem non dimittere) as if it were part of the biblical lemma.
  • The other Ambrosiaster page. Ambrosiaster is also a witness on the perpetual-virginity timeline, where he gives the Epiphanian reading of the Lord’s “brethren.” The commentary quoted here on 1 Corinthians and the commentary on Galatians quoted there are two parts of the same Pauline commentary corpus, both dated c. 375 on these pages.
  • The attribution to Ambrose. The commentary long circulated under the name of Ambrose of Milan. Erasmus cast doubt on the ascription in 1527; the name “Ambrosiaster” was once credited to Erasmus, but René Hoven (1969) credited it to the Maurists, and others to Franciscus Lucas Brugensis.6 Ambrose himself is on this timeline (c. 385): he forbids remarriage while the spouse lives and rests the bar on the equality of the sexes (“what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man”). Erasmus, arguing in 1519 for the innocent party’s remarriage, knew this commentary under Ambrose’s name (see his page).
  • Use in later debate. Ambrosiaster’s reading that Matthew’s exception permits the innocent husband to remarry, not only to separate, is the ancient precedent Erasmus cited, under Ambrose’s name, for the same reading in the sixteenth century (see his page).
