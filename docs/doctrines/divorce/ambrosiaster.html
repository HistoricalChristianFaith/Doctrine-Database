Ambrosiaster on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Commentary on 1 Corinthians, part of the earliest complete Latin commentary on the thirteen Pauline epistles; “Ambrosiaster” is the modern name for the anonymous Roman churchman who wrote it under Pope Damasus, c. 366–384, long transmitted under the name of Ambrose) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading Paul’s divorce charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) with strict attention to its wording, Ambrosiaster reaches the most permissive verdict of any early Latin writer — and a frankly unequal one. He holds that an innocent man who divorces an adulterous wife may marry another, because Paul’s prohibition of remarriage is addressed only to the woman: “a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.” The wife who leaves even an adulterous or apostate husband, by contrast, may neither remarry nor return. He also treats a marriage broken by an unbelieving spouse’s desertion as no longer binding — the earliest clear Latin statement of the “Pauline privilege.” His double standard rests less on the Gospel than on Roman law and his theology of male headship, and it is the strongest patristic Latin support for divorce-with-remarriage — a Western outlier against the developing indissolubility consensus.

Claims & evidence

An innocent husband may divorce an adulterous wife and marry again

Coming to the second half of Paul’s charge — “and that the husband should not divorce his wife” — Ambrosiaster fastens on the fact that Paul, having told the woman who separates to “remain single,” adds no such words to the man. From that silence he draws a right for the husband that he has just denied the wife:

And a husband should not divorce his wife. He says that a husband should not divorce his wife. But the following words are to be understood: Except on account of fornication. He did not add the words that he wrote concerning the woman: But if she separates, she should remain as she is, because it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins. For a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:10–11), in David G. Hunter’s translation of the Latin.1 This is the crux the lead flags: alone among the early Latin fathers, Ambrosiaster reads Matthew’s exception clause as licensing not merely separation but a fresh marriage — for the man. He treats the wife’s adultery as a definitive breaking of the bond that frees the innocent husband, exactly as Roman law treated it. Where Clement, Tertullian, the Council of Elvira, and Basil all held that a new marriage while the first spouse lives is itself adultery, Ambrosiaster grants the husband precisely that liberty.

The wife has no such right — the double standard

Turning first to the woman, Ambrosiaster had already ruled out for her what he will shortly grant the man. Even a wife who leaves an adulterous or apostate husband, on his reading, is barred from remarriage:

For it is not permitted for a woman to remarry, if she divorces her own husband on account of fornication or apostasy or if he seeks to use her under the compulsion of his lewd desires, since the woman, as the inferior party, does not enjoy the same rights as the man, who is the superior. But if her husband commits apostasy or desires to enjoy his wife in a perverted manner, the woman can neither marry another man nor return to her husband.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:11), Hunter’s translation.2 The asymmetry is explicit and theological: “the woman, as the inferior party, does not enjoy the same rights as the man.” The same commentary, in Gerald Bray’s rendering, compresses the point — “A woman may not marry if she has left her husband because of fornication or apostasy… But if the husband turns away from the faith or desires to have extramarital sexual relations, the wife may neither marry another nor return to him. The husband should not divorce his wife, but one should add the clause ‘except for fornication.’”3 Ambrosiaster assumes throughout that a woman may divorce her husband for his sexual misconduct — itself a striking concession — but denies her any second marriage; only the man walks free to marry again.

A marriage deserted for God’s sake is no longer binding — the Pauline privilege

On Paul’s further case — the believer whose unbelieving spouse departs — Ambrosiaster treats the abandoned union as dissolved by the departure, since it was never a marriage “dedicated to God”:

A marriage contracted without devotion to God is not binding, and for that reason it is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God. But the unbelieving partner sins both against God and against the marriage, because he or she is unwilling to live in a marriage relationship dedicated to God. It is not right to go to court over this because the one who leaves the marriage is doing so out of hatred for God, and for this reason he or she is not to be considered worthy of such attention.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians (on 7:15), Bray’s translation.4 This gloss on “is not under bondage” is commonly read as the earliest clear patristic statement of what later canon law would call the Pauline privilege — the deserted believer’s freedom from a marriage broken by an unbeliever’s hostility to the faith. Ambrosiaster does not spell out a right to a new marriage here as bluntly as he does for the wronged husband above, but he plainly holds that the bond does not survive such a desertion, so that the believer who abandons it “because of God” commits no sin.

Notes

  • Roman law, not just theology, drives the double standard. As David Hunter argues, Ambrosiaster’s asymmetry “fit well into a culture in which a married woman’s sexual transgressions drew more serious criticism than those of a married man.” In Roman law adultery was a wife’s crime; a husband’s extramarital sex with a low-status woman “had no legal bearing at all,” and Constantine’s divorce legislation (Codex Theodosianus 3.16.1, a.d. 331) was harsher on wives who sought divorce than on husbands. Ambrosiaster’s unusual interest in Roman law is well attested; he is also one of the very few early writers who denied that women bear the image of God, appealing to their exclusion from legal roles. Hunter cautions, though, against dismissing his teaching as merely “eccentric”: on other matters (nuptial blessings, clerical continence, the offices of bishop and presbyter) Ambrosiaster is a reliable witness to contemporary Roman church practice, and his frank permission of remarriage may reflect a Western practice less monolithic than the later indissolubility consensus suggests.5
  • Text-critical footing. The key clause survives in the manuscript recensions with only a small variant: the bracketed uxorem in “si dimiserit [uxorem] peccantem” was added in the later (gamma) recension; the sense — “it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins” — is stable across the tradition. Hunter also notes that the CSEL editor prints Ambrosiaster’s own paraphrase (uirum uxorem non dimittere) as if it were part of the biblical lemma.
  • The other Ambrosiaster page. Ambrosiaster appears on this wiki also as a witness on the perpetual-virginity timeline, where he gives the Epiphanian reading of the Lord’s “brethren.” The commentary quoted here on 1 Corinthians and the commentary on Galatians quoted there are two parts of the same Pauline commentary corpus, both dated c. 375 on this wiki.
  • Feeds the arguments. Ambrosiaster is the strongest early Latin support for the reading that Matthew’s exception clause permits remarriage, not just separation — the sub-question weighed under the Arguments section of the summary — and a named forerunner of Erasmus’ sixteenth-century case for the same. His argument that Paul’s silence toward the husband is meaningful, however, is precisely what the indissolubilist tradition (and Augustine’s hesitations) would resist.
