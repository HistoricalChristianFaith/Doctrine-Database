Pope Innocent I on divorce

Dates: c. 405 (Innocent I was bishop of Rome 401–417; the two decretal letters below date from 15 February 404 and 20 February 405) · Wikipedia
Summary: Two decretal letters of Innocent I, bishop of Rome, give rulings on divorce and remarriage in answer to questions put to Rome by two Gallic bishops. Innocent states as the rule “for everyone” that a person who marries again while the first spouse lives is an adulterer: both partners in the second union, and their new spouses, are adulterers, and all are to be kept from communion. The rulings quoted make no separate provision for the spouse wronged by adultery. His decretals were later copied into the canon-law collections (Ivo of Chartres, Gratian).

Claims & evidence

Remarriage after divorce and exclusion from communion (to Exuperius of Toulouse, 405)

The fuller statement comes in the decretal Consulenti tibi (20 February 405) to Exuperius, bishop of Toulouse, who had asked Rome for rulings on a list of disciplinary cases. On divorce-and-remarriage Innocent rules that the new union is adultery for both parties, and quoting the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9) he extends the charge to the new spouses they take.

You have also asked about those who, after divorce, have married again. It is clear that both parties are adulterers. Those who, while the wife is living, although their marriage has been dissolved, hasten to another union cannot be other than adulterers…

— Innocent I, Epistle 6 (Consulenti tibi) to Exuperius, cap. 6, trans. J. Stevenson.1 In the Latin, even where “the marriage seems to have been dissolved” (quamvis dissociatum videatur esse conjugium), those who rush to a second union “cannot but be seen as adulterers,” so much so that “even those persons to whom they have joined themselves are seen to have committed adultery”; the discipline is exclusion: “and therefore all such are to be kept from the communion of the faithful” (omnes a communione fidelium abstinendos). Innocent grounds the ruling on Matthew’s saying (“whoever puts away his wife and marries another commits adultery; likewise he who marries her who is put away commits adultery”) and does not address the exception clause in the passage quoted.

The general rule: a woman who remarries while her husband lives is held an adulteress (to Victricius of Rouen, 404)

A year earlier, in the decretal to Victricius, bishop of Rouen (15 February 404), Innocent had stated the same principle as the rule “for everyone,” invoking it a fortiori to condemn a consecrated virgin who marries. The rule for ordinary spouses is stated as already observed, the premise from which the case of the virgin follows.

For if this rule is observed in the case of everyone — that any woman who marries another while her husband lives is held an adulteress, and leave to do penance is not granted her unless one of the two has died — how much more is it to be held of her who had first joined herself to the immortal Bridegroom and afterwards passed over to human marriage!

— Innocent I, Epistle 2 to Victricius, cap. 13 (editorial translation of the Latin of Migne, PL 20).2 Innocent here states that the remarried woman is not granted leave even to do penance until a spouse dies (nec ei agendae poenitentiae licentia concedatur, nisi unus ex eis defunctus fuerit): while both live, the second union is held to be a continuing adultery.

Notes

  • Innocent and the Gallic councils. Innocent’s decretals, as quoted, admit no remarriage while the first spouse lives; Elvira, Jerome and Augustine also bar it (see their pages). Later in the fifth century the Council of Vannes (c. 465) and, in 506, the Council of Agde excluded from communion men who remarried without proof of the wife’s adultery. David G. Hunter (2018) reads these canons as indicating that “it was normal procedure in the Gallic church of the fifth century for a man to be allowed to remarry, if he divorced his first wife for adultery,” and quotes Philip Lyndon Reynolds that the Vannes canon “simply translates [the exception clause of] Matthew 19:9 into ecclesiastical law.”3 The canons and their readings are on the Gallic councils page.
  • The decretal form. Earlier bishops of Rome had taught on divorce, but Innocent’s letters are decretals — rulings issued in answer to formal inquiries, styled after imperial rescripts — and were copied into the canonical collections and, centuries later, into Gratian’s Decretum (C. 27 q. 1 c. 10) and Ivo of Chartres.
  • On the Victricius passage. No published English translation of Epistle 2 was located; the rendering above is editorial, made from the verified Latin of Migne’s Patrologia Latina vol. 20 (reproduced with its column numbers on Wikisource). The clause quoted is the general rule Innocent states in passing; his immediate subject is the consecrated virgin who marries.
