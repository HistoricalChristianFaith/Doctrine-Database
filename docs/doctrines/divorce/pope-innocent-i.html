Pope Innocent I on divorce

Dates: c. 405 (Innocent I was bishop of Rome 401–417; the two decretal letters below date from 15 February 404 and 20 February 405) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest firm papal legislation on divorce and remarriage. Answering questions put to Rome by two Gallic bishops, Innocent lays down as the rule “for everyone” that a person who marries again while the first spouse lives is an adulterer — and, crucially, admits no exception for the innocent party of an adulterous marriage: both partners in the second union, and their new spouses, are adulterers, and all are to be kept from communion. His decretals fed the later canon-law collections (Gratian, Ivo) and fixed the strict Roman line against the more permissive Gallic conciliar practice of his own century.

Claims & evidence

Remarriage after divorce is adultery on both sides — and bars from communion (to Exuperius of Toulouse, 405)

The clearest statement comes in the decretal Consulenti tibi (20 February 405) to Exuperius, bishop of Toulouse, who had asked Rome for rulings on a list of disciplinary cases. On divorce-and-remarriage Innocent is categorical: the new union is adultery for both parties, and quoting the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9) he extends the charge even to the new spouses they take.

You have also asked about those who, after divorce, have married again. It is clear that both parties are adulterers. Those who, while the wife is living, although their marriage has been dissolved, hasten to another union cannot be other than adulterers…

— Innocent I, Epistle 6 (Consulenti tibi) to Exuperius, cap. 6, trans. J. Stevenson.1 The Latin makes the reach of the ruling plain — even where “the marriage seems to have been dissolved” (quamvis dissociatum videatur esse conjugium), those who rush to a second union “cannot but be seen as adulterers,” so much so that “even those persons to whom they have joined themselves are seen to have committed adultery” — and the discipline is exclusion: “and therefore all such are to be kept from the communion of the faithful” (omnes a communione fidelium abstinendos). No allowance is made for the spouse who was the injured party. On the ground of Matthew’s own saying (“whoever puts away his wife and marries another commits adultery; likewise he who marries her who is put away commits adultery”), Innocent treats the exception clause exactly as Tertullian, Elvira, and Jerome had: it may license the dismissal, but never a new marriage while the first partner lives.

The general rule: any woman who remarries while her husband lives is an adulteress (to Victricius of Rouen, 404)

A year earlier, in the decretal to Victricius, bishop of Rouen (15 February 404), Innocent had stated the same principle as the settled rule “for everyone,” invoking it a fortiori to condemn a consecrated virgin who marries. The a fortiori is what makes it valuable: the strict rule for ordinary spouses is assumed as already established, the premise from which the harder case follows.

For if this rule is observed in the case of everyone — that any woman who marries another while her husband lives is held an adulteress, and leave to do penance is not granted her unless one of the two has died — how much more is it to be held of her who had first joined herself to the immortal Bridegroom and afterwards passed over to human marriage!

— Innocent I, Epistle 2 to Victricius, cap. 13 (editorial translation of the Latin of Migne, PL 20).2 So severe is the rule that Innocent bars the remarried woman not only from communion but even from the discipline of penance until a spouse dies (nec ei agendae poenitentiae licentia concedatur, nisi unus ex eis defunctus fuerit) — the reconciliation the church otherwise offers the lapsed is withheld while the offence, a subsisting adultery, continues. The bond survives the divorce; only death dissolves it.

Notes

  • The strictest Latin line, and the fault line with the Gallic councils. Innocent’s Roman decretals admit no adultery exception for remarriage — the reading of Elvira, Jerome, and (soon) Augustine. Yet in the very same century the regional councils of Gaul went the other way: the Council of Vannes (c. 465) and the Council of Agde (506) punished remarriage only where the wife’s adultery was not proven — presupposing that a husband whose case was adjudicated could remarry. Innocent’s decretals and the Gallic canons thus mark the two poles of Western practice, the tension modern scholarship (David G. Hunter, Philip Reynolds) works over.
  • What “the first papal legislation” means. Earlier bishops of Rome had taught on divorce, but Innocent’s letters are decretals — authoritative rulings issued in answer to formal inquiries, styled after imperial rescripts — and were copied into the canonical collections and, centuries later, into Gratian’s Decretum (C. 27 q. 1 c. 10) and Ivo of Chartres. They are the point at which the strict reading passes from patristic exhortation into papal law.
  • On the Victricius passage. No published English translation of Epistle 2 was located; the rendering above is editorial, made from the verified Latin of Migne’s Patrologia Latina vol. 20 (reproduced with its column numbers on Wikisource). The clause quoted is the general rule Innocent states in passing; his immediate subject is the fallen consecrated virgin.
