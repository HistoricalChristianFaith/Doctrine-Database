Gregory of Nazianzus on divorce

Dates: c. 380 (Oration 37, “On the Words of the Gospel, ‘When Jesus Had Finished These Sayings,’” on Matthew 19:1–12, preached during his Constantinople episcopate, c. 379–381) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Theologian, expounding Jesus’ divorce dialogue at Constantinople, protests against the sexual double standard of the laws of his day — that a wife’s adultery was severely punished while a husband’s fornication “has no account to give.” “I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom,” he says: the unequal laws are man-made (“they who made the Law were men”), whereas God’s law is one for both sexes, grounded in creation (“one Maker of man and woman”), in the fall (both Adam and Eve sinned), and in redemption (Christ died for both). On the divorce question itself, Christ in his reading allows “only separation from the whore,” and “in all other things He commands patience.” His near-contemporaries Basil and Ambrosiaster treat husband and wife differently, Basil by appeal to “custom” and Ambrosiaster by appeal to headship.

Claims & evidence

The double standard on chastity as man-made law (Oration 37.6)

Taking up the Pharisees’ question “is it lawful for a man to put away his wife for every cause?”, Gregory turns first to the inequality of the law that punished the wife’s adultery but excused the husband’s. He rejects it and names its source: the law was made by men, and so falls hard on women.

Chastity, in respect of which I see that the majority of men are ill-disposed, and that their laws are unequal and irregular. For what was the reason why they restrained the woman, but indulged the man, and that a woman who practises evil against her husband’s bed is an adulteress, and the penalties of the law for this are very severe; but if the husband commits fornication against his wife, he has no account to give? I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom. They who made the Law were men, and therefore their legislation is hard on women, since they have placed children also under the authority of their fathers, while leaving the weaker sex uncared for.

— Oration 37.6 (NPNF trans.). Gregory’s answer to the convention that counted only the wife an adulteress is that the standard binds both sexes alike.1

One law for both sexes, argued from creation, fall, and redemption (Oration 37.6–7)

Against the man-made inequality Gregory sets the “equality of the legislation” that God ordains. He argues it on three fronts: creation (one Maker made male and female), the fall (both Adam and Eve sinned, neither stronger nor weaker), and redemption (Christ was made flesh, died, and saves for the woman as for the man) — concluding that the “one flesh” must have “equal honour.”

God doth not so… See the equality of the legislation. There is one Maker of man and woman; one debt is owed by children to both their parents. How then dost thou demand Chastity, while thou dost not thyself observe it? How dost thou demand that which thou dost not give? How, though thou art equally a body, dost thou legislate unequally? If thou enquire into the worse—The Woman Sinned, and so did Adam. The serpent deceived them both; and one was not found to be the stronger and the other the weaker. But dost thou consider the better? Christ saves both by His Passion. Was He made flesh for the Man? So He was also for the woman. Did He die for the Man? The Woman also is saved by His death… They two, He says, shall be one Flesh; so let the one flesh have equal honour.

— Oration 37.6–7 (NPNF trans.). Lactantius, some decades earlier, and Jerome, writing later (“what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men”), also argue for a single standard for husband and wife (see their pages).2

On divorce itself (Oration 37.8)

Gregory then turns to the divorce question proper. In his reading the Mosaic law allowed divorce “for every cause,” but Christ allows it for one — the wife’s fornication — and enjoins patient endurance for every lesser fault.

Now the Law grants divorce for every cause; but Christ not for every cause; but He allows only separation from the whore; and in all other things He commands patience. He allows to put away the fornicatress, because she corrupts the offspring; but in all other matters let us be patient and endure… If you see lines or marks upon her, take away her ornaments; if a hasty tongue, restrain it… It is uncertain which is in danger, the separator or the separated.

— Oration 37.8 (NPNF trans.). The passage speaks of “separation” for fornication and does not address a second marriage after it.3

Context

  • The double standard and the exception. Gregory’s protest concerns the sexual double standard. Like Hermas, Clement, Tertullian and Chrysostom he names fornication as the one cause of putting a wife away. Basil refers the harsher treatment of the wife to “custom,” and Ambrosiaster grounds the husband’s greater liberty in “headship”; Gregory calls the inequality man-made law. All three wrote in the 370s–380s.
  • The Latin parallel. Lactantius had argued in the Latin West a generation earlier against the law that counted only the wife an adulteress: faith “must be kept with the other by each party in a marriage.”
  • Oration 37 also touches remarriage after bereavement — “the first is law, the second is indulgence, the third is transgression” — the deprecation of successive marriages (digamy) traced on the remarriage timeline; that theme is distinct from the divorce question handled here.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus is also a witness on other timelines — the Old Testament canon, the Theotokos, infant baptism, and prayer to the saints among them. Bishop of Constantinople during the council of 381, he is one of the three Cappadocian Fathers.
