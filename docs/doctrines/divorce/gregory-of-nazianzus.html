Gregory of Nazianzus on divorce

Dates: c. 380 (Oration 37, “On the Words of the Gospel, ‘When Jesus Had Finished These Sayings,’” on Matthew 19:1–12, preached during his Constantinople episcopate, c. 379–381) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Theologian, expounding Jesus’ divorce dialogue at Constantinople, delivers the sharpest patristic protest against the sexual double standard the church’s civil law enforced — that a wife’s adultery was severely punished while a husband’s fornication “has no account to give.” “I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom,” he says: the unequal laws are man-made (“they who made the Law were men”), whereas God’s law is one for both sexes, grounded in creation (“one Maker of man and woman”), in the fall (both Adam and Eve sinned), and in redemption (Christ died for both). Yet on the divorce question itself Gregory holds the strict Eastern line: Christ permits putting a wife away “only… from the whore,” and “in all other things He commands patience.” He is thus the Eastern egalitarian counterpoint to Basil’s candidly-admitted “custom” double standard and to Ambrosiaster’s headship-based one — a near-contemporary of both — reaching the same strict verdict on the bond by the opposite road of the sexes’ equality before God.

Claims & evidence

The double standard on chastity is unequal, man-made law (Oration 37.6)

Taking up the Pharisees’ question “is it lawful for a man to put away his wife for every cause?”, Gregory turns first to the inequality of the civil law that punished the wife’s adultery but excused the husband’s. He refuses it outright and names its source: the law was made by men, and so falls hard on women.

Chastity, in respect of which I see that the majority of men are ill-disposed, and that their laws are unequal and irregular. For what was the reason why they restrained the woman, but indulged the man, and that a woman who practises evil against her husband’s bed is an adulteress, and the penalties of the law for this are very severe; but if the husband commits fornication against his wife, he has no account to give? I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom. They who made the Law were men, and therefore their legislation is hard on women, since they have placed children also under the authority of their fathers, while leaving the weaker sex uncared for.

— Oration 37.6 (NPNF trans.). The protest is aimed squarely at the Roman–ecclesiastical convention that counted only the wife an adulteress; Gregory answers it not by loosening the marriage bond but by insisting the standard bind both sexes alike.1

God’s law is one for both sexes — grounded in creation, fall, and redemption (Oration 37.6–7)

Against the man-made inequality Gregory sets the “equality of the legislation” that God ordains. He argues it on three fronts: creation (one Maker made male and female), the fall (both Adam and Eve sinned, neither stronger nor weaker), and redemption (Christ was made flesh, died, and saves for the woman as for the man) — concluding that the “one flesh” must have “equal honour.”

God doth not so… See the equality of the legislation. There is one Maker of man and woman; one debt is owed by children to both their parents. How then dost thou demand Chastity, while thou dost not thyself observe it? How dost thou demand that which thou dost not give? How, though thou art equally a body, dost thou legislate unequally? If thou enquire into the worse—The Woman Sinned, and so did Adam. The serpent deceived them both; and one was not found to be the stronger and the other the weaker. But dost thou consider the better? Christ saves both by His Passion. Was He made flesh for the Man? So He was also for the woman. Did He die for the Man? The Woman also is saved by His death… They two, He says, shall be one Flesh; so let the one flesh have equal honour.

— Oration 37.6–7 (NPNF trans.). This is the most fully-argued patristic case for a single sexual standard on both husband and wife, decades after the Latin egalitarian protest of Lactantius and anticipating the equality argument Jerome would later press — “what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” — to reach the same strict conclusion.2

On divorce itself: Christ permits separation only for fornication, and patience in all else (Oration 37.8)

Having settled the standard of chastity, Gregory states his verdict on the divorce question proper. He reads Matthew’s exception strictly: the Mosaic bill allowed divorce “for every cause,” but Christ narrowed it to one — the wife’s fornication — and even there commands only dismissal, enjoining patient endurance for every lesser fault.

Now the Law grants divorce for every cause; but Christ not for every cause; but He allows only separation from the whore; and in all other things He commands patience. He allows to put away the fornicatress, because she corrupts the offspring; but in all other matters let us be patient and endure… If you see lines or marks upon her, take away her ornaments; if a hasty tongue, restrain it… It is uncertain which is in danger, the separator or the separated.

— Oration 37.8 (NPNF trans.). Gregory thus lands, on the bond itself, exactly where the strict Eastern tradition does: fornication alone licenses putting a wife away, and even that is dismissal, not a licence for anything more. His distinctive contribution is not a looser verdict but a stricter symmetry — the very equality that the double-standard writers denied.3

Notes

  • Protesting the inequality, not loosening the bond. Gregory’s target is the sexual double standard, not the strict reading of the exception clause. He joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, and Chrysostom in permitting divorce for fornication only — but where Basil admitted the harsher treatment of the wife rested on “custom” rather than the Gospel, and Ambrosiaster grounded a permissive male privilege on the husband’s “headship,” Gregory attacks the inequality itself as man-made law that God’s single standard overturns. He is the Eastern egalitarian counterpoint to both, and a near-contemporary (all three fall in the 370s–380s).
  • The Latin parallel. The same egalitarian protest had been made in the Latin West a generation earlier by Lactantius, who overturned the Roman law that counted only the wife an adulteress: faith “must be kept with the other by each party in a marriage.” Gregory’s Greek version is the fuller and more famous of the two.
  • Oration 37 also touches remarriage after bereavement — “the first is law, the second is indulgence, the third is transgression” — the deprecation of successive marriages (digamy) traced on the remarriage timeline; that theme is distinct from the divorce question handled here.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus appears on this wiki also as a witness on other timelines — the Old Testament canon, the Theotokos, infant baptism, and prayer to the saints among them. Bishop of Constantinople during the council of 381, he is one of the three Cappadocian Fathers.
