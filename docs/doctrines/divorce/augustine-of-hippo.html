Augustine of Hippo on divorce

Dates: c. 419 (the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius and On Marriage and Concupiscence; the earlier On the Sermon on the Mount dates c. 393–396 and On the Good of Marriage c. 401) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, gives the Latin West its decisive theology of indissolubility. Where earlier witnesses had asserted that the bond survives divorce, Augustine supplies the reason: marriage among Christians carries a sacramentum — a sacred, permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church — so that the bond “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them.” From this it follows that even after a licit separation for adultery neither party may marry again while the other lives; a second union is not marriage but adultery. He argues the point across four works over a quarter-century — the Sermon on the Mount commentary, On the Good of Marriage, On Marriage and Concupiscence, and the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius — and, uniquely, wrestles openly with how to reconcile Matthew’s exception clause with the unqualified prohibition in Mark and Luke and with Paul’s charge in 1 Corinthians 7, admitting the gospel words are obscure. This sacramentum synthesis became the settled Latin, and later Roman Catholic, doctrine.

Claims & evidence

The exception is fornication alone, and even the innocent party may not remarry (On the Sermon on the Mount, c. 393–396)

Augustine’s earliest treatment of the divorce sayings — the exposition of Matthew 5:31–32 in the first book of On the Sermon on the Mount — already reaches the strict reading, and it is the very passage that later prompted the layman Pollentius to write to him in protest. Moses’ bill of divorcement, Augustine explains, was a hard-heartedness concession meant to restrain a worse evil (the murder of unwanted wives); Christ restores the creation order, making “the single exception of fornication” and calling remarriage after divorce adultery.

For however hard-hearted a man may be who wishes to put away his wife, when he reflects that, on a writing of divorcement being given her, she could then without risk marry another, he would be easily appeased. Our Lord, therefore, in order to confirm that principle, that a wife should not lightly be put away, made the single exception of fornication; but enjoins that all other annoyances… be borne with fortitude for the sake of conjugal fidelity and for the sake of chastity; and he also calls that man an adulterer who should marry her that has been divorced by her husband.

— Augustine, On the Sermon on the Mount I.14.39, NPNF1 vol. 6. Reading the exception together with Paul’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11), Augustine holds that the bar on remarriage falls on both parties alike: the wife who departs must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband,” and “according to a similar rule, if he shall put her away, he is to remain unmarried, or be reconciled to his wife.”1

The bond is a sacramentum, dissolved only by death (On the Good of Marriage, c. 401)

In On the Good of Marriage Augustine names the three goods of marriage — offspring, fidelity, and the sacramentum (the sacred, unbreakable bond) — and it is the third that carries the indissolubility. The strength of the marriage tie, he argues, is inexplicable unless the union signifies “some greater matter”; that sacred signification is why the bond holds even against the will of those who try to break it.

…what is the meaning of this so great strength of the marriage bond? Which I by no means think could have been of so great avail, were it not that there were taken a certain sacrament of some greater matter from out this weak mortal state of men, so that, men deserting it, and seeking to dissolve it, it should remain unshaken for their punishment. Seeing that the compact of marriage is not done away by divorce intervening; so that they continue wedded persons one to another, even after separation; and commit adultery with those, with whom they shall be joined, even after their own divorce.

— Augustine, On the Good of Marriage 7, NPNF1 vol. 3. He states the conclusion twice more. A Christian marriage “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them” — so that even a childless couple “may not… separate… and… join themselves unto others” without committing adultery (§17); and it is “by reason of” the “sanctity of the Sacrament” that it is “unlawful for one who leaves her husband, even when she has been put away, to be married to another, so long as her husband lives, no not even for the sake of bearing children” (§32).2

The sacramentum theologized: a permanent seal, like baptism (On Marriage and Concupiscence, c. 419–421)

In On Marriage and Concupiscence Augustine gives the fullest account of why the bond cannot be dissolved: the sacramental tie is a permanent thing, guaranteed by the marriage of Christ and the Church it signifies, and it persists even where the union is broken by sin — exactly as the “sacrament” of baptism remains on an apostate. The chapter title in the manuscripts already reads “Marriage Indissoluble; The World’s Law About Divorce Different from the Gospel’s.”

…a certain sacramental bond in marriage… Of this bond the substance undoubtedly is this, that the man and the woman who are joined together in matrimony should remain inseparable as long as they live; and that it should be unlawful for one consort to be parted from the other, except for the cause of fornication. For this is preserved in the case of Christ and the Church; so that, as a living one with a living one, there is no divorce, no separation for ever… Thus between the conjugal pair, as long as they live, the nuptial bond has a permanent obligation, and can be cancelled neither by separation nor by union with another.

— Augustine, On Marriage and Concupiscence I.10[XI], NPNF1 vol. 5. The world’s law, he notes, “allows a divorce… without even the allegation of guilt… a concession which… even the holy Moses extended… because of the hardness of their hearts” — but under the gospel “whosoever does this is held to be guilty of adultery.” The sacramental seal, like that of baptism, is “lost neither by divorce nor by adultery.”3

The controversy with Pollentius, and an honest admission of obscurity (On Adulterous Marriages, c. 419)

The two books On Adulterous Marriages — the only treatise of the first five Christian centuries devoted wholly to divorce and remarriage — answer Pollentius, who had read Augustine’s strict exposition of the Sermon on the Mount and objected. Pollentius argued from Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) that a man who dismisses an adulterous wife may licitly remarry, and read Paul’s prohibition (1 Corinthians 7:11) as applying only to those who separate for lesser causes. Augustine refuses the distinction: he interprets Matthew’s exception by the unqualified prohibition of Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18, and holds that even a divorce for fornication leaves the bond intact, so that remarriage remains adultery.

…any union with other persons while their first husband or wife is alive is not marriage but adultery.

— Augustine, On Adulterous Marriages II.18–19 (trans. Roland Teske), The Works of Saint Augustine I/9. What sets the work apart is Augustine’s candour: he grants that the gospel words on the exception are genuinely obscure and that he does not regard the question as fully solved — a rare admission that the strict reading he defends is an inference drawn to harmonize texts that do not plainly agree, rather than a self-evident command.4

Notes

  • Augustine’s originality is not the verdict — the separation-not-remarriage reading was already the line of Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Elvira, Basil, Chrysostom, and Jerome — but the rationale. By grounding indissolubility in the sacramentum, the sacred sign of Christ and the Church, he turned a disciplinary rule into a doctrine of the nature of Christian marriage, and gave the Latin West the framework the medieval canonists and, later, the Council of Trent would build on.
  • Like Jerome, and against the permissive double standard of the fellow-Latin Ambrosiaster, Augustine applies the bar to husband and wife alike (“according to a similar rule, if he shall put her away, he is to remain unmarried”). His even-handedness follows from the sacramental logic: the bond binds both, because it signifies one indivisible union.
  • The honest wrestling in On Adulterous Marriages is itself notable. Augustine does not pretend the New Testament speaks with one plain voice; he concedes the obscurity of the exception clause and reaches his conclusion by reading Matthew in the light of Mark, Luke, and Paul — the “harder” texts governing the “easier.” The strictness is the product of a deliberate interpretive choice, openly acknowledged as such.
  • The same Augustine appears on this wiki elsewhere as a witness on the infant baptism and other timelines. On divorce he is the hinge between the patristic period and the medieval Latin settlement: after him, indissolubility grounded in the sacramentum is the Western default, and the later concessive currents — the Eastern “economy” reported by Origen and defended by Epiphanius, the permissive Latin reading of Ambrosiaster — stand as the roads not taken in the West.
