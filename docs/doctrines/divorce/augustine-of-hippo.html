Augustine of Hippo on divorce

Dates: c. 419 (the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius and On Marriage and Concupiscence; the earlier On the Sermon on the Mount dates c. 393–396 and On the Good of Marriage c. 401) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, grounds the indissolubility of marriage in a theological reason: marriage among Christians carries a sacramentum — a sacred, permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church — so that the bond “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them.” On his account, even after a licit separation for adultery neither party may marry again while the other lives; a second union is not marriage but adultery. He argues the point across four works over a quarter-century — the Sermon on the Mount commentary, On the Good of Marriage, On Marriage and Concupiscence, and the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius — and discusses openly how to reconcile Matthew’s exception clause with the unqualified prohibition in Mark and Luke and with Paul’s charge in 1 Corinthians 7, granting that the gospel words are obscure.

Claims & evidence

The exception clause and remarriage (On the Sermon on the Mount, c. 393–396)

Augustine’s earliest treatment of the divorce sayings is the exposition of Matthew 5:31–32 in the first book of On the Sermon on the Mount; it is the passage that later prompted Pollentius to write to him in objection. Moses’ bill of divorcement, Augustine explains, was a hard-heartedness concession meant to restrain a worse evil (the murder of unwanted wives); Christ restores the creation order, making “the single exception of fornication” and calling remarriage after divorce adultery.

For however hard-hearted a man may be who wishes to put away his wife, when he reflects that, on a writing of divorcement being given her, she could then without risk marry another, he would be easily appeased. Our Lord, therefore, in order to confirm that principle, that a wife should not lightly be put away, made the single exception of fornication; but enjoins that all other annoyances… be borne with fortitude for the sake of conjugal fidelity and for the sake of chastity; and he also calls that man an adulterer who should marry her that has been divorced by her husband.

— Augustine, On the Sermon on the Mount I.14.39, NPNF1 vol. 6. Reading the exception together with Paul’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11), Augustine holds that the bar on remarriage falls on both parties alike: the wife who departs must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband,” and “according to a similar rule, if he shall put her away, he is to remain unmarried, or be reconciled to his wife.”1

The bond as sacramentum (On the Good of Marriage, c. 401)

In On the Good of Marriage Augustine names the three goods of marriage — offspring, fidelity, and the sacramentum (the sacred, unbreakable bond) — and ties the bond’s permanence to the third. The strength of the marriage tie, he argues, could not be accounted for unless the union signifies “some greater matter”; that sacred signification is why the bond holds even against the will of those who try to break it.

…what is the meaning of this so great strength of the marriage bond? Which I by no means think could have been of so great avail, were it not that there were taken a certain sacrament of some greater matter from out this weak mortal state of men, so that, men deserting it, and seeking to dissolve it, it should remain unshaken for their punishment. Seeing that the compact of marriage is not done away by divorce intervening; so that they continue wedded persons one to another, even after separation; and commit adultery with those, with whom they shall be joined, even after their own divorce.

— Augustine, On the Good of Marriage 7, NPNF1 vol. 3. He states the conclusion twice more. A Christian marriage “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them” — so that even a childless couple “may not… separate… and… join themselves unto others” without committing adultery (§17); and it is “by reason of” the “sanctity of the Sacrament” that it is “unlawful for one who leaves her husband, even when she has been put away, to be married to another, so long as her husband lives, no not even for the sake of bearing children” (§32).2

The sacramental bond compared with baptism (On Marriage and Concupiscence, c. 419–421)

In On Marriage and Concupiscence Augustine sets out his account of the bond: the sacramental tie is a permanent thing, grounded in the marriage of Christ and the Church it signifies, and it persists where the union is broken by sin, as the “sacrament” of baptism remains on an apostate. The chapter title in the NPNF translation reads “Marriage Indissoluble; The World’s Law About Divorce Different from the Gospel’s.”

…a certain sacramental bond in marriage… Of this bond the substance undoubtedly is this, that the man and the woman who are joined together in matrimony should remain inseparable as long as they live; and that it should be unlawful for one consort to be parted from the other, except for the cause of fornication. For this is preserved in the case of Christ and the Church; so that, as a living one with a living one, there is no divorce, no separation for ever… Thus between the conjugal pair, as long as they live, the nuptial bond has a permanent obligation, and can be cancelled neither by separation nor by union with another.

— Augustine, On Marriage and Concupiscence I.10[XI], NPNF1 vol. 5. The world’s law, he notes, “allows a divorce… without even the allegation of guilt… a concession which… even the holy Moses extended… because of the hardness of their hearts” — but under the gospel “whosoever does this is held to be guilty of adultery.” In a later chapter (ch. 19, “Blessing of Matrimony”) he calls the sacramental bond one “lost neither by divorce nor by adultery.”3

The controversy with Pollentius (On Adulterous Marriages, c. 419)

The two books On Adulterous Marriages — described as the only treatise of the first five Christian centuries devoted wholly to divorce and remarriage (⚠ unverified) — answer Pollentius, who had read Augustine’s exposition of the Sermon on the Mount and objected. Pollentius argued from Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) that a man who dismisses an adulterous wife may licitly remarry, and read Paul’s prohibition (1 Corinthians 7:11) as applying only to those who separate for lesser causes. Augustine rejects the distinction: he interprets Matthew’s exception by the unqualified prohibition of Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18, and holds that even a divorce for fornication leaves the bond intact, so that remarriage remains adultery.

…any union with other persons while their first husband or wife is alive is not marriage but adultery.

— Augustine, On Adulterous Marriages II.18–19 (trans. Roland Teske), The Works of Saint Augustine I/9. In the work Augustine grants that the gospel words on the exception are obscure and does not regard the question as fully solved; he reaches his reading by interpreting Matthew’s exception through Mark, Luke and Paul.4

Reception and context

  • Earlier writers on this timeline — Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, the Council of Elvira, Chrysostom and Jerome — also treat a new marriage while the first spouse lives as adultery (see their pages). Augustine gives as his ground the sacramentum, the sacred sign of Christ and the Church, and treats it as belonging to the nature of Christian marriage.
  • Like Jerome, Augustine applies the bar to husband and wife alike (“according to a similar rule, if he shall put her away, he is to remain unmarried”); the Latin commentator Ambrosiaster distinguishes the husband’s case from the wife’s.
  • The retraction on “spiritual fornication.” In the earlier Sermon on the Mount Augustine had asked “in what latitude of meaning fornication is to be understood” and argued a broad answer — that idolatry, unbelief, and covetousness are all species of “fornication,” so the exception is “general and universal”.1 In the Retractations he qualified it: “not every sin is spiritual fornication,” whether that is “the fornication for which divorce is allowed is a most knotty question,” and “it is no question at all” that divorce is allowed for carnal sin.5
  • Augustine’s doubts. Near the end of the first book On Adulterous Marriages Augustine wrote that “the whole question of marriage is still very unclear and most complex” and that he would not claim to “have yet unraveled it fully”; in the Retractations (426 or 427) he wrote of the work, “I do not think that I concluded the matter, although I shed light on many of its obscurities.” David G. Hunter (2018), who quotes both passages in translation, dates the treatise c. 421 and describes Pollentius as an “otherwise unknown correspondent,” “probably a bishop or presbyter.” Hunter cites Marie-François Berrouard (1968), Goulven Madec (1996) and the Orthodox bishop Peter L’Huillier (1988) as having noted Augustine’s reticence or doubts on whether a man who divorced an adulterous wife might remarry; John Rist called L’Huillier’s reading “self-indulgent psychologizing.”6
  • Augustine is also a witness on the infant baptism and other timelines.
