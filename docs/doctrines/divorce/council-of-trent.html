The Council of Trent on divorce

Dates: 1563 (Session 24, “Doctrine and Canons on the Sacrament of Matrimony,” promulgated 11 November 1563; the ecumenical council convened at Trent in the Tyrol met, with interruptions, 1545–1563) · Wikipedia
Summary: The definitive Roman Catholic dogmatic close of the divorce question against the Reformers. Session 24 grounds marriage’s indissolubility in the creation ordinance and Christ’s word, then in a series of canons anathematizes the errors of Erasmus, Luther, and Calvin: that the bond may be dissolved for heresy, desertion, or adultery, and that the innocent party may remarry while the other lives. Canon 7 — the decisive one — was worded with deliberate care so as not to condemn the Eastern churches that permit remarriage by pastoral “economy,” anathematizing instead only those who say the church errs in teaching indissolubility.

Claims & evidence

Indissolubility grounded in the creation ordinance (the doctrine chapter)

The introductory doctrine of Session 24 does not argue the point so much as restate the whole line the timeline has traced from Clement and Tertullian to Augustine: marriage is perpetual by its institution, made so by God at the beginning and confirmed by Christ. The chapter opens by putting the doctrine on the lips of Adam:

The first parent of the human race, under the influence of the divine Spirit, pronounced the bond of matrimony perpetual and indissoluble, when he said; This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. Wherefore, a man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they two shall be one flesh.

— Session 24, doctrine on the sacrament of matrimony.1 The chapter goes on to teach that Christ “merited for us… that grace which might perfect that natural love, and confirm that indissoluble union, and sanctify the married,” and that marriage is one of the seven sacraments of the New Law — the very premise Luther had denied in order to dissolve Augustine’s sacramentum. So where Luther and Calvin had made adultery or desertion break the bond, Trent reasserts the bond as a sacramental sign that only death can end.

Canon 7 — the anathema on divorce-with-remarriage for adultery

The heart of the settlement is canon 7, which fixes as dogma the strict, separation-not-remarriage reading of Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9) held by Chrysostom, Jerome, and Augustine — and directly repudiates the Erasmian reading revived by the Reformers:

If any one saith, that the Church has erred, in that she hath taught, and doth teach, in accordance with the evangelical and apostolical doctrine, that the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties; and that both, or even the innocent one who gave not occasion to the adultery, cannot contract another marriage, during the life-time of the other; and, that he is guilty of adultery, who, having put away the adulteress, shall take another wife, as also she, who, having put away the adulterer, shall take another husband; let him be anathema.

— Session 24, canon 7.2 Every clause answers the exact points Erasmus had reopened: the bond survives the adultery; neither party, “even the innocent,” may remarry while the other lives; a new marriage after putting away an adulterous spouse is itself adultery — the very verdict of Hermas and Clement, now under anathema.

The careful wording — not condemning the Greeks

The grammar of canon 7 is conspicuously indirect. It does not anathematize “anyone who says a marriage can be dissolved for adultery” (the form proposed earlier); it anathematizes anyone who says the church errs in teaching that it cannot. That awkward construction is deliberate. When the decree was debated in August 1563, the delegates of the Republic of Venice — whose domains included Greek-rite Christians who dissolved marriages for adultery and permitted remarriage — protested that the original wording would condemn those Eastern Christians directly. In deference the Council recast the canon so that the anathema falls on a denial of the church’s authority to teach indissolubility, not on the Eastern practice itself.3 The point was pressed at the Second Vatican Council by the Melkite archbishop Elias Zoghby, who argued that Trent’s anathema therefore does not reach “what the Orthodox call the principle of economy”; and it has been urged again in the modern debate over the divorced-and-remarried by the Jesuit journal La Civiltà Cattolica.4 So the council that closed the question in the West left, in the very wording of its close, a studied silence toward the tolerant Eastern strand that Origen first noticed, Epiphanius defended, and the Council in Trullo gave its graded penance.

The neighbouring canons — heresy, desertion, and the one dissolution Rome kept

Canon 7 is bracketed by canons that close the other Protestant escape routes and mark the one exception Rome retained. Canon 5 anathematizes the claim “that on account of heresy, or irksome cohabitation, or the affected absence of one of the parties, the bond of matrimony may be dissolved” — barring desertion and incompatibility, the further grounds Luther and Calvin had allowed from 1 Corinthians 7:15.5 Canon 6, by contrast, defends the single case Western canon law did treat as dissoluble — a marriage “contracted, but not consummated” ended by the solemn profession of religion by one party — the exception that keeps the rule.6

Notes

  • Dating. The council sat across three periods (1545–1547, 1551–1552, 1562–1563); Session 24, with its matrimony decree, belongs to the third period and was promulgated 11 November 1563. This page is anchored to that year, the close of the whole conciliar process on marriage.
  • Translation. Quotations follow the standard nineteenth-century English of J. Waterworth (London, 1848), the version reproduced on the linked texts. The later Schroeder translation (1941) renders canon 7 to the same sense: “the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved by reason of adultery… let him be anathema.”
  • What Trent did not do to the East. The care taken over canon 7 is a genuine datum, not apologetics: the recast wording leaves the Eastern discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) formally untouched by the anathema, which is why the point recurs whenever Catholic–Orthodox difference over remarriage is discussed. Trent settled the Latin question; it did not pronounce the Greeks anathematized for their practice.
