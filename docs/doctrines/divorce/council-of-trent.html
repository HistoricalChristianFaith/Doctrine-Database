The Council of Trent on divorce

Dates: 1563 (Session 24, “Doctrine and Canons on the Sacrament of Matrimony,” promulgated 11 November 1563; the ecumenical council convened at Trent in the Tyrol met, with interruptions, 1545–1563) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Catholic Church’s ecumenical council answered the Reformers on divorce in its twenty-fourth session. Session 24 grounds marriage’s indissolubility in the creation ordinance and Christ’s word, then in a series of canons anathematizes positions taught by Erasmus, Luther and Calvin: that the bond may be dissolved for heresy, desertion, or adultery, and that the innocent party may remarry while the other lives. Canon 7 anathematizes those who say the church errs in teaching indissolubility; Giancarlo Pani (2014) reports that it was so worded in order not to condemn the Greek-rite Christians who permitted remarriage.

Claims & evidence

Indissolubility grounded in the creation ordinance (the doctrine chapter)

The introductory doctrine of Session 24 teaches that marriage is perpetual by its institution, made so by God at the beginning and confirmed by Christ. The chapter opens by putting the doctrine on the lips of Adam:

The first parent of the human race, under the influence of the divine Spirit, pronounced the bond of matrimony perpetual and indissoluble, when he said; This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. Wherefore, a man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they two shall be one flesh.

— Session 24, doctrine on the sacrament of matrimony.1 The chapter goes on to teach that Christ “merited for us… that grace which might perfect that natural love, and confirm that indissoluble union, and sanctify the married,” and that marriage is one of the seven sacraments of the New Law, which Luther had denied (see his page). Luther and Calvin had allowed adultery and desertion as grounds for dissolving the bond; Trent teaches the bond as an indissoluble union confirmed by Christ’s grace.

Canon 7 — the anathema on divorce-with-remarriage for adultery

Canon 7 addresses the reading of Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9) under which adultery dissolves the bond and frees the innocent party to remarry, the reading Erasmus had argued and the Reformers adopted:

If any one saith, that the Church has erred, in that she hath taught, and doth teach, in accordance with the evangelical and apostolical doctrine, that the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties; and that both, or even the innocent one who gave not occasion to the adultery, cannot contract another marriage, during the life-time of the other; and, that he is guilty of adultery, who, having put away the adulteress, shall take another wife, as also she, who, having put away the adulterer, shall take another husband; let him be anathema.

— Session 24, canon 7.2 The clauses correspond to points Erasmus had argued: that the bond is dissolved by adultery, that the innocent party may remarry while the other lives, and that a new marriage after putting away an adulterous spouse is not adultery. Hermas, Clement, Chrysostom, Jerome and Augustine had also treated a new marriage after putting away an adulterous spouse as adultery (see their pages).

The wording of canon 7 and the Greek-rite Christians

Canon 7 is worded indirectly. It does not anathematize “anyone who says a marriage can be dissolved for adultery” (the form proposed earlier, according to Giancarlo Pani); it anathematizes anyone who says the church errs in teaching that it cannot. In Pani’s account, when the decree was debated in August 1563, the delegates of the Republic of Venice — whose domains included Greek-rite Christians who dissolved marriages for adultery and permitted remarriage — protested that the original wording would condemn those Eastern Christians directly. The Council then recast the canon so that the anathema falls on a denial of the church’s authority to teach indissolubility, not on the Eastern practice itself.3 The point was pressed at the Second Vatican Council by the Melkite archbishop Elias Zoghby, who argued that Trent’s anathema therefore does not reach “what the Orthodox call the principle of economy”; and Pani raised it again in the Jesuit journal La Civiltà Cattolica (2014), during the modern debate over the divorced and remarried.4 Earlier Eastern leniency toward remarriage is recorded on the pages of Origen, Epiphanius and the Council in Trullo.

The neighbouring canons: heresy, desertion, and the unconsummated marriage

Canon 7 stands between canons on other grounds of dissolution. Canon 5 anathematizes the claim “that on account of heresy, or irksome cohabitation, or the affected absence of one of the parties, the bond of matrimony may be dissolved” — among them desertion, a further ground Luther and Calvin had allowed from 1 Corinthians 7:15.5 Canon 6 affirms that a marriage “contracted, but not consummated” is dissolved by the solemn profession of religion by one party.6

Context

  • Dating. The council sat across three periods (1545–1547, 1551–1552, 1562–1563); Session 24, with its matrimony decree, belongs to the third period and was promulgated 11 November 1563. This page is anchored to that year.
  • Translation. Quotations follow the standard nineteenth-century English of J. Waterworth (London, 1848), the version reproduced on the linked texts. The later Schroeder translation (1941) renders canon 7 to the same sense: “the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved by reason of adultery… let him be anathema.”
  • Canon 7 and the Eastern practice. On the reading of Zoghby and Pani, the recast wording leaves the Eastern discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) outside the anathema, and the canon does not pronounce the Greeks anathematized for their practice.4 The Catholic Culture report on Pani’s article adds that the final canon “still clearly affirms that remarriage is forbidden.”3
