The Council of Elvira on divorce

Dates: c. 306 (a synod of nineteen bishops and some two dozen presbyters met at Elvira — Iliberri, near modern Granada in southern Spain — early in the fourth century; the year is variously placed between c. 300 and c. 306) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving Western conciliar canon law, and with it the first time a church council — not a single teacher — legislates on divorce and remarriage. Among its eighty-one canons, three (8, 9, and 10) penalize the woman who leaves a husband and marries again: she is barred from communion, in the gravest case “even when death approaches.” The canons take the marriage bond to survive the divorce — a new marriage while the first spouse lives is the offence — and so put into enforceable discipline the “separation, not remarriage” reading that Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian had already taught. Eight years later the Council of Arles (314) met the same case with a gentler instrument — counsel, not excommunication.

Claims & evidence

Canon 8: the wife who leaves without cause and remarries — no communion, even at death

The harshest of the three canons targets the woman who abandons a husband for no grave reason and takes another man. For her the church reserves its severest penalty — exclusion from communion with no reconciliation even on the deathbed, a sentence Elvira otherwise keeps for idolatry and murder.

Women who without acceptable cause leave their husbands and join another man may not receive communion even when death approaches.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 8.1 The offence is not the leaving as such but the leaving “without acceptable cause” joined to a second union: the wife has treated a subsisting marriage as though it were dissolved. That the penalty runs to the very hour of death marks how the council rated the wrong — a permanent, unrepented adultery, not a lapse to be absolved.

Canon 9: even against an adulterous husband, no remarriage while he lives

Canon 9 is the decisive one, for it removes the very excuse the earlier writers had wrestled with. Suppose the husband is himself the guilty party — an adulterer — and the baptized wife leaves him on that ground. May she then marry again? The council’s answer is no: the innocence of her cause licenses the separation, never a new marriage while the man she left is alive.

A baptized woman who leaves an adulterous husband who has been baptised, for another man, may not marry him. If she does, she may not receive communion until her former husband dies, unless she is seriously ill.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 9.2 Here the bar is stated in its purest form. The wife has every sympathy the case can give — she is baptized, her husband is the adulterer — and still the second marriage is forbidden, the ban lifting only when death dissolves the first bond. This is exactly the point at which Tertullian had said divorce-for-adultery “has even in Christ a defender” yet “marriage will not be lawful”: Elvira turns that teaching into law. The one relaxation — communion “if she is seriously ill” — is the deathbed mercy Canon 8 withholds, a measure of how the council graded the two cases.

Canon 10: and the one who knowingly marries a man who deserted his wife

Canon 10 runs the same logic outward to third parties and to converts. Its first clause is a concession of the convert’s past — a woman deserted by a merely catechumen (unbaptized) husband, who then remarried, may still be baptized, the sin lying outside the covenant she is only now entering. Its closing clause, though, extends the divorce discipline: a Christian woman who marries a man knowing he deserted a former wife without cause shares his offence and draws Canon 8’s deathbed penalty.

If an unbaptised woman marries another man after being deserted by her husband who was a catechumen, she may still be baptised. This is also true for female catechumens. If a Christian woman marries a man in the knowledge that he deserted his former wife without cause, she may receive communion only at the time of her death.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 10.3 The knowing second wife is treated as Clement had treated the second husband — a partner in the adultery, because it is her marriage that keeps the deserted spouse’s bond broken. (The neighbouring Canon 11 applies the same rule to a female catechumen who knowingly marries such a man: her baptism is deferred five years, unless illness intervenes.)

The reading the canons enforce

Read together, the three canons legislate the “separation, not remarriage” doctrine already met on this timeline in Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian — but now with teeth. Three features stand out. First, the marriage bond is treated as surviving the divorce: the penalties fall on the second marriage, and they lift precisely when the first spouse dies (“until her former husband dies”), the one event that truly dissolves the bond. Second, the innocence of the divorcing party is irrelevant to remarriage: even the wife of an adulterer, who may rightly leave, may not marry again while he lives. Third, the discipline is enforceable — exclusion from the Eucharist, in the worst case with no reconciliation — where the earlier witnesses had only exhorted. Elvira is thus the hinge where the patristic teaching on divorce becomes church law.

The Council of Arles (314): the same case, answered by counsel

Eight years after Elvira, the Council of Arles in Gaul — a much larger, Western-wide synod convened under Constantine — faced the mirror-image case: a young Christian husband who catches his wife in adultery. Its canon 10 reaches the same conclusion Elvira did — he ought not to remarry while she lives — but by a markedly softer instrument. It does not forbid and it does not excommunicate; it counsels.

Concerning those who apprehend their wives in adultery, and the same persons are faithful youths and are prevented from marrying (again), be it resolved that, as much as is able, they be counseled not to take other wives while their own wives are still living, even if the latter are adulterous.

— Council of Arles (314), Canon 10 (numbered 11 in some editions).4 The wording carries a famous tension: the young men “are prevented from marrying” (the indissolubilist premise Elvira shares), yet the council will only see them “counseled” not to, “as much as is able.” Read strictly, it presupposes that remarriage is illicit and merely declines to attach a penalty; read leniently, the retreat to counsel — and the silence about excommunication — is itself a softening of Elvira’s rigor. Either way it fixes the same target from the husband’s side that Elvira fixed from the wife’s: no new marriage while the adulterous spouse still lives. Between them, the two councils are the earliest conciliar canon law on divorce in the Latin church.

Notes

  • The gendered framing. Every one of Elvira’s divorce canons is written about the woman who leaves and remarries; Arles’ canon 10 addresses the husband wronged by an adulterous wife. The corpus does not thereby exempt men — the underlying rule (the bond survives, remarriage while the spouse lives is adultery) is symmetrical, as Hermas had already insisted (“man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way”) — but the canons legislate the cases the bishops actually faced, and those are cast in the idiom of their society.
  • Dating and numbering. Elvira’s year is debated: older reference works place it c. 300–303, others c. 305–306; the canon numbers used here (8, 9, 10, 11) are the standard enumeration of the eighty-one canons. Arles’ divorce canon is cited as Canon 10 in some editions and Canon 11 in others, an artefact of differing counts of the Arles canons; the text is the same.
  • How it sits beside Origen. A generation before Elvira, Origen had recorded — with disapproval — that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living.” Elvira and Arles are the answer of the Western episcopate to precisely that laxity: where some bishops had tolerated remarriage “in comparison with worse things,” these councils write the prohibition into enforceable canon law. The tolerant strand Origen noticed would resurface, and be formalized, in the Eastern practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) — a divergence the later timeline will trace.
