The Council of Elvira on divorce

Dates: c. 306 (a synod of nineteen bishops and some two dozen presbyters met at Elvira — Iliberri, near modern Granada in southern Spain — early in the fourth century; the year is variously placed between c. 300 and c. 306) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant Western conciliar canon law, and the earliest extant conciliar legislation on divorce and remarriage on this timeline. Among its eighty-one canons, three (8, 9, and 10) penalize the woman who leaves a husband and marries again: she is barred from communion, in the gravest case “even when death approaches.” In the canons the offence is a new marriage while the first spouse lives, and the penalty of canon 9 lifts when the former husband dies. Hermas, Clement and Tertullian had also taught that a separated spouse may not remarry (see their pages). About eight years later the Council of Arles (314) took up the husband’s case and resolved that he be counselled not to remarry.

Claims & evidence

Canon 8: the wife who leaves without cause and remarries

Canon 8 concerns the woman who leaves a husband without acceptable cause and takes another man. It excludes her from communion with no reconciliation even on the deathbed, a penalty the council is reported to apply elsewhere to idolatry and murder (⚠ unverified).

Women who without acceptable cause leave their husbands and join another man may not receive communion even when death approaches.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 8.1 The canon names two elements: the leaving “without acceptable cause” (nulla praecedente causa) and the second union. The penalty extends to the hour of death.

Canon 9: the wife of an adulterous husband

Canon 9 takes the case in which the husband is himself an adulterer and the baptized wife leaves him. The council forbids her to marry another while the man she left is alive.

A baptized woman who leaves an adulterous husband who has been baptised, for another man, may not marry him. If she does, she may not receive communion until her former husband dies, unless she is seriously ill.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 9.2 The exclusion lasts until the former husband dies. Tertullian had written that divorce for adultery “has even in Christ a defender” and that for the divorced “marriage will not be lawful” (see his page). Canon 9 allows communion “if she is seriously ill,” which canon 8 does not.

Canon 10: converts, and the woman who knowingly marries a man who deserted his wife

Canon 10 turns to converts and to third parties. Its first clause concerns the convert’s past — a woman deserted by a merely catechumen (unbaptized) husband, who then remarried, may still be baptized, the sin lying outside the covenant she is only now entering. Its closing clause concerns a Christian woman who marries a man knowing he deserted a former wife without cause: she may receive communion only at death.

If an unbaptised woman marries another man after being deserted by her husband who was a catechumen, she may still be baptised. This is also true for female catechumens. If a Christian woman marries a man in the knowledge that he deserted his former wife without cause, she may receive communion only at the time of her death.

— Council of Elvira, Canon 10.3 Clement had likewise held the second husband guilty with the divorcing one (see his page). (The neighbouring Canon 11 applies the same rule to a female catechumen who knowingly marries such a man: her baptism is deferred five years, unless illness intervenes.)

The three canons together

Three features recur across the canons. The penalties fall on the second marriage, and in canon 9 they lift when the first spouse dies (“until her former husband dies”). The wife of an adulterer, who leaves him, is still forbidden to marry again while he lives. And the sanction is exclusion from the Eucharist, in canon 8 with no reconciliation; the earlier witnesses on this timeline are individual writers rather than synods.

The Council of Arles (314): the same case, answered by counsel

Eight years after Elvira, the Council of Arles in Gaul — a much larger, Western-wide synod convened under Constantine — faced the mirror-image case: a young Christian husband who catches his wife in adultery. Its canon 10 resolves that he be counselled not to remarry while she lives; it attaches no penalty.

Concerning those who apprehend their wives in adultery, and the same persons are faithful youths and are prevented from marrying (again), be it resolved that, as much as is able, they be counseled not to take other wives while their own wives are still living, even if the latter are adulterous.

— Council of Arles (314), Canon 10 (numbered 11 in some editions).4 The canon says the young men “are prevented from marrying (again),” and resolves that they be “counseled” not to, “as much as is able.” Elvira’s canons address the wife’s case; Arles’ canon addresses the husband’s. The two councils are the earliest extant conciliar canons on divorce in the Latin church.

Context

  • The gendered framing. Every one of Elvira’s divorce canons is written about the woman who leaves and remarries; Arles’ canon 10 addresses the husband wronged by an adulterous wife. Hermas had written that “man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way” (see his page); the councils’ canons are each framed around one party’s case.
  • Dating and numbering. Elvira’s year is debated: older reference works place it c. 300–303, others c. 305–306; the canon numbers used here (8, 9, 10, 11) are the standard enumeration of the eighty-one canons. Arles’ divorce canon is cited as Canon 10 in some editions and Canon 11 in others, an artefact of differing counts of the Arles canons; the text is the same. Josep Vilella (2014) argues that the canons attributed to the “council of Elvira” derive from a variety of different times and places, a view David G. Hunter (2018) calls convincing.5
  • Lehmkuhl’s reading. Augustinus Lehmkuhl (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) cites canon 9 as maintaining “without the least ambiguity the permanence of the marriage bond, even in the case of adultery,” and reads Arles as declaring “the illicit character of such a second marriage, because it says of these husbands, ‘They are forbidden to marry.’”6
  • Origen’s report. Some decades before Elvira, Origen had recorded that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living,” a permission given “in comparison with worse things” (see his page). The Eastern practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) appears later on the timeline.
