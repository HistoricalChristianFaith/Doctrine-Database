Ambrose of Milan on divorce

Dates: c. 385 (the divorce material stands in the Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8, on Luke 16:18, completed c. 389, and in On Abraham, Bk 1, c. 382–387) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan and the mentor of Augustine, gives the strict Latin verdict on divorce two decades before Augustine grounds it: the marriage bond is not dissolved while both partners live, so a man may dismiss an adulterous wife but may not marry another — “the marriage which you imagine is adultery.” He treats Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart concession that Christ withdrew (“he who divorces his wife, tears his flesh, divides his body”), and reads Paul’s desertion case (1 Corinthians 7:15) narrowly — the abandoned believer is freed from blame, not licensed to remarry. Crucially, and unlike his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, Ambrose rests the bar on the equality of the sexes before God: “what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man… A husband owes the same chastity to his wife as a wife owes to her husband.” So he stands with Lactantius, Gregory of Nazianzus, and Jerome in reaching the strict conclusion by the egalitarian road, against the double standard of Basil and Ambrosiaster.

Claims & evidence

The bond is not dissolved: divine law forbids divorce (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

Opening his exposition of Luke’s exceptionless saying (Luke 16:18, “Everyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery”), Ambrose sets divine law against the Roman civil right of dismissal. A man thinks he may put his wife away because human law permits it; but the creation ordinance, “What God has joined together, let no man separate,” overrides the legislators themselves.

Therefore, do not divorce your wife, lest you deny God, the author of your union… Listen to what the Lord has said: ‘Whoever divorces his wife makes her commit adultery’ (Matthew 5:32). For if it is not permissible to change one’s spouse while the husband is alive, the desire to sin can easily creep in… So you dismiss your wife as if it were a right, without any crime; and you think it is allowed to you, because human law does not prohibit it; but divine law does prohibit it. If you obey men, fear God. Hear the law of the Lord, to which even those who enact laws obey: What God has joined together, let no man separate (Matthew 19:6).

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8 (on Luke 16:18). Ambrose makes the divine ordinance override the permissive Roman statute, exactly the tension between civil right and Gospel that Jerome and Augustine would press after him.1

Remarriage while the first spouse lives is adultery (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8; On Abraham 1)

Because the bond survives the divorce, a second marriage is not marriage but adultery. In the Luke exposition Ambrose puts the case bluntly: whether the dismissed wife stays single or marries again, the husband who cast her out is guilty — and the “marriage” she contracts is adultery.

Suppose she does not marry after being divorced. And should not these things displease you, to whom she keeps faith with an adulterer? Suppose she does marry. This is your crime of necessity; and the marriage which you imagine is adultery. For what does it matter, whether you allow it openly by confessing the crime, or admit an adulterer under the guise of a husband…?

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. The same bar is stated positively in On Abraham, addressed to the baptismal candidates: the living spouse blocks any new union.2

You are bound to your wife, do not seek a way out: because it is not allowed for you, while your wife is alive, to take another wife. For seeking another, when you have your own, is a crime of adultery, which is even more serious because you think that you should seek authority for your own sin through the law.

— On Abraham, Bk 1 (§59). So Ambrose lands, before Augustine, on the settled Latin verdict: dismissal of an adulterous wife is licit, remarriage while she lives is not.3

Moses’ bill of divorce was a hardness-of-heart concession (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

To the objection “How did Moses command to give a bill of divorce?” (Deuteronomy 24:1) Ambrose answers with Christ’s own reply: the bill was a permission granted for hardness of heart, not a divine command, and it never overturned the creation law of “one flesh.”

For the hardness of your hearts, Moses permitted you to give a bill of divorce, and to dismiss your wives: but from the beginning it was not so (Matth. XIX, 8). Moses permitted, he says, not God commanded; but from the beginning the law of God is. What is the law of God? A man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife: and they shall be two in one flesh (Gen. II, 24). Therefore, he who divorces his wife, tears his flesh, divides his body.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. The reading is the common patristic one — the Mosaic concession bends the creation ordinance without abolishing it, and the Gospel restores the original — shared with Tertullian, Origen, and later Augustine.4

The Pauline case: the deserted believer is freed from blame, not licensed to remarry (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

Ambrose does engage the one apparent exception — Paul’s ruling that a believer deserted by an unbeliever “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15). He resolves it not by dissolving the marriage but by denying that a Christian-pagan union was ever “joined by God” in the first place; where the unbeliever departs, the Apostle merely lifts blame from the one abandoned, without commanding or authorizing the departure.

And he added: But if the unbeliever separates, let him separate (Ibid., 15). At the same time, the Apostle denied that the law is divine, so that any marriage can be dissolved; he neither commanded nor gave authority to anyone who wants to leave, but he removed blame from the one who is abandoned.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. So Ambrose reads the desertion case far more narrowly than his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, who took the same verse as freeing the abandoned believer to remarry (the seed of the later “Pauline privilege”). For Ambrose, the deserted spouse is exculpated, not liberated to a new marriage.5

One chastity for both sexes (On Abraham 1)

What most sharply distinguishes Ambrose from Ambrosiaster is the ground he gives. Warning the men of his congregation against fornication, Ambrose flatly denies the Roman double standard that bound only the wife: the husband owes his wife exactly the chastity he demands of her.

Every act of fornication is adultery, and what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man. A husband owes the same chastity to his wife as a wife owes to her husband. Whatever is committed with a woman who is not a lawful wife is condemned as the crime of adultery.

— On Abraham, Bk 1 (§25). This is the egalitarian rationale Jerome would echo (“what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men”) and the Greek Gregory of Nazianzus pressed at the same time (“there is one Maker of man and woman”) — the same equal standard, reaching the same strict verdict, that Ambrosiaster denied by appeal to male headship.6

Notes

  • The two Latin “Ambroses” reach opposite verdicts. For roughly a millennium the permissive commentary of Ambrosiaster — which licenses an innocent husband to remarry a divorced wife on the ground that “the man is the head of the woman” — circulated under Ambrose’s own name; Erasmus coined the label “Ambrosiaster” (“would-be Ambrose”) once he doubted the attribution. The real Ambrose, on display here, holds the strict indissolubility line and rests it on the equality of the sexes — the exact reverse of the pseudonymous work’s permissive, headship-based reading. When later writers (including Erasmus reviving the permissive case) cited “Ambrose” for divorce-with-remarriage, they were in fact citing Ambrosiaster.
  • Strict verdict, egalitarian ground. Ambrose joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Chrysostom, and Jerome in the separation-not-remarriage reading — but with Lactantius, Gregory, and Jerome he grounds it on a single sexual standard for both spouses, not the double standard Basil traced to “custom” and Ambrosiaster to headship.
  • As the bishop who baptized and formed Augustine, Ambrose supplies the verdict Augustine would later ground in the marriage bond as sacramentum. Ambrose appears on this wiki also as a witness on other timelines — the perpetual virginity of Mary and the Old Testament canon among them.
