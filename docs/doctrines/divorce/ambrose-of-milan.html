Ambrose of Milan on divorce

Dates: c. 385 (the divorce material stands in the Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8, on Luke 16:18, completed c. 389, and in On Abraham, Bk 1, c. 382–387) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan, who baptized Augustine, teaches that divine law forbids a man to dismiss his wife “without any crime” and to marry another while she lives — “the marriage which you imagine is adultery.” He treats Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart concession that Christ withdrew (“he who divorces his wife, tears his flesh, divides his body”), and reads Paul’s desertion case (1 Corinthians 7:15) as removing blame from the abandoned believer, without speaking of a new marriage. Unlike the Latin commentator Ambrosiaster, Ambrose rests the bar on the equality of the sexes before God: “what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man… A husband owes the same chastity to his wife as a wife owes to her husband.” Lactantius, Gregory of Nazianzus and Jerome also argue from a single standard for both sexes; Basil reports a custom that treats husband and wife differently, and Ambrosiaster appeals to male headship.

Claims & evidence

Divine law set against the civil right of dismissal (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

Opening his exposition of Luke’s exceptionless saying (Luke 16:18, “Everyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery”), Ambrose sets divine law against the Roman civil right of dismissal. A man thinks he may put his wife away because human law permits it; but the creation ordinance, “What God has joined together, let no man separate,” overrides the legislators themselves.

Therefore, do not divorce your wife, lest you deny God, the author of your union… Listen to what the Lord has said: ‘Whoever divorces his wife makes her commit adultery’ (Matthew 5:32). For if it is not permissible to change one’s spouse while the husband is alive, the desire to sin can easily creep in… So you dismiss your wife as if it were a right, without any crime; and you think it is allowed to you, because human law does not prohibit it; but divine law does prohibit it. If you obey men, fear God. Hear the law of the Lord, to which even those who enact laws obey: What God has joined together, let no man separate (Matthew 19:6).

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8 (on Luke 16:18). Ambrose sets the divine ordinance above what “human law does not prohibit.” Jerome and Augustine also contrast civil divorce law with the Gospel (see their pages).1

Remarriage while the first spouse lives (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8; On Abraham 1)

For Ambrose a second marriage contracted while the first spouse lives is adultery. In the Luke exposition he puts the case this way: whether the dismissed wife stays single or marries again, the husband who cast her out is guilty — and the “marriage” she contracts is adultery.

Suppose she does not marry after being divorced. And should not these things displease you, to whom she keeps faith with an adulterer? Suppose she does marry. This is your crime of necessity; and the marriage which you imagine is adultery. For what does it matter, whether you allow it openly by confessing the crime, or admit an adulterer under the guise of a husband…?

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. The same bar is stated positively in On Abraham, addressed to the baptismal candidates: the living spouse blocks any new union.2

You are bound to your wife, do not seek a way out: because it is not allowed for you, while your wife is alive, to take another wife. For seeking another, when you have your own, is a crime of adultery, which is even more serious because you think that you should seek authority for your own sin through the law.

— On Abraham, Bk 1 (§59). In both works the bar falls on a new marriage “while your wife is alive.”3

Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart concession (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

To the objection “How did Moses command to give a bill of divorce?” (Deuteronomy 24:1) Ambrose answers with Christ’s own reply: the bill was a permission granted for hardness of heart, not a divine command, and it never overturned the creation law of “one flesh.”

For the hardness of your hearts, Moses permitted you to give a bill of divorce, and to dismiss your wives: but from the beginning it was not so (Matth. XIX, 8). Moses permitted, he says, not God commanded; but from the beginning the law of God is. What is the law of God? A man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife: and they shall be two in one flesh (Gen. II, 24). Therefore, he who divorces his wife, tears his flesh, divides his body.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. On this reading the Mosaic concession did not abolish the creation ordinance, which the Gospel restores; Tertullian, Origen and Augustine also read the bill of divorce as a concession to hardness of heart (see their pages).4

The Pauline case of desertion (Exposition of Luke, Bk 8)

Ambrose also takes up Paul’s ruling that a believer deserted by an unbeliever “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15). He reads it through the question whether a Christian-pagan union was “joined by God”; where the unbeliever departs, the Apostle, in his reading, lifts blame from the one abandoned, without commanding or authorizing the departure.

And he added: But if the unbeliever separates, let him separate (Ibid., 15). At the same time, the Apostle denied that the law is divine, so that any marriage can be dissolved; he neither commanded nor gave authority to anyone who wants to leave, but he removed blame from the one who is abandoned.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Bk 8. The passage speaks of removing blame from the one abandoned and does not speak of a new marriage. Ambrosiaster treats the same verse as leaving the deserted believer no longer bound (see his page).5

One chastity for both sexes (On Abraham 1)

Ambrose and Ambrosiaster differ in the ground they give. Warning the men of his congregation against fornication, Ambrose rejects a standard that bound only the wife: the husband owes his wife the same chastity he asks of her.

Every act of fornication is adultery, and what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man. A husband owes the same chastity to his wife as a wife owes to her husband. Whatever is committed with a woman who is not a lawful wife is condemned as the crime of adultery.

— On Abraham, Bk 1 (§25). Jerome (“what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men”) and, in the same decades, Gregory of Nazianzus (“there is one Maker of man and woman”) state a single standard in similar terms; Ambrosiaster appeals to male headship to distinguish the husband’s case from the wife’s.6

Reception and context

  • Ambrose and Ambrosiaster. The commentary of Ambrosiaster — which allows an innocent husband who divorces an adulterous wife to marry again, on the ground that “the man is the head of the woman” — long circulated under Ambrose’s name (see the Ambrosiaster page on the attribution). Ambrose’s own works quoted here forbid remarriage while the spouse lives and rest the bar on the equality of the sexes. Erasmus knew the Ambrosiaster commentary under the name of Ambrose (see his page).
  • The ground of the bar. Of the writers on this timeline who forbid remarriage while the first spouse lives, Lactantius, Gregory of Nazianzus and Jerome, like Ambrose, appeal to a single sexual standard for both spouses; Basil reports a “custom” that treats them differently, and Ambrosiaster grounds his distinction in headship.
  • Ambrose baptized Augustine, whose treatment of the marriage bond as sacramentum is on his own page. Ambrose is also a witness on other timelines — the perpetual virginity of Mary and the Old Testament canon among them.
