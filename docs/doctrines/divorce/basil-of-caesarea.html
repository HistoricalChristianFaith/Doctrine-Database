Basil of Caesarea on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Canonical Epistles to Amphilochius of Iconium — Letters 188, 199, 217) · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, answered a series of disciplinary questions from his younger colleague Amphilochius in three long letters (188, 199, 217), the “Canonical Epistles,” later received into the Eastern canonical collections. On divorce they set out a penitential procedure, and they state openly a double standard: the same act — abandoning a spouse and taking another — is judged more severely in a woman than in a man, a difference Basil attributes to custom while holding that the Lord’s sentence “applies… to men and women alike.” A husband who falls into fornication is a fornicator, but his wife must take him back; an adulterous wife is expelled and may not be received. A wife may not leave even an adulterous husband; if she does, and remarries, she is an adulteress. Basil repeatedly refers the stricter treatment of wives to “custom.”

Claims & evidence

The Lord’s sentence and custom (Letter 188, canon 9)

In the ninth canon of the first canonical letter Basil states that the Lord’s sentence applies to both sexes, and records that the church’s custom treats them differently.

The sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication, applies, according to the argument, to men and women alike. Custom, however, does not so obtain. Yet, in relation with women, very strict expressions are to be found… Yet custom ordains that men who commit adultery and are in fornication be retained by their wives. Consequently I do not know if the woman who lives with the man who has been dismissed can properly be called an adulteress; the charge in this case attaches to the woman who has put away her husband, and depends upon the cause for which she withdrew from wedlock.

— Basil, Letter 188 (Canonica Prima), canon 9, NPNF2 vol. 8. He then takes up the grounds a wife might give for leaving and rejects them in turn: if she is beaten and refuses to submit, “it would be better for her to endure than to be separated from her husband”; if she objects to financial loss, “she would not have sufficient ground”; even if her reason is “his living in fornication we do not find this in the custom of the church.” Of the man who himself deserts his wife, Basil applies the saying: “if the man who has deserted his wife goes to another, he is himself an adulterer because he makes her commit adultery; and the woman who lives with him is an adulteress.”1

The husband’s fornication and the wife’s (Letter 199, canon 21)

Canon 21 of the second letter sets the two cases side by side. A married man who falls into fornication is punished as a fornicator — but, Basil says, there is no canon making him an adulterer so long as the woman he sinned with was unmarried; and his wife is bound to receive him. The adulterous wife, by contrast, is “expelled.”

If a man living with a wife is not satisfied with his marriage and falls into fornication, I account him a fornicator, and prolong his period of punishment. Nevertheless, we have no canon subjecting him to the charge of adultery, if the sin be committed against an unmarried woman… He, however, who has committed fornication is not to be cut off from the society of his own wife. So the wife will receive the husband on his return from fornication, but the husband will expel the polluted woman from his house. The argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.

— Letter 199 (Canonica Secunda), canon 21. Basil again refers the discipline to custom: “the argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.”2

The deserted husband and the abandoned wife (Letter 199, canons 35 & 48)

Two further canons in the same letter set the man’s and the woman’s cases side by side. The man deserted by his wife is “deserving of pardon” and may communicate; the woman abandoned by her husband “ought… to remain as she is” — she may not remarry, on pain of being called an adulteress by the Lord’s own words.

In the case of a man deserted by his wife, the cause of the desertion must be taken into account. If she appear to have abandoned him without reason, he is deserving of pardon, but the wife of punishment. Pardon will be given to him that he may communicate with the Church.

— Letter 199, canon 35.3

The woman who has been abandoned by her husband, ought, in my judgment, to remain as she is. The Lord said, “If any one leave his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, he causeth her to commit adultery;” thus, by calling her adulteress, He excludes her from intercourse with another man. For how can the man being guilty, as having caused adultery, and the woman, go without blame, when she is called adulteress by the Lord for having intercourse with another man?

— Letter 199, canon 48. Basil rests the woman’s obligation on Matthew 5:32; Hermas, Clement and Tertullian also bar a separated spouse from a new marriage (see their pages). For the deserted man canon 35 speaks of “pardon.”4

The penance for abandoning a lawful wife (Letter 217, canon 77)

The third letter fixes the penitential tariff. One who deserts a lawful spouse bears “the penalty of adultery,” and Basil sets out the graduated seven-year penance the church required for restoration to communion — a year weeping outside the doors, two as a “hearer,” three kneeling, then standing with the faithful in the seventh before being admitted to the oblation.

He who abandons the wife, lawfully united to him, is subject by the sentence of the Lord to the penalty of adultery. But it has been laid down as a canon by our Fathers that such sinners should weep for a year, be hearers for two years, in kneeling for three years, stand with the faithful in the seventh; and thus be deemed worthy of the oblation, if they have repented with tears.

— Letter 217 (Canonica Tertia), canon 77. The penance runs through the stations of weeping, hearing, kneeling and standing; the canon attaches “the penalty of adultery” to the man who “abandons the wife, lawfully united to him.”5

Reception and context

  • The three “canonical letters” (188, 199, 217), written c. 374–375 to Amphilochius of Iconium, were later received into the Eastern canonical corpus and given conciliar force by the Council in Trullo (692), which enumerated Basil’s canons among the church’s binding rules (see the Council in Trullo page). The twelfth-century commentaries of Zonaras and Balsamon on these canons are on the Byzantine canonists page.
  • Basil’s repeated “custom, however, does not so obtain” / “the custom has so obtained” distinguishes what he takes the Lord’s words to require (equal treatment of men and women) from what the church practises (a stricter standard for wives); he legislates the practice and says “the argument here is not easy.” A century earlier Origen had reported that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living” (see his page). The Eastern Orthodox practice of pastoral oikonomia (“economy”), in the accounts of John Meyendorff and Bishop Athenagoras (Peckstadt), is described in the introduction to the timeline, and readings of Basil’s canons in its Basil entry.
  • On the exception clause, canon 48 quotes Matthew 5:32 to bar the abandoned wife from a new marriage, and canon 9 speaks of withdrawing from wedlock “save on account of fornication” without addressing remarriage. The Council of Elvira and Augustine apply their rules to husband and wife alike; Basil applies his unequally and attributes the difference to custom. John M. Rist, who held that most patristic authors rejected second marriages after divorce, wrote that “Basil’s name must be added to that of Ambrosiaster as a man prepared, less willingly though more fatefully, at least to tolerate a second marriage after divorce in limited circumstances.”6
