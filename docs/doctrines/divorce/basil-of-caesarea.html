Basil of Caesarea on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Canonical Epistles to Amphilochius of Iconium — Letters 188, 199, 217) · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, answered a series of disciplinary questions from his younger colleague Amphilochius in three long letters (188, 199, 217) that the Eastern church later received as canon law — the “Canonical Epistles.” On divorce they give the fullest procedural settlement of the fourth-century East, and they do so with unusual candour about a double standard: the same act — abandoning a spouse and taking another — is judged far more harshly in a woman than in a man, and Basil says so openly, grounding the difference not in Scripture but in custom. A husband who falls into fornication is a fornicator, but his wife must take him back; an adulterous wife is expelled and may not be received. A wife may not leave even an adulterous husband; if she does, and remarries, she is an adulteress. Repeatedly Basil notes that “custom” is stricter on women than the Lord’s words strictly warrant — a self-aware tension between scriptural equality and customary inequality that became foundational for later Byzantine practice.

Claims & evidence

Christ’s rule binds men and women alike — but “custom” does not (Letter 188, canon 9)

The ninth canon of the first canonical letter is Basil’s most self-aware statement of the tension. He begins by conceding the Lord’s rule falls on both sexes equally, then records that the church’s practice does not follow suit — a candid admission that the operative discipline is harsher on the wife than the divorce saying itself requires.

The sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication, applies, according to the argument, to men and women alike. Custom, however, does not so obtain. Yet, in relation with women, very strict expressions are to be found… Yet custom ordains that men who commit adultery and are in fornication be retained by their wives. Consequently I do not know if the woman who lives with the man who has been dismissed can properly be called an adulteress; the charge in this case attaches to the woman who has put away her husband, and depends upon the cause for which she withdrew from wedlock.

— Basil, Letter 188 (Canonica Prima), canon 9, NPNF2 vol. 8. The wife’s grounds for leaving are then weighed and, in nearly every case, denied: if she is beaten and refuses to submit, “it would be better for her to endure than to be separated from her husband”; if she objects to financial loss, “she would not have sufficient ground”; even if her reason is “his living in fornication we do not find this in the custom of the church.” The one asymmetry Basil keeps from Scripture is the man’s culpability when he initiates: “if the man who has deserted his wife goes to another, he is himself an adulterer because he makes her commit adultery; and the woman who lives with him is an adulteress.”1

The husband’s fornication is a lesser charge; the wife must take him back (Letter 199, canon 21)

Canon 21 of the second letter states the double standard at its baldest. A married man who falls into fornication is punished as a fornicator — but, Basil says, there is no canon making him an adulterer so long as the woman he sinned with was unmarried; and his wife is bound to receive him. The adulterous wife, by contrast, is “expelled.”

If a man living with a wife is not satisfied with his marriage and falls into fornication, I account him a fornicator, and prolong his period of punishment. Nevertheless, we have no canon subjecting him to the charge of adultery, if the sin be committed against an unmarried woman… He, however, who has committed fornication is not to be cut off from the society of his own wife. So the wife will receive the husband on his return from fornication, but the husband will expel the polluted woman from his house. The argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.

— Letter 199 (Canonica Secunda), canon 21. Once again Basil flags the discipline as custom, not derivation: “the argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.” He does not pretend the asymmetry follows from the Gospel; he reports that it is what the church does.2

The deserted man is pardonable; the abandoned woman should stay unmarried (Letter 199, canons 35 & 48)

Two further canons in the same letter set the man’s and the woman’s cases side by side. The man deserted by his wife is “deserving of pardon” and may communicate; the woman abandoned by her husband “ought… to remain as she is” — she may not remarry, on pain of being called an adulteress by the Lord’s own words.

In the case of a man deserted by his wife, the cause of the desertion must be taken into account. If she appear to have abandoned him without reason, he is deserving of pardon, but the wife of punishment. Pardon will be given to him that he may communicate with the Church.

— Letter 199, canon 35.3

The woman who has been abandoned by her husband, ought, in my judgment, to remain as she is. The Lord said, “If any one leave his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, he causeth her to commit adultery;” thus, by calling her adulteress, He excludes her from intercourse with another man. For how can the man being guilty, as having caused adultery, and the woman, go without blame, when she is called adulteress by the Lord for having intercourse with another man?

— Letter 199, canon 48. Basil here rests the woman’s obligation directly on Matthew 5:32 — the same “separation, not remarriage” reading of the exception clause held by Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian — while for the man the operative standard is the milder “pardon” of canon 35.4

Abandoning a lawful spouse incurs the penalty of adultery — a seven-year penance (Letter 217, canon 77)

The third letter fixes the penitential tariff. One who deserts a lawful spouse bears “the penalty of adultery,” and Basil sets out the graduated seven-year penance the church required for restoration to communion — a year weeping outside the doors, two as a “hearer,” three kneeling, then standing with the faithful in the seventh before being admitted to the oblation.

He who abandons the wife, lawfully united to him, is subject by the sentence of the Lord to the penalty of adultery. But it has been laid down as a canon by our Fathers that such sinners should weep for a year, be hearers for two years, in kneeling for three years, stand with the faithful in the seventh; and thus be deemed worthy of the oblation, if they have repented with tears.

— Letter 217 (Canonica Tertia), canon 77. The penance itself — weeping, hearing, kneeling, standing — is the standard four-station Eastern discipline; what marks the divorce case is that mere abandonment of a lawful spouse, apart from any second union, already draws the adulterer’s penalty.5

Notes

  • The three “canonical letters” (188, 199, 217), written c. 374–375 to Amphilochius of Iconium, were later received into the Eastern canonical corpus and given conciliar force by the Council in Trullo (692), which enumerated Basil’s eighty-five canons among the church’s binding rules. The divorce canons summarized here thus became the operative marriage-discipline of the Byzantine church.
  • Basil’s repeated “the custom is otherwise” / “the custom has so obtained” is a datum for the debate over Eastern pastoral oikonomia (“economy”): he distinguishes what the Lord’s words strictly require (equal treatment of men and women) from what the church practices (a harsher standard for wives), and legislates the practice while acknowledging it is not a straight derivation from Scripture. That candid gap between rule and custom — first reported by Origen a century earlier (“some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living”) — is the seed of the later Orthodox settlement, in which the bond’s indissolubility is upheld in principle yet relaxed by pastoral discretion. The point is weighed among the divorce-doctrine arguments on the summary’s Arguments section.
  • On the exception clause itself Basil is a witness for the strict, separation-not-remarriage reading: canon 48 quotes Matthew 5:32 to bar the abandoned wife from a new marriage, and canon 9 grants divorce “save on account of fornication” without licensing remarriage while the first spouse lives. Where he diverges sharply from the Western trajectory (Elvira, later Augustine) is in the frankly unequal application — the Latin canons press toward a single rule binding both sexes, whereas Basil codifies a double standard he traces to custom.
