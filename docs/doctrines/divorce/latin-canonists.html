The Latin canonists (Gratian and Peter Lombard) on divorce

Dates: c. 1140–1155 — Gratian, whose Decretum (the Concordia discordantium canonum) was completed after the Second Lateran Council of 1139 · Wikipedia; Peter Lombard (c. 1096–1160), bishop of Paris, whose Sentences Book IV is dated c. 1155–58 · Wikipedia
Summary: The two twelfth-century textbooks that fixed the settled law and theology of marriage for the Latin West. Gratian’s Decretum gathers the discordant patristic and conciliar authorities on divorce into a single harmonized rule — separation for fornication is licit, but the bond survives and neither party may remarry while the other lives — and in doing so transmits the one permissive Latin text (the “Ambrose” commentary now known to be Ambrosiaster) alongside the strict ones, then reinterprets it away toward indissolubility. Peter Lombard’s Sentences supply the theology: marriage is a sacramentum, a sacred sign of the union of Christ and the Church, made by present-tense consent, and therefore not to be separated — the dismissed spouse must remain unmarried. Together they hand the framework of Augustine to the scholastics: the Sentences became the textbook every theologian (including Aquinas) commented on, and the Decretum the foundation of the canon law the Council of Trent inherited.

Claims & evidence

Gratian’s governing rule: fornication permits separation, never remarriage while the spouse lives (C. 32 q. 7)

Gratian frames the divorce question through a fictitious legal case (Causa 32) and answers it in his own connecting dicta. Having marshalled the authorities — headed by Augustine’s rule that “when a divorce intervenes, the nuptial covenant is not abolished” (Interueniente diuortio non aboletur confederatio nuptialis) — he states the settled rule most sharply in the dictum after canon 16:

His auctoritatibus euidentissime monstratur, quod quicumque causa fornicationis uxorem suam dimiserit, illa uiuente aliam ducere non poterit, et, si duxerit, reus adulterii erit.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7, dictum post c. 16 (Friedberg col. 1143–44), digitized Friedberg edition (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek) — translating: “By these authorities it is shown most evidently that whoever has dismissed his wife for cause of fornication cannot, while she lives, marry another; and if he does, he will be guilty of adultery.” The quaestio’s canon-rubrics say the same in Gratian’s own headings — “the bond of marriage cannot be dissolved by fornication” (Vinculum coniugii fornicatione dissolui non potest), “a marriage once proven entered is by no reasoning dissolved,” and “she is proven an adulteress who marries another while her husband lives.”1 So Gratian’s harmonized rule is exactly the separation-not-remarriage reading of Tertullian, Jerome, and Augustine.

He transmits the permissive “Ambrose” (Ambrosiaster) text — then reinterprets it toward indissolubility (C. 32 q. 7 c. 17 + dictum)

Gratian’s method — the concordia discordantium canonum, the “harmony of discordant canons” — requires him to set the strict authorities beside the one Latin text that pulls the other way: the commentary on 1 Corinthians 7 circulating under the name of Ambrose, which modern scholarship assigns to Ambrosiaster. Gratian reproduces its permissive gloss verbatim as canon 17:

“Uxor a uiro non discedat, nisi causa fornicationis; quod si discesserit, aut maneat innupta, aut reconcilietur uiro suo; et uir similiter non dimittat uxorem.” Ideo non subdit de uiro quod de uxore premisit, quia uiro licet ducere aliam.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7 c. 17 (Friedberg col. 1143–44), digitized Friedberg edition — translating: “‘Let not a wife depart from her husband, save for cause of fornication; but if she departs, let her either remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband; and let the husband likewise not dismiss his wife.’ Therefore he does not add of the husband what he laid down of the wife, because the husband is permitted to marry another.” This is precisely the double-standard licence Ambrosiaster drew from Paul’s silence about the man — the husband may remarry, the wife may not. Rather than let the discordant text stand, Gratian harmonizes it away: he reports that some hold the permissive “Ambrose” passage to be an interpolation (a falsatoribus dicitur insertum), and that others, wishing to save it, restrict it to incestuous fornication only — concluding that, since no authority permits remarriage while the first spouse lives, the text can mean only that the innocent party may remarry after the guilty spouse’s death.

Some, however, wanting to defend Ambrose’s opinion, contend that his statement should not be understood as applying to any kind of fornication for which the husband would be allowed to dismiss his wife and marry another during the life time of the first… but that it should be understood as applying to incestuous fornication… However, since no authority permits a husband to marry another wife while the first one is still alive… not that a husband can take another wife while the first one is still alive, but that after the death of the unfaithful… the other partner… can have another relationship; the unfaithful partner… may not remarry.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7, dictum (“V. Pars,” after c. 18; Friedberg col. 1145–46), digitized Friedberg edition; English from the translation of John Wijngaards, as published online. So the discordant permissive voice, once assembled into the Decretum, is neutralized in the very act of transmission — the harmonization the later canonists and Aquinas inherit already resolved toward the strict reading.2

Peter Lombard: marriage is made by present-tense consent (Sentences IV, d. 27)

Where Gratian fixes the law, Peter Lombard fixes the theology. His marriage treatise in Book IV of the Sentences gives the definition that would govern the schools — marriage is “the marital union of a man and a woman… maintaining an undivided manner of life” (individuam vitae consuetudinem retinens) — and, decisively, locates what makes a marriage not in consummation but in present-tense consent:

Efficiens autem causa matrimonii est consensus, non quilibet, sed per verba expressus, nec de futuro sed de praesenti.

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 27, c. 3, LombardPress critical text (cf. the same words quoted by Aquinas, Scriptum super Sententiis IV, d. 27, at Corpus Thomisticum) — translating: “The efficient cause of matrimony is consent — not just any consent, but that expressed through words, and not of the future but of the present.” This consensus de praesenti doctrine — the exchange of present consent, not the wedding-night, constitutes the bond — became the settled Western law of marriage-formation, and set the terms of the scholastic debate every later master (Bonaventure, Aquinas, Scotus) argued on Lombard’s distinctions.3

Peter Lombard: marriage as sacramentum — a sign of Christ and the Church, and therefore not separated (Sentences IV, dd. 26, 31)

Lombard grounds indissolubility, as Augustine had, in the marriage sacramentum — the sign of the union of Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5:31–32):

Cum igitur coniugium sacramentum sit, et sacrum signum est et sacrae rei, scilicet coniunctionis Christi et Ecclesiae, sicut ait Apostolus: … “Relinquet homo patrem et matrem et adhaerebit uxori suae, et erunt duo in carne una.”

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 26, c. 6, LombardPress critical text — translating: “Since, therefore, marriage is a sacrament, it is both a sacred sign and [a sign] of a sacred thing — namely, of the union of Christ and the Church, as the Apostle says: ‘A man shall leave father and mother and cleave to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh.’” From the three goods of marriage — “fidelity, offspring, sacrament” (fides, proles, sacramentum) — it is the sacramentum that carries indissolubility: the good “that the marriage not be separated, and that the man or woman dismissed not be joined to another, not even for the sake of offspring” (in sacramento, ut coniugium non separetur, et dimissus vel dimissa nec causa prolis alteri conjungatur).4

Peter Lombard: adultery permits separation only, and reciprocally — the dismissed must remain unmarried (Sentences IV, d. 35)

Applied to the exception clause, Lombard’s doctrine is the strict one, and he presses it with the egalitarian edge of Jerome, whom he quotes: the Lord “commanded that a wife not be dismissed except for cause of fornication, and, if she has been dismissed, that she remain unmarried,” and “the same liberty is not taken from women” (eadem licentia non tollitur feminis). The right to separate for adultery is reciprocal, and the guilty party forfeits it:

Si vero quaeritur, an adulter adulteram possit dimittere causa fornicationis, dicimus, quod nequit adultera uxor dimitti a viro, nisi et ipse expers fornicationis existat, et e converso… Ex his apparet, quod adulter adulteram dimittere non valet, et e converso.

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 35, c. 2, LombardPress critical text — translating: “If it is asked whether an adulterer may dismiss an adulteress for cause of fornication, we say that an adulterous wife cannot be dismissed by the husband unless he too is free of fornication, and vice versa… From these it appears that an adulterer cannot dismiss an adulteress, and vice versa.” So Lombard holds the bond intact under adultery — dismissal from bed and board is permitted, remarriage is not — and binds the sexes to a single standard, against the double standard of Ambrosiaster that Gratian was busy neutralizing.5

Notes

  • Two textbooks, one settlement. Gratian and Peter Lombard are the twin foundations of the Latin high-medieval synthesis: the Decretum (c. 1140) became the base text of canon law, glossed and expanded into the Corpus Iuris Canonici; the Sentences (c. 1155–58) became the standard theology textbook of the universities, on which every master lectured. On divorce they agree entirely — the bond is indissoluble, adultery licenses separation but not remarriage — and between them they transmit Augustine’s sacramentum to Aquinas, who wrote his own Commentary on the Sentences (the source of the Summa Supplement’s divorce questions) on Lombard’s Book IV.
  • The “Ambrose” problem. Gratian’s wrestling with the permissive text is a snapshot of a millennium-long confusion: the commentary he cites as “Ambrose” is Ambrosiaster’s, misattributed until the sixteenth century. Because it bore the great name of Ambrose, it could not simply be dismissed — hence Gratian’s labour to brand it an interpolation or narrow it to incest. The same text, under the same false name, is the ancient ally Erasmus would later invoke to reopen the whole question in favour of remarriage.
  • East and West in one century. While Gratian and Peter Lombard were fixing the Latin law of indissolubility, Zonaras and Balsamon were writing the East’s authoritative commentary on the same strict canons — but reading them through imperial civil law toward the pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) that permits a bounded remarriage. The twelfth century is thus the moment the two traditions’ settled divorce law visibly diverges from the shared patristic inheritance.
  • The Latin passages above are quoted verbatim from the digitized primary editions (the Friedberg Corpus Iuris Canonici for Gratian; the LombardPress critical text for the Sentences). The English renderings are translations of that verified Latin (the standard published English of the Sentences is Giulio Silano’s PIMS translation); the Gratian harmonizing dictum is given in John Wijngaards’ published translation, cited above.
