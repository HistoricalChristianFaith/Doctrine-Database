The Latin canonists (Gratian and Peter Lombard) on divorce

Dates: c. 1140–1155 — Gratian, whose Decretum (the Concordia discordantium canonum) was completed after the Second Lateran Council of 1139 · Wikipedia; Peter Lombard (c. 1096–1160), bishop of Paris, whose Sentences Book IV is dated c. 1155–58 · Wikipedia
Summary: Two twelfth-century textbooks of the Latin West, one of canon law and one of theology. Gratian’s Decretum gathers the patristic and conciliar authorities on divorce and states a single rule — separation for fornication is licit, and neither party may remarry while the other lives. It reproduces the one permissive Latin text it cites (the “Ambrose” commentary now attributed to Ambrosiaster) alongside the strict ones, and then reads it as permitting remarriage only after the guilty spouse’s death. Peter Lombard’s Sentences treat marriage as a sacramentum, a sacred sign of the union of Christ and the Church, made by present-tense consent, and hold that the dismissed spouse must remain unmarried. The Sentences became the textbook on which later theologians (including Aquinas) wrote commentaries, and the Decretum the base text of the medieval canon law.

Claims & evidence

Gratian’s rule: fornication permits separation, not remarriage while the spouse lives (C. 32 q. 7)

Gratian frames the divorce question through a fictitious legal case (Causa 32) and answers it in his own connecting dicta. Having gathered the authorities — headed by Augustine’s rule that “when a divorce intervenes, the nuptial covenant is not abolished” (Interueniente diuortio non aboletur confederatio nuptialis) — he states the rule in the dictum after canon 16:

His auctoritatibus euidentissime monstratur, quod quicumque causa fornicationis uxorem suam dimiserit, illa uiuente aliam ducere non poterit, et, si duxerit, reus adulterii erit.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7, dictum post c. 16 (Friedberg col. 1143–44), digitized Friedberg edition (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek) — translating: “By these authorities it is shown most evidently that whoever has dismissed his wife for cause of fornication cannot, while she lives, marry another; and if he does, he will be guilty of adultery.” The quaestio’s canon-rubrics say the same — “the bond of marriage cannot be dissolved by fornication” (Vinculum coniugii fornicatione dissolui non potest), “a marriage once proven entered is by no reasoning dissolved,” and “she is proven an adulteress who marries another while her husband lives.”1 Tertullian, Jerome and Augustine also bar remarriage after separation for fornication (see their pages).

The “Ambrose” (Ambrosiaster) text and Gratian’s reading of it (C. 32 q. 7 c. 17 and dictum)

Gratian’s method — the concordia discordantium canonum, the “harmony of discordant canons” — sets beside the strict authorities a Latin text that reads otherwise: the commentary on 1 Corinthians 7 circulating under the name of Ambrose, now attributed to Ambrosiaster (see his page). Gratian reproduces its gloss as canon 17:

“Uxor a uiro non discedat, nisi causa fornicationis; quod si discesserit, aut maneat innupta, aut reconcilietur uiro suo; et uir similiter non dimittat uxorem.” Ideo non subdit de uiro quod de uxore premisit, quia uiro licet ducere aliam.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7 c. 17 (Friedberg col. 1143–44), digitized Friedberg edition — translating: “‘Let not a wife depart from her husband, save for cause of fornication; but if she departs, let her either remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband; and let the husband likewise not dismiss his wife.’ Therefore he does not add of the husband what he laid down of the wife, because the husband is permitted to marry another.” This is the reading Ambrosiaster drew from Paul’s silence about the man — the husband may remarry, the wife may not. Gratian then reports that some hold the “Ambrose” passage to be an interpolation (a falsatoribus dicitur insertum), and that others, wishing to defend it, restrict it to incestuous fornication; he concludes that, since no authority permits remarriage while the first spouse lives, the text means that the innocent party may remarry after the guilty spouse’s death.

Some, however, wanting to defend Ambrose’s opinion, contend that his statement should not be understood as applying to any kind of fornication for which the husband would be allowed to dismiss his wife and marry another during the life time of the first… but that it should be understood as applying to incestuous fornication… However, since no authority permits a husband to marry another wife while the first one is still alive… not that a husband can take another wife while the first one is still alive, but that after the death of the unfaithful… the other partner… can have another relationship; the unfaithful partner… may not remarry.

— Gratian, Decretum, C. 32 q. 7, dictum (“V. Pars,” after c. 18; Friedberg col. 1145–46), digitized Friedberg edition; English from the translation of John Wijngaards, as published online. The Decretum thus transmits the “Ambrose” text together with Gratian’s reading of it.2

Peter Lombard: marriage is made by present-tense consent (Sentences IV, d. 27)

Peter Lombard’s marriage treatise in Book IV of the Sentences defines marriage as “the marital union of a man and a woman… maintaining an undivided manner of life” (individuam vitae consuetudinem retinens) and locates what makes a marriage not in consummation but in present-tense consent:

Efficiens autem causa matrimonii est consensus, non quilibet, sed per verba expressus, nec de futuro sed de praesenti.

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 27, c. 3, LombardPress critical text (cf. the same words quoted by Aquinas, Scriptum super Sententiis IV, d. 27, at Corpus Thomisticum) — translating: “The efficient cause of matrimony is consent — not just any consent, but that expressed through words, and not of the future but of the present.” Later masters, among them Bonaventure, Aquinas and Scotus, lectured on Lombard’s distinctions, including this consensus de praesenti teaching.3

Peter Lombard: marriage as sacramentum, a sign of Christ and the Church (Sentences IV, dd. 26, 31)

Lombard grounds indissolubility, as Augustine had, in the marriage sacramentum — the sign of the union of Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5:31–32):

Cum igitur coniugium sacramentum sit, et sacrum signum est et sacrae rei, scilicet coniunctionis Christi et Ecclesiae, sicut ait Apostolus: … “Relinquet homo patrem et matrem et adhaerebit uxori suae, et erunt duo in carne una.”

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 26, c. 6, LombardPress critical text — translating: “Since, therefore, marriage is a sacrament, it is both a sacred sign and [a sign] of a sacred thing — namely, of the union of Christ and the Church, as the Apostle says: ‘A man shall leave father and mother and cleave to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh.’” Of the three goods of marriage — “fidelity, offspring, sacrament” (fides, proles, sacramentum) — Lombard assigns indissolubility to the sacramentum: the good “that the marriage not be separated, and that the man or woman dismissed not be joined to another, not even for the sake of offspring” (in sacramento, ut coniugium non separetur, et dimissus vel dimissa nec causa prolis alteri conjungatur).4

Peter Lombard: adultery permits separation, reciprocally; the dismissed remain unmarried (Sentences IV, d. 35)

On the exception clause Lombard quotes Jerome: the Lord “commanded that a wife not be dismissed except for cause of fornication, and, if she has been dismissed, that she remain unmarried,” and “the same liberty is not taken from women” (eadem licentia non tollitur feminis). The right to separate for adultery is reciprocal, and the party who is also guilty forfeits it:

Si vero quaeritur, an adulter adulteram possit dimittere causa fornicationis, dicimus, quod nequit adultera uxor dimitti a viro, nisi et ipse expers fornicationis existat, et e converso… Ex his apparet, quod adulter adulteram dimittere non valet, et e converso.

— Peter Lombard, Sentences IV, d. 35, c. 2, LombardPress critical text — translating: “If it is asked whether an adulterer may dismiss an adulteress for cause of fornication, we say that an adulterous wife cannot be dismissed by the husband unless he too is free of fornication, and vice versa… From these it appears that an adulterer cannot dismiss an adulteress, and vice versa.” Lombard permits dismissal for adultery, does not permit remarriage, and applies one standard to both sexes; Ambrosiaster had applied different standards to husband and wife (see his page).5

Notes

  • Two textbooks. The Decretum (c. 1140) became the base text of medieval canon law, glossed and later included in the Corpus Iuris Canonici; the Sentences (c. 1155–58) became the standard theology textbook of the universities. On divorce they agree: adultery permits separation and not remarriage while the other spouse lives. Aquinas wrote his own Commentary on the Sentences on Lombard’s Book IV, the source of the Summa Supplement’s divorce questions.
  • The “Ambrose” text. The commentary Gratian cites as “Ambrose” is now attributed to Ambrosiaster; on its medieval attribution to Ambrose of Milan see the Ambrosiaster page. Gratian reports two responses to it: that it was interpolated, and that it concerns incest only. Erasmus cites the same commentary (see his page).
  • The Eastern commentators. In the same century Zonaras and Balsamon wrote commentaries on the Eastern canons on divorce, reading them alongside imperial civil law (see their page).
  • The Latin passages above are quoted verbatim from the digitized primary editions (the Friedberg Corpus Iuris Canonici for Gratian; the LombardPress critical text for the Sentences). The English renderings are translations of that Latin (the standard published English of the Sentences is Giulio Silano’s PIMS translation); the Gratian dictum is given in John Wijngaards’ published translation, cited above.
