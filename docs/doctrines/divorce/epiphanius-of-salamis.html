Epiphanius of Salamis on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Panarion, or Medicine-Chest against Heresies, compiled c. 375–378) · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, wrote the Panarion against eighty heresies. In section 59 of the Panarion, answering the Novatianist “Purists” (Cathari) who barred the remarried from communion, he sets down a principle of concession to weakness: one who cannot keep continence after a spouse dies or after a divorce — expressly including divorce “for some reason, fornication, adultery or something else” — may lawfully take a second partner, and “God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness.” The second marriage is tolerated, not commended: the once-married is “more highly respected.” Augustinus Lehmkuhl (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) read the passage as speaking of a second marriage after the death of the divorced wife.1

Claims & evidence

The Cathari and the “twice-married” (Panarion 59.3.1)

The target of section 59 is the Novatianist sect Epiphanius calls the “Purists” (Katharoi, Cathari), who — beyond refusing to readmit the lapsed — would not commune with anyone who took a second spouse. Epiphanius states their position in order to answer it, calling it “perfectly silly” (59.3.2).

And next, the same people have pressed on from this and invented some other things. For they too say that they have the same faith which we do, but they will not communicate with the twice-married. For if someone marries a second wife after baptism, they never admit him again.

— Panarion 59.3.1 (Frank Williams trans.). In the passage that follows (59.4), Epiphanius treats both those who remarry after a spouse’s death and those who remarry after a divorce under one principle of mercy.2

The concession to weakness (Panarion 59.4.8–10)

Having said that the once-married are “more highly respected,” Epiphanius holds that the church does not reject the person who, unable to bear continence, marries again — and he lists divorce alongside bereavement as an occasion where the second marriage is tolerated, not censured.

But <this> can be tolerated <in> the laity as a concession to weakness — even remarriage after the first wife’s death by those who cannot stop with the first wife. (9) And the husband of [only] one wife is more highly respected and honored by all members of the church. But if the man could not be content with the one wife, who had died, <or> if there has been a divorce for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else — and the man marries a second wife or the woman a second husband, God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness. (10) The holy word and God’s holy church show mercy to such a person, particularly if he is devout otherwise and lives by God’s law — not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive, but <by letting> him marry a second wife lawfully if the opportunity arises, after being parted from the first.

— Panarion 59.4.8–10 (Frank Williams trans.). In Williams’ translation the passage names divorce “for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else,” after which the one “parted from the first” may “marry a second wife lawfully” and remains in the church; it excludes having “two wives at once while the one is still alive.” Epiphanius frames it as the mercy of “the holy word and God’s holy church” toward weakness.3

The first marriage and the second (Panarion 59.6.3)

Epiphanius restates the principle as a scale of honour: the first union is a divine ordinance, the second (and even a further) is borne with as a concession.

How much more, surely, [must this apply to] one who is lawfully married to a second wife! The first wife is a divine ordinance; the second, a concession to human weakness. And even if one marries a further wife [after the second], his weakness is still tolerated.

— Panarion 59.6.3 (Frank Williams trans.). Here Epiphanius’ primary case is remarriage after a spouse’s death (digamy), grounded on Paul’s “a wife is bound by law so long as her husband liveth… only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39); the “concession to human weakness” formula is the one used at 59.4.4

Reception and context

  • Other Eastern witnesses. Origen had reported that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living”; Basil legislated a double standard he referred to “custom” (see their pages). Epiphanius presents the concession as the mind of “God’s word” and “God’s holy church.” The Eastern Orthodox practice of pastoral oikonomia (“economy”), in the accounts of John Meyendorff and Bishop Athenagoras (Peckstadt), is described in the introduction to the timeline.
  • How 59.4 has been read. Augustinus Lehmkuhl (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) wrote that Epiphanius, “who is especially reproached with teaching that the husband who had put away his wife because of adultery or another crime was allowed by Divine law to marry another,” “is speaking in reality of a second marriage after the death of the divorced wife,” declared “less honourable.”1 Frank Williams’ translation renders the case as a second marriage “after being parted from the first,” following “a divorce for some reason.” The Greek text has variants: Williams notes that, “because this apparently lax attitude toward divorce is surprising in Epiphanius,” C. Riggi (“Nouvelle lecture du Panarion LIX (Epiphane et le divorce)”) “returns practically to the text of Petavius, though with some modifications.”3
  • Epiphanius is also a witness on other timelines — the Old Testament canon, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and the divine name among them. His Panarion (“Medicine-Chest,” c. 375–378) catalogues eighty sects that Epiphanius counts as heresies and argues against them; section 59 answers the Novatianist “Purists.”
