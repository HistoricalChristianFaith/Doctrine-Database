Epiphanius of Salamis on divorce

Dates: c. 375 (the Panarion, or Medicine-Chest against Heresies, compiled c. 375–378) · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, was among the fiercest heresy-hunters of the fourth-century East — yet on divorce his verdict is the most openly lenient of any early witness on this timeline. In section 59 of the Panarion, refuting the rigorist Novatianist “Purists” (Cathari) who barred the remarried from communion, he sets down the Eastern principle of concession to weakness: one who cannot keep continence after a spouse dies or after a divorce — expressly including divorce “for some reason, fornication, adultery or something else” — may lawfully take a second partner, and “God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness.” It is the clearest patristic statement that remarriage after divorce is tolerated for the incontinent — not commended, but not condemned — and a foundational datum for the later Orthodox discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

Claims & evidence

The rigorists err in barring the “twice-married” from the church (Panarion 59.3.1)

The target of section 59 is the Novatianist sect Epiphanius calls the “Purists” (Katharoi, Cathari), who — beyond refusing to readmit the lapsed — would not commune with anyone who took a second spouse. Epiphanius states their position in order to refute it as “perfectly silly.”

And next, the same people have pressed on from this and invented some other things. For they too say that they have the same faith which we do, but they will not communicate with the twice-married. For if someone marries a second wife after baptism, they never admit him again.

— Panarion 59.3.1 (Frank Williams trans.). The “twice-married” (digamoi) here covers both those who remarry after a spouse’s death and those who remarry after a divorce; Epiphanius’ refutation folds the two cases together under a single principle of mercy.1

Remarriage after divorce is tolerated as a concession to weakness (Panarion 59.4.8–10)

This is the decisive passage. Having granted that the once-married and continent are “more highly respected,” Epiphanius insists the church does not reject the person who, unable to bear continence, marries again — and he lists divorce alongside bereavement as an occasion where the second marriage is tolerated, not censured.

But <this> can be tolerated <in> the laity as a concession to weakness — even remarriage after the first wife’s death by those who cannot stop with the first wife. (9) And the husband of [only] one wife is more highly respected and honored by all members of the church. But if the man could not be content with the one wife, who had died, <or> if there has been a divorce for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else — and the man marries a second wife or the woman a second husband, God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness. (10) The holy word and God’s holy church show mercy to such a person, particularly if he is devout otherwise and lives by God’s law — not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive, but <by letting> him marry a second wife lawfully if the opportunity arises, after being parted from the first.

— Panarion 59.4.8–10 (Frank Williams trans.). Two things are carefully held together. First, the concession is real and explicitly covers divorce: the incontinent party who is “parted from the first” may “marry a second wife lawfully” and remains in the church. Second, it is not a licence for bigamy — “not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive” — nor a commendation, since the once-married is “more highly respected.” The frame is pastoral mercy toward human frailty, not a dissolution-and-remarriage right in the Western legal sense.2

The first marriage is God’s ordinance; the second, a tolerated weakness (Panarion 59.6.3)

Epiphanius restates the principle as a graded scale of honour: the first union is willed by God, the second (and even a further) is borne with as a concession — the church punishes neither.

How much more, surely, [must this apply to] one who is lawfully married to a second wife! The first wife is a divine ordinance; the second, a concession to human weakness. And even if one marries a further wife [after the second], his weakness is still tolerated.

— Panarion 59.6.3 (Frank Williams trans.). Here Epiphanius’ primary case is remarriage after a spouse’s death (digamy), grounded on Paul’s “a wife is bound by law so long as her husband liveth… only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39); but the “concession to human weakness” formula is the same one under which 59.4 admitted divorce-and-remarriage, so the two cases are governed by one rule of mercy.3

Notes

  • Epiphanius stands with Origen and Basil as an Eastern witness to the church tolerating what its strict rule forbade — but he is the most explicit of the three. Origen only reported that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living” (and disapproved); Basil legislated a double standard he traced to “custom.” Epiphanius, by contrast, positively defends the concession as the mind of “God’s word” and “God’s holy church.” The passage is a foundational datum for the later Orthodox settlement of pastoral oikonomia (“economy”) — the bond’s indissolubility upheld in principle, yet relaxed by pastoral discretion for the weak. The point is weighed among the divorce-doctrine arguments on the summary’s Arguments section.
  • A disputed reading. Because so lax an attitude to divorce is unexpected in a rigorist heresy-hunter, the passage has been read more restrictively. The older Catholic tradition (following the Catholic Encyclopedia, s.v. “Divorce”) took Epiphanius to mean a second marriage only after the death of the divorced first wife, and to declare it merely “less honourable” rather than sinless. The Greek text is itself contested: Frank Williams’ edition notes the variant readings adopted by Petavius and revived by C. Riggi, which bear on exactly how much is conceded. On any reading, however, the plain sense of 59.4.9 — “if there has been a divorce… and the man marries a second wife… God’s word does not censure them” — extends the concession to remarriage after divorce, which is why the passage is cited across the confessional divide.
  • Epiphanius already appears on this wiki as a witness on other timelines — the Old Testament canon, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and the divine name among them. His Panarion (“Medicine-Chest,” c. 375–378) catalogues and refutes eighty heresies; section 59 answers the Novatianist “Purists.”
