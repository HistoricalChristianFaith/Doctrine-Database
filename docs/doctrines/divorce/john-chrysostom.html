John Chrysostom on divorce

Dates: c. 390 (the Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, preached at Antioch) · Wikipedia
Summary: John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch around 390 and later archbishop of Constantinople, teaches in his homilies on Matthew that a divorce does not end the marriage: a man who dismisses his wife (except for fornication) makes her an adulteress and is himself to blame even if he takes no new wife; anyone who marries a divorced woman commits adultery, “for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her.” He names fornication as the one ground on which Christ leaves “one manner of dismissal.” On Paul’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) he holds the separated wife to “remain unmarried, or be reconciled” — “so as not to introduce any other to be her husband.” In a separate Homily on the Bill of Divorce he sets this rule against the permissions of the civil law.

Claims & evidence

Dismissing a wife makes her an adulteress; marrying a divorced woman is adultery (Homily 17 on Matthew, on Matthew 5:31–32)

Expounding the Sermon on the Mount’s divorce saying, Chrysostom places guilt on both men in the case — the one who dismisses and the one who marries the dismissed woman — and holds the first culpable even if he never remarries, because he has “made the first an adulteress.”

And observe Him everywhere addressing His discourse to the man. Thus, “He that putteth away his wife,” saith He, “causeth her to commit adultery, and he that marrieth a woman put away, committeth adultery.” That is, the former, though he take not another wife, by that act alone hath made himself liable to blame, having made the first an adulteress; the latter again is become an adulterer by taking her who is another’s.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 17, NPNF1 vol. 10. On Chrysostom’s reading the second man is an adulterer because he takes “her who is another’s.”1

The cast-out wife “continues to be the wife of him that expelled her” (Homily 17 on Matthew)

Chrysostom states that divorce does not end the marriage. He takes up the objection that the second husband is innocent because “the other hath cast her out,” and answers that the woman remains the first man’s wife, so that a second union is adultery.

For tell me not this, “the other hath cast her out;” nay, for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her. Then lest He should render the wife more self-willed, by throwing it all upon him who cast her out, He hath shut against her also the doors of him who was afterwards receiving her; in that He saith, “He who marrieth her that is put away committeth adultery;” and so makes the woman chaste even though unwilling, and blocks up altogether her access to all.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 17, NPNF1 vol. 10. Of the husband he writes that Christ “leaves one manner of dismissal, when He saith, ‘Except for the cause of fornication’”; of the wife, that she must “either keep the husband, who was originally allotted to her, or being cast out of that house, not have any other refuge.”2

Christ “gives the law with His own authority” (Homily 62 on Matthew, on Matthew 19:9)

On the second divorce discourse — Jesus’ reply to the Pharisees at Matthew 19 — Chrysostom notes that Christ first silences his questioners from creation and Moses, and then pronounces the prohibition as a standing command, on the same footing as his rulings on foods and the Sabbath.

“But I say unto you, Whosoever shall put away his wife except it be for fornication, and marry another, committeth adultery.” For since he had stopped their mouths, He then gives the law with His own authority, like as touching the meats, like as touching the Sabbath.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 62, NPNF1 vol. 10. Chrysostom presents the prohibition, with its exception “except it be for fornication,” as the law Christ lays down on his own authority.3

The separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled” (Homily 19 on 1 Corinthians, on 1 Corinthians 7:10–11)

On Paul’s charge Chrysostom holds that a separation, if it happens, does not open into a second marriage: where husband and wife live apart, the wife remains with her husband and may not take another.

Now what is that which “to the married the Lord commanded? That the wife depart not from her husband: but if she depart, let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband.”… it were better, he says, that such things should not be at all; but however if they take place, let the wife remain with her husband, if not to cohabit with him, yet so as not to introduce any other to be her husband.

— Homily on the First Epistle to the Corinthians 19, NPNF1 vol. 12. On both the Gospel and the Pauline texts Chrysostom bars a new marriage while the first spouse lives.4

Notes

  • Chrysostom explains why Christ is “everywhere addressing His discourse to the man”: Christ threatens the husbands in order to correct the wives — “the woman is rather a weak creature. For this cause letting her go, in his threatening against the men He fully corrects her remissness.” Basil, by contrast, records a “custom” that treats wives more strictly than husbands (see his page).
  • Chrysostom also devoted a whole sermon to the subject — the Homily on the Bill of Divorce (De libello repudii, an exegesis of 1 Corinthians 7:39–40), preached at Antioch. There he sets the marriage bond, broken by death, against the permissions of the civil law, warning that God “will not judge you… according to those laws… but according to those He Himself established”; in his account a remarriage the state allows is adultery before God while the first spouse lives.5
  • On the exception clause Chrysostom allows the husband to send away a wife for fornication and bars a new marriage while the first spouse lives, as do Hermas, Clement and Tertullian (see their pages). His near-contemporaries Ambrosiaster (in the West) and Epiphanius (in the East) allowed a second marriage after divorce in some cases (see their pages). Origen had earlier reported church leaders permitting remarriage while a spouse lived; later Byzantine practice is recorded on the Byzantine canonists and Council in Trullo pages.
