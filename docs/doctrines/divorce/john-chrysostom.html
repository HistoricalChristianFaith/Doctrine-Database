John Chrysostom on divorce

Dates: c. 390 (the Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, preached at Antioch) · Wikipedia
Summary: John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch around 390 and later archbishop of Constantinople, is the great Greek preacher of strict indissolubility. In his running homilies on Matthew he teaches that the marriage bond survives a divorce: a man who dismisses his wife (except for fornication) makes her an adulteress and is himself to blame even if he takes no new wife; anyone who marries a divorced woman commits adultery, “for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her.” The one lawful ground for sending a wife away is fornication, and even then no new marriage follows while the first spouse lives. On Paul’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) he holds the separated wife to “remain unmarried, or be reconciled” — never to “introduce any other to be her husband.” He is thus the leading Eastern witness for the separation-not-remarriage reading against which the later Byzantine practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) is measured.

Claims & evidence

Dismissing a wife makes her an adulteress; marrying a divorced woman is adultery (Homily 17 on Matthew, on Matthew 5:31–32)

Expounding the Sermon on the Mount’s divorce saying, Chrysostom presses the guilt onto both men in the case — the one who dismisses and the one who marries the dismissed woman — and insists the first is culpable even if he never remarries, because he has “made the first an adulteress.”

And observe Him everywhere addressing His discourse to the man. Thus, “He that putteth away his wife,” saith He, “causeth her to commit adultery, and he that marrieth a woman put away, committeth adultery.” That is, the former, though he take not another wife, by that act alone hath made himself liable to blame, having made the first an adulteress; the latter again is become an adulterer by taking her who is another’s.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 17, NPNF1 vol. 10. The bond, on Chrysostom’s reading, is not severed by the divorce — which is why the second man is an adulterer for taking a woman who is still married in God’s sight.1

The cast-out wife “continues to be the wife of him that expelled her” (Homily 17 on Matthew)

The heart of Chrysostom’s indissolubility is a single clause: divorce does not end the marriage. He anticipates the objection that the second husband is innocent because “the other hath cast her out,” and rejects it — the woman remains the first man’s wife, so a second union is adultery and every door to it is shut.

For tell me not this, “the other hath cast her out;” nay, for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her. Then lest He should render the wife more self-willed, by throwing it all upon him who cast her out, He hath shut against her also the doors of him who was afterwards receiving her; in that He saith, “He who marrieth her that is put away committeth adultery;” and so makes the woman chaste even though unwilling, and blocks up altogether her access to all.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 17, NPNF1 vol. 10. The only lawful dismissal is for fornication — “even for him He leaves one manner of dismissal, when He saith, ‘Except for the cause of fornication’” — and Chrysostom treats even that as a licence to send away, not to remarry, since the woman must “either keep the husband, who was originally allotted to her, or being cast out of that house, not have any other refuge.”2

Christ “gives the law with His own authority” (Homily 62 on Matthew, on Matthew 19:9)

On the second divorce discourse — Jesus’ reply to the Pharisees at Matthew 19 — Chrysostom notes that Christ first silences his questioners from creation and Moses, and only then pronounces the prohibition as a standing command, on the same footing as his rulings on clean foods and the Sabbath.

“But I say unto you, Whosoever shall put away his wife except it be for fornication, and marry another, committeth adultery.” For since he had stopped their mouths, He then gives the law with His own authority, like as touching the meats, like as touching the Sabbath.

— Homily on the Gospel of Matthew 62, NPNF1 vol. 10. The exception (“except it be for fornication”) is again the single ground; the prohibition of remarriage-while-married is the law Christ lays down on his own authority.3

The separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled” (Homily 19 on 1 Corinthians, on 1 Corinthians 7:10–11)

Chrysostom reads Paul’s own divorce charge the same way: separation, if it happens, may not open into a second marriage. Even where husband and wife live apart, the wife stays bound to the first man and may not take another.

Now what is that which “to the married the Lord commanded? That the wife depart not from her husband: but if she depart, let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband.”… it were better, he says, that such things should not be at all; but however if they take place, let the wife remain with her husband, if not to cohabit with him, yet so as not to introduce any other to be her husband.

— Homily on the First Epistle to the Corinthians 19, NPNF1 vol. 12. So on both the Gospel and the Pauline texts Chrysostom yields the same rule: the bond outlasts the separation, and remarriage while the first spouse lives is barred.4

Notes

  • Chrysostom’s habit of “everywhere addressing His discourse to the man” is not, on his reading, a double standard but a rhetorical strategy: Christ threatens the husbands in order to protect the wives — “the woman is rather a weak creature. For this cause letting her go, in his threatening against the men He fully corrects her remissness.” This is the opposite move from Basil, who codified a frankly unequal “custom” harsher on wives; Chrysostom presses the single rule onto the men precisely to secure the wife’s equal standing.
  • Beyond the running homilies, Chrysostom devoted a whole sermon to the subject — the Homily on the Bill of Divorce (De libello repudii, an exegesis of 1 Corinthians 7:39–40), preached at Antioch. There he sets the marriage bond, broken only by death, against the permissions of the civil law, warning that God “will not judge you… according to those laws… but according to those He Himself established” — so a remarriage the state allows is still, before God, adultery while the first spouse lives.5
  • On the exception clause Chrysostom belongs with the strict, separation-not-remarriage line of Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, and Basil — fornication licenses sending the wife away, never a new marriage. He stands directly against the permissive Latin reading of his near-contemporary Ambrosiaster (a man may remarry after divorcing a sinning wife) and against the concession-to-weakness defended by Epiphanius. As the most influential Greek preacher of indissolubility, Chrysostom is the standard against which the later Byzantine relaxation into pastoral oikonomia — the gap between the Lord’s rule and the church’s practice first flagged by Origen and Basil — is measured.
