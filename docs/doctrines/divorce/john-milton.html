John Milton on divorce

Dates: c. 1643 (b. 1608 – d. 1674) · Wikipedia
Summary: The radical Protestant outlier who pressed the Erasmian opening far past adultery and desertion. In four prose tracts of 1643–1645 — headed by The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce (1643, expanded 1644) — Milton argued that a settled incompatibility of mind that defeats the chief end of marriage (mutual society and help) is itself a just ground for divorce, well beyond the adultery-and-desertion the magisterial Reformers allowed. He read Christ’s prohibition as aimed only at hard-hearted, capricious dismissal, Moses’ “some uncleanness” broadly, and Matthew’s exception as illustrative, not exhaustive. A named forerunner of modern “irretrievable breakdown” / no-fault reasoning, he was condemned by his own Puritan contemporaries.

Claims & evidence

Incompatibility of mind is a just ground for divorce — a greater one than sexual impotence

The very “position” that heads the treatise states the thesis that scandalized his contemporaries: a fixed, unchangeable contrariety of temperament that frustrates the peace of married life is a stronger reason for divorce than bodily frigidity (the one ground the canon law already conceded).

That indisposition, unfitnes, or contrariety of mind, arising from a cause in nature unchangeable, hindring, and ever likely to hinder the main benefits of conjugall society, which are solace and peace, is a greater reason of divorce then naturall frigidity, especially if there be no children, and that there be mutuall consent.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 1, the position (Chapter I), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room.

The chief end of marriage is mutual society and conversation, not procreation or remedy of lust

The whole case rests on a redefinition of marriage’s purpose. Where the tradition ranked children and the “remedy against fornication” first, Milton reads Genesis 2:18 (“It is not good that the man should be alone”) to make companionship the highest end — so a marriage that fails at that fails at its chief end, whatever else it achieves.

It is not good, saith he, that man should be alone; I will make him a help meet for him. From which words so plain, lesse cannot be concluded, nor is by any learned Interpreter, then that in Gods intention a meet and happy conversation is the chiefest and the noblest end of mariage.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 1, Chapter II, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. He defines marriage itself as “the apt and cheerfull conversation of man with woman, to comfort and refresh him against the evil of solitary life.”

Christ’s prohibition was aimed at hard-hearted, capricious dismissal — not at a just and necessary divorce

Milton meets the Gospel head-on. Christ, he argues, was rebuking the Pharisees’ “unbounded licence” — the wanton, malicious putting-away Moses had tolerated “for the hardness of your hearts” — and it would have been out of place, in that hostile audience, to explain the “good mans requisit liberty” to divorce for genuine, blameless incompatibility.

Moses for the hardnesse of your heart suffer'd you, that is, such as you to put away your wives… for it was seasonable that they should hear their own unbounded licence rebukt, but not seasonable for them to hear a good mans requisit liberty explain'd.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 2, Chapter VIII, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. On the same ground he reads Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1) broadly — of “some displeasing natural quality or unfitnes” of mind or body, not adultery alone.

“Fornication” in Matthew’s exception is to be taken broadly — the exception illustrative, not exhaustive

Rather than let Matthew’s “except for fornication” (Matthew 19:9) cap the grounds at adultery, Milton widens porneia itself to cover an entrenched, contemptuous unfitness for the marriage — so the clause names one instance of the deeper failure, not the sole permitted ground.

fornication is tak'n in Scripture for such a continual headstrong behaviour, as tends to plain contempt of the husband.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 2, Chapter XVIII, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. He develops the point-by-point exegesis of the four key passages — Genesis 1:27–28 & 2:18, Deuteronomy 24:1–2, Matthew 5:31–32 & 19:3–11, and 1 Corinthians 7:10–16 — in the companion tract Tetrachordon (1645).

Notes

  • The Protestant left boundary. Milton radicalizes the pastoral argument Erasmus had made — that to bind spouses who have come to detest one another breeds “mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder” — but where Erasmus, Luther, and Calvin stopped at adultery and desertion as bond-dissolving grounds, Milton adds bare incompatibility. He is the point where the Protestant dissolution principle is pressed to its logical extreme, a named forerunner of modern “irretrievable breakdown” / no-fault reasoning.
  • Condemned by his own side. The tracts appeared during the Westminster Assembly and were denounced as scandalous: the Stationers’ Company complained of the unlicensed book to Parliament, and the Presbyterian divine Herbert Palmer, preaching before both Houses on 13 August 1644, branded the divorce tract a “wicked booke” that “deserves to be burnt.” The Westminster Confession (1648) would soon reassert the narrower adultery-and-desertion rule as the confessional Protestant settlement. (See Ernest Sirluck’s introduction to the Yale Complete Prose Works of John Milton, vol. 2 (1959).)
  • Milton returned to the theme three more times in 1644–1645 — The Judgement of Martin Bucer (marshalling a Reformer’s support), Tetrachordon (the full four-passage exegesis), and Colasterion (a reply to his critics) — the four together known as the “divorce tracts.”
