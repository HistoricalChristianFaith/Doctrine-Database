John Milton on divorce

Dates: c. 1643 (b. 1608 – d. 1674) · Wikipedia
Summary: The English poet and pamphleteer John Milton, in four prose tracts of 1643–1645 — headed by The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce (1643, expanded 1644) — argued that a settled incompatibility of mind that defeats the chief end of marriage (mutual society and help) is itself a just ground for divorce, beyond the adultery and desertion allowed by Luther, Calvin and the other magisterial Reformers. He read Christ’s prohibition as aimed at hard-hearted, capricious dismissal, Moses’ “some uncleanness” broadly, and the “fornication” of Matthew’s exception as covering more than adultery. The tracts were denounced at the time by the Presbyterian divine Herbert Palmer, among others (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Incompatibility of mind as a ground for divorce, compared with natural frigidity

The “position” that heads the treatise states the thesis: a fixed, unchangeable contrariety of temperament that frustrates the peace of married life is a greater reason for divorce than bodily frigidity (a ground the canon law already recognized).

That indisposition, unfitnes, or contrariety of mind, arising from a cause in nature unchangeable, hindring, and ever likely to hinder the main benefits of conjugall society, which are solace and peace, is a greater reason of divorce then naturall frigidity, especially if there be no children, and that there be mutuall consent.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 1, the position (Chapter I), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room.

The chief end of marriage as mutual society and conversation

Milton’s case rests on his account of marriage’s purpose. He reads Genesis 2:18 (“It is not good that the man should be alone”) as making companionship the highest end of marriage, ranked above children and the “remedy against fornication” — so that a marriage that fails at that fails at its chief end.

It is not good, saith he, that man should be alone; I will make him a help meet for him. From which words so plain, lesse cannot be concluded, nor is by any learned Interpreter, then that in Gods intention a meet and happy conversation is the chiefest and the noblest end of mariage.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 1, Chapter II, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. He defines marriage itself as “the apt and cheerfull conversation of man with woman, to comfort and refresh him against the evil of solitary life.”

Christ’s prohibition and the Pharisees’ “unbounded licence”

Christ, Milton argues, was rebuking the Pharisees’ “unbounded licence” — the putting-away Moses had tolerated “for the hardness of your hearts” — and it would not have been “seasonable,” before that audience, to explain the “good mans requisit liberty” to divorce for blameless incompatibility.

Moses for the hardnesse of your heart suffer'd you, that is, such as you to put away your wives… for it was seasonable that they should hear their own unbounded licence rebukt, but not seasonable for them to hear a good mans requisit liberty explain'd.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 2, Chapter VIII, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. On the same ground he reads Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1) broadly — of “some displeasing natural quality or unfitnes” of mind or body, not adultery alone.

“Fornication” in Matthew’s exception, taken broadly

Milton reads the porneia of Matthew’s “except for fornication” (Matthew 19:9) as covering an entrenched, contemptuous unfitness for the marriage, so that the clause names one instance of a wider failure rather than the sole permitted ground.

fornication is tak'n in Scripture for such a continual headstrong behaviour, as tends to plain contempt of the husband.

— The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce, Book 2, Chapter XVIII, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room. He develops the exegesis of the four key passages — Genesis 1:27–28 & 2:18, Deuteronomy 24:1–2, Matthew 5:31–32 & 19:3–11, and 1 Corinthians 7:10–16 — in the companion tract Tetrachordon (1645).

Notes

  • Milton and the earlier Protestant writers. Erasmus had argued that to bind spouses who have come to detest one another breeds “mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder”; Erasmus, Luther and Calvin named adultery and desertion as grounds that dissolve the bond (see their pages). Milton adds incompatibility of mind.
  • Contemporary reception. The tracts appeared during the Westminster Assembly and were denounced: the Stationers’ Company complained of the unlicensed book to Parliament, and the Presbyterian divine Herbert Palmer, preaching before both Houses on 13 August 1644, called the divorce tract a “wicked booke” that “deserves to be burnt.” (See Ernest Sirluck’s introduction to the Yale Complete Prose Works of John Milton, vol. 2 (1959).) The Westminster Confession (1646/1648) names adultery and wilful desertion as the grounds for dissolving a marriage (see its page).
  • Milton wrote on the theme three more times in 1644–1645 — The Judgement of Martin Bucer (presenting Bucer’s views on divorce), Tetrachordon (the four-passage exegesis), and Colasterion (a reply to his critics) — the four together known as the “divorce tracts.”
