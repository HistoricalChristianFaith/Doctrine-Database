The Gallic councils (Vannes and Agde) on divorce

Dates: c. 465 (the Council of Vannes in Brittany, dated by Hefele to 465, within the range c. 461–491) and 506 (the Council of Agde in Septimania, 10 September 506, under Caesarius of Arles) · Wikipedia (Agde)
Summary: Two regional councils of late-antique Gaul that legislate divorce and remarriage in the opposite direction from Pope Innocent I’s strict Roman decretals. Both presuppose that a husband whose wife is proven adulterous may lawfully dismiss her and marry again: they excommunicate only the man who remarries without proving the adultery (Vannes) or without first having the case adjudicated by the provincial bishops (Agde). They thus write Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) into ecclesiastical law as a real ground for remarriage — the permissive Latin strand otherwise associated with Ambrosiaster, and a conciliar forerunner of the reading Erasmus would revive.

Claims & evidence

Council of Vannes (c. 465), canon 2: excommunication for remarriage without proof of the adultery

The Breton bishops meeting at Vannes penalize the man who dismisses his wife and marries another — but the canon is carefully drawn. It cites Matthew’s exception (“except on account of fornication”) and falls only on the husband who has remarried “without having given proof of the adultery.” The offence, in other words, is not remarriage as such but remarriage on an unproven charge.

We have decreed that those men who after leaving their wives, as is said in the gospel, «except on account of fornication», have married other women without having given proof of the adultery, should likewise be cut off from communion, lest through our indulgence their sins are overlooked and provide an invitation to others to the license of error.

— Council of Vannes, canon 2, trans. David G. Hunter.1 The logic is the mirror image of Elvira’s. Elvira barred remarriage even for the wife of a proven adulterer; Vannes assumes that a husband who proves his wife’s adultery is free to remarry, and punishes only the man who skips the proof. As Hunter observes, the canon “simply translates the exception clause of Matthew 19:9 into ecclesiastical law” — the exception licensing not merely separation but a new marriage for the innocent party.2

Council of Agde (506), canon 25: no remarriage until the case is tried before the bishops

Forty years later the council held at Agde under Caesarius of Arles legislates the same assumption through procedure. It excommunicates the layman who casts off his wife and rushes into “unlawful or others’ unions” — but the trigger is jumping the process: dismissing the wife before stating the grounds before the provincial bishops and before she is condemned by their judgment.

Laymen who separate themselves from their unfaithful wives without having waited for the sentence of the comprovincial bishops, in order unlawfully to enter into other unions, must be excluded from Church communion and from intercourse with the faithful.

— Council of Agde, canon 25, trans. Hefele.3 The fuller Latin shows the machinery: such a man “stains faith and marriages” (fidem et coniugia maculant) precisely because he dismisses the wife “before he has stated the grounds of separation before the comprovincial bishops and before she is condemned by judgment” (prius uxores quam iudicio damnentur abiecerint). The canon does not forbid dismissal-and-remarriage outright; it requires that the wife’s guilt be established in an episcopal court first. The implication, again, is that once adultery is duly proven, the husband’s remarriage is not the thing condemned.

The reading the canons enforce

Read together, Vannes and Agde put into enforceable Gallic law a reading of Matthew’s exception that Innocent I, Elvira, and Augustine all refused: that a wife’s proven adultery dissolves the marriage and frees the innocent husband to marry again. Both councils hedge it — the adultery must be proven, before witnesses (Vannes) or before a bishops’ tribunal (Agde) — which is why the penalty falls on the man who remarries on his own say-so. But the hedge is itself the tell: you do not require proof of a ground that could never license the act. As David G. Hunter argues, these Gallic canons are among the clearest evidence that the early Western church did not uniformly forbid remarriage after divorce — the strict decretals of Rome and the permissive canons of Gaul coexisted in the same century.

Notes

  • Where they sit in the timeline. These councils are the permissive Latin counter-current to the strict line running from Elvira through Jerome, Innocent I, and Augustine. Their nearest patristic kin is the commentator Ambrosiaster, the one early Latin exegete to permit the innocent husband’s remarriage — though the councils rest the permission on a proven adultery adjudicated by the church, not on Ambrosiaster’s theology of male “headship.” A millennium later Erasmus would revive the same exception-permits-remarriage reading, and the Reformers fix it as the Protestant position.
  • The gendered framing. Both canons are cast in terms of the husband who dismisses an adulterous wife — the case the bishops actually faced. Neither states a reciprocal right for the wife; that asymmetry is the idiom of the society, not necessarily a considered denial (cf. the equal-standard arguments of Lactantius, Gregory of Nazianzus, and Jerome).
  • Dating. Vannes is variously placed between c. 461 and c. 491; Hefele dates the synod to 465. Agde is firmly dated to 10 September 506. The canon numbers used here (Vannes 2, Agde 25) follow the standard enumeration printed in Mansi and the modern critical edition (CCSL 148).
