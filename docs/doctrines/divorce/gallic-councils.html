The Gallic councils (Vannes and Agde) on divorce

Dates: c. 465 (the Council of Vannes in Brittany, dated by Hefele to 465, within the range c. 461–491) and 506 (the Council of Agde in Septimania, 10 September 506, under Caesarius of Arles) · Wikipedia (Agde)
Summary: Two regional councils of late-antique Gaul whose canons on divorce excommunicate the man who dismisses his wife and remarries without proving her adultery (Vannes) or without first having the case judged by the provincial bishops (Agde). David G. Hunter (2018) reads them as presupposing that a husband whose wife’s adultery is proven may marry again, and so as writing Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) into church law as a ground for remarriage, a reading he sets beside Ambrosiaster’s. Pope Innocent I’s decretals forbid such remarriage (see his page).1

Claims & evidence

Council of Vannes (c. 465), canon 2: excommunication for remarriage without proof of the adultery

The Breton bishops meeting at Vannes penalize the man who dismisses his wife and marries another. The canon cites Matthew’s exception (“except on account of fornication”) and names the husband who has remarried “without having given proof of the adultery.”

We have decreed that those men who after leaving their wives, as is said in the gospel, «except on account of fornication», have married other women without having given proof of the adultery, should likewise be cut off from communion, lest through our indulgence their sins are overlooked and provide an invitation to others to the license of error.

— Council of Vannes, canon 2, trans. David G. Hunter.2 Elvira had barred remarriage for the wife of an adulterous husband. Hunter reads the Vannes canon as indicating “that it was normal procedure in the Gallic church of the fifth century for a man to be allowed to remarry, if he divorced his first wife for adultery,” provided the adultery was proven; in Philip Reynolds’ words, which Hunter quotes, it “simply translates [the exception clause of] Matthew 19:9 into ecclesiastical law.”1

Council of Agde (506), canon 25: the case tried before the bishops

About forty years later the council held at Agde under Caesarius of Arles excommunicates the layman who casts off his wife in order to enter “unlawful or others’ unions” (illicita aut aliena), where he dismisses her before stating the grounds before the provincial bishops and before she is condemned by their judgment.

Laymen who separate themselves from their unfaithful wives without having waited for the sentence of the comprovincial bishops, in order unlawfully to enter into other unions, must be excluded from Church communion and from intercourse with the faithful.

— Council of Agde, canon 25, trans. Hefele.3 In the fuller Latin, such a man “stains faith and marriages” (fidem et coniugia maculant) because he dismisses the wife “before he has stated the grounds of separation before the comprovincial bishops and before she is condemned by judgment” (prius uxores quam iudicio damnentur abiecerint). Hunter reads the canon as evidence that “a woman’s adultery constituted a dissolution of the marriage and justified the remarriage of her husband, as long as the offense was properly proven before church authorities.”1

How the canons have been read

David G. Hunter (2018) argues that the requirement of proof — the adultery established before the church (Vannes) or before a bishops’ tribunal (Agde) — presupposes that proven adultery frees the husband to marry again, and he cites these canons as evidence “that the position outlined by Ambrosiaster persisted, despite the emergence of Augustine’s theology of indissolubility.”1 Augustinus Lehmkuhl (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909), without naming these councils, wrote that “on account of defective morality, there are to be found regulations of particular synods which permitted unjustifiable concessions,” while “the synods of all centuries, and more clearly still the decrees of the popes, have constantly declared that divorce which annulled the marriage and permitted remarriage was never allowed.”4

Context

  • Neighbouring witnesses. Elvira, Jerome, Innocent I and Augustine forbid remarriage while the first spouse lives (see their pages). The commentator Ambrosiaster permits the innocent husband’s remarriage on a ground of male “headship”; the councils speak of proof of adultery before the church. In the sixteenth century Erasmus argued that the exception permits remarriage (see his page).
  • The gendered framing. Both canons are cast in terms of the husband who dismisses an adulterous wife; neither states a reciprocal right for the wife. Lactantius, Gregory of Nazianzus and Jerome argue for a single standard for both sexes (see their pages).
  • Dating. Vannes is variously placed between c. 461 and c. 491; Hefele dates the synod to 465. Agde is firmly dated to 10 September 506. The canon numbers used here (Vannes 2, Agde 25) follow the standard enumeration printed in Mansi and the modern critical edition (CCSL 148).
