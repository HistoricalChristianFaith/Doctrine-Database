Athenagoras of Athens on divorce

Dates: c. 177 (A Plea for the Christians) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pleading the Christians’ case before Marcus Aurelius and Commodus, Athenagoras cites Jesus’ divorce saying to prove the chastity of his people — and glosses it as a bar on both divorce and any remarriage after it: God does “not permit a man to send her away… nor to marry again.” It is the earliest surviving patristic quotation of the divorce logion itself (Matthew 19:9), and Athenagoras treats the marriage bond as surviving the divorce — a hard pole for indissolubility a generation before Tertullian.

Claims & evidence

To divorce and remarry is adultery — the bond survives the divorce

In chapter 33 of the Plea, arguing that Christians are so far from the licentiousness their accusers imagine that they scarcely indulge even lawful appetite, Athenagoras moves from marriage-for-children, to the honour paid to lifelong celibates, to the rule of one marriage — and there quotes the Lord’s divorce saying directly, adding his own gloss on what it forbids.

For we bestow our attention, not on the study of words, but on the exhibition and teaching of actions, — that a person should either remain as he was born, or be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery. “For whosoever puts away his wife,” says He, “and marries another, commits adultery;” not permitting a man to send her away whose virginity he has brought to an end, nor to marry again. For he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer, resisting the hand of God, because in the beginning God made one man and one woman, and dissolving the strictest union of flesh with flesh, formed for the intercourse of the race.

— Athenagoras, A Plea for the Christians 33, ANF vol. 2. The middle sentence is a near-verbatim citation of Matthew 19:9 (in the shorter, exceptionless run the Greek fathers commonly quote), and the clause that follows it is Athenagoras’ own reading: God does “not permit” a man to send his wife away at all, and does not permit him to marry again. Divorce does not sever the bond; a remarriage while the first wife lives is adultery precisely because, in God’s eyes, she is still his wife. This is the indissolubility reading in its purest early form, stated not as legislation but as proof of Christian purity to a pagan court.

Notes

  • Chapter 33 does double duty. The clause on remarriage after divorce (“whosoever puts away his wife… and marries another, commits adultery”) belongs to this divorce timeline; the surrounding claim — that even a widower who remarries after his first wife has died is a “cloaked adulterer” — belongs to the deprecation of second marriage after bereavement (digamy) and is weighed on Athenagoras’ remarriage page. Both facets flow from one premise: “in the beginning God made one man and one woman,” the creation ordinance of Genesis 2:24.
  • Athenagoras is the first witness on this timeline to quote the divorce logion as such. The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140) had ruled the case without citing the saying, and Justin (c. 155) quoted only the second half (“whosoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery”). Athenagoras supplies the first half — the man who initiates the divorce — and, like both predecessors, attaches no “except for fornication.” His silence on the exception is another datum for the argument over whether the exception licenses remarriage: three of the earliest readers in a row give no hint that a divorce frees anyone to marry again.
  • His gloss reads Christ as “not permitting a man to send her away” — a stronger claim than Hermas or Justin voiced, for it forbids the divorce itself, not merely the subsequent remarriage. Where Hermas made separation from an unrepentant adulteress all but obligatory for the innocent husband, Athenagoras, addressing the ordinary case, presses toward no putting-away at all. The tension between the two is the seam later fathers work: is the divorce for adultery a duty (Hermas) or itself forbidden (the drift of Athenagoras’ wording)?
