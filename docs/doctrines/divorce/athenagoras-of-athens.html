Athenagoras of Athens on divorce

Dates: c. 177 (A Plea for the Christians) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pleading the Christians’ case before Marcus Aurelius and Commodus, Athenagoras cites Jesus’ divorce saying as evidence of his people’s chastity — and glosses it as a bar on both divorce and any remarriage after it: God does “not permit a man to send her away… nor to marry again.” It is the earliest extant patristic quotation on this timeline of the first half of the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9), the man who divorces and remarries, and Athenagoras treats a remarriage after divorce as adultery.

Claims & evidence

The divorce saying in the Plea, chapter 33

In chapter 33 of the Plea, arguing that Christians are so far from the licentiousness their accusers imagine that they restrain even lawful appetite, Athenagoras moves from marriage-for-children, to the honour paid to lifelong celibates, to the rule of one marriage — and there quotes the Lord’s divorce saying directly, adding his own gloss on what it forbids.

For we bestow our attention, not on the study of words, but on the exhibition and teaching of actions, — that a person should either remain as he was born, or be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery. “For whosoever puts away his wife,” says He, “and marries another, commits adultery;” not permitting a man to send her away whose virginity he has brought to an end, nor to marry again. For he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer, resisting the hand of God, because in the beginning God made one man and one woman, and dissolving the strictest union of flesh with flesh, formed for the intercourse of the race.

— Athenagoras, A Plea for the Christians 33, ANF vol. 2. The middle sentence is a near-verbatim citation of Matthew 19:9 (without the exception clause), and the clause that follows it is Athenagoras’ own reading: God does “not permit” a man to send his wife away, and does not permit him to marry again. He grounds this in the creation of “one man and one woman” and the “strictest union of flesh with flesh.” The passage is addressed to a pagan court as a description of Christian practice, not as church legislation.

Context and placement on the timeline

  • Chapter 33 does double duty. The clause on remarriage after divorce (“whosoever puts away his wife… and marries another, commits adultery”) belongs to this divorce timeline; the surrounding claim — that even a widower who remarries after his first wife has died is a “cloaked adulterer” — belongs to the deprecation of second marriage after bereavement (digamy) and is treated on Athenagoras’ remarriage page. Both facets flow from one premise: “in the beginning God made one man and one woman,” the creation ordinance of Genesis 2:24.
  • Athenagoras is the earliest extant witness on this timeline to quote the first half of the divorce logion. The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140) had ruled the case without citing the saying, and Justin (c. 155) quoted only the second half (“whosoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery”). Athenagoras supplies the first half — the man who initiates the divorce — and, like both predecessors, attaches no “except for fornication.” None of the three speaks of a divorce that frees either party to marry again.
  • His gloss reads Christ as “not permitting a man to send her away” — wording that bears on the divorce itself as well as on the subsequent remarriage. Hermas, addressing the case of an unrepentant adulteress, required the husband to separate from her; Athenagoras, addressing the ordinary case, does not discuss divorce for adultery.
