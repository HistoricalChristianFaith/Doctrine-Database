Tertullian on divorce

Dates: c. 207 (Against Marcion IV) · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering Marcion’s charge that Christ’s ban on divorce contradicts Moses’ bill of divorcement, Tertullian gives the fullest early treatment of the divorce question yet on this timeline. Against Marcion he argues that Christ did not abolish Deuteronomy 24 but limited it: the prohibition is conditional, aimed at the man who dismisses his wife in order to marry another, while divorce “when justly deserved” — for adultery — “has even in Christ a defender.” Yet a new marriage while the first spouse lives is adultery, because the bond persists. In the later, rigorist On Monogamy he tightens this into the sharpest early statement of the position: divorce is permitted for one cause only, and even the justly-divorced may never marry again.

Claims & evidence

Against Marcion: Christ limited Moses’ bill of divorcement, He did not abolish it

Marcion had made Christ’s divorce saying a proof-text for his two-gods thesis: the Creator’s Moses permitted divorce (Deuteronomy 24:1), the good Christ forbade it, so the two cannot be the same author. In Against Marcion IV.34 Tertullian takes the antithesis head-on and dissolves it. Christ, he says, quoting the Pharisee-controversy, grounds the ban in creation — “Moses, because of the hardness of your hearts, suffered you to give a bill of divorcement; but from the beginning it was not so” — and so does not annul the Mosaic provision but restores its first purpose.

But Christ prohibits divorce, saying, “Whosoever putteth away his wife, and marrieth another, committeth adultery; and whosoever marrieth her that is put away from her husband, also committeth adultery.” In order to forbid divorce, He makes it unlawful to marry a woman that has been put away… Now, by this answer of His (to the Pharisees), He both sanctioned the provision of Moses, who was His own (servant), and restored to its primitive purpose the institution of the Creator, whose Christ He was… must He not have rather exculpated than abolished the enactment of Moses?

— Tertullian, Against Marcion IV.34, ANF vol. 3. The key move is that Christ’s prohibition is conditional. Tertullian reads “whosoever putteth away his wife, and marrieth another” as supplying its own limiting case: the sin lies in dismissing a wife so as to take a second one.

I maintain, then, that there was a condition in the prohibition which He now made of divorce; the case supposed being, that a man put away his wife for the express purpose of marrying another… Permanent is the marriage which is not rightly dissolved; to marry, therefore, whilst matrimony is undissolved, is to commit adultery. Since, therefore, His prohibition of divorce was a conditional one, He did not prohibit absolutely; and what He did not absolutely forbid, that He permitted on some occasions, when there is an absence of the cause why He gave His prohibition.

— Against Marcion IV.34. On this reading divorce for a just cause survives. Tertullian cites Matthew’s exception explicitly — “For in the Gospel of Matthew he says, ‘Whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery’” — and turns it against Marcion’s own encratism: if divorce were absolutely forbidden, what is a Christian husband to do “if his wife commit adultery? Shall he keep her?” His answer is emphatic.

Divorce, therefore, when justly deserved, has even in Christ a defender. So that Moses for the future must be considered as being confirmed by Him, since he prohibits divorce in the same sense as Christ does, if any unchastity should occur in the wife… He prohibits divorce when He will have the marriage inviolable; He permits divorce when the marriage is spotted with unfaithfulness.

— Against Marcion IV.34. So Tertullian is the first witness here to state, in so many words, both halves of what became the Latin settlement: divorce (as separation) is not merely tolerated but defended where there is adultery — yet the bond is not dissolved, and to “marry whilst matrimony is undissolved” is itself adultery. Like Clement, Athenagoras, Justin, and the Shepherd before him, he reads Matthew’s exception as licensing the putting-away, not a new marriage.

On Monogamy: divorce for one cause only, and never remarriage

In the later On Monogamy (c. 217, from Tertullian’s rigorist Montanist period) the same doctrine is pressed to its hardest edge. Divorce “was not from the beginning,” and Christ abolished it precisely to “strengthen that which was from the beginning — the permanent conjunction… of two into one flesh” — permitting it for a single cause.

…the fact that (he) who shall have dismissed his wife, except on the ground of adultery, makes her commit adultery; and (he) who shall have married a (woman) dismissed by her husband, of course commits adultery. A divorced woman cannot even marry legitimately… And the reason why He has abolished divorce, which “was not from the beginning,” is, that He may strengthen that which “was from the beginning” — the permanent conjunction, (namely), of “two into one flesh”… permitting divorce to no cause but one — if, (that is), the (evil) against which precaution is taken chance to have occurred beforehand.

— Tertullian, On Monogamy 9, ANF vol. 4. The contrast he draws with pagan Rome makes the rigor unmistakable: the Christian may divorce an adulterous wife, but the divorce buys no liberty to remarry.

But they indulge in promiscuous adulteries, even without divorcing (their partners): to us, even if we do divorce them, even marriage will not be lawful.

— On Monogamy 9. This is the sharpest early formulation of the “separation, not remarriage” reading — divorce is a remedy that severs the household but not the bond.

Notes

  • Tertullian’s handling of Deuteronomy 24 in Against Marcion is an early worked answer to the standing puzzle of why “Moses permitted” what Christ forbade. His solution — the Mosaic bill was a concession “because of the hardness of your hearts,” not a contrary law, so that Christ “exculpated” rather than “abolished” it — feeds directly into the argument over the hardness-of-heart concession weighed elsewhere on this doctrine.
  • The two works pull in the same direction but at different pressures. Against Marcion (c. 207), written to refute a heretic who forbade all marriage, stresses that Christ “permits divorce when the marriage is spotted with unfaithfulness” — divorce is defended. On Monogamy (c. 217), written from Tertullian’s later Montanist rigorism against second marriages of any kind, stresses the other half — that even a justified divorce leaves “marriage… not lawful.” The underlying reading is constant: divorce for adultery is licit, remarriage while the spouse lives is not.
  • Distinct from Tertullian’s well-known deprecation of remarriage after widowhood (digamy), argued in To His Wife and On Monogamy and treated on the remarriage timeline. The material here concerns the separate divorce facet — putting away a living spouse and whether it frees either to marry again — though in On Monogamy 9 the two facets converge in his claim that God will not have a divorced woman joined to another “while her husband liveth” any more than after his death.
