Lactantius on divorce

Dates: c. 305 (the Divine Institutes, composed c. 303–311 during and after the Great Persecution; Lactantius, c. 250–c. 325, a Latin rhetorician from Roman North Africa who taught rhetoric at Nicomedia under Diocletian and, after his conversion, tutored Constantine’s son Crispus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Treating marriage in the sixth book of the Divine Institutes under the head of continence, Lactantius rejects the Roman-law double standard that counted only the wife an adulteress and holds that the divine law binds husband and wife “with equal right”: a married man who takes a mistress is as much an adulterer as a wife who takes another man, and the husband must keep the very continence he demands of his wife. On divorce he stands with the strict Latin line — adultery may ground the dismissal of a wife, but a new marriage while the first spouse lives is adultery, whether one marries a divorced woman or puts one away to wed another. So he is a distinctively even-handed early Western witness for the separation-not-remarriage reading, grounding it on the equality of the sexes rather than, as later writers would, a hierarchy of husband over wife.

Claims & evidence

The marriage law binds husband and wife by equal right — against the Roman double standard

Lactantius turns from public morals to the “precepts of the marriage bed,” and at once sets the Christian rule against the Roman one. Where Roman law reckoned adultery a wife’s crime and left the philandering husband untouched, he insists the divine law measures both by one standard:

God limits them, not only within private walls, but even by the precepts of the marriage bed, so that when anyone has a wife, he may not wish to have besides a slave or free concubine, but he must keep the faith of his marriage contract. For such is not the case, as is the interpretation of public law, that she alone is the adulteress who has another man, while the male is free from the charge of adultery, though he have many mistresses. The divine law so joins two with equal right into a marriage, which is two in one flesh, that whoever breaks apart the joining of the body is regarded as an adulterer.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. Sr. Mary Francis McDonald (Fathers of the Church 49); the Ante-Nicene Fathers edition prints this chapter untranslated, in the original Latin.1 The load-bearing phrase is pari iure — “with equal right.” It is the mirror image of the reasoning Ambrosiaster would give two generations later (“a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman”) and of the “custom” Basil would concede he could not defend from Scripture. Lactantius makes the equal standard not a concession but the very content of the divine law.

The husband must keep the continence he demands of his wife

The equality is not merely a matter of blame after the fact; it is a positive demand on the husband, who is to teach chastity by his own example rather than exact of his wife what he will not furnish himself:

Faith, then, must be kept with the other by each party in a marriage; in fact, the wife is to be taught by the example of continence to act chastely. It is evil to exact that which you yourself are not able to exhibit. This iniquity assuredly brought it about that there were adulteries, women bearing it ill that they were keeping faith with men not exhibiting mutual love.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.2 He clinches the point with the golden rule — that the height of justice is not to do to another what you would not want done to you — and with a line from Quintilian: “A man who does not keep away from another’s marriage is not a guardian of his own either.” The husband’s infidelity, on this reading, is not a lesser fault than the wife’s but the very thing that provokes hers.

Divorce for adultery permits dismissal, not remarriage

Having stated the mutual obligation, Lactantius closes off the loopholes a reader might seek in it. The one ground for putting a wife away is her adultery; but even that licenses only the dismissal, never a fresh marriage while the first spouse lives:

But, however, lest anyone think that he is able to circumscribe the divine precepts, there are added these points, that all calumny and chance for fraud be removed; he is an adulterer who takes a wife who has been sent away by her husband; and so is he who has, aside from the crime of adultery, put a wife away that he may take another. God did not intend for that “one flesh” to be separated and torn apart.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.3 Both halves of the later Latin settlement are here: to marry a woman divorced from her husband is adultery, and to divorce one’s own wife (except for her adultery) in order to marry another is adultery. The reason is the “one flesh” of Genesis 2:24, which God will not have “separated and torn apart” — the same creation ground Tertullian and, later, Augustine rest the indissoluble bond on.

Even the wish is adultery

Lactantius extends the prohibition inward, to the Sermon-on-the-Mount teaching that adultery is committed first in the heart:

Furthermore, not only adultery must be avoided, but even the very consideration of it, lest anyone look upon another woman and desire her in his heart. The mind becomes adulterous if it has fashioned for itself even an image of this pleasure. For it is truly the mind which sins.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.4 The chastity he requires is therefore not merely a rule of outward conduct binding one sex; it is an interior discipline binding both spouses equally — the frame within which his rejection of the double standard sits.

Notes

  • The Western counterweight to the double standard. Lactantius’ pari iure is the clearest early-Latin statement that the marriage law binds the man exactly as it binds the woman, written decades before Basil of Caesarea codified a frank double standard he traced to bare “custom” (the wife takes back the fornicating husband; the husband expels the polluted wife) and before Ambrosiaster rested a permissive double standard for the husband on male headship. Where those two make the sexes unequal — one leniently, one strictly — Lactantius makes them equal, and equally bound. He is thus a Latin parallel to the Greek Gregory of Nazianzus, who a little later attacked the same inequality in the marriage laws (“the men made the laws… I do not accept this legislation”).
  • Strict, but by a different road than his successors. On the bottom-line question — may the divorced remarry while the first spouse lives? — Lactantius answers no, joining Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Chrysostom, Jerome, and Augustine. But he reaches it from the equality of the sexes rather than from a doctrine of the bond’s ontology; the sacramentum theology that would ground indissolubility for the Latin West is Augustine’s later contribution. On the equality premise Lactantius in fact agrees with Jerome, who argued the bar on remarriage from the same principle that “what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men.”
  • Feeds the arguments. Lactantius belongs to the patristic consensus weighed under the summary’s Arguments section — that the exception clause licenses separation, not remarriage (the remarriage-or-separation question), and that the marriage bond survives every divorce (the indissolubility argument). His distinctive contribution is not the verdict but the egalitarian ground he gives for it.
  • On the text and its translation. The Divine Institutes is Lactantius’ major apologetic work, seven books written c. 303–311 and dedicated (in a later recension) to Constantine. The nineteenth-century Ante-Nicene Fathers edition judged chapter VI.23, on marriage and sexual continence, too indelicate to render, and printed it in the original Latin; the English quoted here is the modern translation of Sr. Mary Francis McDonald (Fathers of the Church 49, 1964). The chapter does not treat every marriage question a reader might expect — it says nothing, for instance, of putting away a barren wife — but its rule against dismissing a wife “that he may take another” (save for her adultery) bars remarriage generally.
