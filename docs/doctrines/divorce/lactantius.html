Lactantius on divorce

Dates: c. 305 (the Divine Institutes, composed c. 303–311 during and after the Great Persecution; Lactantius, c. 250–c. 325, a Latin rhetorician from Roman North Africa who taught rhetoric at Nicomedia under Diocletian and, after his conversion, tutored Constantine’s son Crispus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Treating marriage in the sixth book of the Divine Institutes under the head of continence, Lactantius rejects the Roman-law standard that counted only the wife an adulteress and holds that the divine law joins husband and wife “with equal right”: a married man who takes a mistress is as much an adulterer as a wife who takes another man, and the husband must keep the continence he demands of his wife. On divorce he writes that a man who marries a woman sent away by her husband is an adulterer, and so is a man who, “aside from the crime of adultery,” puts away his wife to take another. Whether the clause leaves a man who has divorced an adulterous wife free to remarry is read differently: David G. Hunter (2018), following Pierre Nautin, reads it so; the page’s Notes record the readings.

Claims & evidence

The marriage law binds husband and wife by equal right

Lactantius turns from public morals to the “precepts of the marriage bed,” and sets the Christian rule against the Roman one. Where, as he describes it, public law reckoned adultery a wife’s crime and left the husband with mistresses free of the charge, he holds that the divine law measures both by one standard:

God limits them, not only within private walls, but even by the precepts of the marriage bed, so that when anyone has a wife, he may not wish to have besides a slave or free concubine, but he must keep the faith of his marriage contract. For such is not the case, as is the interpretation of public law, that she alone is the adulteress who has another man, while the male is free from the charge of adultery, though he have many mistresses. The divine law so joins two with equal right into a marriage, which is two in one flesh, that whoever breaks apart the joining of the body is regarded as an adulterer.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. Sr. Mary Francis McDonald (Fathers of the Church 49); the Ante-Nicene Fathers edition prints this chapter untranslated, in the original Latin.1 The Latin phrase is pari iure — “with equal right.” Ambrosiaster, writing later in the fourth century, reasons otherwise (“a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman”), and Basil refers a stricter treatment of wives to “custom” (see their pages). Lactantius states the equal standard as the content of the divine law.

The husband must keep the continence he demands of his wife

The equality is also a positive demand on the husband, who is to teach chastity by his own example rather than exact of his wife what he will not furnish himself:

Faith, then, must be kept with the other by each party in a marriage; in fact, the wife is to be taught by the example of continence to act chastely. It is evil to exact that which you yourself are not able to exhibit. This iniquity assuredly brought it about that there were adulteries, women bearing it ill that they were keeping faith with men not exhibiting mutual love.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.2 He adds the golden rule — that the height of justice is not to do to another what you would not want done to you — and a line from Quintilian: “A man who does not keep away from another’s marriage is not a guardian of his own either.” In his account the husband’s infidelity is what provokes the wife’s.

Dismissal, adultery and a new marriage

Having stated the mutual obligation, Lactantius adds rules “that all calumny and chance for fraud be removed”:

But, however, lest anyone think that he is able to circumscribe the divine precepts, there are added these points, that all calumny and chance for fraud be removed; he is an adulterer who takes a wife who has been sent away by her husband; and so is he who has, aside from the crime of adultery, put a wife away that he may take another. God did not intend for that “one flesh” to be separated and torn apart.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.3 Two cases are named: to marry a woman sent away by her husband is adultery, and to put away one’s own wife, “aside from the crime of adultery,” in order to take another is adultery. The reason given is the “one flesh” of Genesis 2:24, which God will not have “separated and torn apart”; Tertullian also appeals to the “one flesh” in On Monogamy 9.

The wish as adultery

Lactantius extends the prohibition inward, to the Sermon-on-the-Mount teaching that adultery is committed first in the heart:

Furthermore, not only adultery must be avoided, but even the very consideration of it, lest anyone look upon another woman and desire her in his heart. The mind becomes adulterous if it has fashioned for itself even an image of this pleasure. For it is truly the mind which sins.

— Divine Institutes VI.23, trans. McDonald.4 The chastity he requires is an interior discipline as well as a rule of outward conduct, and it binds both spouses.

Notes

  • The equal standard. Lactantius’ pari iure states that the marriage law binds the man as it binds the woman. Basil of Caesarea, c. 375, sets out a different standard for the two sexes and refers it to “custom” (the wife takes back the fornicating husband; the husband expels the adulterous wife), and Ambrosiaster grounds a different standard for the husband on male headship. Gregory of Nazianzus objected to unequal marriage laws in his Oration 37: “I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom. They who made the Law were men.” Jerome argued from the principle that “what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” (see his page).
  • How the remarriage clause has been read. David G. Hunter (University of Kentucky, 2018) holds that Lactantius “cited the Matthean exception as if it allowed a man to remarry after divorcing his wife for infidelity,” and that “the most obvious reading of Lactantius’s text is that a man was allowed to remarry, if he divorced his first wife because of her adultery”; he adds the Epitome 61 (66), in his own translation, “God has commanded that a wife should not be divorced, unless she has been convicted of adultery, so that the bond of the conjugal pact may never be dissolved, unless infidelity has broken it,” and reports Pierre Nautin’s observation that Lactantius regarded marriage in Roman legal terms as a “pact” (foedus) dissolved by the “crime of adultery.” Hunter notes that Philip Reynolds “doubts that Lactantius would have extended the same privilege to the woman.” John M. Rist, whose essay Hunter answers, wrote that second marriages “are forbidden during the lifetime of the original spouse” for the overwhelming majority of patristic authors, and that “among identifiable sources the only immediately obvious exception to that rule is Ambrosiaster.”5 The dispute over Matthew’s exception clause is recorded under the timeline’s Disputed readings.
  • On the text and its translation. The Divine Institutes is Lactantius’ major apologetic work, seven books written c. 303–311 and dedicated (in a later recension) to Constantine. The nineteenth-century Ante-Nicene Fathers edition printed chapter VI.23, on marriage and sexual continence, untranslated in the original Latin; the English quoted here is the modern translation of Sr. Mary Francis McDonald (Fathers of the Church 49, 1964). The chapter says nothing of putting away a barren wife.
