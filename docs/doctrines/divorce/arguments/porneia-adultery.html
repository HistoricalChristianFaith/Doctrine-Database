Does porneia in the exception clause mean a wife’s marital unfaithfulness?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the majority reading, ancient and modern: the “fornication” (porneia) of Matthew 5:32 and 19:9 is a married woman’s sexual infidelity, named by the wider word for illicit intercourse rather than the narrow “adultery” (moicheia) precisely because it takes in the whole genus of which her adultery is one species · advanced by the mainstream of the fathers and modern commentators alike

Related arguments: the exception clause — see also Does porneia mean an unlawful (incestuous) marriage? · Does porneia mean betrothal unchastity? · Does porneia extend to spiritual fornication?

The claim

After this our Saviour says, not at all permitting the dissolution of marriages for any other sin than fornication alone, when detected in the wife, “Whosoever shall put away his own wife, saving for the cause of fornication, maketh her an adulteress.”

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew XIV.24.1 The reading weighed here is the plain and majority one: the exception ground is a wife’s sexual unfaithfulness within marriage — adultery in substance, though Matthew names it by the broader term porneia (“fornication,” illicit intercourse of every kind) rather than the specific moicheia (“adultery”).

Who advances it

This is the reading of nearly every early witness who glosses the ground at all. Tertullian simply renders the clause “except on the ground of adultery.”2 John Chrysostom takes the exception to be the wife “defiling herself,” the one case in which forcing the husband to keep her would “make the matter end again in adultery.”3 Jerome reads it as the wife who “divides one flesh into another… through fornication” — an adulteress, whom the husband may freely dismiss.4 Theophylact of Ohrid glosses it flatly — “if she has committed adultery” — and Theodore of Mopsuestia and Apollinaris of Laodicea read the same, Apollinaris even reaching secret or unproven adultery, “because it is still adultery.”5 Modern exegesis holds the same ground by a slightly different lexical route: R. T. France (NICNT), Craig Keener, Ulrich Luz (Hermeneia), and W. D. Davies with Dale Allison (ICC) take porneia as sexual immorality broadly — the wider category that includes a wife’s adultery — and the rabbinics scholar David Instone-Brewer reaches the same practical result from the Jewish side, reading Jesus’ exception as the Shammaite ground of Deuteronomy 24’s “indecency,” i.e. sexual immorality.6

The evidence for

Two things carry it. First, the lexicon: porneia is, as the standard lexicon (Bauer–Danker) puts it, used “of every kind of unlawful sexual intercourse” — “unlawful sexual intercourse, prostitution, unchastity, fornication.” A married woman’s sexual sin falls squarely inside that range; nothing in the word excludes it, and the whole ancient discussion assumes it is the paradigm case.7 Second, the context: in both Matthean passages the man is putting away his wife (gynē), in the fully-married frame of Deuteronomy 24 that the Pharisees raise (“is it lawful to put away one’s wife?”), and the consequence Jesus draws — that dismissing her “makes her commit adultery,” and that whoever marries her commits adultery — only makes sense if the exception concerns the marriage bond that adultery violates. The reading needs no special lexical sense, no reconstructed background, and no harmonizing hypothesis: it takes the words in their ordinary force in their own setting.

The one genuine puzzle it must answer is why Matthew wrote porneia and not moicheia, the exact word for “adultery,” which he knows and uses (and which he lists beside porneia as a distinct sin at Matthew 15:19).8 The majority answer is that Matthew chose the wider word on purpose: the ground is not the technical crime of “adultery” alone but the broader class of grave sexual immorality — of which a wife’s adultery is the central instance, but which need not be pedantically limited to a single legal category. On this reading the distinctness of porneia from moicheia is not an objection but the point: Jesus permits dismissal for sexual unfaithfulness described at its widest, not for the “any cause” grounds the Hillelites allowed.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the exception concerns a wife’s sexual unfaithfulness is as close to a fixed point as this contested field has. It is the unbroken patristic gloss, the natural sense of the words, and the reading that fits the married-couple context without special pleading. Every rival reading — incestuous union, betrothal-unchastity, spiritual fornication — is in effect an argument that the plain sense cannot be right and must be displaced; each therefore carries the burden of proof, and the plain reading stands unless one of them discharges it.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two cautions. (1) It is imprecise to say porneia here simply means “adultery” (as Tertullian’s Latin renders it): that collapses porneia into moicheia and invites the very objection the majority itself presses — had Jesus or Matthew meant only the narrow crime, the narrow word lay ready to hand. The disciplined form of the reading says porneia is the wider genus of sexual immorality that includes the wife’s adultery, not a synonym for it.9 (2) The same lexical datum — that porneia is broader than moicheia — is what the rival readings seize on to argue that it means something other than a wife’s adultery (an incestuous union, or a betrothed girl’s unchastity). The width of the word does not by itself decide the case; it is the context (a man and his wife, in a Deut-24 frame) that anchors the reference to marital infidelity. The reading is sound because word and context together point one way, not because the word alone settles it.

Assessment

Sound. That the exception clause licenses dismissal for a wife’s sexual unfaithfulness is the mainstream reading of the fathers and of modern critical commentary alike, grounded in the ordinary sense of porneia and in the married-couple context of both Matthean passages. The precise formulation matters: porneia is best taken not as a bare synonym for “adultery” but as the wider term for illicit sexual intercourse of which the wife’s adultery is the paradigm — a distinction that both explains Matthew’s word-choice and answers the rival readings that trade on the very same breadth. The debates that remain are downstream of this: whether the dismissal it licenses also frees the innocent party to remarry (weighed across the timeline from Hermas to Erasmus), not whether the ground is marital unfaithfulness.
