Does porneia extend to spiritual fornication (idolatry, apostasy)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reading that the “fornication” of the exception clause is not (or not only) carnal but the wider scriptural “fornication” of idolatry, unbelief, and covetousness, so a spouse’s apostasy could ground dismissal: a real strand in the fathers, but one its own greatest proponent (Augustine) publicly walked back · advanced by Augustine of Hippo (later retracted), taken up by Aquinas

Related arguments: the exception clause — see also Does porneia mean a wife’s marital unfaithfulness? · Does porneia mean an unlawful (incestuous) marriage? · Does porneia mean betrothal unchastity?

The claim

Here there arises a second question, when the Lord allows a wife to be put away for the cause of fornication, in what latitude of meaning fornication is to be understood in this passage… whether… that fornication… which is committed in acts of uncleanness; or… as meaning all unlawful corruption, such as idolatry or covetousness, and therefore, of course, every transgression of the law on account of the unlawful lust [involved in it].

— Augustine, Our Lord’s Sermon on the Mount I.16.43–46.1 The reading weighed here takes “fornication” in the exception in its broad biblical register — the “fornication” the prophets charge against Israel’s idolatry — so that a spouse’s idolatry, unbelief, or apostasy would fall under the exception as a species of porneia.

Who advances it

The great proponent is Augustine. In Our Lord’s Sermon on the Mount (c. 393–396) he presses a chain-argument to the broad sense: “if unbelief is fornication, and idolatry unbelief, and covetousness idolatry, it is not to be doubted that covetousness also is fornication… which fornication… we are compelled to understand as being general and universal.” His worked example is a wife who compels her husband to sacrifice to idols: putting her away is “for the cause of fornication, not only on her part… but on his own also.”2 The strand continues in the scholastics: Thomas Aquinas lays down that “fornication is twofold, namely corporal and spiritual,” and that a believer may put away a spouse who “wishes to lead a man to unbelief” (reading the exception through Paul’s mixed-marriage rule, 1 Corinthians 7); and Rabanus Maurus frames “one carnal cause… that is, fornication, and… one spiritual, that is, the fear of God.”3

The evidence for

The reading is not arbitrary. “Fornication” genuinely carries the metaphorical sense of covenant-infidelity throughout Scripture: the prophets brand Israel’s idolatry “playing the harlot” (Hosea, Ezekiel 23, Jeremiah 3), and the Greek Old Testament renders that with the same porneia-family words. So the word’s range demonstrably reaches past carnal sin to apostasy. Augustine’s further link runs through Paul: if the apostle allows the believer deserted by an unbeliever to be free (1 Corinthians 7:15) and Jesus forbids dismissal “except for fornication,” then — Augustine reasons — the unbelief that grounds that Pauline freedom must itself be a kind of “fornication,” else the two sayings collide. On its own terms the chain is valid; it is the vehicle by which the exception could be stretched to cover a spouse’s idolatry or apostasy, and it supplied later ages a scriptural handle for treating grave spiritual betrayal as marriage-breaking.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That “fornication” has a real metaphorical range (idolatry as harlotry) is undeniable, and the impulse to harmonize Jesus’ exception with Paul’s desertion concession is legitimate — the two do have to be read together. As a piece of biblical-theology the “spiritual fornication” motif is sound.

What is overstated or imprecise. Applied to this clause the reading over-reaches, and Augustine himself said so. In the Retractations he stepped back: “not every sin is spiritual fornication… Whether this be the fornication for which divorce is allowed is a most knotty question — for it is no question at all that it is allowed for the fornication by carnal sin.”4 Three defects account for the retreat. (1) The chain proves too much: if every transgression is a species of “fornication,” then dismissal is allowed for any sin at all, and the exception — meant to restrict the grounds for divorce against the Hillelite “any cause” — swallows the very rule it qualifies. (2) The context is carnal: the consequence Jesus draws (dismissing her “makes her commit adultery”) fixes the reference on the sexual bond, not on belief. (3) The Pauline harmonization double-counts: desertion by an unbeliever is already handled on its own footing (the “Pauline privilege”) and need not be smuggled into porneia to be accommodated. The strongest thing to be said for the reading is that its own most eminent advocate reduced it to the undisputed core — carnal unchastity — and left the broad sense a “knotty question” he no longer pressed.

Assessment

Weak. The spiritual-fornication reading rests on a genuine biblical usage (idolatry as harlotry) and a legitimate harmonizing instinct, and it had a real place in the exegesis of Augustine, Aquinas, and the medieval glosses — so it is not baseless. But as a reading of the exception clause it does not hold on its best case: it makes the qualifying exception devour the prohibition it exists to narrow, it runs against the carnal reference fixed by the context, and the desertion cases it wants to cover are already provided for by Paul’s own concession. Decisively, its greatest proponent retracted the broad form and conceded that the carnal sense is “no question at all.” The right conclusion is Augustine’s own second thought: the “fornication” of the exception is the carnal unfaithfulness of a spouse, and the spiritual-fornication motif, true in itself, is not what this clause is doing.
