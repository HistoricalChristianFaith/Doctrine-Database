Does 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 bar the separated from remarriage, even in the adultery case?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — in 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 Paul relays the Lord’s own charge (“not I, but the Lord”): a wife should not separate, “but if she depart, let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband,” and the husband should not put away his wife. This is the New Testament’s plainest bar on the separated party’s remarriage — no porneia exception, an explicit command to stay single — and the strict tradition (Chrysostom, Augustine, later Aquinas) presses it as the strongest single text against the Erasmian remarriage-license. But Paul is addressing believer–believer separation for non-adultery reasons, and his reconciliation-aim presupposes a bond no adultery has severed; whether the charge reaches the innocent party after a divorce for unfaithfulness — the very case Matthew’s exception is about — is exactly what is disputed · bears directly on the dissolution question

See also: does the exception permit remarriage, or only separation? · the Pauline privilege — does desertion free the believer to remarry? · is the marriage bond indissoluble while both spouses live? · does “makes her an adulteress” presuppose the wife’s remarriage?

The claim

And unto the married I command, yet not I, but the Lord, Let not the wife depart from her husband: but and if she depart, let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband: and let not the husband put away his wife.

— 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 (KJV). The interpretation weighed here is the strict reading of this text: because Paul flags it as the Lord’s own command (“not I, but the Lord”) and yet attaches no exception for adultery, and because he tells the woman who does separate to “remain unmarried, or be reconciled” — the two options being singleness or return, never a new husband — the passage bars the separated party’s remarriage across the board, the innocent as much as the guilty. So read, 7:10–11 is the clearest apostolic witness for the “separation, not remarriage” reading of Matthew’s exception, and Paul — the New Testament writer closest to the Lord’s living teaching — is its decisive proof.

Who advances it

The reading is the patristic consensus and, later, the settled Latin one. Chrysostom takes Paul to bar every second union: the separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband… if not to cohabit with him, yet so as not to introduce any other to be her husband.”1 Augustine, answering Pollentius, governs Matthew’s exception by the unqualified Mark, Luke, and this Pauline charge, and concludes that “any union with other persons while their first husband or wife is alive is not marriage but adultery.”2 Thomas Aquinas states the mechanism most exactly: the separated wife must “remain unmarried, as long as the husband is alive, because although the marriage is dissolved as to bed and board, not as to bond” — and the same rule binds the husband, “a similar form is kept in regard to the man and to the woman.”3 Theophylact reads it the same way, and modern strict exegesis of 1 Corinthians (Gordon Fee, Anthony Thiselton, David Garland) agrees that in these two verses Paul flatly refuses a separated believer the option of remarriage while the spouse lives.4

The evidence for

Paul relays the dominical charge directly, and adds no exception. Everywhere else in the chapter Paul distinguishes his own counsel (“I say, not the Lord,” 7:12; “I have no commandment of the Lord,” 7:25) from what he has from Jesus; here alone he says “not I, but the Lord.” This is therefore Paul’s report of Jesus’ teaching itself, given a bare twenty-odd years after the fact and to a Gentile church — and it carries no “except for fornication.” That silence is heavy: if the Matthean exception were a licence to remarry, the apostle who transmits the Lord’s divorce command to the churches is the last witness one would expect to omit it. His form matches the exceptionless Mark and Luke, not the Matthean doublet.5

The command names the options, and remarriage is not among them. “Let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband” is a closed disjunction: stay single, or go back. A third door — take another husband — is precisely what the sentence excludes. And the reason it excludes it is the reason the strict fathers give: the first bond has not dissolved, so the way back stands open, which is why the separated must keep themselves available for it. Aquinas’ distinction captures the logic exactly — the separation ends bed and board, not the bond — and it is the same logic Hermas and Clement gave for barring the innocent husband’s remarriage: the door to reconciliation must stay open.6

The counterweight

Paul is treating groundless separation of Christian couples, not the adultery case. The charge falls in the block addressed to two-believer marriages (7:10–11); only at 7:12 does Paul turn “to the rest” — the mixed marriages — on his own authority. And the separations he has in view are not adulteries: the early commentators who knew the setting name the motives as ascetic continence, faintheartedness, and bad temper. Chrysostom: “on the score of continence and other pretexts, and because of infirmities of temper, it fell out that separations took place.” Theophylact: “divorces… happen out of a love for continence, or out of faintheartedness, or for other reasons.”7 Paul’s reconciliation-clause confirms it: “or be reconciled” presupposes a marriage that is still savable, one no porneia has definitively broken. So the text’s own frame is a couple who have parted without the one ground Jesus allowed — and to that couple Paul says, rightly, remarry no one; go back. It does not follow that Paul is ruling on the innocent party of a marriage broken by adultery, the case Matthew’s exception raises. On the Erasmian reading (and on David Instone-Brewer’s socio-historical reconstruction), Paul here is refusing Corinthian believers a groundless Roman-style divorce-by-separation, while assuming — not repealing — the recognized biblical grounds (the Lord’s porneia; and, Instone-Brewer argues, the neglected marital obligations of Exodus 21:10–11) on which a wronged spouse is genuinely freed. Read so, 7:10–11 leaves the exception untouched rather than overriding it.8

The innocent-party cost. Extended to the adultery case, the strict reading makes even a wronged spouse’s remarriage adultery — the innocent penalized for the guilty party’s sin. Origen already felt the strain, judging that to forbid dismissing a wife guilty of “poisoning… or any form of murder” while adultery alone frees the husband left “something monstrous” either way; Erasmus pressed the same pastoral point, that a reading which chains the faithful to the treacherous cannot be what Christ intended for mercy’s sake. This is a coherence cost, not a refutation: the strict tradition answers that the bond’s permanence is an objective fact the innocent party’s hardship does not dissolve — a hard saying, accepted, not a contradiction. But it is a real weight on the reading, and one of the levers by which the Erasmian side moved the Reformers off the patristic consensus.9

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a bar on the separated party’s remarriage in the case Paul is actually treating — a Christian couple parted without adultery — 7:10–11 is clear, direct, and decisive: the apostle relays the Lord’s command, gives no exception, names singleness-or-reconciliation as the only options, and every ancient reader took it as a categorical bar resting on a bond that survives the separation. It is the strongest single New Testament text for “separation, not remarriage,” and its silence on porneia — from the writer closest to Jesus’ own teaching — is a genuine and standing difficulty for any reading that lets Matthew’s exception license a fresh marriage.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two limits keep the claim honest. (1) Paul’s charge addresses non-adultery separation, so extending it to bar remarriage “even in the adultery case” reaches beyond what the passage is treating. The block is about two-believer marriages parted for continence, temper, or faintheartedness (7:10–11, before the mixed-marriage case at 7:12), and the “or be reconciled” option presupposes a marriage no porneia has severed. To read the verses as settling the innocent party’s case after a divorce for unfaithfulness is to make them answer a question they do not raise — a less-probable extension, not the text’s plain scope. (2) The exceptionless form is powerful evidence but not proof that the exception cannot reach remarriage. Paul’s omission of porneia tells the same way against the Erasmian reading as Mark’s and Luke’s do — strongly — yet the strict side must still explain why Matthew twice adds a clause the others lack, and the source-critical question of whether that clause is Matthew’s own or an abbreviated qualification the others assumed remains open (weighed under why Matthew alone carries the exception). The innocent-party cost is a real burden the reading carries, not a knockdown of it. So the verses are conclusive within their scope and inconclusive beyond it — which is exactly why they do not, by themselves, close the larger dispute.

Assessment

Contested. That 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 bars the separated party’s remarriage is beyond doubt for the case Paul is treating — a believing couple parted without adultery, told to stay single or reconcile, on a bond the separation has not dissolved; here the text is the plainest such command in the New Testament, and its exceptionless, directly-dominical form is a heavy weight against the Erasmian license. What is genuinely disputed is the interpretation actually at issue: that the charge bars remarriage even in the adultery case, so settling against the innocent party. That extension reaches past the passage’s scope — Paul’s frame is groundless separation, and his reconciliation-aim presupposes a marriage no porneia has broken — and it carries the innocent-party cost Origen felt and Erasmus pressed. The strict tradition (Chrysostom, Augustine, Aquinas) reads the bar as categorical and grounds it on the surviving bond; the Erasmian and socio-historical readings (Instone-Brewer) confine it to no-grounds separation and leave Matthew’s exception standing. The verses are decisive within their scope and underdetermined beyond it, and the reception splits along the same fault line as Matthew’s exception itself — so this argument shares the contested verdict of the larger remarriage-or-separation and Pauline-privilege questions it feeds, strong by the exceptionless-witness measure, weaker on the “even in the adultery case” reach, with no clear winner.
