Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause?

Disputed reading · on Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

Related readings: Does Mark 10:12 (“if a woman shall put away her husband”) address Jesus’ own Jewish setting, or extend his saying to readers under Roman and Greek law? · Does Malachi 2:16 say that God hates divorce, that a man who hates his wife may send her away, or that the man who hates and divorces covers his garment with violence?

The passage

And I say unto you, Whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery: and whoso marrieth her which is put away doth commit adultery.

— Matthew 19:9 (KJV), source.

But I say unto you, That whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery.

— Matthew 5:32 (KJV), source. Four New Testament witnesses report Jesus’ divorce saying, and only Matthew qualifies it, twice: “saving for the cause of fornication” (parektos logou porneias, 5:32) and “except it be for fornication” (mē epi porneia, 19:9). Paul (c. 55) gives the Lord’s charge with no qualification — the wife is “not to depart… but and if she depart, let her remain unmarried” (1 Corinthians 7:10–11); Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18 also have no exception, and Mark adds a clause on the wife who puts away her husband.1

The question

Is Matthew’s exception part of what Jesus taught, left unstated in the shorter forms; an addition by Matthew to the saying he received; or a phrase that grants no exception at all? The answer bears on what the earliest recoverable teaching of Jesus on divorce was, and so on how each tradition has read the shorter forms beside Matthew’s.

The exception belongs to Jesus’ teaching; the shorter forms leave it unstated

On this reading Matthew states in full a teaching that Mark, Luke and Paul give in brief, and the exception is the Lord’s own.

Held by: Augustine (Latin, On Adulterous Marriages, c. 419);2 Craig S. Keener (…And Marries Another, 1991);3 David Instone-Brewer (Tyndale House, Cambridge, 2002; grants that Matthew added the phrases, holds that they restore the original debate);4 Athenagoras (Peckstadt) of Sinope (Orthodox, 2005).5

Augustine reads the four texts as one teaching: “Matthew spoke of this type of adultery. Such a one is not the only one who commits adultery, but, as we read in Mark…”2 Keener argues that Jesus’ absolute-sounding sayings are hyperbolic, and that a first-century hearer would assume the customary exceptions unless told otherwise.3

Instone-Brewer reads the Pharisees’ question, “Is it lawful… for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3), as the Hillelite “any matter” divorce. “Any matter” and “except indecency” were the catchwords of the schools of Hillel and Shammai in their debate on Deuteronomy 24:1. “Although I will agree that Matthew has probably added these phrases to the tradition that he received, I will also argue that he has correctly reinserted something that was present in the original debate. These phrases (or their equivalent) were removed when the debate was summarized for oral or written transmission. They were so obvious and well known to the original audience that they were considered superfluous.” Jesus’ “resounding no” was aimed at the “newly invented ‘any cause’ divorce,” and the evangelists “summarized the whole debate in one sentence” that “their first readers understood clearly.”4

Athenagoras and Ware ground the Orthodox Church’s practice in Matthew 19:9 as the Lord’s own word. Athenagoras writes that “the Orthodox Church can however permit divorce and remarriage on the grounds of interpretation of what the Lord says in Matt. 19:9,” and quotes Kallistos Ware: “Since Christ, according to the Matthaean account, allowed an exception to His general ruling about the indissolubility of marriage, the Orthodox Church also is willing to allow an exception.”5

The clause leaves Jesus’ rule without a true exception

On this reading Matthew’s words do not open a ground for divorce: either they set porneia aside, or they name a divorce that Jewish law already compelled, or they name unions that were never lawful marriages.

Held by: I. Abrahams (Studies in Pharisaism and the Gospels, 1917); Bruce Vawter (Catholic, Catholic Biblical Quarterly, 1954; he set the proposal aside in 1977); Joseph A. Fitzmyer (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1976; holds the unlawful-union sense of porneia; holds that Matthew added the clause);6 the New American Bible (United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, note on Matthew 5:32).7,8

Vawter proposed that mē epi porneia means “not even for fornication,” a preterition that sets the question of porneia aside rather than granting an exception. Matthew then agrees with Mark, Luke and Paul, and the saying is absolute in every Gospel.8

Abrahams, Lövestam and Bockmuehl argue from Jewish law. The Law prescribed death for adultery (Deuteronomy 22:22), and where that was not enforced, a husband was bound to divorce an adulterous wife. In Instone-Brewer’s summary of their view, “Jesus’ exception referred to compulsory divorces that could not be prevented. In other words, Jesus told his followers: ‘Do not divorce for any matter, except adultery, for which divorce is unavoidable.’ Jesus did not want to allow divorce for adultery, but there was no choice within Judaism. For those outside the constraints of Jewish rulers, like Paul’s Gentile churches, there was no need to have this exception. That is why neither Paul nor Mark (who wrote for Gentiles) mentioned it.”9 Read this way, the four witnesses give one rule against divorce: Matthew, writing for readers under Jewish law, notes the one case their law decided for them, and Paul and Mark, writing for Gentiles, had no such case to note.

The New American Bible’s note on Matthew 5:32 reads the clause of marriages forbidden by Mosaic law between persons of certain blood or legal relationship (Leviticus 18:6–18): “Marriages of that sort were regarded as incest (porneia), but some rabbis allowed Gentile converts to Judaism who had contracted such marriages to remain in them. Matthew’s ‘exceptive clause’ is against such permissiveness for Gentile converts to Christianity… In this interpretation, the clause constitutes no exception to the absolute prohibition of divorce when the marriage is lawful.”7 Fitzmyer, who takes porneia in the same sense, writes that “the exception for an illicit union (or for a marital situation that should not have been entered into to begin with) may be said not to render the prohibition of divorce less absolute.”6 On this form the clause concerns Gentile converts living in unions within the Levitical degrees, which were never lawful marriages, and the rule for a lawful marriage stands as absolute in Matthew as in Mark, Luke and Paul.

Matthew added the exception to the saying he received

On this reading the exceptionless form of Paul, Mark and Luke is the earlier one, and Matthew added the clause for the community he wrote for.

Held by: Rudolf Bultmann (Lutheran, History of the Synoptic Tradition, 1921); Joseph A. Fitzmyer (Catholic, Jesuit; Theological Studies, 1976); W. D. Davies and Dale C. Allison (Matthew, ICC, 1988–97); Ulrich Luz (Matthew, Hermeneia).10

Fitzmyer holds that Matthew took the pericope from Mark and “cast the Streitgespräch in terms of the Hillel-Shammai dispute” by adding “for any cause.” He gives the two reasons critical commentators reject the exception’s authenticity: “(i) the greater difficulty in explaining how the more absolute forms of the prohibition in Paul, Mark, and Luke would then have arisen (especially difficult in… Cor 7:10: to think that Paul would so record the absolute, unqualified form of the prohibition as a saying of the Kyrios in a context in which he himself makes an exception); (ii) the tendency otherwise attested in Matthew of adding things to the sayings of Jesus,” such as the extra petitions of the Lord’s Prayer, additions to the Beatitudes and Peter’s confession. “These two considerations make it almost certain that the exceptive phrases stem from the pen of the Evangelist, faced with a problem to resolve in the community for which he was writing.”10

Fitzmyer identifies that problem from Qumran: porneia means “illicit marital unions within the degrees of kinship proscribed by Lv 18:6-18,” and “Matthew’s exceptive phrases solve a problem for Gentile Christians living in the same community with Jewish Christians, who were still observing Mosaic regulations.”11 The Qumran Temple Scroll and Damascus Document show Palestinian Jews who opposed divorce, and so supply “an intelligible matrix” for an absolute saying in Jesus’ own setting. Of the sayings, he judges that two “may plausibly be regarded as traceable to Jesus himself”: “What therefore God has joined together, let not man put asunder,” and the saying on divorce and remarriage, “best preserved in Lk 16:18a-b.”12 On this reading the absolute saying shared by Paul, Mark and Luke is the earliest recoverable form of Jesus’ teaching, and Matthew’s phrases are the evangelist’s pastoral application of it to the mixed community he wrote for.
