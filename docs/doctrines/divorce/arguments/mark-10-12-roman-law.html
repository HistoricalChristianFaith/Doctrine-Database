Does Mark 10:12 (“if a woman shall put away her husband”) address Jesus’ own Jewish setting, or extend his saying to readers under Roman and Greek law?

Disputed reading · on Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

Related readings: Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause? · Does Malachi 2:16 say that God hates divorce, that a man who hates his wife may send her away, or that the man who hates and divorces covers his garment with violence?

The passage

And he saith unto them, Whosoever shall put away his wife, and marry another, committeth adultery against her. And if a woman shall put away her husband, and be married to another, she committeth adultery.

— Mark 10:11–12 (KJV), source. Alone among the four New Testament forms of the divorce saying, Mark gives a second, mirror clause in which the wife “puts away” (apolysasa) her husband. Mark sets the dialogue “by the farther side of Jordan” (10:1). Ancient readers already remarked on the wife’s case: Victor of Antioch, in the catena on Mark, writes that “the Saviour forbids this, which was neither plain, nor known to all, though it was contrary to nature,” and Bede allows that a wife may dismiss a husband on the ground of fornication or the fear of God, “as many are read to have done for the sake of religion.”1

The question

Did a wife’s divorcing her husband belong to the world of Jesus’ Jewish hearers, so that the clause can address them, or only to the Roman and Greek law of Mark’s Gentile readers, so that the clause is Mark’s extension of the saying? The answer bears on how Mark’s form of the saying relates to Matthew’s and to Paul’s, and on what Jesus himself said.

Wives in Jesus’ own world divorced their husbands, and the clause addresses them

On this reading a wife-initiated divorce was a known Jewish reality, most notoriously in the Herodian house, and the clause fits Jesus’ own setting.

Held by: Ernst Bammel (Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft, 1970); Bernadette J. Brooten (Evangelische Theologie, 1982); Larry W. Hurtado (Mark, NIBC, 1989); David Instone-Brewer (Harvard Theological Review, 1999; Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible, 2002).2

Bammel gathered the evidence for a Jewish woman’s right to divorce her husband, and Brooten argued that Jewish women could initiate divorce, so that Mark 10:11–12 need not be read as a Gentile-audience adaptation.2 The fifth-century-BC Jewish marriage contracts from Elephantine let either spouse end the marriage: “Should Miptahiah rise up in an assembly… and say, ‘I divorce (lit., I hate) my husband Eshor,’ the divorce fee is on her head.”3 Josephus records that Salome “sent [Costobarus] a bill of divorce and dissolved her marriage with him,” and that Herodias “divorced herself from her husband, while he was alive, and was married to Herod [Antipas], her husband’s brother”; Instone-Brewer counts other Herodian women who did the same.4

Instone-Brewer reads the Judaean-desert papyrus Ṣe’elim 13, as Tal Ilan did, as a bill of divorce from a woman, Shelamzion, to her husband: “the document is clearly addressed to the husband, and it uses the language of a standard divorce certificate.” He holds that “even before the announcement of the Se’elim get, there was a consensus that women could, under many circumstances, gain a divorce from their husbands within first-century Palestinian Judaism,” and that “some women also took the law into their own hands.”5

The Herodias affair was the scandal for which John the Baptist rebuked Antipas and died (Mark 6:17–18), and Mark sets this dialogue beyond the Jordan, in Antipas’s own tetrarchy. Hurtado holds that Jesus may prohibit both husbands and wives from initiating divorce precisely so as to condemn Herod and Herodias.6 Instone-Brewer applies the Lukan form of the saying the same way: it “describes the actions of Herod, who divorced his wife in order to marry Herodias, and Herodias, who divorced her husband Philip in order to marry Herod.”5 On this reading the wife’s clause is a word spoken into Jesus’ own Galilee and Perea, aimed at the most public marriage of the day.

Mark extended the saying to readers under Roman and Greek law

On this reading the wife’s clause is Mark’s addition, applying the saying to Gentile communities where a wife could divorce her husband.

Held by: Joseph A. Fitzmyer (Catholic, Jesuit; Theological Studies, 1976); R. T. France (The Gospel of Mark, NIGTC, 2002); John R. Donahue and Daniel J. Harrington (The Gospel of Mark, Sacra Pagina, 2002); Adela Yarbro Collins (Mark, Hermeneia, 2007).7

Fitzmyer: “V. 12 is a further Marcan extension of the first logion, introduced to suit the contingencies of Gentile Christian communities in areas where Roman and Greek law prevailed and where a woman was permitted to divorce her husband. The Evangelist Mark has thus extended the logion to a new situation, whereas it was originally formulated in terms of the usual OT understanding of the marriage bond, in which only the man—as ba’al—was able to divorce his wife.” Elephantine lay in Egypt, and “the evidence for such a practice in Palestine itself is meager indeed, almost nonexistent.”7

Under the halakhah descended from Deuteronomy 24:1 the husband writes the bill of divorce; the Mishnah’s tractate Gittin is framed around a husband delivering the get, and a wife could petition a court to compel her husband to divorce her. Josephus states the rule: “with us it is lawful for a husband to do so; but a wife, if she departs from her husband, cannot of herself be married to another, unless her former husband put her away.”8 Fitzmyer reads Salome’s divorce as one Josephus “clearly regards… as an illegal exception,” and France reads the Herodias affair as an exception that confirms the rule.7

In classical Roman law either spouse could dissolve a marriage by unilateral notice, and wife-initiated divorce was common in the Greco-Roman world.9 Mark elsewhere explains Jewish matters to outsiders: he translates Aramaic (talitha cumi, ephphatha), glosses the washing customs of the Pharisees (7:3–4), and converts the widow’s two mites into a Roman quadrans (12:42).10 France judges that the balancing clause may point to an origin for Mark’s Gospel, or at least for this tradition, in Rome. On this reading v. 12 applies Jesus’ principle symmetrically to the case Mark’s readers met every day, and is one datum among several for the Gospel’s Gentile audience.
