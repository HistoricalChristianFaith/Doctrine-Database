Does porneia mean betrothal unchastity (the betrothal view)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reading that porneia means sexual sin discovered during the Jewish betrothal, so Jesus permits only the breaking of a betrothal (as Joseph contemplated with Mary) and forbids all divorce of a consummated marriage: an elegant harmonization with the absolute Mark and Luke, but it forces porneia into an unattested narrow sense and cannot survive the married-couple context of Matthew 19 · advanced by Abel Isaksson and John Piper

Related arguments: the exception clause — see also Does porneia mean a wife’s marital unfaithfulness? · Does porneia mean an unlawful (incestuous) marriage? · Does porneia extend to spiritual fornication?

The claim

Matthew includes the exception clause in particular to exonerate Joseph, but also in general to show that the kind of “divorce” that one might pursue during a betrothal on account of fornication is not included in Jesus’ absolute prohibition… the primary contextual evidence for Matthew’s usage is that he conceives of porneia as something different than adultery.

— John Piper, “Divorce & Remarriage: A Position Paper”.1 On this reading Jesus permits no divorce of a true marriage at all: the exception covers only the dissolution of a legally-binding betrothal on discovery of the betrothed’s unchastity, so Matthew agrees exactly with the exceptionless Mark and Luke.

Who advances it

The scholarly foundation is Abel Isaksson, Marriage and Ministry in the New Temple (Lund, 1965), whose treatment of Matthew 19:3–12 is the standard defense; A. Ott had argued similarly earlier.2 William Heth and Gordon Wenham gave it a chapter in Jesus and Divorce, and it circulates among evangelicals through Charles Ryrie and, most prominently, John Piper, whose position paper defends it as the reading that keeps Jesus’ prohibition absolute.3 Like the incest reading, it is a modern proposal with no patristic advocate in the surveyed record; its motive, again, is to let the interpreter hold divorce to be wholly forbidden while still accounting for Matthew’s clause.

The evidence for

Its strongest support is the immediate Matthean context of the word apolyō (“divorce/put away”). In Matthew 1:18–19 Joseph, though only betrothed to Mary, is called her “husband” and resolves “to divorce her” (apolysai) on suspicion of unchastity — the very verb of 5:32 and 19:9, applied to a betrothal.4 Since first-century Jewish betrothal was a binding bond dissolved only by a bill of divorce, the vocabulary of “divorce” genuinely did cover the breaking of an engagement. Piper lists four claimed advantages: the reading (1) keeps Matthew from contradicting the absolute Mark and Luke — and his own 19:3–8; (2) explains why porneia rather than moicheia, since a betrothed girl’s sin is not yet the “adultery” of a wife; (3) squares with Matthew’s use of porneia for fornication at 15:19; and (4) fits the wider context of Joseph’s contemplated “divorce.”5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The linguistic hook is real: apolyō does cover ending a betrothal, and Matthew 1:19 proves it within the same Gospel. The reading’s instinct — that Matthew should not be read to contradict the absolute logia — is a legitimate harmonizing concern, and its account of the word-choice (porneia, not moicheia) is at least coherent.

What is overstated or imprecise. The decisive objection is the context of Matthew 19. There the question is explicitly whether a man may put away his wife (gynē), grounded in Moses’ bill of divorce for a married woman (Deuteronomy 24:1–4), answered by the creation of “one flesh” that a betrothed but unconsummated couple has not yet become — a fully-married frame from start to finish, in which importing a betrothal-only sense is a non-sequitur.6 The advantages Piper lists are shared by the ordinary reading: it too has porneia broader than moicheia (a genus including the wife’s adultery), it too fits 15:19, and it too can be squared with the absolute logia on the standard view that Mark and Luke simply compress a saying Matthew reports more fully. The betrothal reading buys its harmony at the price of confining porneia to a betrothed girl’s premarital sin — a narrow, otherwise-unattested sense the word is being “squeezed into,” against the same lexical breadth its proponents elsewhere invoke.7 And it leaves Jesus with no answer at all to the actual question asked — the divorce of a wife — which is not a plausible outcome of a passage framed entirely around that question.

Assessment

Weak. The betrothal view is a coherent, well-motivated harmonization with a genuine textual foothold (Joseph’s “divorce” of the betrothed Mary), and it is honestly advocated by real scholars; it is not baseless. But it does not hold on its best case. Matthew 19 is set from beginning to end within a married-couple, Deuteronomy-24 frame — the man is putting away his wife, not breaking an engagement — and the reading survives only by giving porneia a narrow betrothal-sense it is nowhere else shown to bear, while the advantages it claims over the plain reading (the porneia/moicheia distinction, agreement with Mark and Luke) are equally available without it. The harmonizing payoff is real but purchased too dearly.
