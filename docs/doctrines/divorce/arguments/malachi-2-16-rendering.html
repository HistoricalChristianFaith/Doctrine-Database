Does Malachi 2:16 say that God hates divorce, that a man who hates his wife may send her away, or that the man who hates and divorces covers his garment with violence?

Disputed reading · on Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

Related readings: Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause? · Does Mark 10:12 (“if a woman shall put away her husband”) address Jesus’ own Jewish setting, or extend his saying to readers under Roman and Greek law?

The passage

For the Lord, the God of Israel, saith that he hateth putting away: for one covereth violence with his garment, saith the Lord of hosts: therefore take heed to your spirit, that ye deal not treacherously.

— Malachi 2:16 (KJV), source. The verse closes a rebuke of men who have dealt “treacherously” with “the wife of thy youth” (2:13–16). Modern English versions divide between a first-person “I hate divorce, says the Lord” (RSV, NRSV, NASB, NIV 1984) and a rendering in which a divorcing man is the one who hates (NIV 2011, ESV, CSB).1

The key clause is kî śānē’ šallaḥ (כִּי־שֶׂנֵא שַׁלַּח). The Masoretes point śānē’ as a third-person masculine singular (“he hates”) or a participle (“one hating”); the first-person “I hate” would be śānē’tî. Šallaḥ is a Piel form that can be parsed as an imperative (“send away!”), an infinitive (“sending away”) or a finite verb (“he sends away”).2 The ancient versions render the clause as follows:3

  • Septuagint: alla ean misēsas exaposteilēs… — “but if, hating, you send [her] away…”; some manuscripts read an imperative, “if you hate, send away!”
  • Vulgate: cum odio habueris dimitte — “when you hold [her] in hatred, put [her] away.”
  • Targum Jonathan (Aramaic): “But if you hate her, divorce her.”
  • The Qumran Minor-Prophets scroll (4QXIIa): “but if you hate [her], send [her] away.”
  • The Syriac Peshitta lacks the clause.

The question

Who is the one who “hates” in Malachi 2:16, God or a divorcing husband, and is the clause a divine statement about divorce, a concession to a husband who hates his wife, or a censure of the man who divorces her out of hatred? The answer governs how the verse has been adduced in the Christian and Jewish debate over divorce.

A concession: “if you hate her, send her away”

On this reading the verse lets a husband who hates his wife divorce her, echoing the allowance of Deuteronomy 24.

Held by: Rabbi Yehuda (Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 90b);4 Jerome (Latin, the Vulgate and Commentary on Malachi);5 John Calvin (Reformed, Commentary on Malachi; grants the rendering, rejects the concession);6 Obedben Lumanze (Old Testament Essays, 2024; grants the imperative parsing, holds the text too “vague and complicated” to serve as a proof text against divorce).7

The Talmud records the reading in a single line: “Rabbi Yehuda says: The verse means that if you hate your wife, send her away.”4 The Targum, the Vulgate and the Qumran scroll all read the clause this way, with a conditional “if” and the imperative “send away.” Lumanze argues from the form of the verb: the exact spelling and pointing of šallaḥ occurs some fifteen times in the Hebrew Bible, and in eleven of the cases he lists it is read as an imperative, so “it is safer to translate šllḥ as an imperative rather than as infinitive construct,” in the second person, “in consonance with those of the Targum and Latin Vulgate.”7

Jerome renders “Since you have hated, put away, says the Lord God of Israel; but iniquity shall cover his garment,” and reads the verse as the people’s appeal to Moses: “God commanded through Moses that when we have hated our wives, we should put them away.” The prophet’s answer, in Jerome’s reading, is Christ’s: “This was indeed commanded in the law, but because of the hardness of your heart,” so that “whoever, except for the cause of fornication, unjustly puts away his wife, iniquity shall cover his garment.”5 Calvin keeps the rendering “If thou hatest, dismiss” and reads it as a comparison: Malachi’s hearers were taking second wives while keeping the first, and “it is a lighter crime to dismiss a wife than to marry many wives,” so the prophet speaks “not that he grants indulgence to divorce… but that he might by this circumstance enhance the crime.” Coverdale’s Bible, the Great Bible and the Geneva Bible printed “if he hate her, put her away,” and the 1611 King James Version kept it in its margin.6 Read as Jerome reads it, Malachi 2:16 stands with Deuteronomy 24:1 as the Law’s allowance, and the Gospel’s word on hardness of heart and the cause of fornication is the Lord’s fuller treatment of the same question.

God speaks of his own hatred of divorce

On this reading God is the one who hates, and the verse states his opposition to divorce in the climax of Malachi’s rebuke.

Held by: Rabbi Yoḥanan (Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 90b);4 Pieter A. Verhoef (Haggai and Malachi, NICOT, 1987); Andrew E. Hill (Malachi, Anchor Bible, 1998); Alan Branch (Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, 2011).8

In the Talmud, against Rabbi Yehuda, “Rabbi Yoḥanan says: The verse means that one who sends his wife away is hated by God.”4 The King James translators (1611) made God the subject of the hating and kept the third-person verb by supplying “saith that”: “the Lord… saith that he hateth putting away.” The RSV, NASB, NIV (1978) and NRSV render “I hate divorce”; the Jewish Publication Society’s Tanakh (1985) has “For I detest divorce—said the Lord,” and the Catholic New American Bible, Revised Edition (2011), “For I hate divorce, says the Lord, the God of Israel.”8

Verhoef keeps God as subject by reading the form as a participle with an unexpressed pronoun: “We prefer the reading according to which God is the subject, and only the Masoretic punctuation is altered to provide a participle with a suppressed personal pronoun: ‘I hate, I am hating.’” Hill takes the subject from verse 15, where God is “the One”: “‘Indeed, [The One] hates divorce!’ Yahweh, the God of Israel, has said,” a reading that, he writes, “preserves the integrity of the MT” and shows “the juxtaposition of ‘The One’ (Yahweh) and ‘sending away’ (i.e., the dissolution of ‘one’ through divorce).”8

Branch adds that God refers to himself in the third person elsewhere in Malachi (1:9), that the verse is framed as God’s own speech, and that every rendering must supply words (the HCSB brackets “[his wife]”). He reads kî as causal, “for,” because verse 16 is “the climax to all that has been said previously in 2:10–15,” and notes that the verse is one of only two places in the Minor Prophets where God is called “Lord God of Israel.” In his words, “Mal 2:16 provides the cause for God’s strong moral disapproval of the low view of marriage described in 2:10–15: Because the LORD hates divorce, your actions are wrong!”8 Read this way, the verse names God’s own hatred of divorce as the ground of the whole rebuke of verses 10–16.

A censure of the man who hates and divorces: he “covers his garment with violence”

On this reading the divorcing husband is the one who hates, and the verse condemns the man who sends his wife away out of aversion.

Held by: Cyril of Alexandria (Greek, Commentary on the Twelve Prophets, d. 444);9 Martin Luther (Lutheran, German Bible and lectures on the Minor Prophets, as quoted by Collins); David Clyde Jones (Journal of Biblical Literature, 1990); C. John Collins (Presbyterion, 1994; “Malachi 2:16 Again,” 2005).10

Cyril reads the Septuagint’s conditional as a warning: “if, having hated her, you send her away… that is, if making the dismissal plausible and well-reasoned, you should write on the bill, ‘Because I have hated her, I will send her away’; then, impiety would cover the your thoughts,” since “the pretense for hating is a vain one.”9 Luther’s German Bible reads “he who bears her ill will and repudiates her… covers his garment with iniquity,” and his lectures say that “whoever divorces his wife because he hates her is revealed as a violator… This stain covers him like a cloak.”10

Jones and Gordon Hugenberger read the Greek as a participle plus subjunctive, “if hating you divorce,” a protasis awaiting its apodosis, and the imperative “if you hate, divorce!” as a separate fifth-century reading later conflated with it.11 Collins finds the Hebrew intelligible as it stands: šallaḥ as a Piel perfect gives two asyndetic perfects, “he hated, he divorced,” with a hypothetical man as subject, and the following wəkissâ (“and he will cover”) opens the apodosis. Malachi elsewhere sets “says the Lord” between the halves of a conditional (2:2), and in sixteen of its seventeen other occurrences the formula follows God speaking in the first person. Collins renders: “For the man who hates and divorces, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence.”12 Hugenberger, following Raymond Westbrook, reads “hates [and] divorces” as an ancient Near Eastern legal phrase for divorce without adequate grounds.13

Its holders read the verse with the surrounding rebuke, in which the men of Judah are charged with dealing treacherously with “the wife of thy youth” (2:14–16): the clause names the treachery, and its censure falls on faithless, aversion-driven divorce.
