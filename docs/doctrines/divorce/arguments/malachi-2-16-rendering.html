Malachi 2:16 — “I hate divorce,” or “the man who hates and divorces”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — this is a translation and text-critical adjudication, and its two layers come apart. That the verse is not a first-person divine oracle — God flatly declaring “I hate divorce” — is close to settled: the Hebrew verb is third-person (“he hates”), and every ancient version (Septuagint, Vulgate, Targum, and the Qumran Minor-Prophets scroll) makes the divorcing man, not God, the subject of a conditional sentence. But the positive reconstruction stays genuinely disputed — whether the clause permits hate-motivated divorce (“if you hate her, divorce her”) or condemns it (“the man who hates and divorces… covers his garment with violence”) — and the passage is among the most obscure in the Hebrew Bible. The doctrinal upshot is directional: as a proof-text for a categorical divine prohibition of divorce, Malachi 2:16 is materially weaker than its popular citation assumes, though under every reading it still censures the treacherous divorce the prophet is rebuking · the rendering does not settle the doctrine: it constrains how the verse may be adduced, not whether divorce dissolves the bond, which is weighed on the remarriage-or-separation page.

The claim

For the Lord, the God of Israel, saith that he hateth putting away: for one covereth violence with his garment, saith the Lord of hosts: therefore take heed to your spirit, that ye deal not treacherously.

— Malachi 2:16 (KJV). This verse is the Old-Testament proof-text most often cited for God’s opposition to divorce — usually in the modern form “I hate divorce, says the Lord” (so RSV, NRSV, NASB, NIV 1984, ESV 2001), read as a categorical divine condemnation of divorce as such. The interpretation weighed here is the rival that the grammar and the ancient versions support: the Hebrew does not put “hate” in God’s mouth in the first person but describes a man who hates and divorces his wife — so the target is treacherous, aversion-driven divorce, not divorce per se. The choice materially changes how strong an Old-Testament witness against divorce this verse is.1

Who advances it

The “man who hates and divorces” reading is not a novelty but a recovery: for over two millennia no ancient version rendered the verse “God hates divorce.” Its modern text-critical case was made in detail by Gordon P. Hugenberger (Marriage as a Covenant, 1994) and David Clyde Jones (“A Note on the LXX of Malachi 2:16,” 1990), and defended by C. John Collins and Douglas Stuart; the translation committees of the NIV (2011), ESV (2016), and CSB revised their text to it, against the older “I hate divorce” that the NASB and NRSV retain. On this wiki the argument is engaged by David Instone-Brewer, who reads the verse the same way. The rival it contests — the first-person oracle “I hate divorce” — is not a fringe misreading but the dominant modern-English rendering from the RSV onward; the dispute is precisely that a rendering with almost no ancient support came to be treated as the plain sense.

The Hebrew crux

The consonantal Hebrew of the key clause is kî śānē’ šallaḥ (כִּי־שֶׂנֵא שַׁלַּח) — four ambiguous words on which the whole rendering turns. Śānē’ is pointed by the Masoretes as a third-person masculine singular (“he hates”) or a participle (“one hating”), not the first-person “I hate” (which would be śānē’tî); and šallaḥ is a Piel form — an imperative (“divorce!”), infinitive, or finite verb (“he divorces”) — rather than the noun “divorce” the traditional reading needs as the object of God’s hatred.2

To reach “I hate divorce” the translator must do two strained things at once: read a third-person verb (“he hates”) as a first-person one, and treat a verbal form (“send away”) as an abstract noun (“putting away, divorce”). The King James translators felt the difficulty and did not take that path; they supplied “saith that” and kept the third person — “the Lord… saith that he hateth putting away” — reporting God’s hatred at one remove rather than quoting a first-person oracle. The word order compounds the problem: “says the Lord” (’āmar YHWH) stands after “hates,” exactly where Malachi elsewhere drops the formula between the two halves of a conditional sentence (as at 2:2), so the natural parse is a protasis — “if a man hates… (says the Lord)…” — whose apodosis is the next clause, “he covers his garment with violence.”3

The ancient versions are unanimous against “I hate divorce”

Whatever the residual difficulty of the Hebrew, the oldest translators agree on one thing: the subject who hates is the man, in a conditional sentence, not God in a categorical one. None reads “God hates divorce.”

  • Septuagint (2nd c. BC): “alla eὶn misēsas exaposteilēs…” — “but if, hating, you send [her] away…” Hugenberger and Jones stress a precision usually missed: the Greek is a participle plus subjunctive (“if hating you divorce”), a protasis awaiting its apodosis — matching the Hebrew conditional — not the bare imperative “if you hate, divorce!” that a variant fifth-century reading introduced and that came to be mistaken for “the LXX.”
  • Vulgate (Jerome, c. 400): “cum odio habueris, dimitte” — “when you hold [her] in hatred, put [her] away.”
  • Targum Jonathan (Aramaic): “But if you hate her, divorce her.”
  • The Qumran Minor-Prophets scroll (4QXIIa) likewise reads “but if you hate [her], send [her] away,” aligning with the Targum and Vulgate.

Jerome preserves the point twice over in his own Commentary on Malachi: his Vulgate renders the clause “when you have hatred, let it go [dimitte], says the Lord God of Israel; but iniquity will cover its clothing,” and he quotes the Greek as “but if you hate her and divorce her, says the Lord God of Israel, he will cover your thoughts of wickedness.” He then reads the whole verse as an echo of the Deuteronomy-24 concession — “when we hate our wives, we should divorce them… because of the hardness of your hearts” — the divorcer’s “garment of iniquity” covering him for a divorce that is not “for the cause of fornication.” So the most authoritative Latin exegete of the passage neither knew nor taught “God hates divorce.”4

Where the versions themselves divide — the live residue

The versions agree that the man is the subject; they divide on what is then predicated of him, and this is the genuinely unsettled layer. Two reconstructions remain in play, and they point in opposite moral directions:

(a) Permissive. “If you hate her, divorce her” (Targum, Vulgate, 4QXIIa, the imperative-LXX variant) — a grudging allowance echoing Moses’ hardness-of-heart concession, with the “covering violence” clause a separate warning. This is how Jerome took it. Its cost is that it sits awkwardly with the surrounding rebuke, in which the prophet is plainly indicting the men of Judah for dealing “treacherously” with “the wife of thy youth” (2:14–16).

(b) Condemnatory. “For the man who hates and divorces — says the Lord — covers his garment with violence” (Hugenberger, Collins, Stuart; NIV 2011, ESV 2016, CSB). Here “hates and divorces” is the protasis and “covers his garment with violence” the apodosis — a flat censure of the treacherous divorce Malachi is attacking, fitting the context exactly. This is now the majority reconstruction among recent critical commentators and the reading behind the newest major English translations.5

Both (a) and (b) deny the categorical “God hates divorce”; they differ only on whether the man’s hate-motivated divorce is tolerated or rebuked. Reading (b), which the context favours, still yields a strong prophetic disapproval of the specific divorces in view — men casting off the wives of their youth — but as a condemnation of faithless divorce, not a blanket statement that all divorce is hateful to God.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The negative finding is robust and nearly consensual: the first-person oracle “I hate divorce” is the weakest of the live options. It requires reading a third-person verb as first-person and a verbal form as an abstract noun; it is unsupported by the Septuagint, the Vulgate, the Targum, and the Qumran scroll, every one of which makes the man the subject of a conditional; and the KJV itself declined it. That the popular proof-text over-reads the verse into a categorical divine prohibition is not seriously in doubt.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two cautions cut against pressing the recovery too hard. First, the positive reconstruction is not settled: the versions themselves split between a permissive “if you hate her, divorce her” and a condemnatory “the man who hates and divorces… covers his garment with violence,” and honest commentators concede that 2:16 is one of the most textually and grammatically obscure verses in the Hebrew Bible — so the confident modern “the man who hates and divorces” is a best reconstruction, not a certainty, and the older translations that keep “I hate divorce” are wrong on the grammar but not thereby foolish. Second, the recovery is sometimes inflated into “Malachi does not disapprove of the divorces at all” — which the context will not bear: whichever rendering wins, the men are being rebuked for dealing “treacherously” with “the wife of thy youth,” so the verse remains a prophetic censure of treacherous divorce, just not the categorical “God hates all divorce” the proof-text tradition made of it. The rendering is a wording-precision issue and should be kept distinct from the separate, doctrinal question of whether any divorce dissolves the marriage bond.6

Assessment

Contested. The verse’s rendering has two layers that must be rated separately. That Malachi 2:16 is not a first-person divine oracle — that “I hate divorce” strains the third-person Hebrew and finds no support in any ancient version — is well-grounded, approaching settled; on that much the popular categorical proof-text is the weaker reading. But the positive reconstruction is genuinely disputed even among those who reject “I hate divorce,” splitting between a permissive “if you hate her, divorce her” (Targum, Vulgate, Jerome) and a condemnatory “the man who hates and divorces… covers his garment with violence” (Hugenberger, Collins; NIV 2011, ESV 2016), and the Hebrew is conceded to be among the most obscure in the book — so no single rendering commands consensus. The directional finding for the doctrine is clear: the verse is a materially weaker witness for a categorical prohibition of divorce than its citation implies, while remaining, under every reading, a censure of the treacherous divorce the prophet rebukes. This adjudicates the wording only; whether the exception licenses remarriage is weighed on the remarriage-or-separation page, and the Mosaic concession it echoes on the Deuteronomy-24 page.
