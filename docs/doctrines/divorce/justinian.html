Justinian I on divorce

Dates: c. 482–565 (Roman emperor, r. 527–565; his marriage-and-divorce legislation — Novels 22, 117, 134 — dates 535–556) · Wikipedia
Summary: The civil law of the Christian empire, rather than a churchman’s exegesis. In three Novellae Justinian legislated the grounds, procedure, and penalties of divorce for Roman society — recognizing divorce by mutual consent and “friendly” dissolution (bona gratia) in Novel 22 (535), restricting it to an enumerated list of causes in Novel 117 (542), and abolishing consensual divorce in Novel 134 (556) — before his successor Justin II restored it (Novel 140, 566). The Novels treat husband and wife differently on the grounds for divorce and on waiting periods before remarriage. The Orthodox historian John Meyendorff describes divorce in Byzantium as “a purely civil procedure, whereas remarriage involved penitential discipline” (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Novel 22 (535): divorce by mutual consent and “friendly” dissolution (bona gratia)

Justinian’s marriage law lays down that a marriage may be dissolved by the parties themselves — by common consent, by a “friendly” divorce (bona gratia) for some reasonable cause, or for fault.

Marriages are dissolved by the parties thereto, if living, either by common consent—and no legislation is necessary for such a case, since the agreement disposes of everything—or (by agreement) for some reasonable reason when the divorce is called bona gratia, or because of some guilt or no guilt at all.

— Novel 22, ch. 4, trans. Fred H. Blume, Annotated Justinian Code, Justinian’s Novels.1 The same law prescribes waiting periods before remarriage, and treats the sexes differently: a divorced woman “must not remarry until after the expiration of a year… That shall not apply to the husband; and he may immediately remarry… because in such case no confusion of offspring can result” — the year’s delay imposed on the wife alone by “the constitution of Anastasius” even where the marriage is dissolved bona gratia with no fault on either side.2

Novel 117 (542): the enumerated causes, and adultery

Seven years later Justinian fixed “the sole grounds for which the husband or wife may legally send a bill of divorce.” A husband may divorce his wife and keep her dowry for a closed list of causes headed by adultery — after a formal accusation proven: “If the husband thinks that he is able to convict his wife of adultery, he shall first make an accusation against his wife… and if such accusation is proven and the husband has sent a bill of divorce, he shall have the prenuptial gift and the dowry.”3

The wife’s grounds are listed separately. A husband’s unchastity as such is not among them; if he keeps another woman in their own house, or steadily in another house in the same city, and persists after his wife’s repeated reproaches, she may dissolve the marriage.

If the husband lives with some other woman in the same house in which he lives with his wife… or is shown to have steadily lived with some other woman in some other house in the same city, and that he has been repeatedly censured by his wife… without however refraining from such dissoluteness, the wife shall have the right to dissolve the marriage on that ground.

— Novel 117, ch. 9, trans. Blume.4 The same Novel restricts consensual divorce, permitting it now only for the sake of “living chastely” (monastic vows): “some of the parties have heretofore dissolved a marriage between them by consent, we do not permit that to be done hereafter, unless that is done through the desire of living chastely.” It also sets a penalty for a wife who divorces without one of the lawful causes: she forfeits her dowry to the children and “shall… be delivered to the bishop of the city… so that such bishop may see that she is thrown into a convent, to remain there during the remainder of her life,” while a husband who does the same pays a property penalty.5

Novel 134 (556): consensual divorce abolished, monastic confinement for both parties

In the last of the three Novels Justinian abolished divorce by mutual consent, allowing no bill of divorce except on the enumerated causes and prescribing lifelong monastic confinement for a couple who dissolve their marriage otherwise.

we direct that no bill of divorce shall be sent, or if sent, shall be valid, for any cause other than those specified, nor shall the marriage be dissolved by consent, but faults shall be reciprocally forgiven. 1. If they dare to dissolve the marriage for any cause other than those specified by us, their property shall be given to their descendants… and the man and the woman shall be thrown into a monastery for the rest of their life…

— Novel 134, ch. 11, trans. Blume.6

Novel 140 (566): Justin II restores consensual divorce

Justinian died in 565; in the next year his nephew and successor Justin II repealed the ban, restoring divorce by mutual consent and remitting Justinian’s penalty.

we ordain, that, according to the ancient law marriage may be dissolved by mutual consent, and the penalty provided in the constitution of our father for those who dissolve marriage by mutual consent, shall not longer be in force. For marriage is entered into through mutual affection, it is rightly dissolved by a contrary sentiment by those who declare such sentiment in a bill of divorce.

— Novel 140, ch. 1 (Justin II, 14 September 566), trans. Blume.7 Justin II’s preface names what he undoes: “a dissolution of a marriage in this manner was, in the native tongue called a friendly divorce (bona gratia). Later, however, a law was enacted by our father… which forbids the dissolution of marriage by mutual consent… But many have come before us, who bear the marriage contracted by them illy and abhor it… and they ask that the marriage may be dissolved.”8

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Justinian’s Novels are the civil divorce law of the eastern empire, the setting of the later Eastern canons. The Council in Trullo met in 692 under Justinian II. John Meyendorff (Orthodox historian, 1990) writes that until the ninth century “the Byzantines generally concluded marriages as civil contracts without any involvement of the Church in the legal aspect of the marriage contract,” that marital problems “were approached by church authority on the level of pastoral, sacramental, and penitential discipline, not civil law,” and that “thus divorce was a purely civil procedure, whereas remarriage involved penitential discipline.” Of the remarriage of Constantine VI (795), condoned “by economy” by the patriarch and protested by the Stoudite monks, he writes: “For the politically minded patriarchs, oikonomia was becoming a form of realistic accommodation with the powers-that-be. For the monks, it was an expression of divine mercy for the repentant sinner.”9
  • Husband and wife. Under the Novels a wife’s proven adultery is a ground for the husband’s divorce, while a husband’s unchastity gives the wife a ground when it becomes open, persistent concubinage. Basil refers a comparable difference in church discipline to “custom,” and Ambrosiaster to male “headship”; Lactantius and Gregory of Nazianzus had objected to such differences (see their pages).
  • The sequence of the law. Between 535 and 566 the law moved from recognizing consensual divorce (Novel 22), to restricting it (Novel 117), to abolishing it (Novel 134), to restoring it (Justin II’s Novel 140).
  • Translation and text. Quotations follow Fred H. Blume’s annotated English translation of the Novellae (2nd ed., University of Wyoming). Blume’s manuscript prints “bona gratis” at Novel 22 ch. 4, a slip for the bona gratia spelled elsewhere in the same Novel; the term is given here in its standard form. The Novels also survive in S. P. Scott’s The Civil Law (vols. 16–17).
