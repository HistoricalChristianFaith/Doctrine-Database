Justinian I on divorce

Dates: c. 482–565 (Roman emperor, r. 527–565; his marriage-and-divorce legislation — Novels 22, 117, 134 — dates 535–556) · Wikipedia
Summary: Not a churchman’s exegesis but the civil law of the Christian empire. In three Novellae Justinian codified the grounds, procedure, and penalties of divorce for Roman society — recognizing divorce by mutual consent and “friendly” dissolution (bona gratia) in Novel 22 (535), narrowing it to an enumerated list of “just causes” in Novel 117 (542), and abolishing consensual divorce altogether in Novel 134 (556) — before his successor Justin II restored it (Novel 140, 566). This state law of divorce-with-remarriage, unequal in its treatment of husband and wife, is the civil-law substrate on which the Eastern church’s later practice of remarriage by pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) grew — the “accommodation with the powers-that-be” that practice’s own defenders concede.

Claims & evidence

Novel 22 (535): divorce by mutual consent and “friendly” dissolution (bona gratia) recognized

Justinian’s comprehensive marriage law lays down that a marriage may be dissolved by the parties themselves — by common consent, by a “friendly” divorce (bona gratia) for some reasonable cause, or for fault — treating the bond, in Roman fashion, as a tie that human agreement can undo.

Marriages are dissolved by the parties thereto, if living, either by common consent—and no legislation is necessary for such a case, since the agreement disposes of everything—or (by agreement) for some reasonable reason when the divorce is called bona gratia, or because of some guilt or no guilt at all.

— Novel 22, ch. 4, trans. Fred H. Blume, Annotated Justinian Code, Justinian’s Novels.1 The same law prescribes waiting periods before remarriage, and does so unequally: a divorced woman “must not remarry until after the expiration of a year… That shall not apply to the husband; and he may immediately remarry… because in such case no confusion of offspring can result” — the year’s delay imposed on the wife alone by “the constitution of Anastasius” even where the marriage is dissolved bona gratia with no fault on either side.2

Novel 117 (542): the enumerated “just causes,” and a frank double standard on adultery

Seven years later Justinian narrowed the field, fixing “the sole grounds for which the husband or wife may legally send a bill of divorce.” A husband may divorce his wife and keep her dowry for a closed list of causes headed by adultery — but only after a formal accusation proven: “If the husband thinks that he is able to convict his wife of adultery, he shall first make an accusation against his wife… and if such accusation is proven and the husband has sent a bill of divorce, he shall have the prenuptial gift and the dowry.”3

The wife’s grounds are listed separately — and reveal the same unequal reckoning of adultery that Basil had codified from “custom” a lifetime earlier. A husband’s simple unchastity is not among the wife’s grounds; only if he keeps another woman openly, in their own house or notoriously in the same city, and persists after his wife’s repeated reproaches, may she dissolve the marriage.

If the husband lives with some other woman in the same house in which he lives with his wife… or is shown to have steadily lived with some other woman in some other house in the same city, and that he has been repeatedly censured by his wife… without however refraining from such dissoluteness, the wife shall have the right to dissolve the marriage on that ground.

— Novel 117, ch. 9, trans. Blume.4 The same Novel begins to close the door on consensual divorce, permitting it now only for the sake of “living chastely” (monastic vows): “some of the parties have heretofore dissolved a marriage between them by consent, we do not permit that to be done hereafter, unless that is done through the desire of living chastely.” And it introduces the penalty that would harden in Novel 134 — a wife who divorces without one of the lawful causes forfeits her dowry to the children and “shall… be delivered to the bishop of the city… so that such bishop may see that she is thrown into a convent, to remain there during the remainder of her life,” while the like-offending husband pays only a property penalty.5

Novel 134 (556): consensual divorce abolished, monastic confinement for both parties

In the last of the three Novels Justinian abolished divorce by mutual consent outright, allowing no bill of divorce except on the enumerated causes and prescribing lifelong monastic confinement for a couple who dissolve their marriage otherwise — the civil law now bent as close to the church’s indissolubility as an emperor dared bring it.

we direct that no bill of divorce shall be sent, or if sent, shall be valid, for any cause other than those specified, nor shall the marriage be dissolved by consent, but faults shall be reciprocally forgiven. 1. If they dare to dissolve the marriage for any cause other than those specified by us, their property shall be given to their descendants… and the man and the woman shall be thrown into a monastery for the rest of their life…

— Novel 134, ch. 11, trans. Blume.6

Novel 140 (566): Justin II reverses the ban and restores consensual divorce

The reversal came at once. Justinian died in 565; in the next year his nephew and successor Justin II repealed the ban, restoring divorce by mutual consent and remitting Justinian’s penalty — the plainest proof that this was civil policy, swinging with the emperor, not the fixed law of the Gospel.

we ordain, that, according to the ancient law marriage may be dissolved by mutual consent, and the penalty provided in the constitution of our father for those who dissolve marriage by mutual consent, shall not longer be in force. For marriage is entered into through mutual affection, it is rightly dissolved by a contrary sentiment by those who declare such sentiment in a bill of divorce.

— Novel 140, ch. 1 (Justin II, 14 September 566), trans. Blume.7 Justin II’s preface names exactly what he undoes: “a dissolution of a marriage in this manner was, in the native tongue called a friendly divorce (bona gratia). Later, however, a law was enacted by our father… which forbids the dissolution of marriage by mutual consent… But many have come before us, who bear the marriage contracted by them illy and abhor it… and they ask that the marriage may be dissolved.”8

Notes

  • Why this sits on the divorce timeline. Justinian is not a witness to what Jesus permitted but to the civil framework the Eastern church came to work within. The Council in Trullo (692), meeting under Justinian II a century and a half later, legislates its graded penance against exactly this backdrop of imperial divorce law; and the developed Byzantine practice of remarriage by pastoral “economy” — as John Meyendorff concedes — grew up when divorce was “a purely civil procedure” and the church, made society’s registrar of marriage, accommodated the state’s grounds. This is the “entanglement with imperial civil law” the oikonomia argument weighs as a cost against that practice’s legitimacy.
  • The double standard. The Novels enact in state law the same asymmetry Basil traced to “custom” and Ambrosiaster to male “headship”: a wife’s adultery is a straightforward ground for the husband’s divorce and remarriage, whereas a husband’s unchastity gives the wife a ground only when it becomes open, persistent concubinage — the very inequality Lactantius and Gregory of Nazianzus had protested against in the name of the sexes’ equality before God.
  • The pendulum. Between 535 and 566 the law swung from recognizing consensual divorce (Novel 22), to restricting it (Novel 117), to abolishing it (Novel 134), to restoring it (Justin II’s Novel 140) — a volatility that itself illustrates how far the empire’s divorce discipline tracked imperial policy rather than a settled reading of Scripture.
  • Translation and text. Quotations follow Fred H. Blume’s annotated English translation of the Novellae (2nd ed., University of Wyoming). Blume’s manuscript prints “bona gratis” at Novel 22 ch. 4, an evident slip for the bona gratia spelled consistently elsewhere in the same Novel; the term is given here in its standard form. The Novels also survive in S. P. Scott’s The Civil Law (vols. 16–17).
