Origen of Alexandria on divorce

Dates: c. 246 (Commentary on Matthew XIV, written at Caesarea) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the fourteenth book of his Commentary on Matthew, expounding the Pharisees’ “is it lawful to put away a wife for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3–12), Origen gives the fullest early Greek exegesis of the divorce sayings. He reads Moses’ bill of divorcement as a concession “for the hardness of your hearts,” holds that Christ permits dissolution of marriage for fornication alone, and wrestles openly with whether other grave crimes (poisoning, murder) might equally warrant it. His single most consequential remark is an aside: “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason.” It is the earliest surviving notice of church leaders tolerating remarriage after divorce — the seed of the later Eastern practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) — and Origen records it with disapproval yet a striking measure of understanding.

Claims & evidence

Christ answers the “for every cause” question: Moses’ bill was a concession, not the original design

Origen takes the Pharisees’ question — “Is it lawful for a man to put away his own wife for every cause?” — as a trap: had Christ said yes, they would charge him with “dissolving marriages for trifles”; had he said no, with forcing a man to “dwell with a woman, even with sins.” The Saviour answers “blamelessly” by going behind Moses to creation.

…in the first place, rejecting the opinion that a wife was to be put away for every cause, and, in the second place, giving answer to the question about the bill of divorcement; for He saw that not every cause is a reasonable ground for the dissolution of marriage… And, as tending to convince them that they should not put away their wife for every cause, is it said, “What God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.”

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew XIV.16, ANF vol. 10. The Mosaic permission (Deuteronomy 24:1), Origen argues under the heading “Some Laws Given by Concession to Human Weakness,” belongs to a class of enactments framed for the people’s frailty rather than as the ideal — and, he adds, the new covenant has laws of the same order.

…some of the laws were written not as excellent, but as by way of accommodation to the weakness of those to whom the law was given; for something of this kind is indicated in the words, “Moses for your hardness of heart suffered you to put away your wives;” but that which is pre-eminent and superior to the law… is indicated in this, “But from the beginning it hath not been so.” But in the new covenant also there are some legal injunctions of the same order… because of our hardness of heart, it has been written on account of our weakness, “But because of fornications, let each man have his own wife.”

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.23. So Origen classes even Paul’s marital counsel (1 Corinthians 7:2, 6) as accommodation “by way of permission, not of commandment” — the same hardness-of-heart logic Tertullian had applied to Deuteronomy 24, now extended across both testaments.

The pivotal aside: some church rulers have permitted remarriage while the first spouse lives

Immediately after classing the concession-laws, Origen turns to a contemporary fact he plainly finds irregular yet does not simply condemn. It is the earliest datable report of Christian leaders allowing a divorced woman to remarry during her husband’s lifetime — the practice later formalized in the Christian East as pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

But now contrary to what was written, some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living, doing contrary to what was written, where it is said, “A wife is bound for so long time as her husband liveth,” and “So then if while her husband liveth, she shall be joined to another man she shall be called an adulteress,” not indeed altogether without reason, for it is probable this concession was permitted in comparison with worse things, contrary to what was from the beginning ordained by law, and written.

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.23. Two things are equally notable. First, Origen states flatly that the permission runs “contrary to what was written” — against Romans 7:2–3 and 1 Corinthians 7:39, which bind a wife to her husband so long as he lives — so he does not endorse it as sound doctrine. Second, he refuses to call it baseless: it was allowed “not indeed altogether without reason… in comparison with worse things.” That is precisely the logic of pastoral oikonomia — a lesser irregularity tolerated to prevent a greater sin — two centuries before Basil’s canons or the Council in Trullo would give it canonical shape. Origen names no bishop and endorses no rule; he registers the practice as already present in the mid-third-century church, disapproves of it as written, and concedes its pastoral motive.

The exception clause: dissolution for fornication alone — and the puzzle of graver crimes

On Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9; cf. 5:32) Origen is firm that Christ licenses the putting-away for one cause only.

After this our Saviour says, not at all permitting the dissolution of marriages for any other sin than fornication alone, when detected in the wife, “Whosoever shall put away his own wife, saving for the cause of fornication, maketh her an adulteress.”

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.24. But he does not leave it there. With unusual candour he presses the hard case the rule creates: what of a wife guilty of something worse than adultery — poisoning, infanticide, murder? May her husband not put her away, when Christ names only fornication?

But it might be a subject for inquiry if on this account He hinders any one putting away a wife, unless she be caught in fornication, for any other reason, as for example for poisoning, or for the destruction during the absence of her husband from home of an infant born to them, or for any form of murder whatsoever… In either case there appears to be something monstrous, whether it be really monstrous, I do not know; for to endure sins of such heinousness which seem to be worse than adultery or fornication, will appear to be irrational; but again on the other hand to act contrary to the design of the teaching of the Saviour, every one would acknowledge to be impious.

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.24. Origen leaves the dilemma open — “you can inquire yourselves” — unwilling either to widen the exception past “fornication” or to pretend the strict reading has no cost. It is a rare early admission that the exception clause, read narrowly, produces results that “appear… irrational,” and it shows the porneia debate already live in the mid-third century. On the man’s side he keeps the bond intact: “as a woman is an adulteress, even though she seem to be married to a man, while the former husband is still living, so also the man who seems to marry her who has been put away, does not so much marry her as commit adultery with her.”

Notes

  • The XIV.23 aside — that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason” — is the single most-cited patristic datum for the antiquity of tolerated remarriage after divorce. It cuts two ways in the later dispute: proponents of the indissolubility settlement read it as Origen condemning an abuse (“contrary to what was written”), while defenders of Eastern oikonomia read the same sentence as Origen conceding its pastoral reasonableness (“in comparison with worse things”). Both halves are in the text; the passage is weighed as a key witness under the divorce-doctrine arguments on the summary’s Arguments section.
  • Origen’s handling of the exception clause — dissolution for “fornication alone” — sits with Clement’s and Tertullian’s reading that Matthew’s exception governs the putting-away, not a licence to remarry; unlike them, Origen also airs the counter-pressure (graver crimes) without resolving it, and reports a contrary practice in the churches. He is thus both a witness for the strict exception and the earliest witness to its pastoral relaxation.
  • Much of XIV.16–22 reads the divorce texts allegorically — the “bill of divorcement” as God’s writ against the synagogue (Isaiah 50:1), the “two become one flesh” as Christ and the Church. The material summarized here is drawn from the literal level Origen himself flags (“because of the literal expression these things must further be said”, XIV.23), where he treats the marriage law as law.
