Origen of Alexandria on divorce

Dates: c. 246 (Commentary on Matthew XIV, written at Caesarea) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the fourteenth book of his Commentary on Matthew, expounding the Pharisees’ “is it lawful to put away a wife for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3–12), Origen gives an extended early Greek exegesis of the divorce sayings. He reads Moses’ bill of divorcement as a concession “for the hardness of your hearts,” holds that Christ permits dissolution of marriage for fornication alone, and asks whether other grave crimes (poisoning, murder) might equally warrant it, leaving the question open. In the same passage he records that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason.” It is the earliest extant notice of church leaders permitting remarriage after divorce while the first spouse lived; Origen calls the permission “contrary to what was written” and allows that it was given “in comparison with worse things.”

Claims & evidence

Christ answers the “for every cause” question: Moses’ bill as a concession

Origen takes the Pharisees’ question — “Is it lawful for a man to put away his own wife for every cause?” — as a trap: had Christ said yes, they would charge him with “dissolving marriages for trifles”; had he said no, with forcing a man to “dwell with a woman, even with sins.” The Saviour, in Origen’s account, answers “blamelessly” by going behind Moses to creation.

…in the first place, rejecting the opinion that a wife was to be put away for every cause, and, in the second place, giving answer to the question about the bill of divorcement; for He saw that not every cause is a reasonable ground for the dissolution of marriage… And, as tending to convince them that they should not put away their wife for every cause, is it said, “What God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.”

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew XIV.16, ANF vol. 10. The Mosaic permission (Deuteronomy 24:1), Origen argues under the heading “Some Laws Given by Concession to Human Weakness,” belongs to a class of enactments framed for the people’s frailty rather than as the ideal — and, he adds, the new covenant has laws of the same order.

…some of the laws were written not as excellent, but as by way of accommodation to the weakness of those to whom the law was given; for something of this kind is indicated in the words, “Moses for your hardness of heart suffered you to put away your wives;” but that which is pre-eminent and superior to the law… is indicated in this, “But from the beginning it hath not been so.” But in the new covenant also there are some legal injunctions of the same order… because of our hardness of heart, it has been written on account of our weakness, “But because of fornications, let each man have his own wife.”

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.23. Origen classes Paul’s marital counsel (1 Corinthians 7:2, 6) as accommodation “by way of permission, not of commandment,” applying to both testaments the hardness-of-heart reasoning Tertullian had applied to Deuteronomy 24.

Some rulers of the church have permitted remarriage while the first spouse lives (XIV.23)

Immediately after classing the concession-laws, Origen turns to a contemporary practice. It is the earliest extant datable report of Christian leaders allowing a divorced woman to remarry during her husband’s lifetime.

But now contrary to what was written, some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living, doing contrary to what was written, where it is said, “A wife is bound for so long time as her husband liveth,” and “So then if while her husband liveth, she shall be joined to another man she shall be called an adulteress,” not indeed altogether without reason, for it is probable this concession was permitted in comparison with worse things, contrary to what was from the beginning ordained by law, and written.

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.23. The sentence makes two statements. Origen says the permission runs “contrary to what was written” — against Romans 7:2–3 and 1 Corinthians 7:39, which bind a wife to her husband so long as he lives. He also says it was allowed “not indeed altogether without reason… in comparison with worse things.” Origen names no bishop and no church; he reports the practice as present in his own day.

The exception clause: dissolution for fornication alone, and the question of graver crimes

On Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9; cf. 5:32) Origen holds that Christ licenses the putting-away for one cause only.

After this our Saviour says, not at all permitting the dissolution of marriages for any other sin than fornication alone, when detected in the wife, “Whosoever shall put away his own wife, saving for the cause of fornication, maketh her an adulteress.”

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.24. He then raises the case of a wife guilty of something he counts worse than adultery — poisoning, infanticide, murder — and asks whether her husband may put her away, when Christ names only fornication.

But it might be a subject for inquiry if on this account He hinders any one putting away a wife, unless she be caught in fornication, for any other reason, as for example for poisoning, or for the destruction during the absence of her husband from home of an infant born to them, or for any form of murder whatsoever… In either case there appears to be something monstrous, whether it be really monstrous, I do not know; for to endure sins of such heinousness which seem to be worse than adultery or fornication, will appear to be irrational; but again on the other hand to act contrary to the design of the teaching of the Saviour, every one would acknowledge to be impious.

— Commentary on Matthew XIV.24. Origen leaves the dilemma open — “you can inquire yourselves” — neither widening the exception past “fornication” nor setting the question aside. On the man’s side he treats the first marriage as still in force: “as a woman is an adulteress, even though she seem to be married to a man, while the former husband is still living, so also the man who seems to marry her who has been put away, does not so much marry her as commit adultery with her.”

Notes

  • The XIV.23 report — that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason” — joins a judgement (“contrary to what was written”) to an acknowledged motive (“in comparison with worse things”). Later Eastern canons on divorce and the practice of pastoral oikonomia (“economy”) are recorded on the Basil of Caesarea and Council in Trullo pages and on the timeline.
  • On the exception clause Origen allows dissolution for “fornication alone” and, like Clement and Tertullian, treats a woman married to another while her husband lives as an adulteress. He also raises the question of graver crimes without resolving it, and reports a contrary practice among some church leaders.
  • Much of XIV.16–22 reads the divorce texts allegorically — the “bill of divorcement” as God’s writ against the synagogue (Isaiah 50:1), the “two become one flesh” as Christ and the Church. The material summarized here is drawn from the literal level Origen himself marks (“because of the literal expression these things must further be said”, XIV.23), where he treats the marriage law as law.
