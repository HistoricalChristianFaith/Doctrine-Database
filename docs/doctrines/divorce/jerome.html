Jerome on divorce

Dates: c. 394 (the Letter 55 to Amandus; the Commentary on Matthew follows in 398 and the eulogy of Fabiola in 399) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome, the Vulgate translator and the towering Latin scholar of the age, is the strictest Western voice on this timeline and the one who fixes the indissolubility rule for the Latin church. Across three works of the 390s — a letter of casuistry to the presbyter Amandus (c. 394), his Commentary on Matthew (398), and the eulogy of the Roman noblewoman Fabiola (399) — he holds that Matthew’s exception licenses only the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, never a new marriage while the first partner lives: “yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another.” Against the permissive reading of his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, Jerome applies the bar equally to husband and wife — “what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” — and treats a second marriage during the first spouse’s lifetime as adultery requiring penance. The Fabiola case shows the doctrine worked out on a real, pitiable life: he upholds the rule without flinching, yet celebrates the penance that restored her to communion.

Claims & evidence

Even against an adulterous, sodomite husband the wife may not remarry while he lives (Letter 55 to Amandus, c. 394)

The presbyter Amandus of Bordeaux had put to Jerome, on behalf of a “sister” (thought to be Fabiola herself), a hard pastoral case: may a woman who left an adulterous, sodomite husband and, under compulsion, married another during his lifetime, receive communion without doing penance? Jerome answers with an emphatic negative, grounding it in Paul (Romans 7:1–3) and in Christ’s own word (Matthew 5:32). No fault of the husband — not even the gravest — frees the wife to marry again.

The apostle has thus cut away every plea and has clearly declared that, if a woman marries again while her husband is living, she is an adulteress. You must not speak to me of the violence of a ravisher, a mother’s pleading, a father’s bidding, the influence of relatives, the insolence and the intrigues of servants, household losses. A husband may be an adulterer or a sodomite, he may be stained with every crime and may have been left by his wife because of his sins; yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another.

— Jerome, Letter 55.3 (to Amandus), NPNF2 vol. 6. The remedy Jerome prescribes is not annulment but penance and continence: the “second husband… ought to be called not a husband but an adulterer,” and the penitent must “from the time she begins to repent… have no farther intercourse” with him. He drives the point home with the wound-and-knife image he favours — “Diseased flesh calls for the knife and the searing-iron… to be merciful is cruel.”1

The exception is porneia alone, and it licenses dismissal, not remarriage (Commentary on Matthew, on 19:9, 398)

In his running Commentary on Matthew, dashed off in a fortnight in 398, Jerome reads the exception clause of Matthew 19:9 as strictly as anyone on this timeline. Fornication is the one thing that severs the “one flesh” and so frees the husband to send the wife away — but the reason he must then not take another while she lives is the same indissolubility: the bond is not dissolved by the dismissal.

Only fornication is what overcomes a wife’s affection… Therefore, whenever there is fornication, or even suspicion of fornication, the wife is freely dismissed. And because it could happen that someone falsely accuses the innocent person and implicates them in an old crime due to a second marriage, the husband is commanded to divorce the first wife so that he does not have a second wife while the first one is still alive.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, on Matthew 19:9. On the Sermon’s parallel (Matthew 5:31–32) he explains Moses’ bill of divorcement the way Tertullian and Origen had — a concession “because of the hardness of the hearts of the husbands,” given to forestall the murder of unwanted wives, “not by the will of God but by the necessity of sinners.” The exception, for Jerome, restores the creation order the concession had bent; it does not open a door to a fresh marriage.2

“What is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” (the eulogy of Fabiola, Letter 77, 399)

When Fabiola died in 399, Jerome wrote her eulogy to Oceanus — and, because “at the very outset there is a rock in the path,” had to defend the doctrine against the memory of her own remarriage. He does not exempt her: the Lord’s command binds men and women alike, and it takes away “all pretexts for re-marriage” while the first spouse lives. But he insists the same rule cuts against the Roman double standard that let husbands stray unpunished.

The Lord has given commandment that a wife must not be put away “except it be for fornication, and that, if put away, she must remain unmarried.” Now a commandment which is given to men logically applies to women also… The laws of Caesar are different, it is true, from the laws of Christ… But with us Christians what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men, and as both serve the same God both are bound by the same obligations.

— Jerome, Letter 77.3 (to Oceanus), NPNF2 vol. 6. Fabiola, he explains, had divorced a husband whose vices “not even a prostitute or a common slave could have put up with,” then remarried in ignorance — “she did not know that the rigour of the gospel takes away from women all pretexts for re-marriage so long as their former husbands are alive.” The equal-obligation argument is the same one Jerome pressed on Amandus; here it is deployed sympathetically, to shift the blame from a young widow onto the Roman law that misled her.3

The rule enacted on a real life: Fabiola’s public penance (Letter 77, 399)

The Fabiola case is the timeline’s most vivid picture of indissolubility applied. Jerome upholds the bar on remarriage without softening it — and yet the resolution is not lifelong exclusion but restoration through penance. After her second husband died, Fabiola did public penance before all Rome and was received back into communion, becoming one of Jerome’s celebrated ascetics and the founder of the first Christian hospital.

Yet it was then that she put on sackcloth to make public confession of her error. It was then that in the presence of all Rome… she stood in the ranks of the penitents and exposed before bishop, presbyters, and people—all of whom wept when they saw her weep—her dishevelled hair, pale features, soiled hands and unwashed neck. What sins would such a penance fail to purge away?

— Jerome, Letter 77.4 (to Oceanus), NPNF2 vol. 6. The social-historical detail is precious: a remarried woman, barred by the strict reading from a valid second union, is not permanently cast out but reconciled once the impediment (the living first husband) is gone and penance is done — the Latin church’s discipline shown in operation on a named noblewoman, penance restoring what the second marriage had forfeited.4

Notes

  • Jerome belongs squarely with the strict, separation-not-remarriage line of Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Elvira, Basil, and his Greek near-contemporary John Chrysostom — fornication licenses dismissal, never a new marriage while the first spouse lives. He stands directly against his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, who read Paul’s silence to the man as permitting an innocent husband to remarry, and against the concession-to-weakness defended in the East by Epiphanius.
  • Where Ambrosiaster rested a frank double standard on male headship, Jerome argues the opposite from the equality of the sexes before God: the command “given to men logically applies to women also,” so a husband may no more remarry than a wife. His severity therefore falls even-handedly, closing the very escape (the man’s superior “rights”) that Ambrosiaster had opened.
  • The three works span the strictness in different registers: Letter 55 is cold casuistry to a fellow cleric; the Matthew commentary is terse exegesis; the Fabiola eulogy is the doctrine tested against grief and affection — Jerome plainly admired Fabiola and calls her remarriage “a fault… at the same time… a case of necessity” (“It is better… to marry than to burn”), yet still refuses to call the second union lawful. The tenderness is real; the rule does not bend.
  • Jerome also gives the earliest datable social vignette of the discipline in force: public penance in the ranks of the penitents, reconciliation by the bishop, and full restoration to communion once the first husband was dead — the mechanism by which the Latin church absorbed the human cost of a bar that admitted no valid remarriage. The same Jerome appears on this wiki elsewhere as a witness on the perpetual virginity of Mary and infant baptism timelines.
