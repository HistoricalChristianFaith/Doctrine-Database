Jerome on divorce

Dates: c. 394 (the Letter 55 to Amandus; the Commentary on Matthew follows in 398 and the eulogy of Fabiola in 399) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome, the translator of the Vulgate, writes on divorce in three works of the 390s — a letter of casuistry to the presbyter Amandus (c. 394), his Commentary on Matthew (398), and the eulogy of the Roman noblewoman Fabiola (399). He holds that Matthew’s exception licenses the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, and not a new marriage while the first partner lives: “yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another.” He applies the bar to husband and wife alike — “what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” — where his Latin contemporary Ambrosiaster allowed the husband a second marriage, and he treats a second marriage during the first spouse’s lifetime as requiring penance. In the Fabiola eulogy he applies the rule to a named woman, calling her remarriage “a fault” and describing the public penance that restored her to communion.

Claims & evidence

A wife who left an adulterous husband may not remarry while he lives (Letter 55 to Amandus, c. 394)

The presbyter Amandus of Bordeaux had put to Jerome, on behalf of an unnamed “sister” (her identification with Fabiola is ⚠ unverified), a pastoral case: may a woman who left an adulterous, sodomite husband and, under compulsion, married another during his lifetime, receive communion without doing penance? Jerome answers no, grounding it in Paul (Romans 7:1–3) and in Christ’s word (Matthew 5:32). In his ruling no fault of the husband frees the wife to marry again.

The apostle has thus cut away every plea and has clearly declared that, if a woman marries again while her husband is living, she is an adulteress. You must not speak to me of the violence of a ravisher, a mother’s pleading, a father’s bidding, the influence of relatives, the insolence and the intrigues of servants, household losses. A husband may be an adulterer or a sodomite, he may be stained with every crime and may have been left by his wife because of his sins; yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another.

— Jerome, Letter 55.3 (to Amandus), NPNF2 vol. 6. The remedy Jerome prescribes is penance and continence: the “second husband… ought to be called not a husband but an adulterer,” and the penitent must “from the time she begins to repent… have no farther intercourse” with him. He adds the image of wound and knife — “Diseased flesh calls for the knife and the searing-iron… to be merciful is cruel.”1

The exception and the dismissal of a wife (Commentary on Matthew, on 19:9, 398)

In his Commentary on Matthew, written in a fortnight in 398, Jerome reads the exception clause of Matthew 19:9 as concerning fornication alone, which he says “overcomes a wife’s affection” and permits her dismissal; the husband is not to “have a second wife while the first one is still alive.”

Only fornication is what overcomes a wife’s affection… Therefore, whenever there is fornication, or even suspicion of fornication, the wife is freely dismissed. And because it could happen that someone falsely accuses the innocent person and implicates them in an old crime due to a second marriage, the husband is commanded to divorce the first wife so that he does not have a second wife while the first one is still alive.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, on Matthew 19:9. On the Sermon’s parallel (Matthew 5:31–32) he explains Moses’ bill of divorcement as Tertullian and Origen had — a concession “because of the hardness of the hearts of the husbands,” given to forestall the murder of unwanted wives, “not by the will of God but by the necessity of sinners.”2

“What is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men” (the eulogy of Fabiola, Letter 77, 399)

When Fabiola died in 399, Jerome wrote her eulogy to Oceanus — and, because “at the very outset there is a rock in the path,” addressed her own remarriage. He does not exempt her: the Lord’s command binds men and women alike, and it takes away “all pretexts for re-marriage” while the first spouse lives. He also sets the rule against Roman law, which treated husbands and wives differently.

The Lord has given commandment that a wife must not be put away “except it be for fornication, and that, if put away, she must remain unmarried.” Now a commandment which is given to men logically applies to women also… The laws of Caesar are different, it is true, from the laws of Christ… But with us Christians what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men, and as both serve the same God both are bound by the same obligations.

— Jerome, Letter 77.3 (to Oceanus), NPNF2 vol. 6. Fabiola, he explains, had divorced a husband whose vices “not even a prostitute or a common slave could have put up with,” then remarried in ignorance — “she did not know that the rigour of the gospel takes away from women all pretexts for re-marriage so long as their former husbands are alive.” The argument from equal obligation is the one Jerome put to Amandus; in the eulogy he uses it to place the blame for her remarriage on the Roman law that guided her.3

Fabiola’s public penance (Letter 77, 399)

Jerome holds to the bar on remarriage in Fabiola’s case and describes her restoration through penance. After her second husband died, Fabiola did public penance before all Rome and was received back into communion; she then gave her property to the poor and founded a hospital, which Jerome says she was “the first person to found.”

Yet it was then that she put on sackcloth to make public confession of her error. It was then that in the presence of all Rome… she stood in the ranks of the penitents and exposed before bishop, presbyters, and people—all of whom wept when they saw her weep—her dishevelled hair, pale features, soiled hands and unwashed neck. What sins would such a penance fail to purge away?

— Jerome, Letter 77.4 (to Oceanus), NPNF2 vol. 6. In Jerome’s account a remarried woman, whose second union he does not count a valid marriage, is reconciled after the death of her second husband and the completion of a public penance, before “bishop, presbyters, and people.”4

Notes

  • Jerome bars a new marriage while the first spouse lives, as do Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Elvira, and his Greek contemporary John Chrysostom (see their pages). His Latin contemporary Ambrosiaster read Paul’s silence to the man as permitting an innocent husband to remarry, and in the East Epiphanius allowed a second marriage after divorce as a concession to weakness (see their pages).
  • Ambrosiaster grounds a different standard for the husband on male headship; Jerome argues from the equal obligation of the sexes before God: the command “given to men logically applies to women also,” so a husband may no more remarry than a wife.
  • The three works differ in register: Letter 55 is a ruling to a fellow cleric; the Matthew commentary is brief exegesis; the Fabiola eulogy applies the rule to a woman Jerome praises, whose remarriage he calls “a fault… at the same time… a case of necessity” (“It is better… to marry than to burn”) while not calling the second union lawful.
  • Letter 77 describes the discipline in practice at Rome: public penance in the ranks of the penitents before bishop, presbyters and people, and restoration to communion after the second husband’s death. Jerome is also a witness on the perpetual virginity of Mary and infant baptism timelines.
