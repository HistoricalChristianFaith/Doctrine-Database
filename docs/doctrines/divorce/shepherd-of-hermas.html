The Shepherd of Hermas on divorce

Dates: c. 140 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Shepherd gives the earliest post-apostolic reading of Jesus’ divorce teaching, and it lands in a striking place: the husband who catches his wife in unrepented adultery must put her away — to keep living with her is to “share” her sin — but he may not remarry. He must remain single, holding the door open for her repentance and return. Divorce for adultery is thus made all but obligatory for the innocent party, yet it dissolves nothing: remarriage after it is itself adultery, and man and woman are bound to the same rule.

Claims & evidence

The innocent husband must put away an unrepentant adulteress — but not remarry

Hermas puts the case to the angelic Shepherd directly: if a man detects his believing wife in adultery, does he sin by continuing to live with her? The answer distinguishes knowledge from ignorance, and repentance from persistence.

As long as he remains ignorant of her sin, the husband commits no transgression in living with her. But if the husband know that his wife has gone astray, and if the woman does not repent, but persists in her fornication, and yet the husband continues to live with her, he also is guilty of her crime, and a sharer in her adultery… The husband should put her away, and remain by himself. But if he put his wife away and marry another, he also commits adultery.

— Hermas, The Shepherd, Commandment (Mandate) 4.1, ANF vol. 2. Two rulings are fused here. Separation from a persistently adulterous spouse is not merely permitted but required — to stay is to become a partner in the sin. Yet the same breath forbids the innocent man to remarry: put her away, but “remain by yourself.” This is exactly the shape that would divide the later church — the exception clause of Matthew 5:32 and 19:9 read as licensing separation, not a second marriage.

The put-away wife may repent and return; so the husband must stay single — man and woman alike

The reason the innocent party may not remarry is not merely that the bond survives, but that a door must be kept open: the divorced wife may repent, and if she does, her husband is obliged to take her back.

If the husband do not take her back, he sins, and brings a great sin upon himself; for he ought to take back the sinner who has repented. But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God. In case, therefore, that the divorced wife may repent, the husband ought not to marry another, when his wife has been put away. In this matter man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.

— Hermas, The Shepherd, Commandment (Mandate) 4.1, ANF vol. 2. The single-repentance framing ties divorce to the Shepherd’s larger theme (one post-baptismal repentance for the servant of God), but the ruling on marriage is what matters for the timeline: a second marriage would foreclose the reconciliation the gospel demands, so the innocent must wait. And the reciprocity is explicit — the wronged wife owes the same forbearance to a repentant husband. There is no double standard of the kind Roman law allowed.

Notes

  • This is the divorce facet of Hermas — put-away-but-do-not-remarry. The same Commandment 4 also answers a different question a few lines later (may a widow or widower remarry? — “There is no sin in marrying again”), which is treated on the remarriage timeline. Keep the two apart: Hermas permits remarriage after a spouse’s death while forbidding it after divorce for adultery.
  • Hermas already forbids remarriage to the innocent party — the one Matthew’s exception is usually thought to protect. That the earliest post-apostolic witness reads the exception as permitting separation but not a new marriage is a datum for the later argument over whether the exception clause licenses remarriage at all: the “separation-only” reading is not a late Latin invention but the oldest attested one.
  • The severity is one-directional. Where later Latin writers (Tertullian, Augustine) would build a doctrine of the bond’s indissolubility, Hermas reaches a nearly identical practical rule from a different motive — not the metaphysics of “one flesh” but the pastoral demand that the door to repentance stay open.
