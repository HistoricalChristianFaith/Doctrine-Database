The Shepherd of Hermas on divorce

Dates: c. 140 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Shepherd is among the earliest extant post-apostolic texts to apply Jesus’ divorce teaching. In it the husband who knows his wife persists in unrepented adultery must put her away — to keep living with her is to “share” her sin — but he may not remarry. He is to remain single so that she may repent and return. A second marriage after such a separation is called adultery, and man and woman are bound to the same rule.

Claims & evidence

The husband of an unrepentant adulteress is to put her away and not remarry

Hermas puts the case to the angelic Shepherd directly: if a man detects his believing wife in adultery, does he sin by continuing to live with her? The answer distinguishes knowledge from ignorance, and repentance from persistence.

As long as he remains ignorant of her sin, the husband commits no transgression in living with her. But if the husband know that his wife has gone astray, and if the woman does not repent, but persists in her fornication, and yet the husband continues to live with her, he also is guilty of her crime, and a sharer in her adultery… The husband should put her away, and remain by himself. But if he put his wife away and marry another, he also commits adultery.

— Hermas, The Shepherd, Commandment (Mandate) 4.1, ANF vol. 2. The passage contains two rulings. Separation from a persistently adulterous spouse is required — to stay is to become a “sharer” in the sin. The same sentence forbids the husband to remarry: he is to “put her away, and remain by himself.” Hermas does not quote the exception clause of Matthew 5:32 and 19:9; his ruling allows separation for adultery and forbids a second marriage.

The put-away wife may repent and return; the same rule for man and woman

The reason Hermas gives for forbidding the husband a second marriage is that the divorced wife may repent, and if she does, her husband is obliged to take her back.

If the husband do not take her back, he sins, and brings a great sin upon himself; for he ought to take back the sinner who has repented. But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God. In case, therefore, that the divorced wife may repent, the husband ought not to marry another, when his wife has been put away. In this matter man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.

— Hermas, The Shepherd, Commandment (Mandate) 4.1, ANF vol. 2. The ruling is tied to the Shepherd’s larger theme of a single post-baptismal repentance (“there is but one repentance to the servants of God”): a second marriage would foreclose the wife’s return, so the husband is to wait. The rule is stated for both sexes: “In this matter man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.”

Notes

  • This is the divorce facet of Hermas — put away, but do not remarry. The same Commandment 4 also answers a different question a few lines later (may a widow or widower remarry? — “There is no sin in marrying again”), which is treated on the remarriage timeline. Hermas permits remarriage after a spouse’s death and forbids it after divorce for adultery.
  • Hermas forbids remarriage to the spouse who has not committed adultery. The passage bears on the later dispute over whether Matthew’s exception clause licenses remarriage, recorded under the timeline’s Disputed readings.
  • Hermas grounds the rule in keeping the way open for the wife’s repentance and return, as quoted above, rather than in an argument from “one flesh.”
