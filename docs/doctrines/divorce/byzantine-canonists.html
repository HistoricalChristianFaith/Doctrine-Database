The Byzantine canonists (Zonaras and Balsamon) on divorce

Dates: c. 1130–1195 — John Zonaras (canonical commentaries dated to the mid-twelfth century) · Wikipedia; Theodore Balsamon, deacon and nomophylax of Hagia Sophia, later titular Patriarch of Antioch, whose great commentary on the Nomocanon in 14 Titles was written c. 1177 · Wikipedia
Summary: The two great commentators on the canons of the Eastern church. Both hold the strict verdict the whole timeline shares — the woman who leaves her husband and marries another is an adulteress (Basil’s canon 9, canonized ecumenically at Trullo, canon 87) — but they gloss it, as Byzantine churchmen, through the civil law of the Christian empire, reading Basil’s canon alongside Justinian’s divorce legislation (Novel 117 = Basilika 28.7). Zonaras (writing first, and read as the stricter of the two) treats Basil’s double standard as the “custom at that time” and notes that the imperial law does grant a wife grounds to divorce an adulterous husband; Balsamon (the official voice, writing under imperial and patriarchal commission) goes further, holding that Justinian’s Novel 117 “all but transformed everything in such a canon.” This subordination of the canon to imperial law is the doctrinal bridge from the strict canons to the developed Byzantine practice of dissolution and remarriage by pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

Claims & evidence

The canon they gloss: Basil’s canon 9, canonized at Trullo (canon 87) — the woman who remarries is an adulteress, on “custom”

The commentary of both men on divorce sits on Basil of Caesarea’s ninth canon (from his canonical Letter 188 to Amphilochius), which the Council in Trullo made binding law and restated as its own canon 87. Basil grants that the Lord’s rule binds the sexes equally, then admits at once that the church’s practice does not:

The sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication, applies, according to the argument, to men and women alike. Custom, however, does not so obtain… the man who has been abandoned is pardonable, and the woman who lives with such a man is not condemned.

— Basil, Letter 188 (to Amphilochius), canon 9, NPNF2 vol. 8 (New Advent). Trullo canon 87 canonizes exactly this: “She who has left her husband is an adulteress if she has come to another, according to the holy and divine Basil…”1 It is on this canon — the double standard Basil himself traced not to the Gospel but to “custom” (synētheia) — that Zonaras and Balsamon write the twelfth-century East’s authoritative gloss.

Zonaras: the canon is the “custom at that time,” and the imperial Novel grants the wife a ground

John Zonaras, a retired imperial official who wrote his canonical commentaries as a monk, reads the canons with an eye to akribeia (strictness), but frankly relativizes Basil’s asymmetry as the practice of an earlier age and sets it beside the later civil law, which does allow a wife to dissolve her marriage for her husband’s open adultery:

But this great Father [Basil] states that these things prevail according to the ecclesiastical custom at that time. And from the Novel of the emperor Justinian promulgated later concerning the dissolution of marriage, which is situated in book 28, title 7 of the Basilika, the following is reckoned amongst the causes by which it is permissible for women to dissolve marriage; if the husband lies with another woman in the same house or city, and after being warned on the part of the wife, he does not desist from sexual intercourse with that woman, it is permitted for wives to dissolve marriage on account of jealousy.

— John Zonaras, commentary on Basil, canon 9 (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma, vol. IV), trans. Patrick Viscuso, as reproduced online. Even the stricter commentator, then, holds Basil’s harsher rule for the wife to be time-bound custom, and reads it through the imperial law that had since (in Justinian’s Novel 117) given the wife an enumerated ground to divorce an unchaste husband — the ground Basil had denied her.2

Balsamon: Justinian’s Novel 117 “all but transformed everything in such a canon”

Theodore Balsamon — the official canonist of the age, who wrote his commentary c. 1177 under commission from Emperor Manuel I and the patriarch, and whose method was to harmonize the canons with the imperial law he served — states the subordination of Basil’s canon to the civil legislation at its sharpest. Asked what should be done when a spouse remarries or fornicates, Basil, he says, answered from the writings and custom of his own day; but the emperor’s law has since remade the whole matter:

The saint [Basil], after being asked what ought to happen to the spouses if one of them might enter another marriage or even fornicate, made a reply from various writings and the custom held at that time. However, since Novel 117 of Justinian, situated in title 7 of book 28, all but transformed everything in such a canon, read ye this, and likewise Novel 111.

— Theodore Balsamon, commentary on Basil, canon 9 (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma, vol. IV, p. 122), trans. Patrick Viscuso, as reproduced online. This is Balsamon’s signature move — reading the strict canon through Justinian’s divorce law (Novel 117, embedded in the Basilika at 28.7) rather than against it — and it is the direct doctrinal bridge from the canons of Basil and Trullo to the settled Byzantine practice in which the grounds, procedure, and penalties of divorce and remarriage were fixed by the civil law and administered by the church under pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).3

The hard case of the absent husband: Trullo canon 93 and the “grass widow”

The canon the East applied to the everyday pastoral crisis — the wife of a husband gone missing or away at war who marries again — is Trullo’s canon 93, which the canonists also glossed. It keeps the strict rule (a premature second union is adultery) but softens it with excuse and pardon where the wife acted in ignorance of the first husband’s fate:

If the wife of a man who has gone away and does not appear, cohabit with another before she is assured of the death of the first, she is an adulteress… [yet the soldier’s wife] has some excuse… because the suspicion of death becomes very great… [and the returning husband] may receive his own wife back again, pardon being extended in consideration of their ignorance both to her and to the man who married her in the interim.

— Council in Trullo (692), canon 93, NPNF2 vol. 14 (New Advent). The graded penance and weighed culpability of these canons — the machinery Trullo built — is the seedbed from which the developed Byzantine leniency grew, and which Zonaras and Balsamon, reading it alongside imperial law, transmit to the later tradition.4

Notes

  • Which of the two is “strict”? The handbook contrast — Zonaras the rigorist, Balsamon the accommodator — holds in tendency but not absolutely. Zonaras wrote first, as a monk, and is generally the closer reader of the canon’s letter; Balsamon, the serving nomophylax and later patriarch, wrote to harmonize canon and imperial law and “sometimes copied [Zonaras] verbatim.” On divorce both alike subordinate Basil’s canon to Justinian’s Novel 117; the difference is one of emphasis, Balsamon stating the civil law’s priority more baldly.5
  • Dating Zonaras. His life-dates are given by some references as c. 1070–c. 1140, but his canonical commentaries are usually dated after 1159, so his floruit as a canonist falls in the mid-twelfth century, a generation before Balsamon — hence the combined heading’s c. 1130 anchor. Balsamon (b. c. 1130/40, d. after 1195) wrote the great Nomocanon commentary c. 1177.
  • Relation to the Latin canonists. The same twelfth century that produced Gratian and Peter Lombard in the West produced Zonaras and Balsamon in the East — but the two pairs pull opposite ways from the shared strict inheritance. The Latin canonists harmonize their permissive and strict authorities toward an indissolubility that would harden into the dogma of Trent; the Byzantine canonists read the same strict canons through the empire’s civil law toward the oikonomia that permits a bounded remarriage. Whether that Eastern relaxation is a faithful development or a capitulation is weighed on the oikonomia argument page (assessment: contested).
  • The two headline glosses are Patrick Viscuso’s English translations of the Greek scholia printed beneath Basil’s canon 9 in the standard critical edition (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma tōn theiōn kai hierōn kanonōn, vol. IV), here cited from an online reproduction; the substance — both canonists directing the reader to Justinian’s Novel 117 / Basilika 28.7 — is independently confirmed by the Greek of the edition and by modern scholarship on Balsamon’s canon 9 commentary.
