The Byzantine canonists (Zonaras and Balsamon) on divorce

Dates: c. 1130–1195 — John Zonaras (canonical commentaries dated to the mid-twelfth century) · Wikipedia; Theodore Balsamon, deacon and nomophylax of Hagia Sophia, later titular Patriarch of Antioch, whose great commentary on the Nomocanon in 14 Titles was written c. 1177 · Wikipedia
Summary: Two twelfth-century commentators on the canons of the Eastern church. Both comment on the rule that the woman who leaves her husband and marries another is an adulteress (Basil’s canon 9, adopted at Trullo, canon 87) — but they gloss it, as Byzantine churchmen, through the civil law of the Christian empire, reading Basil’s canon alongside Justinian’s divorce legislation (Novel 117 = Basilika 28.7). Zonaras (writing first) describes Basil’s double standard as the “custom at that time” and notes that the imperial law grants a wife grounds to divorce an adulterous husband; Balsamon (writing under imperial and patriarchal sanction) holds that Justinian’s Novel 117 “all but transformed everything in such a canon.”

Claims & evidence

The canon they gloss: Basil’s canon 9 and Trullo canon 87

The commentary of both men on divorce sits on Basil of Caesarea’s ninth canon (from his canonical Letter 188 to Amphilochius), which the Council in Trullo made binding law and restated as its own canon 87. Basil states that the Lord’s sentence applies to both sexes, and that custom does not so obtain:

The sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication, applies, according to the argument, to men and women alike. Custom, however, does not so obtain… the man who has been abandoned is pardonable, and the woman who lives with such a man is not condemned.

— Basil, Letter 188 (to Amphilochius), canon 9, NPNF2 vol. 8 (New Advent). Trullo canon 87 states: “She who has left her husband is an adulteress if she has come to another, according to the holy and divine Basil…”1 Zonaras and Balsamon comment on this canon, in which Basil refers the different treatment of husband and wife to “custom” (synētheia).

Zonaras: the “custom at that time” and the imperial Novel

John Zonaras, a retired imperial official who wrote his canonical commentaries as a monk, describes Basil’s rule as the custom of Basil’s time and sets it beside the later civil law, which allows a wife to dissolve her marriage for her husband’s open adultery:

But this great Father [Basil] states that these things prevail according to the ecclesiastical custom at that time. And from the Novel of the emperor Justinian promulgated later concerning the dissolution of marriage, which is situated in book 28, title 7 of the Basilika, the following is reckoned amongst the causes by which it is permissible for women to dissolve marriage; if the husband lies with another woman in the same house or city, and after being warned on the part of the wife, he does not desist from sexual intercourse with that woman, it is permitted for wives to dissolve marriage on account of jealousy.

— John Zonaras, commentary on Basil, canon 9 (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma, vol. IV), trans. Patrick Viscuso, as reproduced online. Zonaras reads Basil’s rule for the wife as the custom of Basil’s day, beside the imperial law (Justinian’s Novel 117) that gives the wife an enumerated ground to divorce an unchaste husband, a ground Basil’s canon 9 did not allow her.2

Balsamon: Justinian’s Novel 117 “all but transformed everything in such a canon”

Theodore Balsamon, nomophylax of Hagia Sophia, wrote his commentary c. 1177 under commission from Emperor Manuel I and the patriarch; Maroula Perisanidi (2016) describes his method as harmonizing the canons with imperial law.3 Basil, he says, answered the question of a spouse who remarries or fornicates from the writings and custom of his own day; the emperor’s law has since changed the matter:

The saint [Basil], after being asked what ought to happen to the spouses if one of them might enter another marriage or even fornicate, made a reply from various writings and the custom held at that time. However, since Novel 117 of Justinian, situated in title 7 of book 28, all but transformed everything in such a canon, read ye this, and likewise Novel 111.

— Theodore Balsamon, commentary on Basil, canon 9 (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma, vol. IV, p. 122), trans. Patrick Viscuso, as reproduced online. Balsamon reads the canon of Basil and Trullo through Justinian’s divorce law (Novel 117, embedded in the Basilika at 28.7), and points the reader to Novel 111 as well.4

The absent husband: Trullo canon 93

Trullo’s canon 93, which the canonists also glossed, addresses the wife of a husband gone missing or away at war who marries again. It calls a second union before she is assured of his death adultery, and extends “excuse” and “pardon” where the wife acted in ignorance of the first husband’s fate:

If the wife of a man who has gone away and does not appear, cohabit with another before she is assured of the death of the first, she is an adulteress… [yet the soldier’s wife] has some excuse… because the suspicion of death becomes very great… [and the returning husband] may receive his own wife back again, pardon being extended in consideration of their ignorance both to her and to the man who married her in the interim.

— Council in Trullo (692), canon 93, NPNF2 vol. 14 (New Advent). The canon distinguishes degrees of culpability and extends pardon; it is treated in full on the Council in Trullo page.5

Context

  • The two commentators. Zonaras wrote first, as a monk; Balsamon, the serving nomophylax and later patriarch, wrote to harmonize canon and imperial law and, in Perisanidi’s account, “sometimes copied [Zonaras] verbatim.” On divorce both direct the reader of Basil’s canon 9 to Justinian’s Novel 117.3
  • Dating Zonaras. His life-dates are given by some references as c. 1070–c. 1140, but his canonical commentaries are usually dated after 1159, so his floruit as a canonist falls in the mid-twelfth century, a generation before Balsamon — hence the combined heading’s c. 1130 anchor. Balsamon (b. c. 1130/40, d. after 1195) wrote the great Nomocanon commentary c. 1177.
  • The Latin canonists. The same twelfth century saw the work of Gratian and Peter Lombard in the West; their treatment of divorce is on their own page.
  • The two headline glosses are Patrick Viscuso’s English translations of the Greek scholia printed beneath Basil’s canon 9 in the standard critical edition (Rhalles–Potles, Syntagma tōn theiōn kai hierōn kanonōn, vol. IV), here cited from an online reproduction; the Pemptousia article cited at note 4 gives the Greek of the canon and the same locus for Balsamon’s reference to Novel 117 / Basilika 28.7.
