Clement of Alexandria on divorce

Dates: c. 200 (the Stromata) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the chapter “On Marriage” that closes the second book of the Stromata, Clement makes the divorce sayings turn on a single principle: “Scripture… allows no release from the union.” He is the first witness on this timeline to quote Matthew’s exception clause (“except for the cause of fornication”) — and he reads it as licensing separation from an adulterous spouse, not remarriage: a new marriage “while the other is alive” is itself the fornication the Lord condemns. So the earliest reader to preserve the exception is also an early, explicit witness against its permitting remarriage.

Claims & evidence

“Scripture… allows no release from the union” — the exception licenses separation, not remarriage

Chapter 23 of Stromata II argues that marriage is a “sacred image” to be kept pure, and that the Mosaic law and the Gospel speak with one voice on it. There Clement quotes both halves of the divorce teaching — Matthew’s clause on the husband who puts his wife away, and the saying on the man who marries a divorced woman — and glosses what “fornication” means in the exception.

Now that the Scripture counsels marriage, and allows no release from the union, is expressly contained in the law, “Thou shalt not put away thy wife, except for the cause of fornication;” and it regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive… “He that taketh a woman that has been put away,” it is said, “committeth adultery; and if one puts away his wife, he makes her an adulteress,” that is, compels her to commit adultery. And not only is he who puts her away guilty of this, but he who takes her, by giving to the woman the opportunity of sinning; for did he not take her, she would return to her husband.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata II.23 (“On Marriage”), ANF vol. 2. The reasoning runs in three moves. First, the governing rule: Scripture “allows no release from the union” — the bond does not dissolve. Second, Clement cites the exception itself — “except for the cause of fornication” (a paraphrase of Matthew 5:32 / 19:9) — the first citation of it on this timeline. Third, and decisively, he defines what “fornication” covers: it “regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.” So the clause that appears to open a door Clement reads as bolting another: a spouse may be sent away for adultery, but a remarriage while the first partner still lives is itself the fornication being condemned. The second man is as guilty as the divorcing husband, “for did he not take her, she would return to her husband” — the reconciliation-must-stay-open logic that the Shepherd of Hermas had already made the ground of the same ruling.

Notes

  • Clement is the first witness here to quote Matthew’s exception. The Shepherd (c. 140), Justin (c. 155), and Athenagoras (c. 177) all gave the divorce saying in its bare, exceptionless form. Clement supplies “except for the cause of fornication” — and yet lands in the same place as his predecessors, because he reads the exception as permitting the putting-away, not a new marriage. This is a pointed datum for the argument over whether the exception licenses remarriage: the earliest reader to preserve the clause is also an explicit early witness that it does not free either party to marry again.
  • “It regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive” is the load-bearing sentence. Clement is not saying only porneia (sexual immorality in the first marriage) justifies divorce; he is saying that a second union contracted while the first spouse lives counts as fornication — so the very act the exception seems to permit (remarry after a lawful divorce) is folded back under the sin it was supposed to escape. On this reading the exception and the exceptionless Markan/Lukan sayings (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18) agree.
  • Clement insists “the law is not at variance with the Gospel, but agrees with it——one Lord being the author of both.” The Mosaic penalties for adultery (death, or fire for a priest’s daughter) and the Gospel’s ban on remarriage are for him a single divine intention checking the passions. His interest, unlike the earlier apologists’, is not to prove Christian chastity to a pagan court but to harmonize the two Testaments against the encratite and libertine misreadings he combats through Books II–III of the Stromata.
