Clement of Alexandria on divorce

Dates: c. 200 (the Stromata) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the chapter “On Marriage” that closes the second book of the Stromata, Clement makes the divorce sayings turn on a single principle: “Scripture… allows no release from the union.” He is the earliest extant witness on this timeline to quote Matthew’s exception clause (“except for the cause of fornication”), and in the same sentence he states that Scripture “regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.”

Claims & evidence

“Scripture… allows no release from the union” (Stromata II.23)

Chapter 23 of Stromata II argues that marriage is a “sacred image” to be kept pure, and that the Mosaic law and the Gospel speak with one voice on it. There Clement quotes both halves of the divorce teaching — Matthew’s clause on the husband who puts his wife away, and the saying on the man who marries a divorced woman — and glosses what “fornication” means in the exception.

Now that the Scripture counsels marriage, and allows no release from the union, is expressly contained in the law, “Thou shalt not put away thy wife, except for the cause of fornication;” and it regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive… “He that taketh a woman that has been put away,” it is said, “committeth adultery; and if one puts away his wife, he makes her an adulteress,” that is, compels her to commit adultery. And not only is he who puts her away guilty of this, but he who takes her, by giving to the woman the opportunity of sinning; for did he not take her, she would return to her husband.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata II.23 (“On Marriage”), ANF vol. 2. The passage has three parts. First, the rule: Scripture “allows no release from the union.” Second, Clement cites the exception — “except for the cause of fornication” (a paraphrase of Matthew 5:32 / 19:9) — the earliest extant citation of it on this timeline. Third, he states what the law “regards as fornication”: “the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.” On this account a spouse may be put away for fornication, while a remarriage during the first partner’s lifetime is itself fornication. The second man shares the guilt of the divorcing husband, “for did he not take her, she would return to her husband” — the reason the Shepherd of Hermas had also given, that the way back to reconciliation must stay open.

Context

  • Clement is the earliest extant witness here to quote Matthew’s exception. The Shepherd (c. 140), Justin (c. 155), and Athenagoras (c. 177) all gave the divorce saying in its bare, exceptionless form. Clement quotes “except for the cause of fornication” and, in the same passage, treats a new marriage while the other spouse lives as fornication.
  • In the sentence “it regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive,” Clement applies the word “fornication” to a second union contracted while the first spouse lives, as well as to the cause for which a spouse may be put away. On this reading the exception and the exceptionless Markan and Lukan sayings (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18) agree. How the exception clause has been read is set out under Disputed readings on the timeline.
  • Clement insists “the law is not at variance with the Gospel, but agrees with it——one Lord being the author of both.” The Mosaic penalties for adultery (death, or fire for a priest’s daughter) and the Gospel’s ban on remarriage are for him a single divine intention checking the passions. The earlier apologists on this timeline address a pagan court; Clement’s chapter harmonizes the two Testaments, in a work whose Books II–III answer encratite and libertine readings of marriage.
