Justin Martyr on divorce

Dates: c. 155 · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing to the emperor from Rome, Justin lists Jesus’ divorce saying among the precepts of Christian chastity, in a form without an exception: “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.” He glosses it as condemning remarriage: those who by Roman law take a second spouse after divorce are, “in the eye of our Master,” sinners. In the Second Apology he narrates a case — a Christian wife at Rome who gave her husband a bill of divorce rather than “become a partaker” in his vices — an early datable report of a Christian divorcing, which ended in the execution of her teacher and two others.

Claims & evidence

The divorce saying as a precept of chastity: to marry a divorced woman is adultery

In the First Apology (c. 155), presenting Christ’s ethical teaching to Antoninus Pius, Justin strings together the Sermon-on-the-Mount sayings on chastity and includes the divorce logion — only the second half, the clause Matthew and Luke share (whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery), with no “except for fornication” attached.

Concerning chastity, He uttered such sentiments as these: “Whosoever looketh upon a woman to lust after her, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart before God.” And, “If thy right eye offend thee, cut it out; for it is better for thee to enter into the kingdom of heaven with one eye, than, having two eyes, to be cast into everlasting fire.” And, “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.” … So that all who, by human law, are twice married, are in the eye of our Master sinners, and those who look upon a woman to lust after her.

— Justin, First Apology 15, ANF vol. 1. The phrase rendered “twice married” is digamia, and the ANF editor notes that Justin here means not widow-remarriage nor bigamy but the middle case: taking a second spouse after divorce from the first. On that gloss Justin treats a new marriage after divorce as adultery for either party. He quotes the saying without an exception clause, alongside the prohibition of the lustful look, as a rule of Christian purity.

A Christian wife at Rome gives her husband a bill of divorce

The Second Apology opens with a case Justin says had “recently happened in your city.” A woman who had lived intemperately became a Christian, tried and failed to reform her husband, and — lest she share in his sins by sharing his bed — divorced him. The husband retaliated by denouncing her, and then her teacher, as Christians; three men were executed.

For she, considering it wicked to live any longer as a wife with a husband who sought in every way means of indulging in pleasure contrary to the law of nature, and in violation of what is right, wished to be divorced from him. … But when her husband had gone into Alexandria, and was reported to be conducting himself worse than ever, she—that she might not, by continuing in matrimonial connection with him, and by sharing his table and his bed, become a partaker also in his wickednesses and impieties—gave him what you call a bill of divorce, and was separated from him.

— Justin, Second Apology 2, ANF vol. 1. The report is an early datable notice of a Christian divorcing, and it is the wife who initiates it, on grounds of her husband’s persistent vice. The reason Justin reports — she divorces so as not to “become a partaker” in his impurities — parallels the “sharer in her adultery” language the Shepherd of Hermas uses for a husband who stays with an unrepentant adulterous wife. Justin records no remarriage on the woman’s part; his account concerns her separation and its consequences.

Notes

  • Justin cites only the “whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery” half of the logion — the portion common to Matthew 5:32, 19:9, and Luke 16:18 — and attaches no exception. Whether his silence about “except for fornication” reflects the text he knew or the point he was making is not stated in the text. The passage bears on the later dispute over whether the exception clause licenses remarriage, recorded under the timeline’s Disputed readings.
  • The First Apology states the rule (remarriage after divorce is adultery); the Second Apology reports a case of separation from a spouse’s vice, at Rome in the mid-second century.
  • The husband’s counter-move: unable to proceed against his wife (the emperor had granted her a delay to settle her affairs), he denounced her teacher Ptolemaeus, who confessed and was executed along with two others, Lucius and a third unnamed man. Justin presents the episode as an instance of the injustice the Apology protests.
