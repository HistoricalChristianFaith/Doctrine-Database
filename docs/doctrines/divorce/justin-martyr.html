Justin Martyr on divorce

Dates: c. 155 · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing to the emperor from Rome, Justin lists Jesus’ divorce saying among the precepts of Christian chastity — in its bare, exceptionless form: “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.” He glosses it as condemning remarriage: those who by Roman law take a second spouse after divorce are, “in the eye of our Master,” adulterers. In the Second Apology he then narrates a live case — a Christian wife at Rome who gave her debauched husband a bill of divorce rather than “become a partaker” in his vices — the earliest datable report of a Christian actually acting on this teaching, and the spark of a martyrdom.

Claims & evidence

The divorce saying is a precept of chastity: to marry a divorced woman is adultery

In the First Apology (c. 155), presenting Christ’s ethical teaching to Antoninus Pius, Justin strings together the Sermon-on-the-Mount sayings on chastity and includes the divorce logion — notably only the second half, the clause Matthew and Luke share (whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery), with no “except for fornication” attached.

Concerning chastity, He uttered such sentiments as these: “Whosoever looketh upon a woman to lust after her, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart before God.” And, “If thy right eye offend thee, cut it out; for it is better for thee to enter into the kingdom of heaven with one eye, than, having two eyes, to be cast into everlasting fire.” And, “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.” … So that all who, by human law, are twice married, are in the eye of our Master sinners, and those who look upon a woman to lust after her.

— Justin, First Apology 15, ANF vol. 1. The phrase rendered “twice married” is digamia, and the ANF editor notes that Justin here means not widow-remarriage nor bigamy but the middle case: taking a second spouse after divorce from the first. So Justin reads the saying exactly as its words run — a divorce does not free either party to remarry; a new marriage after it is adultery. He adds no exception clause even though he plainly knows the Matthean tradition, treating the saying as a plain rule of Christian purity alongside the prohibition of the lustful look.

A Christian wife at Rome acts on it — the bill of divorce that led to a martyrdom

The Second Apology opens with a case Justin says had “recently happened in your city.” A woman who had lived intemperately became a Christian, tried and failed to reform her still-debauched husband, and — lest she share in his sins by sharing his bed — divorced him. The husband retaliated by denouncing her, and then her teacher, as Christians; three men were executed.

For she, considering it wicked to live any longer as a wife with a husband who sought in every way means of indulging in pleasure contrary to the law of nature, and in violation of what is right, wished to be divorced from him. … But when her husband had gone into Alexandria, and was reported to be conducting himself worse than ever, she—that she might not, by continuing in matrimonial connection with him, and by sharing his table and his bed, become a partaker also in his wickednesses and impieties—gave him what you call a bill of divorce, and was separated from him.

— Justin, Second Apology 2, ANF vol. 1. This is social-historical gold: the earliest datable notice of a Christian in the act of divorcing, and it is the wife who initiates it, on grounds of her husband’s persistent vice. The reasoning Justin approves — she divorces so as not to “become a partaker” in his impurities — is the same “sharer in her sin” logic the Shepherd of Hermas had used to make separation from an unrepentant adulterer all but obligatory for the innocent party. Justin records no remarriage on the woman’s part, and his First Apology rule would forbid it; the point of the story is her separation and its cost, not a new union.

Notes

  • Justin cites only the “whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery” half of the logion — the portion common to Matthew 5:32, 19:9, and Luke 16:18 — and attaches no exception. Whether his silence about “except for fornication” reflects the text he knew or simply the point he was making is a datum for the argument over whether the exception licenses remarriage: like Hermas before him, Justin gives no hint that a validly divorced person may remarry.
  • The two works pull in one direction. The First Apology supplies the rule (remarriage after divorce is adultery); the Second Apology supplies a case of the rule’s underlying motive in action (separate rather than share a spouse’s sin). Together they show a mid-second-century Roman congregation that both taught the indissolubility-leaning reading and lived by it at real cost.
  • The husband’s counter-move — unable to punish his wife (the emperor had granted her a delay to settle her affairs), he denounced her teacher Ptolemaeus, who confessed and was executed along with two bystanders, Lucius and a third unnamed man. Justin frames the whole episode as a specimen of the injustice the Apology protests; for the divorce timeline it is the human backdrop to the doctrine, not part of the ruling itself.
