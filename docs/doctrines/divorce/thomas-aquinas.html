Thomas Aquinas on divorce

Dates: c. 1270 (the divorce questions of the Summa Theologiae Supplement, compiled after his death in 1274 from his earlier Commentary on the Sentences, Book IV) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas, the Dominican “Angelic Doctor,” gives Augustine’s doctrine of indissolubility its mature scholastic form — the conceptual apparatus the Council of Trent would inherit. He distinguishes the two senses in which a marriage can be “dissolved”: as to the act (separation from bed and board — divortium quoad thorum), which adultery licenses, from as to the tie (dissolution of the bond — divortium quoad vinculum), which is impossible for a ratified and consummated sacramental marriage between the baptized. He treats Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart permission removed by Christ, grounds the indissolubility of marriage in the natural law and in the marriage sacramentum, and handles the case of a convert whose unbelieving spouse departs (the later “Pauline privilege”) as a real dissolution of a non-sacramental union — the one exception that proves the rule. This is the precise scholastic settlement of the Latin doctrine that stood until the Reformers reopened the question.

Claims & evidence

The indissolubility of marriage is of the natural law (Supplement, q. 67, a. 1)

Aquinas opens his treatment of “the bill of divorce” by asking whether the indissolubility of marriage belongs to the natural law — and answers that it does, from the end of marriage itself (the lifelong rearing of offspring) and from Christ’s appeal to creation, “what God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” (Matthew 19:6).

By the intention of nature marriage is directed to the rearing of the offspring, not merely for a time, but throughout its whole life… Therefore, since the offspring is the common good of husband and wife, the dictate of the natural law requires the latter to live together for ever inseparably: and so the indissolubility of marriage is of natural law.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 67, a. 1, New Advent. Answering the objection that indissolubility, being tied to the sacrament, cannot be natural, he replies that it belongs to marriage on two counts — “in so far as the latter is a sign of the perpetual union of Christ with the Church, and in so far as it fulfills an office of nature that is directed to the good of the offspring” — the sacramental ground of Augustine now set within a full natural-law account.1

Fornication permits separation “as to the act,” but the marriage tie remains (Supplement, q. 62)

The whole of question 62 concerns fornication as “the impediment that supervenes to marriage after its consummation” — and Aquinas frames it at the outset as an impediment “to a previous marriage as regards the act, although the marriage tie remains.” So adultery gives the innocent party a right to separate, never to dissolve. He spells out the two grades of that separation:

A husband can put away his wife in two ways. First as to bed only, and thus he may put her away on his own judgment, as soon as he has evidence of her fornication… Secondly, as to bed and board, and in this way she cannot be put away except at the judgment of the Church… Now this putting away is called a divorce.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 62, a. 3, New Advent. This “divorce” (divortium quoad thorum, separation from bed and board) is what the strict tradition had always meant by the exception clause — the innocent spouse “is not bound to marital intercourse with the unfaithful one” — and Aquinas holds the innocent party is even bound to it if the adulterous spouse remains impenitent, “lest he seem to consent to her sin.”2

Nothing supervenient can dissolve the bond, so no remarriage while the other lives (Supplement, q. 62, a. 5)

The decisive ruling — and the point at which Aquinas simply systematizes Augustine. Asked whether a husband may marry again after a divorce for adultery, he denies it flatly, resting the whole answer on Augustine’s On Marriage and Concupiscence:

Nothing supervenient to marriage can dissolve it: wherefore adultery does not make a marriage cease to be valid. For, according to Augustine (De Nup. et Concup. i, 10), “as long as they live they are bound by the marriage tie, which neither divorce nor union with another can destroy.” Therefore it is unlawful for one, while the other lives, to marry again.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 62, a. 5, New Advent. And the bar binds the sexes alike: in a case of divorce husband and wife “are judged on a par with each other, in the sense that the same things are lawful or unlawful to the one as to the other” — the egalitarian standard of Jerome and Ambrose, against the double standard of Basil and Ambrosiaster.3

Moses’ bill of divorce was a hardness-of-heart permission, recalled under the New Law (Supplement, q. 67, a. 3)

Aquinas reads the Mosaic bill of divorcement exactly as the fathers had — not a positive right but a concession to hardness of heart, meant to forestall a worse evil (the murder of unwanted wives), and withdrawn by Christ. Against the objection that Moses’ permission cannot have been sinful, he answers with the Lord’s own words:

our Lord said (Mat. 19:8) that Moses granted the Jews the bill of divorce by reason of the hardness of their heart. But their hardness of heart did not excuse them from sin. Neither therefore did the law about the bill of divorce.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 67, a. 3, New Advent. He accordingly holds that when a divorced wife of the Old Law took another husband the older permission still stood, but “the saying of our Lord refers to the time of the New Law, when the aforesaid permission was recalled” — for under the gospel “the woman… whilst her husband liveth, is bound to the law of her husband” (Romans 7:2).4

The two ways a marriage is “dissolved,” and the Pauline privilege as a real dissolution of a non-sacramental tie (Supplement, q. 59)

The distinction that organizes the whole doctrine Aquinas states most sharply in the question on disparity of worship. A marriage may be “dissolved” in two senses, and the ratified sacramental bond can be dissolved in neither by adultery:

marriage is dissolved in two ways. In one way as to the marriage tie, and thus marriage cannot be dissolved after it is ratified, neither by unbelief nor by adultery. But if it be not ratified, the tie is dissolved, if the one party remain in unbelief, and the other being converted to the faith has married again… In another way marriage is dissolved as to the act, and thus it can be dissolved on account of either unbelief or fornication.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 59, a. 6, New Advent. So the one case in which the tie genuinely dissolves is not the adultery of the baptized but the departure of an unbeliever from a convert — a marriage that was never sacramental. When the unbeliever refuses to cohabit “without insult to the Creator” and strives to draw the convert into unbelief, “the believing husband after parting from her may be united to another in marriage” — the later “Pauline privilege” (1 Corinthians 7:15), handled by Aquinas as an exception that confirms, rather than breaches, the indissolubility of the ratified sacrament: “the firmer tie always looses the weaker if it is contrary to it.”5

Notes

  • Aquinas’ contribution is not a new verdict but a settled apparatus. The separation-not-remarriage reading was already the near-unanimous patristic line (Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, Chrysostom, Jerome) and its rationale was Augustine’s sacramentum. What Aquinas adds is the precise conceptual grid — ratum et consummatum, the quoad thorum/quoad vinculum distinction, and the Pauline privilege as the dissolution of a non-sacramental bond — the vocabulary in which the medieval canonists and the Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563) would fix the doctrine as dogma.
  • The Supplement to the Summa was not written by Aquinas himself: he died (1274) before completing the Tertia Pars, and his secretary Reginald of Piperno compiled the Supplement from Aquinas’ earlier Commentary on the Sentences (Book IV). The doctrine is Aquinas’ own; the arrangement into Summa questions is posthumous. This is why the timeline slots him at c. 1270 — the maturity of his thought — rather than at the date of the compiled text.
  • On one point of exegesis Aquinas follows an Augustine that Augustine himself retracted: the broad “spiritual fornication” reading of the exception clause, on which idolatry and unbelief could count as the “fornication” that grounds dismissal. That reading is weighed on the spiritual-fornication argument page (assessment: weak); Aquinas’ own settled position on the tie is unaffected by it.
  • The wiki’s Arguments section weighs the sub-questions Aquinas’ framework answers: whether the bond is truly indissoluble while both spouses live (contested), whether Matthew’s exception permits remarriage or only separation (contested), and whether Paul’s desertion ground — the Pauline privilege — frees the believer to remarry (contested). Aquinas takes the strict side of each, and it is his systematization that Roman canon law (the modern Code’s canon 1143 on the privilege) still carries.
