Thomas Aquinas on divorce

Dates: c. 1270 (the divorce questions of the Summa Theologiae Supplement, compiled after his death in 1274 from his earlier Commentary on the Sentences, Book IV) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas, Dominican friar and theologian, treats divorce in scholastic questions that draw on Augustine. He distinguishes two senses in which a marriage can be “dissolved”: as to the act (separation from bed, or from bed and board), which fornication permits, and as to the tie, which he holds cannot happen to a marriage once “ratified,” “neither by unbelief nor by adultery.” He treats Moses’ bill of divorce as a permission given for hardness of heart and “recalled” under the New Law, grounds the indissolubility of marriage in the natural law and in its being “a sign of the perpetual union of Christ with the Church,” and holds that where an unbelieving spouse will not live peaceably with a convert, the tie of a marriage not ratified is dissolved when the believer marries again — the case later called the “Pauline privilege.”

Claims & evidence

The indissolubility of marriage is of the natural law (Supplement, q. 67, a. 1)

Aquinas opens his treatment of “the bill of divorce” by asking whether the indissolubility of marriage belongs to the natural law — and answers that it does, from the end of marriage itself (the lifelong rearing of offspring) and from Christ’s appeal to creation, “what God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” (Matthew 19:6).

By the intention of nature marriage is directed to the rearing of the offspring, not merely for a time, but throughout its whole life… Therefore, since the offspring is the common good of husband and wife, the dictate of the natural law requires the latter to live together for ever inseparably: and so the indissolubility of marriage is of natural law.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 67, a. 1, New Advent. Answering the objection that indissolubility, being tied to the sacrament, cannot be natural, he replies that it belongs to marriage on two counts — “in so far as the latter is a sign of the perpetual union of Christ with the Church, and in so far as it fulfills an office of nature that is directed to the good of the offspring.”1

Fornication permits separation “as to the act”; the marriage tie remains (Supplement, q. 62)

Question 62 concerns fornication as “the impediment that supervenes to marriage after its consummation,” and Aquinas frames it at the outset as an impediment “to a previous marriage as regards the act, although the marriage tie remains.” Adultery gives the innocent party a right to separate. He sets out two grades of that separation:

A husband can put away his wife in two ways. First as to bed only, and thus he may put her away on his own judgment, as soon as he has evidence of her fornication… Secondly, as to bed and board, and in this way she cannot be put away except at the judgment of the Church… Now this putting away is called a divorce.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 62, a. 3, New Advent. Under this “divorce” (separation from bed and board, divortium quoad thorum) the innocent spouse “is not bound to marital intercourse with the unfaithful one,” and Aquinas holds the innocent party is even bound to separate if the adulterous spouse remains impenitent, “lest he seem to consent to her sin.”2

No remarriage while the other spouse lives (Supplement, q. 62, a. 5)

Asked whether a husband may marry again after a divorce for adultery, Aquinas answers no, resting the answer on Augustine’s On Marriage and Concupiscence:

Nothing supervenient to marriage can dissolve it: wherefore adultery does not make a marriage cease to be valid. For, according to Augustine (De Nup. et Concup. i, 10), “as long as they live they are bound by the marriage tie, which neither divorce nor union with another can destroy.” Therefore it is unlawful for one, while the other lives, to marry again.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 62, a. 5, New Advent. In a case of divorce, he holds (a. 4), husband and wife “are judged on a par with each other, in the sense that the same things are lawful or unlawful to the one as to the other: but they are not judged on a par with each other in reference to those things, since the reason for divorce is greater in one spouse than in the other.” Jerome and Ambrose also bind husband and wife to one rule; Basil and Ambrosiaster treat them differently (see their pages).3

Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart permission, recalled under the New Law (Supplement, q. 67, a. 3)

Aquinas reads the Mosaic bill of divorcement, as Tertullian, Origen and Jerome had, as a concession to hardness of heart, meant to forestall a worse evil (the murder of unwanted wives), and withdrawn by Christ. Against the objection that Moses’ permission cannot have been sinful, he cites the Lord’s words:

our Lord said (Mat. 19:8) that Moses granted the Jews the bill of divorce by reason of the hardness of their heart. But their hardness of heart did not excuse them from sin. Neither therefore did the law about the bill of divorce.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 67, a. 3, New Advent. He holds that when a divorced wife of the Old Law took another husband the older permission still stood, but “the saying of our Lord refers to the time of the New Law, when the aforesaid permission was recalled” — for under the gospel “the woman… whilst her husband liveth, is bound to the law of her husband” (Romans 7:2).4

The two ways a marriage is “dissolved,” and the convert whose unbelieving spouse departs (Supplement, q. 59)

Aquinas states the distinction in the question on disparity of worship:

marriage is dissolved in two ways. In one way as to the marriage tie, and thus marriage cannot be dissolved after it is ratified, neither by unbelief nor by adultery. But if it be not ratified, the tie is dissolved, if the one party remain in unbelief, and the other being converted to the faith has married again… In another way marriage is dissolved as to the act, and thus it can be dissolved on account of either unbelief or fornication.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 59, a. 6, New Advent. The case in which Aquinas holds the tie dissolves is not the adultery of the baptized but the departure of an unbeliever from a convert, in a marriage he does not count as ratified. When the unbeliever will not cohabit “without insult to the Creator” and strives to draw the convert into unbelief, “the believing husband after parting from her may be united to another in marriage” — the case later called the “Pauline privilege” (1 Corinthians 7:15). His reason: “the firmer tie always looses the weaker if it is contrary to it.”5

Notes

  • Aquinas’ questions draw on earlier witnesses who bar remarriage after divorce while the first spouse lives (Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, Chrysostom, Jerome) and quote Augustine directly. He sets the question out in terms of a marriage “ratified,” of separation “as to the act” and dissolution “as to the marriage tie,” and of the convert whose unbelieving spouse departs. The Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563) and the medieval canonists are recorded on their own pages.
  • The Supplement to the Summa was not written by Aquinas himself: he died (1274) before completing the Tertia Pars, and his secretary Reginald of Piperno compiled the Supplement from Aquinas’ earlier Commentary on the Sentences (Book IV) — the marriage treatise of Peter Lombard, a standard scholastic textbook. The doctrine is Aquinas’ own; the arrangement into Summa questions is posthumous. The timeline places him at c. 1270 rather than at the date of the compiled text.
  • Aquinas counts unbelief, “which is called spiritual fornication,” as “also a motive for divorce, yet not in the same way as bodily fornication”: divorce may be sought “on account of one act of carnal fornication, not, however, on account of one act of unbelief, but on account of inveterate unbelief” (Supplement, q. 62, a. 1, ad 3). Augustine had read the exception clause broadly in his early work and qualified that reading in the Retractations (see his page).6
  • The disputed questions Aquinas addresses — whether the bond remains while both spouses live, whether Matthew’s exception permits remarriage or separation only, and whether the convert deserted by an unbelieving spouse may remarry — are recorded on the timeline and its Disputed readings. The 1983 Code of Canon Law treats the Pauline privilege in canon 1143.
