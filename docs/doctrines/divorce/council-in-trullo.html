The Council in Trullo on divorce

Dates: 692 (the Quinisext Council — Concilium Quinisextum, “in Trullo” from the domed hall of the imperial palace at Constantinople where it met — convened by Justinian II to supply the disciplinary canons the fifth and sixth ecumenical councils had not issued) · Wikipedia
Summary: The council that gave the Eastern church its settled conciliar law on divorce and remarriage. Its canon 87 confirms, with ecumenical authority, the strict Basilian rule that whoever leaves a living spouse and marries another is an adulterer — the East does not license divorce-and-remarriage here. But the same canon, and still more canon 93 on the “grass widow” of a vanished or soldier husband, introduces the machinery the later Eastern discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) would build on: graded penance rather than permanent exclusion, and a careful sorting of culpable from excusable second unions, some of which draw “pardon” and are “not prevented from marrying.” Canon 98 brings the betrothed woman under the same adultery rule. (The council’s treatment of remarriage after bereavement — the twice-, thrice-, and four-times-married — is a distinct question, traced on the remarriage timeline.)

Claims & evidence

Canon 87: to leave a living spouse and marry another is adultery — but penance, not permanent exclusion

The council’s central divorce canon does not reason afresh; it invokes Basil — “the holy and divine Basil” — and through him the prophet Jeremiah, to lay down that a new marriage while the first spouse lives is adultery, and it fixes the same penance whether the offender is the wife who leaves or the husband who dismisses.

She who has left her husband is an adulteress if she has come to another, according to the holy and divine Basil, who has gathered this most excellently from the prophet Jeremiah… If therefore she appears to have departed from her husband without reason, he is deserving of pardon and she of punishment… But he who leaves the wife lawfully given him, and shall take another is guilty of adultery by the sentence of the Lord. And it has been decreed by our Fathers that they who are such must be weepers for a year, hearers for two years, prostrators for three years, and in the seventh year to stand with the faithful and thus be counted worthy of the Oblation.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 87.1 Two things sit side by side here. The verdict is strict and squarely on the indissolubilist side of this timeline: the bond survives the separation, so a second marriage while the first spouse lives is “adultery by the sentence of the Lord.” But the remedy is not the permanent excommunication of Elvira; it is the seven-year graded penance of the Eastern penitential stations — weeper, hearer, prostrator, then restoration to communion. Where Jerome and Augustine in the Latin West argued the rule from “one flesh” and the marriage sacramentum, Trullo simply codifies Basil’s canonical practice for the whole East — adultery named, but healed by penance rather than shut out “even when death approaches.”

Canon 93: the “grass widow” — the vanished husband, the soldier’s wife, and the excuse of ignorance

Canon 93 is where the seed of the later Eastern relaxation is plainest, because it grapples with the hard cases a bare rule cannot settle: the wife of a man who has simply disappeared, or gone to war and not returned. Strictly, if she remarries before she is sure he is dead, she is an adulteress — the bond still binds. Yet the canon at once grants that such a case “has some excuse,” and sorts the wives by culpability.

If the wife of a man who has gone away and does not appear, cohabit with another before she is assured of the death of the first, she is an adulteress. The wives of soldiers who have married husbands who do not appear are in the same case… But the circumstance here has some excuse, in that the suspicion of his death becomes very great. But she who in ignorance has married a man who at the time was deserted by his wife, and then is dismissed because his first wife returns to him, has indeed committed fornication, but through ignorance; therefore she is not prevented from marrying… If a soldier shall return after a long time, and find his wife on account of his long absence has been united to another man, if he so wishes, he may receive his own wife [back again], pardon being extended in consideration of their ignorance both to her and to the man who married her.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 93.2 The strict premise is never abandoned: the returning soldier “may receive his own wife” precisely because the first marriage was never dissolved by his absence. But the canon does three things the older Western law would not. It weighs subjective culpability — the woman who acted “in ignorance” has “committed fornication… but through ignorance,” and so “is not prevented from marrying.” It grades an “excuse” from the objective hardship (“the suspicion of his death becomes very great”). And it extends “pardon” even to the intervening man. This machinery — culpable versus excusable, pardon, tolerated remarriage for the deceived party — is the canonical raw material out of which the Byzantine practice of pastoral oikonomia would later be built.

Canon 98: the betrothed woman brought under the same rule

A short canon completes the pattern, closing the gap between betrothal and marriage: to take a woman betrothed to a still-living man is itself adultery, the betrothal already counting as a bond that a third party may not break.

He who brings to the intercourse of marriage a woman who is betrothed to another man who is still alive, is to lie under the charge of adultery.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 98.3 The canon treats a betrothal as already creating the exclusive claim that later remarriage law protects: the interloper “lies under the charge of adultery” exactly as the man who marries a divorced-but-still-bound wife does in canon 87. It rounds out Trullo’s adultery rule by pushing the bond’s protection back to the promise that precedes the wedding.

What Trullo settles — and what it does not

Read together, the three canons make Trullo the Eastern counterpart to the Western crystallization traced in Elvira, Jerome, and Augustine. The verdict is the same: leaving a living spouse to marry another is adultery, and the bond is not dissolved by the separation. What differs is the instrument and the temper. Rome, Trullo’s own canon 3 says elsewhere, keeps “the rule of exact perfection,” while Constantinople keeps “kindness and consideration,” blending both so “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness.” On divorce that temper shows as graded penance in place of permanent exclusion, and as the careful weighing of “excuse” and “ignorance” in canon 93. It is important to be exact about what this does not yet do: Trullo does not license divorce-and-remarriage. The canons still call the second union adultery; the returning soldier still reclaims a wife whose first marriage never ended. The full Byzantine practice of dissolving marriages by oikonomia and permitting a penanced remarriage develops afterward, in the imperial civil law and the later commentators, not in these canons. What Trullo supplies is the strict rule given ecumenical conciliar authority for the East, plus the pastoral machinery — graded penance, culpability-weighing, pardon — that the later relaxation would read as its warrant.

Notes

  • The two facets of Trullo. This page treats the council’s divorce canons (87, 93, 98 — leaving or losing a living spouse). Its handling of remarriage after bereavement — the bar on twice-married clergy in canon 3, and the graded first/second/third/fourth-marriage discipline that later hardened in the Tomos of Union (920) — is a distinct question, treated on the remarriage timeline. Canon 87 itself flags the divide: the council is careful to distinguish leaving a living spouse (adultery) from the second marriage of the widowed (deprecated, not adultery).
  • “According to the holy and divine Basil.” Canon 2 of Trullo formally ratified Basil’s canonical letters (among other patristic canons) as binding law for the whole church, which is why canon 87 can settle the divorce question simply by invoking him. The double standard Basil had traced to bare “custom” is thus carried into ecumenical canon law — though canon 87 states the penance for “she” and “he” in parallel, softening Basil’s asymmetry on the face of the rule.
  • The oikonomia seed, first noticed by Origen. The tolerant strand that Origen reported four centuries earlier — “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… in comparison with worse things” — and that Epiphanius defended as a “concession to weakness,” reaches in Trullo’s canon 93 its first conciliar expression as a weighing of excusable cases. The wiki does not overstate the step: Trullo weighs culpability and grants pardon; it does not yet declare a bond dissolved.
