The Council in Trullo on divorce

Dates: 692 (the Quinisext Council — Concilium Quinisextum, “in Trullo” from the domed hall of the imperial palace at Constantinople where it met — convened by Justinian II to supply the disciplinary canons the fifth and sixth ecumenical councils had not issued) · Wikipedia
Summary: The council’s canons on divorce and remarriage. Canon 87, invoking “the holy and divine Basil,” rules that she who leaves her husband and comes to another is an adulteress, and that he who leaves his lawful wife and takes another is “guilty of adultery by the sentence of the Lord,” with a seven-year graded penance. Canon 93, on the wife of a vanished or soldier husband, distinguishes culpable from excusable second unions, some of which draw “pardon” and are “not prevented from marrying.” Canon 98 brings the betrothed woman under the same adultery rule. (The council’s treatment of remarriage after bereavement — the twice-, thrice-, and four-times-married — is a distinct question, traced on the remarriage timeline.)

Claims & evidence

Canon 87: leaving a spouse and marrying another, and its penance

The council’s principal divorce canon invokes Basil — “the holy and divine Basil” — and through him the prophet Jeremiah, to lay down that the wife who leaves her husband and comes to another is an adulteress, and that the husband who leaves his lawful wife and takes another is guilty of adultery; for “they who are such” it fixes a graded penance.

She who has left her husband is an adulteress if she has come to another, according to the holy and divine Basil, who has gathered this most excellently from the prophet Jeremiah… If therefore she appears to have departed from her husband without reason, he is deserving of pardon and she of punishment… But he who leaves the wife lawfully given him, and shall take another is guilty of adultery by the sentence of the Lord. And it has been decreed by our Fathers that they who are such must be weepers for a year, hearers for two years, prostrators for three years, and in the seventh year to stand with the faithful and thus be counted worthy of the Oblation.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 87.1 The canon calls the second marriage “adultery by the sentence of the Lord” and prescribes the seven-year graded penance of the penitential stations — weeper, hearer, prostrator, then restoration to communion. The Council of Elvira had denied communion “even when death approaches” to a woman who left her husband and married another (see its page). Jerome and Augustine argued their rule from “one flesh” and the marriage sacramentum; Trullo’s canon rests on Basil’s canonical letters.

Canon 93: the vanished husband, the soldier’s wife, and the excuse of ignorance

Canon 93 takes up the wife of a man who has disappeared, or gone to war and not returned. If she remarries before she is sure he is dead, she is an adulteress; the canon then grants that such a case “has some excuse,” and distinguishes the parties by culpability.

If the wife of a man who has gone away and does not appear, cohabit with another before she is assured of the death of the first, she is an adulteress. The wives of soldiers who have married husbands who do not appear are in the same case… But the circumstance here has some excuse, in that the suspicion of his death becomes very great. But she who in ignorance has married a man who at the time was deserted by his wife, and then is dismissed because his first wife returns to him, has indeed committed fornication, but through ignorance; therefore she is not prevented from marrying… If a soldier shall return after a long time, and find his wife on account of his long absence has been united to another man, if he so wishes, he may receive his own wife [back again], pardon being extended in consideration of their ignorance both to her and to the man who married her.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 93.2 The returning soldier “may receive his own wife.” The canon takes account of subjective culpability — the woman who acted “in ignorance” has “committed fornication… but through ignorance,” and so “is not prevented from marrying”; it allows an “excuse” from hardship (“the suspicion of his death becomes very great”); and it extends “pardon” to the intervening man as well.

Canon 98: the betrothed woman

A short canon applies the charge of adultery to the man who marries a woman betrothed to a still-living man.

He who brings to the intercourse of marriage a woman who is betrothed to another man who is still alive, is to lie under the charge of adultery.

— Council in Trullo, Canon 98.3 The canon treats a betrothal as already creating an exclusive claim: the third party “lies under the charge of adultery.”

The canons read together

Across the three canons, a second union while a spouse (or betrothed) lives is called adultery, and the remedy is graded penance, with “excuse” and “pardon” for cases of ignorance. In its canon 3, on the second marriages of clergy, the council describes the Roman Church as keeping “the rule of exact perfection” and those under Constantinople as keeping “that of kindness and consideration,” blending both so “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness.”3 The later Byzantine canonists’ reading of these canons alongside imperial divorce law is on the Byzantine canonists page.

Context

  • The two facets of Trullo. This page treats the council’s divorce canons (87, 93, 98 — leaving or losing a living spouse). Its handling of remarriage after bereavement — the bar on twice-married clergy in canon 3, and the discipline of second, third and fourth marriages taken up in the Tomos of Union (920) — is a distinct question, treated on the remarriage timeline.
  • “According to the holy and divine Basil.” Canon 2 of Trullo ratified Basil’s canonical letters (among other patristic canons) as binding law, and canon 87 rules by invoking him. It follows Basil’s wording closely, including the “pardon” for the husband whose wife departs “without reason,” and states the case of the wife who leaves (“she”) and of the husband who leaves (“he”) in successive clauses.
  • Earlier reports of leniency. Four centuries earlier Origen had reported that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… in comparison with worse things,” and Epiphanius, in Frank Williams’s translation, had written that God’s word “tolerates” those who marry again after a divorce, a passage Lehmkuhl (1909) reads of a second marriage after the divorced wife’s death (see their pages). Canon 93 is the earliest conciliar text on this timeline to grant pardon to a second union contracted in ignorance.
