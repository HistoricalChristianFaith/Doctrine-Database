David Instone-Brewer on divorce

Dates: c. 2002 (Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible: The Social and Literary Context, Eerdmans, 2002; the popular summary “What God Has Joined” appeared in Christianity Today in October 2007) · Wikipedia
Summary: The modern anchor of this timeline — a rabbinics scholar who reads Jesus’ divorce sayings against their first-century Jewish legal setting and reorganizes the whole debate around it. Instone-Brewer, senior research fellow in rabbinics and the New Testament at Tyndale House, Cambridge, argues that the Pharisees’ “for any cause?” (Matthew 19:3) is a technical reference to the Hillelite “Any Cause” divorce, a then-recent innovation read out of Deuteronomy 24:1; that Jesus rejects that loophole and sides with the school of Shammai in restricting the Deut-24 ground to adultery; and that the other Old-Testament grounds of Exodus 21:10–11 (neglect of food, clothing, and marital love) remain in force, so that Jesus and Paul permit divorce for adultery, neglect, or abuse, with liberty to remarry for the innocent party.

Claims & evidence

The Pharisees’ “any cause” as a technical term for the Hillelite divorce

Instone-Brewer’s reframing turns on reading Matthew 19:3 not as a general question about grounds for divorce but as a request to take sides in the era’s most famous legal quarrel. He narrates the discovery in the first person:

One of my most dramatic findings concerns a question the Pharisees asked Jesus: “Is it lawful to divorce a wife for any cause?” This question reminded me that a few decades before Jesus, some rabbis (the Hillelites) had invented a new form of divorce called the “any cause” divorce.

— “What God Has Joined” (Christianity Today, 2007).1 The phrase “for any cause” (Greek kata pasan aitian), he argues, is not a loose paraphrase but the name of a specific procedure: by the generation before Jesus the school of Hillel had derived a second, near-limitless ground for divorce from the wording of Deuteronomy 24:1. Where Moses allowed a man to divorce a wife in whom he found “some uncleanness” (Hebrew ‘erwat dabar, “a matter of indecency”), the Hillelites split the phrase in two — reading “indecency” and “a matter” as two separate grounds:

They decided this extra word implied another ground for divorce—divorce for “a cause.” They argued that anything, including a burnt meal or wrinkles not there when you married your wife, could be a cause!

— ibid.2 The rival school of Shammai, by contrast, “said Moses’ words were a single phrase that referred to no type of divorce ‘except immorality.’” So the Pharisees’ “for any cause?” is a live technical question: do you, Jesus, endorse the Hillelite “Any Cause” divorce, or the Shammaite restriction?

Jesus rejects the loophole, not divorce as such

Read this way, Jesus’ “no” is aimed narrowly at the Hillelite innovation, not at every Old-Testament ground for divorce:

When Jesus answered with a resounding no, he wasn’t condemning “divorce for any cause,” but rather the newly invented “any cause” divorce.

— ibid.3 On this account Matthew’s exception clause — “except for fornication” (Matthew 19:9) — is not a puzzling Matthean addition but exactly the Shammaite position stated in the Shammaites’ own words (“except immorality”), which is why Mark and Luke, writing for readers who did not know the rabbinic shorthand, could abbreviate the saying without the clause: everyone in the original audience already knew the “Any Cause” debate was in view. This is the backbone of the wider Shammai–Hillel argument weighed on this doctrine’s Arguments section, and it bears directly on what porneia means and why Matthew alone carries the exception.

The forgotten grounds: Exodus 21:10–11 (neglect and abuse)

Instone-Brewer’s second and more distinctive move is that the Deuteronomy-24 debate was never the whole of the biblical law. A second divorce text, Exodus 21:10–11, guaranteed a wife — even a slave wife — three material rights, whose neglect freed her:

Exodus says that everyone, even a slave wife, had three rights within marriage—the rights to food, clothing, and love. If these were neglected, the wronged spouse had the right to seek freedom.

— ibid.4 Because this ground was uncontested in first-century Judaism — no school disputed it — it never surfaces in the Gospel exchanges, which concern only the disputed Deuteronomy-24 point; Jesus’ silence about the Exodus grounds, Instone-Brewer argues, is the silence of assumed agreement, not abolition. Paul then applies the same three rights when he speaks of a husband’s and wife’s conjugal duties and of the deserted believer who “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15): abandonment is the ultimate neglect of all three. His overall conclusion:

Divorce is only allowed for a limited number of grounds that are found in the Old Testament and affirmed in the New Testament: Adultery…Emotional and physical neglect…Abandonment and abuse.

— ibid.5 So the innocent party, validly divorced on any of these grounds, is free to remarry — the divorce dissolving the bond, as in the Erasmian line, but resting the case less on Matthew’s exception clause alone than on the whole Old-Testament–rabbinic frame of divorce law.

Method: reading the texts as a first-century Jew

Instone-Brewer presents the whole reconstruction as a matter of recovered context rather than novel doctrine — the texts read differently once the reader knows the legal vocabulary their first audience shared:

The texts hadn’t changed, but my knowledge of the language and culture in which they were written had. I was now reading them like a first-century Jew would have read them, and this time those confusing passages made more sense.

— ibid.6 The scholarly case is set out at length in Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible: The Social and Literary Context (Eerdmans, 2002), which surveys the ancient Near Eastern, Old Testament, Second Temple, and rabbinic evidence; the pastoral application follows in the companion Divorce and Remarriage in the Church: Biblical Solutions for Pastoral Realities (InterVarsity Press).7

Notes

  • Dating. This page is anchored to 2002, the year of the scholarly monograph that states the thesis in full; the widely read Christianity Today summary (from which the quotations above are drawn, as the freely available statement of the argument) appeared in 2007. Instone-Brewer is a living scholar, so the “date” here marks the publication that seats him on the timeline, not a lifespan.
  • Where he stands in the line. Instone-Brewer is a modern heir of the divorce-with-remarriage reading reopened by Erasmus and fixed by Luther and Calvin — the innocent party may remarry — but he reaches it by a different route and widens it: not from the exception clause construed in isolation, but from the first-century rabbinic frame (siding with Shammai against Hillel on Deuteronomy 24) plus the neglect/abuse grounds of Exodus 21, which the Reformers did not press. On the disputed Deut-24 ground his verdict (adultery only) coincides with the strict Shammaite–patristic reading; on remarriage and on the additional Exodus grounds he is broader than either camp.
  • Reception. The thesis drew sharp response — John Piper published a point-by-point rebuttal (“Tragically Widening the Grounds of Legitimate Divorce”), and Instone-Brewer replied in the Christianity Today weblog — the debate turning on whether the “Any Cause” reconstruction and the Exodus-21 grounds bear the weight he puts on them. That adjudication belongs to the Arguments section, not to this witness page, which records his position; the “Any Cause” siding-with-Shammai claim is weighed in full at Is Jesus siding with Shammai against Hillel’s “any cause” divorce? (assessment contested).
  • Where he over-presses. Two moves in the reconstruction outrun the evidence and should be flagged. (1) He treats “the Any Cause divorce” as a fixed technical name a first-century hearer would recognize, but the named Beit Shammai/Beit Hillel debate survives only in sources redacted c. AD 200 (Mishnah, Sifre); Philo and Josephus attest the permissive practice and the loose phrase “any cause” in Jesus’ own century, but not the title — so the crisp lexical claim is an inference, probable at most. (2) His premise that “all divorces then were Any Cause divorces” over-generalizes, letting every New Testament divorce word be read as targeting only the Hillelite procedure; it strains at Matthew 19:8 (“Moses permitted you to put away your wives”), where “divorce” cannot carry the narrowed sense. The frame is genuine; these strong forms are what the argument page marks down.
