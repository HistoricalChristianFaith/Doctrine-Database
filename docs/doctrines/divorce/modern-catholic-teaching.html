The modern Roman Catholic magisterium on divorce: Familiaris Consortio to Amoris Laetitia

Dates: 1981–2016 (John Paul II’s Familiaris Consortio, 1981; the revised Code of Canon Law, 1983; Francis’s Amoris Laetitia, 2016) · Wikipedia
Summary: After Trent the Roman Catholic Church never abandoned the dogma of indissolubility, and the modern law restates it in the sharpest terms — a ratified and consummated marriage “can be dissolved by no human power and by no cause, except death” (1983 Code, can. 1141). The genuinely live twentieth- and twenty-first-century development is not about the bond but about pastoral discipline: how the church treats the civilly-divorced-and-remarried, and whether they may receive communion. John Paul II’s Familiaris Consortio (1981) kept them within the church but barred them from the Eucharist unless they live “in complete continence”; Francis’s Amoris Laetitia (2016) opened a path of discernment that, on the Buenos Aires interpretation Francis endorsed as having “no other interpretations,” can admit some of them to the sacraments — igniting the dubia controversy over whether this breaks with Familiaris Consortio and Trent. The dogma of the indissoluble bond is untouched; the dispute is over the sacramental economy applied to those whose second union the church still calls objectively irregular — arguably the Latin West feeling its way, from the opposite direction, toward the pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) the East practiced all along.

Claims & evidence

The bond codified: “dissolved by no human power… except death” (1983 Code, can. 1141)

The current law of the Latin church restates the whole strict line the timeline has traced — from Clement and Augustine to Trent — in a single canon on the ratified and consummated (ratum et consummatum) sacramental marriage:

A marriage that is ratum et consummatum can be dissolved by no human power and by no cause, except death.

— Code of Canon Law (1983), can. 1141.1 The Code does recognize that some marriages can be dissolved — but only ones that fall short of that full sacramental completeness. Can. 1143 §1 codifies the “Pauline privilege” first stated in Latin by Ambrosiaster: a marriage of two unbaptized persons “is dissolved by means of the pauline privilege in favor of the faith of the party who has received baptism… provided that the non-baptized party departs,” and can. 1150 lays down that “in a doubtful matter the privilege of faith possesses the favor of the law.”2 So Rome dissolves non-sacramental unions (the Pauline and, by papal act, the “Petrine” privilege) precisely to protect the rule it will not touch: the consummated marriage of two baptized Christians, which nothing but death ends. This is the same limit Trent’s canon 6 marked with the unconsummated union — the exception that confirms the rule.

The divorced-and-remarried: within the church, but barred from the Eucharist (Familiaris Consortio §84, 1981)

The live pastoral question — what to do with Catholics who have divorced and, without an annulment, married again civilly — is set out by John Paul II in the 1981 apostolic exhortation Familiaris Consortio. Its §84 first refuses to write them off:

The Church, which was set up to lead to salvation all people and especially the baptized, cannot abandon to their own devices those who have been previously bound by sacramental marriage and who have attempted a second marriage. The Church will therefore make untiring efforts to put at their disposal her means of salvation.

— Familiaris Consortio §84.3 They are to share in the church’s life, listen to the word, attend Mass, and do works of charity — pastors are to treat them as a “merciful mother” would. But one door stays shut, on the ground that Augustine had made the bond a sign of Christ and the church:

However, the Church reaffirms her practice, which is based upon Sacred Scripture, of not admitting to Eucharistic Communion divorced persons who have remarried. They are unable to be admitted thereto from the fact that their state and condition of life objectively contradict that union of love between Christ and the Church which is signified and effected by the Eucharist.

— Familiaris Consortio §84.4 The one way back to the sacraments runs through continence. Reconciliation, and so the Eucharist, “can only be granted” to those ready to live no longer in contradiction to indissolubility — which means, where a couple cannot separate (for the children’s sake, say), that they “take on themselves the duty to live in complete continence, that is, by abstinence from the acts proper to married couples.”5 This “brother and sister” provision is the fixed discipline that the twenty-first-century debate would reopen.

The discernment path: “no new set of general rules” (Amoris Laetitia ch. 8, 2016)

Chapter 8 of Francis’s 2016 exhortation Amoris Laetitia (“The Joy of Love”), the fruit of the two Synods on the family, turns from a general rule to case-by-case discernment. It declines to issue a “new set of rules”:

…it is understandable that neither the Synod nor this Exhortation could be expected to provide a new set of general rules, canonical in nature and applicable to all cases. What is possible is simply a renewed encouragement to undertake a responsible personal and pastoral discernment of particular cases…

— Amoris Laetitia §300.6 Because subjective culpability can be diminished by circumstances, Francis presses that an objectively irregular state need not mean the loss of grace:

Hence it can no longer simply be said that all those in any “irregular” situation are living in a state of mortal sin and are deprived of sanctifying grace.

— Amoris Laetitia §301.7 The decisive move — and the one the whole controversy turns on — comes in §305 and its footnote 351. The body of §305 holds that a person “in an objective situation of sin… can be living in God’s grace… while receiving the Church’s help to this end,” and the footnote spells out what that help may include:

In certain cases, this can include the help of the sacraments. Hence, “I want to remind priests that the confessional must not be a torture chamber, but rather an encounter with the Lord’s mercy”… I would also point out that the Eucharist “is not a prize for the perfect, but a powerful medicine and nourishment for the weak.”

— Amoris Laetitia §305, note 351.8 Nowhere does the text say in so many words that the divorced-and-remarried may receive communion. Its silence is exactly what made it contested: the sacraments “in certain cases” can be read as a real relaxation of the Familiaris Consortio bar, or as fully compatible with it.

Francis’s authentic reading: the Buenos Aires guidelines, “no other interpretations”

Francis resolved the ambiguity in favour of the permissive reading. In September 2016 the bishops of the Buenos Aires pastoral region issued guidelines on applying chapter 8; their point 6 says that where limitations diminish responsibility, “Amoris Laetitia opens up the possibility of access to the sacraments of Reconciliation and the Eucharist.” Francis wrote back on 5 September 2016 that the document “is very good and completely explains the meaning of chapter VIII of Amoris Laetitia. There are no other interpretations” (“No hay otras interpretaciones”).9 In 2017 both the guidelines and the letter were entered in the Acta Apostolicae Sedis, the church’s official record, with a note that the pope willed them to carry the weight of “authentic Magisterium.” So the admission of some divorced-and-remarried Catholics to communion — under discernment, and still not as a general rule — became the officially endorsed reading of the exhortation.10

The dubia: does this break with Familiaris Consortio and Trent?

The reading did not pass unchallenged. On 19 September 2016 four cardinals — Walter Brandmüller, Raymond Burke, Carlo Caffarra, and Joachim Meisner — submitted five formal questions (dubia, “doubts”) to Francis, made public that November when no answer came. The first asks the whole question directly:

It is asked whether, following the affirmations of Amoris Laetitia (300-305), it has now become possible to grant absolution in the sacrament of penance and thus to admit to holy Communion a person who, while bound by a valid marital bond, lives together with a different person more uxorio without fulfilling the conditions provided for by Familiaris Consortio, 84… Can the expression “in certain cases” found in Note 351 (305) of the exhortation Amoris Laetitia be applied to divorced persons who are in a new union and who continue to live more uxorio?

— the first of the four cardinals’ dubia.11 The dubia frame the exact fault line: more uxorio — living “as spouses” — is precisely what Familiaris Consortio §84’s continence provision forbade for a return to the sacraments. Defenders of Francis, notably the philosopher Rocco Buttiglione, answer that Amoris Laetitia develops rather than contradicts John Paul II: it addresses cases of diminished subjective culpability that the older discipline did not squarely treat, so the bond’s indissolubility and the objective disorder of the second union both stand. Critics reply that admitting to communion a couple living more uxorio, without the continence Familiaris Consortio required, is a change in practice that the doctrine cannot bear. The dispute is over continuity, not over the dogma of the bond — which no party questions.12

Notes

  • What has and has not changed. No modern Catholic document alters the dogma the timeline reaches at Trent: the ratified and consummated sacramental marriage is indissoluble while both spouses live, and a purported second marriage is objectively adulterous. The whole modern development concerns pastoral discipline and access to the sacraments for those in an irregular second union — the level of practice, not of the bond.
  • Placement on the timeline. This block spans 1981–2016 and is set at the timeline’s modern end (after Instone-Brewer, c. 2002) because its defining and still-live moment is the 2016 Amoris Laetitia controversy; its earliest document (Familiaris Consortio, 1981) predates Instone-Brewer, so the block deliberately overlaps him rather than sitting strictly by its opening date.
  • An East–West convergence. The pastoral direction of Amoris Laetitia — condescension to human weakness under discernment, without conceding that the bond has dissolved — runs strikingly parallel to the Eastern “economy” (oikonomia) that Origen first noticed, Epiphanius defended, and Trullo shaped into graded penance. The parallel is weighed on the Eastern “economy” argument page; and it was Trent’s own carefully worded canon 7 — which declined to anathematize the Greek practice — that later defenders invoked to argue Rome had room to move.
  • Sourcing. The Amoris Laetitia quotations follow the official Vatican English translation; because the Vatican’s HTML page for the exhortation is script-rendered, the verbatim text is taken from the Vatican’s official English PDF of the same document. Francis’s Buenos Aires letter exists on the Vatican site only in the Spanish original, so its English here is a translation of that text.
