The Westminster Confession of Faith on divorce

Dates: 1646 (ch. 24, “Of Marriage and Divorce,” §§5–6; the Confession completed by the Westminster Assembly in December 1646, its Scripture proofs added April 1647, adopted whole by the Church of Scotland 27 August 1647) · Wikipedia
Summary: The confession of faith drawn up by the Westminster Assembly. Chapter 24 names two grounds on which the marriage bond may be dissolved — adultery and wilful desertion — and holds that after a divorce for adultery “the innocent party” may “marry another, as if the offending party were dead.” It permits the innocent party’s remarriage; canon 7 of the Council of Trent (1563) had anathematized anyone who says the Church errs in teaching otherwise. It allows “nothing but adultery, or… willful desertion,” fewer grounds than the incompatibility of mind Milton had argued for in 1643–45. The English Parliament’s approval of 1648 omitted the two divorce sections. A document-witness, like the councils, rather than a single teacher.

Claims & evidence

§5 — adultery, divorce, and the innocent party’s remarriage

Section 5 treats two cases: first the betrothal case — unchastity discovered before the marriage is completed voids the contract — then the married case, in which adultery gives the innocent party leave both to divorce and to marry again:

Adultery or fornication committed after a contract, being detected before marriage, giveth just occasion to the innocent party to dissolve that contract. In the case of adultery after marriage, it is lawful for the innocent party to sue out a divorce: and, after the divorce, to marry another, as if the offending party were dead.

— ch. 24, §5.1 The section’s Scripture proofs are Matthew 1:18–20 for the betrothal case, Matthew 5:31–32 for the divorce, and Matthew 19:9 with Romans 7:2–3 (a wife “loosed from the law” at her husband’s death, free to remarry without adultery) for the remarriage. Maurice Roberts (Banner of Truth, 2005) reads the clause “as if the offending party were dead” and these proofs as showing that the Westminster divines understood the “fornication” exception to extend both to the right of divorce and to the subsequent right of remarriage.2 The same question — whether Matthew’s exception permits remarriage — is answered on this timeline by Ambrosiaster, Erasmus, Luther and Calvin in favour of the innocent party’s remarriage, and by Hermas, Chrysostom, Jerome and Augustine against remarriage while the other spouse lives (see their pages).

§6 — two grounds, and an orderly public course

Section 6 names the causes it holds sufficient to dissolve the bond and requires that the cause be tried, not self-declared:

Although the corruption of man be such as is apt to study arguments unduly to put asunder those whom God hath joined together in marriage: yet, nothing but adultery, or such willful desertion as can no way be remedied by the church, or civil magistrate, is cause sufficient of dissolving the bond of marriage: wherein, a public and orderly course of proceeding is to be observed; and the persons concerned in it not left to their own wills, and discretion, in their own case.

— ch. 24, §6.3 The section names two grounds, introduced by “nothing but”: adultery, the ground of the exception clause in Matthew 5 and 19, and “willful desertion,” for which the proofs cite Paul’s “not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15). Among its proofs is also Matthew 19:6, “what therefore God hath joined together.”4 The section further requires “a public and orderly course of proceeding,” with the parties “not left to their own wills, and discretion”. Luther and Calvin had referred divorce cases to the civil magistrate or the consistory ⚠ unverified.

Relation to the Council of Trent and to Milton

The Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563), in canon 7 on matrimony, had anathematized anyone who says the Church errs in teaching that the bond of matrimony “cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties” and that neither party, not even the innocent one, may contract another marriage during the lifetime of the other.5 Section 5 of the Confession holds that adultery dissolves the bond and that the innocent party may marry another. Milton, whose Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce (1643–44) argued for dissolution on the ground of “indisposition, unfitnes, or contrariety of mind,” wrote during the Assembly’s sittings; the Presbyterian divine Herbert Palmer, preaching before Parliament in 1644, called the tract a “wicked booke” (see Milton’s page). Section 6 names adultery and willful desertion as the only sufficient causes.

Drafting and reception — the sections the English Parliament dropped

The Confession was completed by the Westminster Assembly in December 1646 (presented to the House of Commons as The Humble Advice) and its Scripture proofs added in April 1647. The Church of Scotland General Assembly adopted the whole text, chapter 24 §§5–6 included, on 27 August 1647, and it became the doctrinal standard of Presbyterianism in Scotland, and later in America. When the English Parliament approved the Confession in June 1648 (as “Articles of Christian Religion”), it omitted these divorce paragraphs, so that §§5–6 were never enacted in an English confession of faith.6 The Congregationalist Savoy Declaration (1658) and the Second London Baptist Confession (1689) both retitled the chapter simply “Of Marriage” and dropped the two divorce sections — the Savoy preface objecting that “nothing tends more to heighten Dissensions among Brethren, than to determine and adopt the matter of their difference, under so high a Title, as to be an Article of our Faith.” The Petty France essay (2019) and Founders Ministries read the omission not as a rejection of divorce for adultery and desertion (the marriage sections otherwise follow Westminster nearly verbatim) but as a judgement that the disputed application was unfit for a binding creed, left to conscience.7

Notes

  • Dating. This page is anchored to 1646, the year the Assembly completed the Confession containing chapter 24; the Scripture proofs belong to 1647, as does the Church of Scotland’s adoption of the full text. Where other pages on this timeline refer to “the Westminster Confession (1648),” that year is the English Parliament’s approval — which, as above, omitted §§5–6. The text containing the divorce sections is the Assembly’s, as adopted in Scotland (1647), not the English act of 1648.
  • Spelling and later revisions. The Orthodox Presbyterian Church edition quoted here prints “willful” in §6; the Reformed Wiki proof-text edition prints “wilful.” The American revisions of 1788 altered chapters 20.4, 23.3 and 31.2 (on the civil magistrate); a further amendment of 1887 removed the final sentence of chapter 24.4, on marriage with a deceased spouse’s kindred. Sections 24.5–6 were not changed by either revision.8
  • “As if the offending party were dead.” The proof cited for the formula, Romans 7:2–3, concerns the widow’s freedom to remarry; the Confession places the innocent party in that position. The reading of Matthew’s exception as dissolving the bond is the one Chrysostom, Jerome and Augustine did not hold (see their pages); on Trent’s canon 7, see above.
