The Westminster Confession of Faith on divorce

Dates: 1646 (ch. 24, “Of Marriage and Divorce,” §§5–6; the Confession completed by the Westminster Assembly in December 1646, its Scripture proofs added April 1647, adopted whole by the Church of Scotland 27 August 1647) · Wikipedia
Summary: The confessional crystallization of the Erasmian–Reformed settlement on divorce. Chapter 24 fixes as a subscribed article of faith exactly the two grounds the Reformers had settled on — adultery and wilful desertion — each dissolving the marriage bond so that “the innocent party” may remarry “as if the offending party were dead.” It thereby refuses both flanks it stands between: against the Roman indissolubility fixed at Trent it grants dissolution and remarriage; against Milton’s broader incompatibility grounds, published three years earlier, it allows “nothing but adultery, or… wilful desertion.” A document-witness, like the councils, rather than a single teacher.

Claims & evidence

§5 — adultery dissolves the bond and frees the innocent to remarry

Section 5 states the Reformed settlement in two movements: first the betrothal case — unchastity discovered before the marriage is completed voids the contract — then the married case, in which adultery gives the innocent party leave both to divorce and to marry again:

Adultery or fornication committed after a contract, being detected before marriage, giveth just occasion to the innocent party to dissolve that contract. In the case of adultery after marriage, it is lawful for the innocent party to sue out a divorce: and, after the divorce, to marry another, as if the offending party were dead.

— ch. 24, §5.1 The load-bearing phrase is “as if the offending party were dead”: the divines read Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 5:31–32; 19:9) as dissolving the bond itself, so that remarriage is no longer adultery but a liberty as full as widowhood confers — the reading of Ambrosiaster revived by Erasmus and fixed by Luther and Calvin, and the exact point the whole strict patristic line from Hermas to Augustine had denied. The section’s own Scripture proofs make the logic explicit: Matthew 1:18–20 for the betrothal case, Matthew 5:31–32 for the divorce, and Matthew 19:9 with Romans 7:2–3 (a wife “loosed from the law” at her husband’s death, free to remarry without adultery) for the remarriage — the Westminster divines thus understood the “fornication” exception to extend both to the right of divorce and to the subsequent right of remarriage.2

§6 — two grounds only, and an orderly public course

Section 6 does the bounding work: having granted dissolution, it names the only two causes that suffice and insists the cause be tried, not self-declared:

Although the corruption of man be such as is apt to study arguments unduly to put asunder those whom God hath joined together in marriage: yet, nothing but adultery, or such wilful desertion as can no way be remedied by the church or civil magistrate, is cause sufficient of dissolving the bond of marriage: wherein, a public and orderly course of proceeding is to be observed; and the persons concerned in it not left to their own wills and discretion, in their own case.

— ch. 24, §6.3 Two features are decisive. First, the emphatic “nothing but adultery, or such wilful desertion” is a boundary marker: adultery is the ground of Matthew 5 and 19; “wilful desertion” rests on Paul’s “not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15), read as the “Pauline privilege” that frees the abandoned believer — the two grounds and no third. The bond is otherwise binding (Matthew 19:6, “what therefore God hath joined together”), so mere “irksome cohabitation” or incompatibility — the ground Milton had urged in 1643 — is excluded by name of principle if not of person.4 Second, the requirement of “a public and orderly course of proceeding,” with the parties “not left to their own wills and discretion,” guards against private divorce: the same safeguard Luther and Calvin had built in by referring divorce to the civil magistrate or the consistory, now written into the confessional standard itself.

The place in the tradition — between Trent and Milton

The Confession sits, deliberately, between the two positions it rejects. Against the Roman close of the question at Trent (Session 24, 1563), whose canon 7 had anathematized the claim that “the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties” and that the innocent party may remarry, Westminster affirms exactly what Trent condemned: adultery dissolves the bond, and the innocent may marry another. Against Milton, whose Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce (1643–44) had pressed dissolution past adultery and desertion to bare “indisposition, unfitnes, or contrariety of mind,” the “nothing but” of §6 reasserts the narrower adultery-and-desertion rule as the confessional boundary Milton had overshot — a tract his own Puritan contemporaries had branded from the pulpit before Parliament a “wicked booke.” So the Confession canonizes the Erasmian–Reformed reading that Erasmus reopened, Luther and Calvin fixed, and that became the majority Protestant position — while refusing the radical extension that would, three centuries later, become “irretrievable breakdown” no-fault divorce.

Drafting and reception — the sections the English Parliament dropped

The Confession was completed by the Westminster Assembly in December 1646 (presented to the House of Commons as The Humble Advice) and its Scripture proofs added in April 1647. The Church of Scotland General Assembly adopted the whole text, chapter 24 §§5–6 included, on 27 August 1647, and it became the doctrinal standard of Presbyterianism in Scotland, and later in America. But its parliamentary fate in England was different: when the English Parliament approved the Confession in June 1648 (as “Articles of Christian Religion”), it omitted these very divorce paragraphs, so that §§5–6 were never enacted in an English confession of faith.5 That precedent carried forward: the Congregationalist Savoy Declaration (1658) and the Second London Baptist Confession (1689) both retitled the chapter simply “Of Marriage” and dropped the two divorce sections — the Savoy preface objecting that “nothing tends more to heighten Dissensions among Brethren, than to determine and adopt the matter of their difference, under so high a Title, as to be an Article of our Faith.” The point was not that these bodies rejected divorce for adultery and desertion (their marriage sections otherwise follow Westminster nearly verbatim), but that they judged the disputed application unfit for a binding creed and left it to conscience.6

Notes

  • Dating. This page is anchored to 1646, the year the Assembly completed the Confession containing chapter 24; the Scripture proofs belong to 1647, as does the Church of Scotland’s adoption of the full text. Where other pages on this timeline refer to “the Westminster Confession (1648),” that year is the English Parliament’s approval — which, as above, actually excised §§5–6. The confessional standard that fixes the divorce doctrine is the Assembly’s text as adopted in Scotland (1647), not the trimmed English act (1648).
  • Spelling. The Orthodox Presbyterian Church edition quoted here prints “wilful” (the original British spelling); American editions frequently modernize it to “willful.” The American Presbyterian revisions of 1788 altered chapters 20, 23, and 31 (on the civil magistrate), not chapter 24, whose text stands unchanged.
  • “As if the offending party were dead.” The formula is the Confession’s clearest signal that it reads the exception as dissolving the bond, not merely licensing separation: a widow may remarry without sin (Romans 7:2–3, the cited proof), and the innocent party is put in exactly that position. This is the Erasmian reading the strict fathers — Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine — had denied, and that Trent anathematized.
