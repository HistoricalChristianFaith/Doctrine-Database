John Calvin on divorce

Dates: c. 1555 (the Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, whose exposition of Matthew 19:9 is his fullest statement; the divorce teaching is already in the 1546 Commentary on 1 Corinthians and the Geneva Marriage Ordinance; John Calvin, 1509–1564, reformer of Geneva) · Wikipedia
Summary: The reformer who gave the Erasmian–Lutheran reading its most careful exegetical statement and turned it into enforceable civil law. Calvin held that adultery so dissolves the marriage bond that the innocent party — husband or wife alike, on an equal right — is freed to remarry; that a believer maliciously deserted by an unbeliever is likewise released (1 Corinthians 7:15); but that mere quarrels dissolve nothing, so spouses separated short of these grounds stay bound. In Geneva these principles passed into the Marriage Ordinance, which granted divorce with remarriage for adultery and malicious desertion.

Claims & evidence

Adultery dissolves the bond; the innocent spouse is freed to remarry (Matthew 19:9)

Calvin’s fullest treatment stands in the Harmony of the Evangelists on Matthew 19. He begins from the strict premise — a man cannot at will dissolve what God has joined — but reads the exception as a genuine dissolution worked by the adultery itself:

For it is not in the power of a man to dissolve the engagement of marriage, which the Lord wishes to remain inviolate; and so the woman who occupies the bed of a lawful wife is a concubine. But an exception is added; for the woman, by fornication, cuts herself off, as a rotten member, from her husband, and sets him at liberty.

— Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 19:3–9.1 The liberty is mutual: because in the marriage bed “the wife has an equal right,” a husband’s adultery frees the wife exactly as a wife’s frees the husband — “when, by committing adultery, he has dissolved the marriage, the wife is set at liberty.”2 And Calvin expressly attacks the reading — the whole strict tradition from Clement to Jerome — that would grant only separation and enjoin lifelong celibacy on the innocent:

This clause has been very ill explained by many commentators; for they have thought that generally, and without exception, celibacy is enjoined in all cases when a divorce has taken place… As if this liberty of divorce meant only not to lie with his wife.

— Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 19:9.3 Christ’s condemnation of the man who marries a divorced woman, he adds, “is undoubtedly restricted to unlawful and frivolous divorces” — not to the innocent party of a marriage broken by adultery.

The deserting unbeliever frees the believer (1 Corinthians 7:15)

Calvin took Paul’s desertion clause as a second, distinct ground of release. Where the strict fathers had read “is not under bondage” narrowly, Calvin holds that the unbeliever’s departure utterly breaks the bond:

This is the second department of his statement, in which he sets at liberty a believing husband… and in like manner a woman who is, without any fault on her part, repudiated by her husband; for in that case the unbelieving party makes a divorce with God rather than with his or her partner. There is, therefore, in this case a special reason, inasmuch as the first and chief bond is not merely loosed, but even utterly broken through.

— Commentary on the Epistles of Paul to the Corinthians, on 1 Corinthians 7:15.4 This is the “Pauline privilege” first stated in Latin by Ambrosiaster and pressed by Erasmus and Luther; Calvin systematizes it as a rule — “a brother or a sister is, by such rejection, freed from the bond of marriage.”

Quarrels dissolve nothing — separation without remarriage (1 Corinthians 7:10–11)

Calvin was careful to hold the line exactly where his witnesses did in the non-adulterous case. Expounding Paul’s charge that the separated wife “remain unmarried,” he denies that a falling-out frees anyone to remarry:

He does not give permission here to wives to withdraw, of their own accord, from their husbands… but declares, that even those who are not received by their husbands, continue to be bound, so that they cannot take other husbands.

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians, on 7:10–11.5 He explicitly distinguishes this from the adultery case — “that this is not to be understood of those who have been put away for adultery, is evident” — and reconciles it with Matthew: Paul enjoins the separated to remain unmarried or be reconciled “because quarrels and differences do not dissolve a marriage.” So Calvin’s liberty to remarry is narrow: it opens only where adultery or malicious desertion has, on his reading, already destroyed the bond.

Moses’ bill of divorce was a concession, not a right (Deuteronomy 24:1–4)

On the Mosaic permission Calvin follows the hardness-of-heart reading of Christ, treating Deuteronomy 24 as an indulgence that never overturned the creation ordinance:

Although what relates to divorce was granted in indulgence to the Jews, yet Christ pronounces that it was never in accordance with the Law, because it is directly repugnant to the first institution of God, from whence a perpetual and inviolable rule is to be sought.

— Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses (Harmony of the Law), on Deuteronomy 24:1–4.6 The Jews, he warns, “improperly concluded from their impunity that that was lawful, which God did not punish because of the hardness of their hearts”; yet God “chose to make a provision for women who were cruelly oppressed,” tolerating the bill as a mercy rather than commending it.

Enacted as law: the Geneva Marriage Ordinance — ⚠ unverified

Calvin’s exegesis became civil law in Geneva. The Marriage Ordinance he drafted (1545/1546, revised and formally adopted 1561) is reported to have introduced absolute divorce on the grounds of adultery and malicious desertion, allowing innocent husbands and wives alike to sue, and to have permitted the remarriage of divorcées — making Geneva one of the first Protestant polities to enact the Erasmian reading as marriage law. No firsthand quotation of the ordinance text has been sourced here yet; the account rests on the standard modern scholarship (John Witte, Jr. and Robert M. Kingdon).7

Notes

  • Dating. The teaching is stable across three works of Calvin’s maturity: the Commentary on 1 Corinthians (1546), the Geneva Marriage Ordinance (drafted 1545/1546, enacted 1561), and the fullest statement in the Harmony of the Evangelists (1555), which is why this page is anchored to c. 1555. His comment on Deuteronomy 24 comes latest, in the Harmony of the Law (1563).
  • Equality of the right. Calvin’s insistence that the wife has “an equal right” in the marriage bed, so that a husband’s adultery frees her exactly as hers frees him, sharply separates him from the double-standard tradition of Ambrosiaster and Basil — and aligns him with Jerome’s argument from equality, though Jerome used it to bar remarriage for both and Calvin to permit it for both.
  • The Geneva ordinance still wants a primary text. The claim about the ordinance is carried here on secondary scholarship; a firsthand check against the French ordinance (in the Calvini Opera) or its critical English translation would strengthen it.
