Erasmus on divorce

Dates: c. 1519 (the enlarged second edition of the Annotationes in Novum Testamentum, where the long divorce excursus first appears; Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, c. 1466–1536, Dutch Catholic humanist and biblical scholar) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Catholic humanist scholar whose annotations argued for divorce with remarriage. In his critical Greek New Testament and its Annotations Erasmus printed Matthew’s exception as a plain exceptive clause and then, in a treatise-length note on 1 Corinthians 7, argued that the church could and should permit the innocent party in a broken marriage — broken by adultery or desertion — to divorce and marry again, citing the ancient commentary now called Ambrosiaster, which he knew under the name of Ambrose; Tertullian, Jerome and Augustine had forbidden such remarriage. William Heth (1982) records that the reading was taken up by the Reformers and is known as the “Erasmian view.”1

Claims & evidence

The exception clause printed as an exceptive clause (Matthew 19:9)

Erasmus’ first step was textual. Across all six editions of his Greek New Testament (from the Novum Instrumentum omne of 1516) he set Matthew 19:9 to read with the exception standing as an unambiguous exceptive clause — “except for fornication” — rather than the Majority-text and Vulgate wording that could be read as “apart from the matter of fornication”:

ὃς ἂν ἀπολύσῃ τὴν γυναῖκα αὐτοῦ, εἰ μὴ ἐπὶ πορνείᾳ, καὶ γαμήσῃ ἄλλην μοιχᾶται, καὶ ὁ ἀπολυμένην γαμήσας, μοιχᾶται. — “Whoever divorces his wife, except for porneia, and marries another, commits adultery; and he who marries her who is divorced commits adultery.”

— Erasmus’ text of Matthew 19:9, as set out in Davidson Razafiarivony’s study of the clause.2 His annotation sets out what he took the clause to allow — the adulteress has already dissolved the union by her sin, so the innocent husband is free:

For whoever divorces his wife — unless she is an adulteress (for she has stopped being his wife if she has had sexual relations with another man) — forces her into adultery, since if she marries another man she will marry not a husband, but an adulterer; and he who marries a woman who has been thus repudiated does not marry a wife, but an adulteress.

— Erasmus’ annotation on Matthew 19:9, in Leslie McFall’s translation.3 The reading treats the adultery as itself severing the “one flesh,” so that the exception permits not only separation but a dissolution that frees the innocent party to remarry. Clement and Tertullian had read the exception as allowing separation without remarriage (see their pages).

The 1519 annotation on 1 Corinthians 7

The full case is made not on Matthew but in a long excursus attached, from the 1519 edition, to Paul’s words on the widow “free to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39). There, in John B. Payne’s account, Erasmus lays down two propositions: that it should be permissible to dissolve certain marriages — not lightly, but for very grave reasons, and only through the church’s own authorities or recognized judges — and that the innocent party should then be free to marry again.4 His argument is pastoral as well as exegetical: to bind a couple who have come to detest one another, he urges, is dangerous.

In such instances there are often mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder, anticipating nothing but evil. Neither can live continently and if they cleave to each other they perish twice, but if the marriage is dissolved and they are allowed to marry again, it is hoped that they both should be outside peril.

— Erasmus’ annotation on 1 Corinthians 7:39, in John B. Payne’s translation.5 He read Paul’s own concession on the deserting unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15, “is not under bondage”) as freeing the abandoned believer to a new marriage, and argued the case for adultery and desertion together; the Reformers’ teaching on these grounds is on their own pages. Between the 1516 and 1519 editions he widened the Latin term for the offending cause from fornicatio to the broader stuprum, reaching beyond strictly sexual fault.6

Ambrosiaster as an ancient precedent

Erasmus’ ancient precedent was the anonymous fourth-century Roman commentator whom he knew under the name of Ambrose — the writer now called Ambrosiaster, who read Paul’s silence about the separated man as allowing the innocent husband to marry another. John Rist, following Henri Crouzel, called Ambrosiaster “the only immediately obvious exception” among identifiable early sources to the rule against remarriage; Pierre Nautin (1974), followed in David Hunter’s account by Philip Reynolds and by Riccardo Bof and Conrad Leyser, argued that his view was the dominant tradition of the first three centuries (see the timeline).7

The “common opinion” and the response of his contemporaries

In Payne’s account, Erasmus conceded the “common opinion” against him and appealed instead to the freedom of “good men” to revise their judgment for a good cause, hoping that a renewed church would relieve the unhappily married. The theologians of his day, Payne writes, considered both of his propositions — that the church might dissolve a marriage, and that the innocent might remarry — “revolutionary and heretical.” In 1563 the Council of Trent (Session 24, canon 7) anathematized the claim that the church errs in teaching that the bond cannot be dissolved for adultery (see the Council of Trent page).8

Reception and context

  • “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them.” The sixteenth-century saying concerns Erasmus’ relation to the Reformation generally. On divorce, William Heth (1982) writes that the interpretation “first set forth by Erasmus” was “taken up by the reformers” and found its way into the Westminster Confession (which Heth dates 1648), which permits the innocent party to remarry after adultery or wilful desertion, and reports it as the predominant view among contemporary evangelical authors.1 The reformers named in this connection include Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, Bullinger and Calvin (⚠ unverified for Zwingli, Bucer and Bullinger).
  • Provenance of the 1 Corinthians 7 quotations. The pastoral lines and the two propositions above survive in Erasmus’ annotation on 1 Corinthians 7:39 (added in 1519) and recur in his later Institutio christiani matrimonii (1526) and his Responsio… de divortio (1532); they are given here in John B. Payne’s English translation of the annotation, with the critical Latin edited in the Amsterdam Opera omnia VI-8 (van Poll-van de Lisdonk, 2003). The Annotations on 1–2 Corinthians have not yet appeared in the English Collected Works of Erasmus series, so Erasmus’ own defence of these positions is most accessibly read in the translated Reply… on Divorce (CWE 83, 1998).
