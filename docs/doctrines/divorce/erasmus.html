Erasmus on divorce

Dates: c. 1519 (the enlarged second edition of the Annotationes in Novum Testamentum, where the long divorce excursus first appears; Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, c. 1466–1536, Dutch Catholic humanist and biblical scholar) · Wikipedia
Summary: The scholar whose annotations reopened divorce-with-remarriage in the Latin West. In his critical Greek New Testament and its Annotations Erasmus printed Matthew’s exception as a plain exceptive clause and then, in a treatise-length note on 1 Corinthians 7, argued that the church could and should permit the innocent party in a broken marriage — broken by adultery or desertion — to divorce and marry again, reviving the lone patristic dissent of Ambrosiaster against the indissolubility consensus of Tertullian, Jerome, and Augustine. Taken up by the Reformers, this became the “Erasmian view” and the majority Protestant position; it is the hinge between the patristic–medieval settlement and the Protestant divergence.

Claims & evidence

The exception clause printed as a genuine exception (Matthew 19:9)

Erasmus’ first move was textual. Across all six editions of his Greek New Testament (from the Novum Instrumentum omne of 1516) he set Matthew 19:9 to read with the exception standing as an unambiguous exceptive clause — “except for fornication” — rather than the Majority-text and Vulgate wording that could be read as “apart from the matter of fornication”:

ὃς ἂν ἀπολύσῃ τὴν γυναῖκα αὐτοῦ, εἰ μὴ ἐπὶ πορνείᾳ, καὶ γαμήσῃ ἄλλην μοιχᾶται, καὶ ὁ ἀπολυμένην γαμήσας, μοιχᾶται. — “Whoever divorces his wife, except for porneia, and marries another, commits adultery; and he who marries her who is divorced commits adultery.”

— Erasmus’ text of Matthew 19:9, as set out in Davidson Razafiarivony’s study of the clause.1 His interpretive paraphrase spells out what he took the clause to license — the adulteress has already dissolved the union by her sin, so the innocent husband is free:

For whoever divorces his wife — unless she is an adulteress (for she has stopped being his wife if she has had sexual relations with another man) — forces her into adultery, since if she marries another man she will marry not a husband, but an adulterer; and he who marries a woman who has been thus repudiated does not marry a wife, but an adulteress.

— Erasmus’ annotation on Matthew 19:9, in Leslie McFall’s translation.2 The reading treats the adultery as itself severing the “one flesh,” so that the exception is not a licence merely to separate (the reading of Clement, Tertullian, and the whole strict line) but a genuine dissolution that frees the innocent party to remarry — exactly the step the earlier witnesses had refused.

The 1519 annotation on 1 Corinthians 7: the church may dissolve some marriages and free the innocent to remarry

The full case is made not on Matthew but in a long excursus attached, from the 1519 edition, to Paul’s words on the widow “free to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39). There Erasmus lays down two propositions his contemporaries judged revolutionary: that it should be permissible to dissolve certain marriages — not lightly, but for very grave reasons, and only through the church’s own authorities or recognized judges — and that the innocent party should then be free to marry again.3 His argument is as much pastoral as exegetical: to bind a couple who have come to detest one another, he urges, is not mercy but danger.

[Forcing a couple to stay together when they detest one another] is dangerous. It may end in poison… In such instances there are often mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder.

— Erasmus’ annotation on 1 Corinthians 7, as reported in the standard scholarly account of his sacramental theology.4 He read Paul’s own concession on the deserting unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15, “is not under bondage”) as freeing the abandoned believer to a new marriage, and pressed the case for adultery and desertion together — the two grounds the Reformers would fix on. Between the 1516 and 1519 editions he widened the Latin term for the offending cause from fornicatio to the broader stuprum, reaching beyond strictly sexual fault.5

Reviving Ambrosiaster — the lone patristic ally

Erasmus knew he stood against the near-unanimous voice of the fathers, who held every remarriage after divorce, whatever the cause, to be adultery while the first spouse lived. His one ancient ally was the anonymous fourth-century Roman commentator whom he, like everyone before the nineteenth century, knew under the name of Ambrose — the writer modern scholarship calls Ambrosiaster, the single early Latin exegete to read Paul’s silence about the separated man as a licence for the innocent husband to marry another.6 Where the strict tradition treated Ambrosiaster as an outlier to be explained away, Erasmus made him the hinge of a revived reading — which is why the wiki’s Ambrosiaster page can already name him “a named forerunner of Erasmus.”

Aware of breaking with tradition, and judged heretical for it

Erasmus did not pretend to continuity with the fathers on this point. He conceded the “common opinion” against him and appealed instead to the freedom of “good men” to revise their judgment for a good cause, hoping that a renewed church would relieve the miserably married. The theologians of his day answered that both of his propositions — that the church might dissolve a marriage, and that the innocent might remarry — were revolutionary and heretical, and the later Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563) would frame its canons on marriage with exactly this reading in view, anathematizing the claim that the church errs in teaching the bond cannot be dissolved for adultery.7

Notes

  • “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them.” The sixteenth-century tag captures the reception: the reading first set out in the 1519 Annotations was taken up by Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, Bullinger, and Calvin, hardened into the standard Reformed position, and passed through the Reformers into the Westminster Confession of Faith (1648), which permits the innocent party to remarry after adultery or wilful desertion. It is now the majority view in evangelical Protestantism, and is regularly called the “Erasmian view” after its author.8
  • The name and the argument. Because the “exception permits remarriage?” sub-question is weighed adversarially in the summary’s Arguments section, Erasmus figures there too as the named source of the interpretation under assessment — distinct from his place here as a witness on the timeline.
  • Provenance of the 1 Corinthians 7 quotations. The colourful pastoral lines above survive in Erasmus’ annotation on 1 Corinthians 7 and in his later Institutio christiani matrimonii (1526); they are quoted here from the standard modern study of that annotation rather than from the critical Latin edition, which a fuller treatment should consult directly.
