Martin Luther on divorce

Dates: c. 1522 (The Estate of Marriage / Vom ehelichen Leben, the fuller divorce treatment; the earlier grounds are set in The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520; Martin Luther, 1483–1546, the Wittenberg reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther, the Wittenberg reformer, denied that marriage is a sacrament and taught that adultery so breaks the bond that the innocent party may remarry “just as though his spouse had died,” reading Matthew’s exception as Erasmus had. He added desertion (from 1 Corinthians 7) and the refusal of the conjugal duty as further grounds. He wrote that he “so greatly detest[s] divorce” that he would prefer bigamy to it, required a public process before a divorce that frees a person to remarry, and, for couples who simply cannot get along, allowed separation without remarriage.

Claims & evidence

Marriage is no sacrament (The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520)

Augustine had grounded the permanence of marriage in its sacramentum, the sign of the union of Christ and the Church (see his page). Luther, treating the seven sacraments in the Babylonian Captivity, removed marriage from the list:

We said that there is in every sacrament a word of divine promise, to be believed by whoever receives the sign, and that the sign alone cannot be a sacrament. Now we read nowhere that the man who marries a wife receives any grace of God. No, there is not even a divinely instituted sign in marriage, nor do we read anywhere that marriage was instituted by God to be a sign of anything.

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony, Project Wittenberg.1 Because “marriage existed from the beginning of the world and is still found among unbelievers, it cannot possibly be called a sacrament of the New Law and the exclusive possession of the Church.”

Adultery dissolves the bond; the innocent may remarry (The Estate of Marriage, 1522)

In the second part of The Estate of Marriage, “which persons may be divorced,” Luther lays out three grounds for divorce; the second is adultery, and here he reads Matthew’s exception, as Erasmus had, as freeing the innocent spouse to a new marriage:

Here you see that in the case of adultery Christ permits the divorce of husband and wife, so that the innocent person may remarry. For in saying that he commits adultery who marries another after divorcing his wife, “except for unchastity,” Christ is making it quite clear that he who divorces his wife on account of unchastity and then marries another does not commit adultery.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), Luther’s Works vol. 45, trans. Walther I. Brandt.2 Luther’s reason is that the adulterer, by the sin, has already departed from the marriage — is “considered as one dead” — so the innocent spouse may remarry as if widowed:

The temporal sword and government should therefore still put adulterers to death, for whoever commits adultery has in fact himself already departed and is considered as one dead. Therefore, the other may remarry just as though his spouse had died, if it is his intention to insist on his rights and not show mercy to the guilty party.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), LW 45.3 Earlier witnesses on this timeline from Hermas to Jerome permit dismissal for adultery and bar a new marriage while the first spouse lives (see their pages); Luther holds that the adultery itself ends the marriage and permits the innocent party to remarry.

“I should prefer bigamy”: the Babylonian Captivity statement

The Babylonian Captivity had stated the substance two years earlier, with a statement of Luther’s distaste for divorce and of his hesitation:

As to divorce, it is still a moot question whether it be allowable. For my part I so greatly detest divorce that I should prefer bigamy to it, but whether it be allowable, I do not venture to decide… Christ, then, permits divorce, but for the cause of fornication only. … He certainly seems to permit a man to marry another woman in the stead of the one who has been put away.

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony.4 In December 1539 Luther and Melanchthon gave Landgrave Philip of Hesse a confessor’s advice on a second concurrent marriage (see Notes). His published teaching on divorce, in The Estate of Marriage, was divorce with remarriage for the innocent party.

Desertion and refusal of the conjugal duty as further grounds (1 Corinthians 7)

Beyond adultery Luther took Paul’s desertion clause to free the abandoned believer. In the Babylonian Captivity he reasons from 1 Corinthians 7:15:

Here the Apostle gives permission to put away the unbeliever who departs and to set the believing spouse free to marry again. Why should not the same hold true when a believer — that is, a believer in name, but in truth as much an unbeliever as the one Paul speaks of — deserts his wife, especially if he never intends to return?

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony.5 The third ground in The Estate of Marriage rests on 1 Corinthians 7:4–5: the spouse who “deprives and avoids the other, refusing to fulfil the conjugal duty” is “robbing the other of the body she had bestowed upon him. This is really contrary to marriage, and dissolves the marriage.”6 Erasmus had also argued for adultery and desertion as grounds, and the Westminster Confession later names “adultery” and wilful desertion (see their pages).

Public process; incompatibility permits separation, not remarriage

Luther set two limits on the liberty. First, a divorce that frees a person to remarry must be adjudicated publicly, not claimed at will:

But a public divorce, whereby one is enabled to remarry, must take place through the investigation and decision of the civil authority so that the adultery may be manifest to all — or, if the civil authority refuses to act, with the knowledge of the congregation… in order that it may not be left to each one to allege anything he pleases as a ground for divorce.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), LW 45.7 Second, where a couple “cannot get along together” for reasons short of adultery or refusal of the conjugal duty, Luther — following Paul’s charge in 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 — allows the separation but bars remarriage: “they must both refrain from remarrying or else become reconciled.”8 On Luther’s account remarriage is open only where adultery, desertion or refusal of the conjugal duty has already dissolved the bond.

Notes

  • Erasmus and Luther. Erasmus argued for the innocent party’s remarriage in his 1519 Annotations; Luther taught it as pastoral practice in Wittenberg. Calvin and the Westminster Confession (1646/1648), which permit the innocent party to remarry after adultery or wilful desertion, are on their own pages.
  • Two works, two translation lineages. The quotations from The Estate of Marriage follow Walther I. Brandt’s translation in Luther’s Works (American Edition) vol. 45; those from the Babylonian Captivity follow the A. T. W. Steinhäuser translation (Holman, 1915), the ancestor of the LW 36 revision, whose wording differs slightly. Widely digitized editions of both are cited below.
  • The Philip of Hesse affair. On 10 December 1539 Luther and Melanchthon gave Landgrave Philip of Hesse the “secret advice of a confessor” on taking a second wife; on 4 March 1540 Philip married Margarethe von der Saale while remaining married to Christine of Saxony.9 The episode belongs to the question of polygamy rather than the divorce timeline.
