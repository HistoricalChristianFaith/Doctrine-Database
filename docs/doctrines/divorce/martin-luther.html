Martin Luther on divorce

Dates: c. 1522 (The Estate of Marriage / Vom ehelichen Leben, the fuller divorce treatment; the earlier grounds are set in The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520; Martin Luther, 1483–1546, the Wittenberg reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: The first magisterial Reformer to turn the reopened Erasmian reading into a working pastoral rule. Luther denied that marriage is a sacrament at all — pulling out the sacramentum on which Augustine’s indissolubility rested — and taught that adultery so breaks the bond that the innocent party may remarry “just as though his spouse had died,” adding desertion (from 1 Corinthians 7) as a further ground. He detested divorce and reached these conclusions reluctantly, hedging them with civil process and, in the case of mere incompatibility, allowing separation but no remarriage. With Luther the “Erasmian view” passes from a scholar’s annotation into the divorce discipline of a whole church — “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them.”

Claims & evidence

Marriage is no sacrament (The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520)

Luther’s deepest move was to deny the premise on which the Latin doctrine of indissolubility had been built. Where Augustine had made marriage a sacramentum — a sacred, permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church that death alone could dissolve — Luther, treating the seven sacraments in the Babylonian Captivity, struck marriage from the list:

We said that there is in every sacrament a word of divine promise, to be believed by whoever receives the sign, and that the sign alone cannot be a sacrament. Now we read nowhere that the man who marries a wife receives any grace of God. No, there is not even a divinely instituted sign in marriage, nor do we read anywhere that marriage was instituted by God to be a sign of anything.

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony, Project Wittenberg.1 Because “marriage existed from the beginning of the world and is still found among unbelievers, it cannot possibly be called a sacrament of the New Law and the exclusive possession of the Church.” Removing the sacramental bond removed the theological reason the medieval church had given for holding every marriage indissoluble.

Adultery dissolves the bond — the innocent may remarry (The Estate of Marriage, 1522)

In the second part of The Estate of Marriage, “which persons may be divorced,” Luther lays out three grounds for divorce; the second is adultery, and here he reads Matthew’s exception exactly as Erasmus had — as freeing the innocent spouse to a new marriage:

Here you see that in the case of adultery Christ permits the divorce of husband and wife, so that the innocent person may remarry. For in saying that he commits adultery who marries another after divorcing his wife, “except for unchastity,” Christ is making it quite clear that he who divorces his wife on account of unchastity and then marries another does not commit adultery.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), Luther’s Works vol. 45, trans. Walther I. Brandt.2 The mechanism is that the adulterer, by the sin, has already destroyed the marriage — is civilly “as one dead” — so the innocent spouse is a widow(er) in effect:

The temporal sword and government should therefore still put adulterers to death, for whoever commits adultery has in fact himself already departed and is considered as one dead. Therefore, the other may remarry just as though his spouse had died, if it is his intention to insist on his rights and not show mercy to the guilty party.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), LW 45.3 This is the precise opposite of the strict line from Hermas through Jerome: for them the exception licensed dismissal but never a new marriage while the first spouse lived; for Luther the adultery itself severs the “one flesh,” and remarriage follows.

“I should prefer bigamy” — the reluctance, and the Babylonian Captivity statement

Luther arrived at this conclusion warily. The Babylonian Captivity already stated the substance two years earlier, framed by a striking confession of distaste:

As to divorce, it is still a moot question whether it be allowable. For my part I so greatly detest divorce that I should prefer bigamy to it, but whether it be allowable, I do not venture to decide… Christ, then, permits divorce, but for the cause of fornication only. … He certainly seems to permit a man to marry another woman in the stead of the one who has been put away.

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony.4 The “prefer bigamy” line is not idle rhetoric: Luther genuinely held polygamy a lesser evil than divorce in a desperate case — a view that surfaced disastrously in the later counsel to Philip of Hesse (1540). But his settled public teaching was divorce-with-remarriage for the innocent, not bigamy.

Desertion as a further ground — 1 Corinthians 7

Beyond adultery Luther took Paul’s desertion clause to free the abandoned believer. In the Babylonian Captivity he reasons from 1 Corinthians 7:15:

Here the Apostle gives permission to put away the unbeliever who departs and to set the believing spouse free to marry again. Why should not the same hold true when a believer — that is, a believer in name, but in truth as much an unbeliever as the one Paul speaks of — deserts his wife, especially if he never intends to return?

— The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on matrimony.5 The same Pauline text underwrites the third ground in The Estate of Marriage: the spouse who “deprives and avoids the other, refusing to fulfil the conjugal duty” is robbing the partner of what the marriage vow bestowed, “and this is really contrary to marriage, and dissolves the marriage.”6 Adultery and desertion — the two grounds Erasmus had pressed together — become the twin pillars of the Protestant divorce discipline that flows into the Westminster Confession.

Guarded by public process; incompatibility earns separation, not remarriage

Luther fenced the liberty in two ways. First, a divorce that frees a person to remarry must be adjudicated publicly, not claimed at will:

But a public divorce, whereby one is enabled to remarry, must take place through the investigation and decision of the civil authority so that the adultery may be manifest to all — or, if the civil authority refuses to act, with the knowledge of the congregation… in order that it may not be left to each one to allege anything he pleases as a ground for divorce.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), LW 45.7 Second, where a couple simply “cannot get along together” for reasons short of adultery or refusal of the conjugal duty, Luther — following Paul’s own charge in 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 — allows the separation but bars remarriage: “they must both refrain from remarrying or else become reconciled.”8 So Luther is no advocate of divorce-at-will; he keeps the strict tradition’s bar precisely where the fathers had put it, and breaks with it only where adultery or desertion has, on his reading, already dissolved the bond.

Notes

  • “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them.” The reading that Erasmus set out textually and pastorally in his 1519 Annotations becomes, with Luther, the actual divorce practice of an evangelical church. From him it passed to Zwingli, Bucer, Bullinger, and Calvin, hardened into the standard Reformed position, and entered the Westminster Confession of Faith (1648), which permits the innocent party to remarry after adultery or wilful desertion — the two grounds Luther had already fixed.
  • Two works, two translation lineages. The quotations from The Estate of Marriage follow Walther I. Brandt’s translation in Luther’s Works (American Edition) vol. 45; those from the Babylonian Captivity follow the A. T. W. Steinhäuser translation (Holman, 1915), the ancestor of the LW 36 revision, whose wording differs slightly. Widely digitized editions of both are cited below.
  • The Philip of Hesse affair. Luther’s stated preference for bigamy over divorce “in a desperate case” had a notorious sequel: in 1540 he and Melanchthon secretly counselled Landgrave Philip of Hesse that a second concurrent marriage was, as a matter of conscience, preferable to divorce or adultery — advice that badly embarrassed the Reformation once it became public. It belongs to the polygamy question rather than the divorce timeline, but it shows how far Luther’s dread of dissolving a valid bond could be pressed.
