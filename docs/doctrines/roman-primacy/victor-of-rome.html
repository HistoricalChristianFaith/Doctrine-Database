Victor of Rome and the Quartodeciman Controversy

Dates: c. 190 (Victor, bishop of Rome c. 189–199; the Easter-date dispute falls in the tenth year of Commodus and after) · Wikipedia
Summary: Around 190 the churches of Asia and the church of Rome divided over the date of Easter: the Asian churches, on an older tradition, ended the paschal fast on the fourteenth of Nisan (the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever weekday it fell on — the “Quartodeciman” practice), while Rome and most of the rest ended it only on the Sunday of the resurrection. After synods across the world backed the Sunday reckoning, Victor of Rome attempted to cut the Asian churches off from communion as heterodox. Polycrates of Ephesus, speaking for the bishops of Asia, refused to yield, appealing to a chain of apostolic authorities buried in Asia — the apostle Philip, the apostle John, Polycarp — and to his own conscience: “We ought to obey God rather than man.” Other bishops rebuked him; foremost among them Irenaeus of Lyons, who admonished Victor that he should not cut off whole churches over a customary difference, and reminded him that Victor’s own predecessors had kept peace across exactly this disagreement. Whether the episode shows a papal prerogative asserted and recognized, a breach of communion between churches, or an affair internal to the Roman church is disputed; the readings, with their proponents, are set out in the Notes below. The only narrative source is Eusebius, who quotes both Polycrates and Irenaeus at length.

Claims & evidence

The dispute: two traditions for ending the paschal fast (Eusebius, Church History V.23)

Eusebius presents the controversy as a divergence of custom, each side claiming an older or apostolic tradition. The Asian churches followed the fourteenth-of-the-moon reckoning; the rest of the world ended the fast on the Lord’s day:

A question of no small importance arose at that time. For the parishes of all Asia, as from an older tradition, held that the fourteenth day of the moon, on which day the Jews were commanded to sacrifice the lamb, should be observed as the feast of the Saviour’s passover. It was therefore necessary to end their fast on that day, whatever day of the week it should happen to be. But it was not the custom of the churches in the rest of the world to end it at this time, as they observed the practice which, from apostolic tradition, has prevailed to the present time, of terminating the fast on no other day than on that of the resurrection of our Saviour. … There is still … another writing extant of those who were assembled at Rome to consider the same question, which bears the name of Bishop Victor.

— Church History V.23.1–2, source (McGiffert translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II; also read locally in The Church History of Eusebius, Book 5).1 Synods were held across the world — in Palestine, Pontus, Gaul, Osrhoënë, Corinth, and at Rome under Victor — and reached the Sunday reckoning by mutual correspondence. The question that concerns the primacy is not which custom is right, but what Victor did when Asia declined to fall in line.

Victor’s attempt to excommunicate the churches of Asia (V.24.9)

When the Asian bishops, led by Polycrates, held to their custom, Victor moved to sever them from the common communion of the church. Eusebius says that Victor attempted it:

Thereupon Victor, who presided over the church at Rome, immediately attempted to cut off from the common unity the parishes of all Asia, with the churches that agreed with them, as heterodox; and he wrote letters and declared all the brethren there wholly excommunicate.

— Church History V.24.9.2 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, 1920) points to this act as a bishop of Rome claiming “the right to use real jurisdiction over Christians in Asia”: on Eusebius’s text, Victor “meant to cut these people in Asia off, not only from his own communion, but from the unity of the whole Church.”3 Eusebius’s verb is “attempted” (epeirato); whether the sentence took effect he does not record (below).

Polycrates of Ephesus refuses to yield (V.24.2–8)

Polycrates, answering Victor and the church of Rome directly, grounds the Asian custom not in Rome’s counter-tradition but in an apostolic pedigree of his own — Philip, John, Polycarp, and a line of seven bishops in his own family — and closes by refusing to be moved by “terrifying words”:

We observe the exact day; neither adding, nor taking away. For in Asia also great lights have fallen asleep, which shall rise again on the day of the Lord’s coming … Among these are Philip, one of the twelve apostles, who fell asleep in Hierapolis … and, moreover, John, who was both a witness and a teacher, who reclined upon the bosom of the Lord … He fell asleep at Ephesus. And Polycarp in Smyrna, who was a bishop and martyr … All these observed the fourteenth day of the passover according to the Gospel, deviating in no respect, but following the rule of faith. And I also, Polycrates, the least of you all, do according to the tradition of my relatives, some of whom I have closely followed. For seven of my relatives were bishops; and I am the eighth. … I, therefore, brethren, who have lived sixty-five years in the Lord … am not affrighted by terrifying words. For those greater than I have said ‘We ought to obey God rather than man.’

— Church History V.24.2–7.4 Polycrates sets Asia’s apostolic tradition (John and Philip) beside Rome’s, pleads his own long standing and family of bishops, and refuses Victor’s demand with the apostles’ words to the Sanhedrin (Acts 5:29).

The bishops rebuke Victor; Irenaeus admonishes him (V.24.10–18)

Eusebius then reports the reaction of other bishops, including some who agreed with Rome on the Easter date:

But this did not please all the bishops. And they besought him to consider the things of peace, and of neighborly unity and love. Words of theirs are extant, sharply rebuking Victor. Among them was Irenaeus, who, sending letters in the name of the brethren in Gaul over whom he presided, maintained that the mystery of the resurrection of the Lord should be observed only on the Lord’s day. He fittingly admonishes Victor that he should not cut off whole churches of God which observed the tradition of an ancient custom.

— Church History V.24.10–11.5 Irenaeus, who himself kept the Sunday reckoning, then reminds Victor that the very predecessors whose chair he now holds had kept peace across this same disagreement, receiving Quartodecimans to communion rather than casting them out:

Among these were the presbyters before Soter, who presided over the church which thou now rulest. We mean Anicetus, and Pius, and Hyginus, and Telesphorus, and Xystus. They neither observed it themselves, nor did they permit those after them to do so. And yet though not observing it, they were none the less at peace with those who came to them from the parishes in which it was observed … But none were ever cast out on account of this form; but the presbyters before thee who did not observe it, sent the eucharist to those of other parishes who observed it. And when the blessed Polycarp was at Rome in the time of Anicetus, and they disagreed a little about certain other things, they immediately made peace with one another, not caring to quarrel over this matter. For neither could Anicetus persuade Polycarp not to observe what he had always observed with John the disciple of our Lord … But though matters were in this shape, they communed together … And they parted from each other in peace.

— Church History V.24.14–17.6 Eusebius sums up the outcome by punning on the bishop’s name: “Thus Irenaeus, who truly was well named, became a peacemaker in this matter” — and adds that Irenaeus wrote in the same vein “not only with Victor, but also with most of the other rulers of the churches.”7 Eusebius does not record whether Victor’s sentence took effect. Fortescue holds that “Victor intended to do it but did not”: “Either he never published the excommunication or he withdrew it at once.”3

Notes

  • Reception and debate. James F. McCue (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1964) reports that the episode “has been widely accepted as evidence of the assertion (by Victor) and recognition (by Irenaeus) of papal prerogatives,” and reads Eusebius himself as thinking “that Victor acts as one having authority over the universal Church.” T. G. Jalland (The Church and the Papacy, 1944) and N. Maurice-Denis Boulet (1953) read Irenaeus’s account of Anicetus and Polycarp as concerning practice within the Roman church, and Jalland holds that Eusebius read a wider significance into it. McCue inclines to Jalland’s reading, and argues that even if Victor did act against Asia, the act would have been understood as one bishop breaking communion with other churches, not as expulsion from the Church universal.8 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, The Early Papacy, 1920) reads it as a bishop of Rome claiming “the right to use real jurisdiction over Christians in Asia” — “no one denies his claim, though they thought he had better not do so in this case.”3 Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, Paris, “The Church Which Presides in Love”) asks whether Victor’s action shows that he “understood the position of Rome in a way different from Irenaeus,” and answers that the characters of Victor and Stephen “might suffice to make them act as they did, while yet remaining within the ancient ideological framework of priority” — a priority of Rome’s witness among the churches, not a power over them.9
  • “Attempted.” Eusebius says Victor attempted (epeirato) to cut off the Asian churches and “declared” them excommunicate; his narrative closes with the churches still in communion and Irenaeus “the peacemaker.” Whether Victor’s letters ever severed anyone, or only threatened to, he does not say.
  • The same Irenaeus. This is the Irenaeus who, a decade earlier, wrote of the Roman church’s propter potiorem principalitatem — a “pre-eminent authority” with which “every Church should agree” (his c. 180 detail page). How the two texts relate is set out at the disputed reading, Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?
  • Rome’s standing in the episode. Victor convened the Roman synod, corresponded with churches across the world, and was answered directly by Polycrates and reasoned with by Irenaeus. How the episode bears on the kind of primacy Rome held is read differently by the parties named above.
  • The source. The whole episode reaches us through Eusebius (writing c. 325), who quotes the letters of Polycrates and Irenaeus he had before him. The primary documents — Victor’s own letters, Polycrates’ full reply, Irenaeus’s remonstrance — survive only in the extracts Eusebius chose to preserve; his framing of the affair, with Irenaeus as peacemaker, is his own, written in the fourth century.
