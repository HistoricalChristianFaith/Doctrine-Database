The Council of Chalcedon, canon 28 (451)

Dates: 451 (the Fourth Ecumenical Council, convened at Chalcedon by the emperor Marcian; canon 28 passed in a session the Roman legates did not attend and afterwards protested) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chalcedon’s twenty-eighth canon renews and enlarges the ranking of 381: it gives the see of Constantinople “equal privileges” with Old Rome and a wide new jurisdiction, and states its ground — the fathers granted Old Rome its privileges “because it was the royal city,” and awarded New Rome the like “actuated by the same consideration.” How the canon bears on the kind of primacy Rome held is disputed (see the Notes). The council’s synodal letter asked Leo to confirm the canon; Pope Leo the Great, whose Tome the same council had acclaimed, protested canon 28 and refused to ratify it, insisting the order of the sees rested on the unalterable canons of Nicaea — and declaring the bishops’ assent to canon 28 null and void.

Claims & evidence

Canon 28: “equal privileges” for New Rome

The canon takes up the canon of 381, quotes it, and extends it:

Following in all things the decisions of the holy Fathers, and acknowledging the canon, which has been just read, of the One Hundred and Fifty Bishops beloved-of-God… we also do enact and decree the same things concerning the privileges of the most holy Church of Constantinople, which is New Rome. For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges (isa presbeia) to the most holy throne of New Rome, justly judging that the city which is honoured with the Sovereignty and the Senate, and enjoys equal privileges with the old imperial Rome, should in ecclesiastical matters also be magnified as she is, and rank next after her.

— The XXX Canons of the Holy… Synod of Chalcedon, Canon XXVIII, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV; also read locally in The XXX Canons).1 The two thrones are given “equal privileges,” Constantinople ranking “next after” Rome, and the canon goes on to place the metropolitans of Pontus, Asia, and Thrace under Constantinople’s ordaining authority.

The council asks Leo to confirm the canon (Leo, Letter XCVIII)

The council reported its decisions to Leo in a synodal letter. It calls him “the mouthpiece unto all of the blessed Peter” and says that he presided over its bishops “as the head to the members, showing your goodwill in the person of those who represented you”; it then informs him that it has ratified the canon of 381 “which ordains that after your most holy and Apostolic See, the See of Constantinople shall take precedence, being placed second,” reports that his legates “attempted vehemently to resist these decisions,” and asks his consent:

Accordingly, we entreat you, honour our decision by your assent, and as we have yielded to the head our agreement on things honourable, so may the head also fulfil for the children what is fitting.

— The Synod of Chalcedon to Leo (Leo, Letter XCVIII), §§I and IV, source (Feltoe translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XII).2 John Chapman (Benedictine, 1928) reads the canon as “passed subject to the approval of St. Leo.”3

Leo of Rome protests, and declares the canon null

The canon passed in a session the papal legates had left; they lodged an immediate protest, and Leo, when the acts reached him, refused to confirm canon 28 — grounding his refusal on the fixed authority of the Nicene canons and declaring the bishops’ assent void:

For no one may venture upon anything in opposition to the enactments of the Fathers’ canons which many long years ago in the city of Nicæa were founded upon the decrees of the Spirit, so that any one who wishes to pass any different decree injures himself rather than impairs them.

— Leo the Great, Letter CV (to the empress Pulcheria), §2, source (Feltoe translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XII).4 The letter’s own headings frame Leo’s case: “The Nicene Canons are Unalterable and Binding Universally,” and “the Assent of the Bishops is Declared Null and Void.” Leo rested the order of the sees on the Nicene settlement and refused to have that order rewritten.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Canon 28 is the conciliar text that states a ground for Rome’s privileges as well as Constantinople’s; Leo refused it on the ground of the Nicene canons. How the two bear on the kind of primacy Rome held is set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?
  • Reception of the canon. Leo refused canon 28. Chapman reports that Anatolius of Constantinople “humbly begged the Pope’s forgiveness,” and that later “Acacius and his successors at Byzantium consolidated their position, and used the twenty-eighth canon of Chalcedon as having force of law. The Popes protested.”3 Metropolitan Hilarion (Russian Orthodox, 2014) describes canon 28 as the canon “on which the primacy of the Patriarch of Constantinople has been based.”5
  • Companion conciliar data. Canon 28 follows Nicaea’s custom-based regional spheres (325), Sardica’s appeal-right (343), and Constantinople’s ranking of Constantinople second “because Constantinople is New Rome” (381), which canon 28 quotes and extends. Leo’s fuller Petrine theory — the pope as Peter’s heir speaking with Peter’s authority — is set out on his own detail page.
