The Council of Chalcedon, canon 28 (451)

Dates: 451 (the Fourth Ecumenical Council, convened at Chalcedon by the emperor Marcian; canon 28 passed in a session the Roman legates did not attend and afterwards protested) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chalcedon’s twenty-eighth canon renews and enlarges the ranking of 381: it gives the see of Constantinople “equal privileges” with Old Rome and a wide new jurisdiction, and states the ground plainly — the fathers granted Old Rome its privileges “because it was the royal city,” and awarded New Rome the like “actuated by the same consideration.” It is the sharpest conciliar datum for the reading that the fathers grounded Roman primacy in imperial status rather than Petrine right. Pope Leo the Great, whose Tome the same council had acclaimed, protested canon 28 and refused to ratify it, insisting the order of the sees rested on the unalterable canons of Nicaea — and declaring the bishops’ assent to canon 28 null and void.

Claims & evidence

Canon 28: “equal privileges” for New Rome, because the sees rank as their cities

The canon takes up the canon of 381, quotes its logic, and draws it out to its full civic conclusion:

Following in all things the decisions of the holy Fathers, and acknowledging the canon, which has been just read, of the One Hundred and Fifty Bishops beloved-of-God… we also do enact and decree the same things concerning the privileges of the most holy Church of Constantinople, which is New Rome. For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges (isa presbeia) to the most holy throne of New Rome, justly judging that the city which is honoured with the Sovereignty and the Senate, and enjoys equal privileges with the old imperial Rome, should in ecclesiastical matters also be magnified as she is, and rank next after her.

— The XXX Canons of the Holy… Synod of Chalcedon, Canon XXVIII, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV; also read locally in The XXX Canons).1 The reasoning is explicit and reciprocal: Old Rome held its privileges “because it was the royal city,” and New Rome is given the like “because” it too is now an imperial city with sovereignty and senate. On this account the two thrones enjoy “equal privileges,” Constantinople ranking “next after” Rome — and the canon goes on to place the metropolitans of Pontus, Asia, and Thrace under Constantinople’s ordaining authority. The order of the sees is grounded in the standing of their cities, not in an apostle’s commission.

Leo of Rome protests, and declares the canon null

The canon passed in a session the papal legates had left; they lodged an immediate protest, and Leo, when the acts reached him, refused to confirm canon 28 — grounding his refusal on the fixed authority of the Nicene canons and treating the bishops’ assent as extorted and void:

For no one may venture upon anything in opposition to the enactments of the Fathers’ canons which many long years ago in the city of Nicæa were founded upon the decrees of the Spirit, so that any one who wishes to pass any different decree injures himself rather than impairs them.

— Leo the Great, Letter CV (to the empress Pulcheria), §2, source (Feltoe translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XII).2 The letter’s own headings frame Leo’s case: “The Nicene Canons are Unalterable and Binding Universally,” and “the Assent of the Bishops is Declared Null and Void.” Leo did not deny Rome’s primacy — he asserted it — but he rested it, and the whole order of the sees, on the Nicene settlement and apostolic right, and would not have that order rewritten on the civic ground canon 28 openly avowed. The protest is itself the datum: the very grounding the council gave for Rome’s first place is the one Rome most firmly rejected.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline. Canon 28 is the sharpest conciliar statement that the great sees rank by the civil standing of their cities — and it says so of Rome, not only of Constantinople. That makes it the leading conciliar counter-datum to a Petrine grounding of Roman primacy. But the same episode also shows Rome, in Leo, asserting a primacy of its own on apostolic and Nicene ground and refusing the civic account outright: the council and the pope grounded Rome’s pre-eminence in two different things, and the clash is exactly what the honour-versus-jurisdiction question, weighed in the summary’s Arguments section, must adjudicate.
  • What Leo’s refusal does and does not show. It shows that a jurisdictional reading of canon 28’s civic logic was not simply conceded — Rome fought it, and canon 28 was long resisted in the West. It does not show the council agreed with Leo: the assembled fathers did ground the ranking in imperial status, and the Eastern church received canon 28 as law. The two facts stand together on the timeline; neither cancels the other.
  • Companion conciliar data. Canon 28 is the climax of a through-line: Nicaea’s custom-based regional spheres (325), Sardica’s council-granted appeal-right (343), and Constantinople’s ranking of Constantinople second “because Constantinople is New Rome” (381), which canon 28 quotes and extends. Leo’s fuller Petrine theory — the pope as Peter’s heir speaking with Peter’s authority — is set out on his own detail page.
