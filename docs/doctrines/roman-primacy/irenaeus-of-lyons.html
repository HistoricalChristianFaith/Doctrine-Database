Irenaeus of Lyons and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, esp. Book III, written c. 175–185) · Wikipedia
Summary: Around 180, arguing against Gnostics who claimed a secret apostolic tradition, Irenaeus answered that the true tradition is public and traceable through the bishops each apostolic church can name. He needed only one church’s list to make the point — and the church he chose was Rome, “the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded … by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul,” with which “every Church should agree … on account of its pre-eminent authority” (Latin propter potiorem principalitatem). This is the earliest surviving text to appeal explicitly to Rome’s special standing as a touchstone of the faith, and to give the Roman succession-list from Peter and Paul down to the reigning bishop. But two features temper how far it carries the Petrine primacy of later theory: the crucial phrase is a Latin rendering of a lost Greek and is genuinely disputed in meaning, and Irenaeus grounds Rome’s standing in Peter and Paul jointly and in the publicity of its succession — not in Peter alone or in a transmitted Petrine office. This page reports the datum and both limits; Irenaeus’s witness to church order proper — the succession argument as a rule of faith — is treated on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

Rome as the touchstone church — “every Church should agree with this Church” (Against Heresies III.3.2)

Rather than reckon up the succession of every apostolic church, Irenaeus points to one as a sufficient specimen — and the one he picks is Rome:

Since, however, it would be very tedious, in such a volume as this, to reckon up the successions of all the Churches, we do put to confusion all those who, in whatever manner, whether by an evil self-pleasing, by vainglory, or by blindness and perverse opinion, assemble in unauthorized meetings; [we do this, I say,] by indicating that tradition derived from the apostles, of the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded and organized at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul; as also [by pointing out] the faith preached to men, which comes down to our time by means of the successions of the bishops. For it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its pre-eminent authority, that is, the faithful everywhere, inasmuch as the apostolical tradition has been preserved continuously by those [faithful men] who exist everywhere.

— Against Heresies III.3.2, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; also at historicalchristian.faith).1 Three things in this one sentence are pressed by the primacy reading, and each is real: Rome is singled out as the specimen church; it is called the church “founded … by … Peter and Paul”; and “every Church should agree with” it. Taken together they are the earliest explicit appeal in Christian literature to Rome’s special standing as a measure of the true faith.

The contested clause: propter potiorem principalitatem

The pivot — “on account of its pre-eminent authority” — is exactly where the text is least secure. Irenaeus wrote in Greek, but for this passage the Greek is lost; it survives only in an early, wooden Latin translation:

Ad hanc enim ecclesiam propter potiorem principalitatem necesse est omnem convenire ecclesiam.

The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors themselves flag the problem in a footnote to the passage: “Both the text and meaning have here given rise to much discussion. It is impossible to say with certainty of what words in the Greek original ‘potiorem principalitatem’ may be the translation. We are far from sure that the rendering given above is correct, but we have been unable to think of anything better.”2 The manuscripts themselves vary between potiorem and potentiorem, and the phrase has been rendered at least three ways, with very different weight: “pre-eminent authority” (a claim about Rome’s standing to command assent); “more powerful principality / origin” (a claim about its apostolic pedigree — the double foundation by Peter and Paul); or “superior origin” read with the following clause so that it is the faithful who resort to Rome from everywhere who preserve the tradition there, rather than Rome radiating authority outward. Which rendering is right — and therefore whether the sentence asserts Roman jurisdiction, Roman apostolic pedigree, or merely Rome’s convenient prominence as a well-known see where the faith of all is on display — is the whole question, and it turns on words we no longer have. That adjudication is weighed adversarially on its own page — does the potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? (assessment: weak for the jurisdictional reading) — not settled here; this page reports that the clause is early and real but its force is genuinely undetermined by the surviving text.

The Roman succession-list, from Peter and Paul to Eleutherius (Against Heresies III.3.3)

Having named Rome, Irenaeus gives its episcopal succession — the first such list to survive — running from the apostles to the bishop reigning as he wrote:

The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate. Of this Linus, Paul makes mention in the Epistles to Timothy. To him succeeded Anacletus; and after him, in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric. … To this Clement there succeeded Evaristus. Alexander followed Evaristus; then, sixth from the apostles, Sixtus was appointed; after him, Telephorus, who was gloriously martyred; then Hyginus; after him, Pius; then after him, Anicetus. Sorer having succeeded Anicetus, Eleutherius does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate. In this order, and by this succession, the ecclesiastical tradition from the apostles, and the preaching of the truth, have come down to us.

— Against Heresies III.3.3.3 The list is doing a specific job: it demonstrates the unbroken publicity of the Roman teaching-line against the Gnostic claim to a private tradition — the same purpose the succession argument serves throughout Book III. Note that the apostles “commit” the episcopate to Linus, not to a bishop-Peter; Irenaeus does not here call Peter the first bishop of Rome but distinguishes the founding apostles from the succession of bishops they installed. (Eusebius, who preserved the Greek of the neighbouring sentence, likewise begins the count from Linus.)

Grounded in Peter and Paul, and in public succession — not Peter alone (III.3.1–2)

The frame around the Roman passage is the anti-Gnostic argument from public traceability: because the apostles, had they possessed secret mysteries, would have entrusted them precisely to the men they left as bishops, the open succession-lists are proof that no such secret exists.

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times …

— Against Heresies III.3.1.4 Two features of this frame bear directly on how far the passage supports a specifically Petrine primacy. First, Irenaeus attributes Rome’s foundation to Peter and Paul jointly — the “two most glorious apostles” — not to Peter as sole founder or first pope; the honour he pays Rome is the honour of a doubly apostolic see. Second, Rome’s value in the argument is evidential: it is “universally known,” so its public teaching-line is the handiest specimen — a church any reader could check — not a court to which the others are juridically subordinate. Whether “every Church should agree with this Church” means Rome commands and the rest obey, or that Rome’s well-attested faith is the convenient standard against which error is exposed, is precisely what the lost Greek would settle.

Notes

  • Pedigree, evidence, or jurisdiction? That Irenaeus honours Rome above the churches he might have listed is plain, and it is genuine early evidence of Roman pre-eminence — III.3.2 is quoted in the primacy debate as often as any patristic text. What the honour amounts to — a jurisdiction Rome already exercises, an apostolic pedigree that makes its faith authoritative, or an evidential prominence that makes it the readiest test-case — is the contested question, and the loss of the Greek keeps it open. The adjudication belongs to the argument page — does the potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? — not to this timeline block: it rates a primacy of honour well-grounded here but the jurisdictional reading weak.
  • Peter and Paul, not Peter alone. The later Roman theory rests the primacy on Peter singly (the “rock” and the keys of Matthew 16, transmitted through his successors). Irenaeus does not: he names Peter and Paul as co-founders, credits Linus—not a bishop-Peter—with the first episcopate, and cites no Petrine proof-text. His argument is about the continuity and publicity of teaching, which any apostolic church can show, not about a unique commission lodged in Peter’s chair. How much of the developed primacy this will bear is exactly the sort of over-precise inference the Arguments section keeps in view.
  • The tension with Irenaeus’s own later act. A decade or so after writing this, Irenaeus intervened in the Quartodeciman (Easter-date) controversy — not to enforce Rome’s ruling but to restrain Bishop Victor of Rome from excommunicating the churches of Asia, reminding him that his predecessors had kept peace across the disagreement. The same father who grants Rome a “pre-eminent authority” here is the one who checks a Roman bishop’s attempt to exercise authority over other churches there. The two data sit in real tension and should be read together; the Victor episode is set out on the timeline at c. 190.
  • The lost Greek. Because Book III survives in full only in the old Latin version, the load-bearing phrase (potiorem/potentiorem principalitatem) reaches us at one remove. The rendering “pre-eminent authority” above is the Ante-Nicene Fathers translators’ own, offered with their explicit admission that they are “far from sure” it is correct. No responsible reading of this passage can treat the English as if it were Irenaeus’s settled word.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Irenaeus is the father who forged the succession argument into a weapon against the Gnostics (the wider frame this Roman passage sits in), and appears also on other timelines as a witness. The tradition of Peter’s own mission and death at Rome — the ground on which the specifically Petrine claim is later built — is traced on the apostle Peter.
