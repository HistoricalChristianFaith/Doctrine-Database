Irenaeus of Lyons and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, esp. Book III, written c. 175–185) · Wikipedia
Summary: Around 180, arguing against Gnostics who claimed a secret apostolic tradition, Irenaeus answered that the true tradition is public and traceable through the bishops each apostolic church can name. He needed only one church’s list to make the point — and the church he chose was Rome, “the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded … by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul,” with which “every Church should agree … on account of its pre-eminent authority” (Latin propter potiorem principalitatem). This is the earliest extant text to appeal explicitly to Rome’s standing as a touchstone of the faith, and the earliest extant Roman succession-list, from Peter and Paul down to the reigning bishop. The crucial phrase is a Latin rendering of a lost Greek and its meaning is disputed; Irenaeus names Peter and Paul together as Rome’s founders. This page reports the passage and its readings; Irenaeus’s witness to church order proper — the succession argument as a rule of faith — is treated on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

Rome as the touchstone church — “every Church should agree with this Church” (Against Heresies III.3.2)

Rather than reckon up the succession of every apostolic church, Irenaeus points to one as a sufficient specimen — and the one he picks is Rome:

Since, however, it would be very tedious, in such a volume as this, to reckon up the successions of all the Churches, we do put to confusion all those who, in whatever manner, whether by an evil self-pleasing, by vainglory, or by blindness and perverse opinion, assemble in unauthorized meetings; [we do this, I say,] by indicating that tradition derived from the apostles, of the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded and organized at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul; as also [by pointing out] the faith preached to men, which comes down to our time by means of the successions of the bishops. For it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its pre-eminent authority, that is, the faithful everywhere, inasmuch as the apostolical tradition has been preserved continuously by those [faithful men] who exist everywhere.

— Against Heresies III.3.2, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; also at historicalchristian.faith).1 Three features of the sentence are central to the debate: Rome is singled out as the specimen church; it is called the church “founded … by … Peter and Paul”; and “every Church should agree with” it. It is the earliest extant explicit appeal in Christian literature to Rome’s standing as a measure of the true faith.

The disputed clause: propter potiorem principalitatem

The pivotal phrase is “on account of its pre-eminent authority.” Irenaeus wrote in Greek, but for this passage the Greek is lost; it survives only in an early, literal Latin translation:

Ad hanc enim ecclesiam propter potiorem principalitatem necesse est omnem convenire ecclesiam.

The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors comment in a footnote to the passage: “Both the text and meaning have here given rise to much discussion. It is impossible to say with certainty of what words in the Greek original ‘potiorem principalitatem’ may be the translation. We are far from sure that the rendering given above is correct, but we have been unable to think of anything better.”2 The manuscripts themselves vary between potiorem and potentiorem, and the phrase has been rendered at least three ways, with very different weight: “pre-eminent authority” (a claim about Rome’s standing to command assent); “more powerful principality / origin” (a claim about its apostolic pedigree — the double foundation by Peter and Paul); or “superior origin” read with the following clause so that it is the faithful who resort to Rome from everywhere who preserve the tradition there. Which rendering is right — and therefore whether the sentence asserts Roman jurisdiction, Roman apostolic pedigree, or Rome’s prominence as a well-known see where the faith of all is on display — is disputed, and the Greek that would settle the wording is lost. The readings, with their proponents, are set out at the disputed reading, Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

The Roman succession-list, from Peter and Paul to Eleutherius (Against Heresies III.3.3)

Having named Rome, Irenaeus gives its episcopal succession — the earliest such list extant — running from the apostles to the bishop reigning as he wrote:

The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate. Of this Linus, Paul makes mention in the Epistles to Timothy. To him succeeded Anacletus; and after him, in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric. … To this Clement there succeeded Evaristus. Alexander followed Evaristus; then, sixth from the apostles, Sixtus was appointed; after him, Telephorus, who was gloriously martyred; then Hyginus; after him, Pius; then after him, Anicetus. Sorer having succeeded Anicetus, Eleutherius does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate. In this order, and by this succession, the ecclesiastical tradition from the apostles, and the preaching of the truth, have come down to us.

— Against Heresies III.3.3.3 In context the list shows the public continuity of the Roman teaching-line against the Gnostic claim to a private tradition, the purpose the succession argument serves throughout Book III. The apostles “commit” the episcopate to Linus, and the count of bishops runs from him, Eleutherius holding it “in the twelfth place from the apostles.” (Eusebius, who preserved the Greek of the neighbouring sentence, likewise begins the count from Linus.) How the founding apostles stand to the succession they installed, and whether Rome’s distinctive standing in Irenaeus comes from Peter, is read differently by Unger and McCue (see the Notes).

Peter and Paul, and the public succession (III.3.1–2)

The frame around the Roman passage is the anti-Gnostic argument from public traceability: because the apostles, had they possessed secret mysteries, would have entrusted them precisely to the men they left as bishops, the open succession-lists are proof that no such secret exists.

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times …

— Against Heresies III.3.1.4 Irenaeus attributes Rome’s foundation to Peter and Paul jointly, the “two most glorious apostles,” and introduces Rome as a church “universally known.” Whether “every Church should agree with this Church” means that Rome holds a teaching authority the other churches are bound to follow, as Dominic Unger (1952) reads it, or that Rome’s well-attested apostolic faith is a convenient standard against which error is measured, as James McCue (1964) reads it, is disputed.5

Notes

  • Reception and debate. Irenaeus singles out Rome among the apostolic churches he might have listed, and III.3.2 is quoted in the primacy debate as often as any patristic text. Unger sorts its interpreters into two groups, “the one granting the Roman Church a primacy of moral power, of sovereignty, the other denying her this and granting her a primacy of honor only.” John Henry Newman (Catholic, 1845) counts it among the early “indications of what was to be” of the papal supremacy, and Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, Paris) reads potentior principalitas as the Roman church’s “priority” of witness among the churches, neither a power nor a precedence of honour. The readings, with their proponents, are set out at Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?
  • Peter and Paul. Irenaeus names Peter and Paul as co-founders, credits Linus with the first episcopate, and cites no Petrine proof-text; Leo the Great (c. 450, on this timeline) later grounds the Roman primacy in Peter. Unger holds that the distinctive standing Irenaeus gives Rome “was due to Peter alone,” since Ephesus too was founded by Paul; McCue holds that Irenaeus’s argument concerns the public transmission of teaching, which any apostolic church could show.5
  • Irenaeus and Victor. A decade or so after writing this, Irenaeus intervened in the Quartodeciman (Easter-date) controversy, admonishing Bishop Victor of Rome not to excommunicate the churches of Asia and reminding him that his predecessors had kept peace across the disagreement. The same father who writes of Rome’s “pre-eminent authority” here admonished a Roman bishop there; the episode is set out on the Victor of Rome page (c. 190).
  • The lost Greek. Because Book III survives in full only in the old Latin version, the pivotal phrase (potiorem/potentiorem principalitatem) reaches us at one remove. The rendering “pre-eminent authority” above is the Ante-Nicene Fathers translators’ own, offered with their explicit admission that they are “far from sure” it is correct.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Irenaeus developed the succession argument against the Gnostics (the wider frame this Roman passage sits in), and appears also on other timelines as a witness. The tradition of Peter’s own mission and death at Rome — on which the specifically Petrine claim later draws — is traced on the apostle Peter.
