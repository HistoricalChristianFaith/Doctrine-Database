The Council of Sardica, canons 3–5 (343)

Dates: c. 343 (a council convened at Sardica — modern Sofia — by the emperors Constantius and Constans, its Western bishops presided over by Hosius of Cordova; the Eastern bishops withdrew, so it was not ecumenical) · Wikipedia
Summary: Sardica’s third, fourth, and fifth canons grant that a bishop deposed by his provincial colleagues may have his case reopened, and — “to honour the memory of Peter the Apostle” — may take refuge with the bishop of Rome, who could order a fresh trial by the bishops of the neighbouring province and, if he saw fit, send legates to sit as judges. This is the earliest conciliar text giving Rome a defined role in the appeals of other churches, and later Rome adduced it as proof of an appellate primacy. On the timeline it is a genuinely two-sided datum, and it carries two live qualifications: Sardica was a Western council of one party, not an ecumenical one; and in the fifth century its canons were mistaken for Nicene ones by Rome itself — a confusion the African bishops exposed in the affair of Apiarius (c. 418–424).

Claims & evidence

Canon 3: appeal to Julius of Rome, “to honour the memory of Peter”

Bishop Hosius proposes that a bishop condemned by his colleagues, who believes his case sound, may have the question reopened through the bishop of Rome:

But if perchance sentence be given against a bishop in any matter and he supposes his case to be not unsound but good, in order that the question may be reopened, let us, if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle, and let those who gave judgment write to Julius, the bishop of Rome, so that, if necessary, the case may be retried by the bishops of the neighbouring provinces and let him appoint arbiters; but if it cannot be shown that his case is of such a sort as to need a new trial, let the judgment once given not be annulled, but stand good as before.

— The Canons of the Council of Sardica, Canon III (Greek), source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV; also at New Advent).1 Canons 4 and 5 extend the provision: no successor is to be installed in a deposed bishop’s see “unless the bishop of Rome judge and render a decision as to this” (canon 4), and if the appellant asks for it, the bishop of Rome may “send presbyters a latere” to sit as judges “invested with his authority” alongside the provincial bishops (canon 5).1

What the grant is, and is not

The canons are read both ways, and both readings are honest. They plainly assign Rome a real function in the discipline of other churches — the earliest conciliar text to do so — and Rome’s later defenders pressed them as evidence of a jurisdictional, appellate primacy. But the grant is carefully bounded, and cuts against a native supremacy as much as for one:

  • It is conferred by the council, as a favour proposed by Hosius (“let us, if it seem good to your charity…”) — a right the fathers grant, not one they acknowledge Rome already to possess by Petrine descent. If Rome held such a primacy by divine right, a council of bishops would not need to bestow it.
  • The retrial itself is done by “the bishops of the neighbouring provinces,” not by Rome; Rome’s role is to order the rehearing and, at most, send legates — a right of review, not of direct judgment.
  • The motive given is to “honour the memory of Peter the Apostle” — a mark of respect for the see’s dignity, in the idiom of honour rather than of an inherited office.

The council’s standing is itself part of the datum. Sardica was summoned as an ecumenical council, but the Eastern bishops withdrew before it sat and held their own rival synod; what remained was a council of the Western party under Hosius. Its canons never entered the Eastern canonical collections as Sardican, and their authority outside the West was contested from the start.

The Apiarius affair: Sardica’s canons mistaken for Nicaea’s (c. 418–424)

The most telling episode is how these very canons were used — and exposed — three generations later. When the deposed African priest Apiarius appealed to Rome over the head of his own church, Pope Zosimus sent legates to Africa citing a canon that authorized such appeals to Rome, and attributing it to the Council of Nicaea. The African bishops, meeting at Carthage (418–419), could not find the canon in their copies of Nicaea; they wrote to Alexandria and Constantinople for authentic Eastern texts of the Nicene canons, which contained only the twenty genuine canons and not the one Rome had quoted. The disputed canon was in fact the appeal-canon of Sardica — which in the old Latin codices was copied straight after the Nicene canons, under a shared heading and running numeration, so that the two councils’ canons were read as one. Having established the error, the Africans declined to concede the appellate right and, in their letter to Pope Celestine, asked Rome not to admit appeals from Africa across the sea.2

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline: a two-sided datum. Sardica is adduced for a Roman appellate primacy (a council grants Rome a role in bishops’ appeals, “to honour the memory of Peter”) and against a native, divine-right primacy (the right is bestowed by a council, bounded to review, and belongs to a one-party Western synod whose universal authority was never conceded in the East). Both are real. The adjudication of honour-versus-jurisdiction across the whole timeline is weighed in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • The misattribution matters. That Rome in the early fifth century advanced its appellate right on the authority of Nicaea, and that the right turned out to rest on Sardica, is the single sharpest illustration of how the standing of these canons was contested. The African church — the church of Augustine — treated the discovery as reason to resist, not to submit: the case of Apiarius became the classic example, in later controversy, of a fifth-century Western church declining a Roman claim to disciplinary control.
  • Companion conciliar data. Sardica’s bounded, council-granted role should be read beside Nicaea’s custom-based regional spheres (325), Constantinople’s ranking of the sees by civil status (381), and Chalcedon’s grounding of Rome’s privileges in its being “the royal city” (451).
