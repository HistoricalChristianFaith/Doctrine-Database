The Council of Sardica, canons 3–5 (343)

Dates: c. 343 (a council convened at Sardica — modern Sofia — by the emperors Constantius and Constans, its Western bishops presided over by Hosius of Cordova; the Eastern bishops withdrew, so it was not ecumenical) · Wikipedia
Summary: Sardica’s third, fourth, and fifth canons grant that a bishop deposed by his provincial colleagues may have his case reopened, and — “to honour the memory of Peter the Apostle” — may take refuge with the bishop of Rome, who could order a fresh trial by the bishops of the neighbouring province and, if he saw fit, send legates to sit as judges. This is the earliest extant conciliar text giving Rome a defined role in the appeals of other churches, and Rome later cited it for an appellate primacy. Sardica was a Western council from which the Eastern bishops withdrew, not an ecumenical one; and in the fifth century Rome cited its appeal canon as a canon of Nicaea, an attribution the African bishops disputed in the affair of Apiarius (c. 418–424).

Claims & evidence

Canon 3: appeal to Julius of Rome, “to honour the memory of Peter”

Bishop Hosius proposes that a bishop condemned by his colleagues, who believes his case good, may have the question reopened through the bishop of Rome:

But if perchance sentence be given against a bishop in any matter and he supposes his case to be not unsound but good, in order that the question may be reopened, let us, if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle, and let those who gave judgment write to Julius, the bishop of Rome, so that, if necessary, the case may be retried by the bishops of the neighbouring provinces and let him appoint arbiters; but if it cannot be shown that his case is of such a sort as to need a new trial, let the judgment once given not be annulled, but stand good as before.

— The Canons of the Council of Sardica, Canon III (Greek), source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV; also at New Advent).1 Canons 4 and 5 extend the provision: no successor is to be installed in a deposed bishop’s see “unless the bishop of Rome judge and render a decision as to this” (canon 4), and if the appellant asks for it, the bishop of Rome may “send presbyters a latere” to sit as judges “invested with his authority” alongside the provincial bishops (canon 5).1

The terms of the grant

The canons assign Rome a function in the discipline of other churches, the earliest extant conciliar text to do so, and were later pressed as evidence of a jurisdictional, appellate primacy. Their terms:

  • It is proposed by Hosius for the council’s assent (“let us, if it seem good to your charity…”). In the notes of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers edition, Tillemont comments: “This form is very strong to shew that it was a right which the Pope had not had hitherto”; the same notes report Edmund Richer’s view that the provision was “of a purely temporary character,” and Hefele’s quotation of the Protestant Spittler against it.1
  • The retrial itself is done by “the bishops of the neighbouring provinces,” not by Rome; Rome’s role is to order the rehearing, to decide before a deposed bishop’s see is filled, and, if it sees fit, to send legates.
  • The motive given is to “honour the memory of Peter the Apostle.”

The council’s standing also bears on the canons. Sardica was summoned as an ecumenical council, but the Eastern bishops withdrew before it sat and held their own rival synod; what remained was a council of the Western party under Hosius. In the East the Quinisext Council (692), in its second canon, set its seal on the canons “of Sardica” among those of the earlier councils it confirmed,2 and the Greek canonists Zonaras and Balsamon later commented on canon 4.1

The Apiarius affair: Sardica’s canon cited as Nicaea’s (c. 418–424)

Three generations later the canons were cited in the case of Apiarius. When the deposed African priest Apiarius appealed to Rome against the judgment of his own church, Pope Zosimus sent legates to Africa citing a canon that authorized such appeals to Rome, and attributing it to the Council of Nicaea. The African bishops, meeting at Carthage (418–419), could not find the canon in their copies of Nicaea; they wrote to Alexandria and Constantinople for authentic Eastern texts of the Nicene canons, which contained only the twenty canons and not the one Rome had quoted. The disputed canon was the appeal-canon of Sardica — which in the old Latin codices was copied straight after the Nicene canons, under a shared heading and running numeration, so that the two councils’ canons were read as one. Having found the canon to be Sardica’s, the Africans declined to concede the appellate right and, in their letter to Pope Celestine, asked Rome not to admit appeals from Africa across the sea.3

Notes

  • Reception and debate. Spittler, quoted by Hefele, reads the canons as general and permanent, and John Chapman reads them as adding to an appellate right the Pope already held; Tillemont reads it as a right “which the Pope had not had hitherto” and Edmund Richer as a temporary measure, proposed “if it seem good to your charity” at a Western synod from which the Eastern bishops had withdrawn. The readings, with their proponents, are set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?
  • The attribution to Nicaea. In the early fifth century Rome advanced its appellate right on the authority of Nicaea, and the African bishops found the canon to be Sardica’s and declined the appeal-right. John Chapman (Benedictine, 1928) holds that “in the fifth century the Popes appealed occasionally to the principles contained in the canons, under the mistaken belief that they were Nicene,” and reads the canons as adding to the Pope’s “admitted right of hearing appeals at Rome in a Council called by himself.”4
  • Companion conciliar data. Sardica’s canons stand beside Nicaea’s custom-based regional spheres (325), Constantinople’s ranking of the sees by civil status (381), and Chalcedon’s grounding of Rome’s privileges in its being “the royal city” (451).
