1 Clement and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 96 AD (1 Clement, written c. 95–96; Clement floruit as bishop of Rome, d. c. 99) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datum on this timeline. Around 96 the church of Rome sent a long letter across the sea to Corinth to end a schism that had deposed the Corinthian presbyters — rebuking the ringleaders, demanding the deposed men be reinstated, and warning that those who disobey “the words spoken by Him through us” incur “transgression and serious danger.” Adrian Fortescue, Johannes Quasten and Benedict XVI cite the intervention as an exercise of Roman primacy; Philip Schaff and J. B. Lightfoot read it as the Roman church’s authority as a community; James McCue reads it as fraternal admonition; Nicholas Afanasiev reads it as the witness of a church holding priority without power. This page reports what the letter says; the readings are set out at the disputed reading, Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? Clement’s bearing on church order proper (the two‑order ministry, bishop = presbyter, apostolic succession) is treated separately on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

The salutation: a letter sent in the name of the Roman church (1 Clement, salutation)

The letter opens not over any man’s name but corporately, church to church:

The Church of God which sojourns at Rome, to the Church of God sojourning at Corinth, to them that are called and sanctified by the will of God, through our Lord Jesus Christ…

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, salutation, source.1 The name “Clement” appears nowhere in the text; the tradition that he wrote it comes from later witnesses (Dionysius of Corinth, Irenaeus, Eusebius, Jerome), not from the letter’s own claim. It is the Roman church that speaks, in the first person plural (“through us,” “we sent”); McCue concludes that any authority the letter carries is “that of the Roman community, not of an individual within that community,” while Fortescue speaks of “a Bishop of Rome, the Church of Rome, if a man prefers to put it that way” (see the disputed reading). It is the earliest extant instance of the Roman church writing as a body to another church.

Rome intervenes in another city’s dispute and demands the presbyters be restored (1 Clement 47, 44, 57)

The occasion is a revolt at Corinth that had ejected lawful presbyters. Rome treats the affair as its concern, noting that the scandal “has reached not only us, but those also who are unconnected with us”:

It is disgraceful, beloved, yea, highly disgraceful, and unworthy of your Christian profession, that such a thing should be heard of as that the most stedfast and ancient Church of the Corinthians should, on account of one or two persons, engage in sedition against its presbyters.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 47, source.2 The remedy Rome presses is concrete: those it calls blamelessly serving presbyters “cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry” (ch. 44), and the authors of the sedition must yield:

Ye therefore, who laid the foundation of this sedition, submit yourselves to the presbyters, and receive correction so as to repent, bending the knees of your hearts.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 57, source.2 Quasten and Fortescue, who read a Roman primacy in the letter, point to this: a letter from one church, reaching into the governance of another, prescribing who must hold office there and demanding that its dissenters submit.

Obedience to “the words spoken by Him through us” (1 Clement 59, 63)

At its close the letter frames disobedience to its counsel as disobedience to God:

If, however, any shall disobey the words spoken by Him through us, let them know that they will involve themselves in transgression and serious danger…

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 59.3 And again:

For joy and rejoicing will ye afford us if, becoming obedient to the things that have been written by us, ye put an end, by the suggestion of the Holy Spirit, to the unlawful wrath of your discord, according to the supplication which we have made concerning peace and unity in this epistle.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 63.4 Here the letter presents its written direction as given “by the suggestion of the Holy Spirit,” and warns that disobedience is “serious danger.” (Chapters 59–63 survived in a leaf missing from the long-known Codex Alexandrinus and were recovered from the fuller Greek, Latin, Syriac and Coptic witnesses in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; they are quoted here from those complete texts.)

Entreaty, Peter and Paul, and the occasion of the letter

McCue and Afanasiev, who read the letter as admonition or witness rather than command, point to other features of it:

  • Entreaty. Alongside the demand to submit, Rome casts itself as a fellow-sufferer and suppliant — it writes of “the supplication which we have made concerning peace and unity,” asks the Corinthians to “afford us” joy by obeying, and closes by begging them to send its own messengers (Claudius Ephebus, Valerius Bito, Fortunatus) “back speedily to us in peace and with joy.”4
  • Peter and Paul. Peter and Paul are named together, a little earlier in the letter, as the church’s recent martyrs (ch. 5); the letter grounds its appeal in Scripture, the apostolic ordering of the ministry, and the peace of the church.
  • A request from Corinth? The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors, in a bracketed note on the text, remark that the Corinthians asked this of their brethren, the personal friends of their apostle Paul; on that view the letter answers a request within a standing bond between two churches.1

Notes

  • Reception and debate. That Rome wrote to Corinth is not in question; how to read the intervention is disputed. Benedict XVI calls it “a first exercise of the Roman primacy after St Peter’s death”; Lightfoot, “the first step towards papal domination,” a primacy “not of the episcopate, but of the church”; McCue, “an exhortation to repentance addressed by one community to another”; Afanasiev, “the Church bearing witness about what had happened within the Church.” The readings, with their proponents, are set out at the disputed reading, Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?
  • No single ruling bishop named. The letter neither names a single ruling bishop at Corinth to be obeyed nor issues in the name of a single bishop at Rome; it presupposes a college of presbyters at Corinth (see the Government of the Church page) and speaks in the name of the Roman church as a body.
  • How the later tradition read it. Corinth was still reading it publicly in worship some seventy years later (Dionysius of Corinth to Soter of Rome, c. 170), and Irenaeus and Eusebius cite it as a monument of the Roman church’s early witness.
  • The translation and text. The salutation and chs. 44, 47, 57 are quoted from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers); chs. 59 and 63, absent from the older single manuscript, are quoted from the complete text (New Advent’s ANF supplement and the Hoole translation respectively). Chapter numbers follow the standard division.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Clement also stands as a witness on the government of the church (the two-order ministry and apostolic succession), the ministerial priesthood (the divinely ordered, succeeding ministry that presents the church’s offering), and the Old Testament canon (his use of Wisdom and Judith).
