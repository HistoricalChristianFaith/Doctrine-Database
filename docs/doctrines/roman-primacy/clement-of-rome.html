1 Clement and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 96 AD (1 Clement, written c. 95–96; Clement floruit as bishop of Rome, d. c. 99) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datum on this timeline. Around 96 the church of Rome, unbidden by any higher authority, sent a long letter across the sea to Corinth to end a schism that had wrongfully deposed the Corinthian presbyters — rebuking the ringleaders, demanding the deposed men be reinstated, and warning that those who disobey “the words spoken by Him through us” incur “transgression and serious danger.” That one church should so intervene in the internal affairs of another is adduced as the earliest evidence of a Roman primacy of jurisdiction. This page reports the datum and weighs what the letter does and does not claim; the adversarial adjudication — intervention as jurisdiction or as fraternal admonition — is routed to the summary’s Arguments section. Clement’s bearing on church order proper (the two‑order ministry, bishop = presbyter, apostolic succession) is treated separately on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

A letter sent in the name of the whole Roman church — no bishop of Rome named as its author (1 Clement, salutation)

The letter opens not over any man’s name but corporately, church to church:

The Church of God which sojourns at Rome, to the Church of God sojourning at Corinth, to them that are called and sanctified by the will of God, through our Lord Jesus Christ…

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, salutation, source.1 The name “Clement” appears nowhere in the text; the tradition that he wrote it comes from later witnesses (Dionysius of Corinth, Irenaeus, Eusebius, Jerome), not from the letter’s own claim. It is the Roman church that speaks, in the first person plural (“through us,” “we sent”), not a monarch-bishop issuing a decree. This cuts two ways, and both belong on the record: it is the earliest instance of the Roman church acting as a body toward another church — and it is not the act of a single Roman bishop claiming to command by his office.

Rome intervenes in another city’s dispute and demands the presbyters be restored (1 Clement 47, 44, 57)

The occasion is a revolt at Corinth that had ejected lawful presbyters. Rome treats the affair as its concern, noting that the scandal “has reached not only us, but those also who are unconnected with us”:

It is disgraceful, beloved, yea, highly disgraceful, and unworthy of your Christian profession, that such a thing should be heard of as that the most stedfast and ancient Church of the Corinthians should, on account of one or two persons, engage in sedition against its presbyters.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 47, source.2 The remedy Rome presses is concrete: those it calls blamelessly serving presbyters “cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry” (ch. 44), and the authors of the sedition must yield:

Ye therefore, who laid the foundation of this sedition, submit yourselves to the presbyters, and receive correction so as to repent, bending the knees of your hearts.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 57, source.2 The datum the primacy reading rests on is exactly this: an unsolicited letter from one church, reaching into the governance of another, prescribing who must hold office there and demanding that its dissenters submit.

The commanding note: obedience owed to “the words spoken by Him through us” (1 Clement 59, 63)

At its close the letter frames disobedience to its counsel as disobedience to God:

If, however, any shall disobey the words spoken by Him through us, let them know that they will involve themselves in transgression and serious danger…

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 59.3 And again:

For joy and rejoicing will ye afford us if, becoming obedient to the things that have been written by us, ye put an end, by the suggestion of the Holy Spirit, to the unlawful wrath of your discord, according to the supplication which we have made concerning peace and unity in this epistle.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 63.4 This is the strongest primacy-freighted language in the letter: Rome does not merely advise but presents its written direction as Spirit-borne, so that resisting it is grave danger. (Chapters 59–63 survived in a leaf missing from the long-known Codex Alexandrinus and were recovered from the fuller Greek, Latin, Syriac and Coptic witnesses in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; they are quoted here from those complete texts.)

What the letter stops short of saying

The honest counterweight sits inside the same letter, and every clause of it is load-bearing for the fraternal-admonition reading:

  • It entreats as much as it commands. Alongside the demand to submit, Rome casts itself as a fellow-sufferer and suppliant — it writes of “the supplication which we have made concerning peace and unity,” asks the Corinthians to “afford us” joy by obeying, and closes by begging them to send its own messengers (Claudius Ephebus, Valerius Bito, Fortunatus) “back speedily to us in peace and with joy.” The register is that of an anxious elder church, not a tribunal issuing sentence.4
  • It invokes no Petrine succession. Peter and Paul are named together, a little earlier in the letter, as the church’s recent martyr-heroes (ch. 5) — but Rome grounds its appeal in Scripture, the apostolic ordering of the ministry, and the peace of the church, never in a commission descending from Peter to a Roman see. The theory that Rome commands because it is Peter’s chair is entirely absent here; it is read back into the letter from later centuries.
  • The intervention may have been invited. An early editorial note on the text observes that the Corinthians appear to have asked this of their brethren, the personal friends of their apostle Paul; the letter is a response within a standing bond between two apostolic churches, not necessarily an unsolicited assertion of supremacy.1

Notes

  • Jurisdiction or fraternal admonition? That Rome intervened is undisputed; what the intervention proves is the contested question. On one reading it is the first exercise of a Roman primacy of jurisdiction; on another it is the fraternal concern of one apostolic church — the senior church of the imperial capital, and one that had known Paul — for another in crisis, of a piece with the mutual counsel churches gave each other in the sub-apostolic age. The two readings are weighed on their own merits, not asserted here, in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • Absence of a monarch-bishop cuts against the maximal reading. The letter neither names a single ruling bishop at Corinth to be obeyed nor issues from a single ruling bishop at Rome; it presupposes a college of presbyters at Corinth (see the Government of the Church page) and speaks in the name of the Roman church as a body. A primacy of one bishop over the church is not yet in view in the letter’s own self-understanding, whatever weight the act of intervention is given.
  • How the later tradition read it. The letter’s standing was high: Corinth was still reading it publicly in worship some seventy years later (Dionysius of Corinth to Soter of Rome, c. 170), and Irenaeus and Eusebius cite it as a monument of the Roman church’s early witness. That reception is itself part of the primacy dossier — it shows Rome’s voice being valued abroad — but reception as an honoured letter is a different thing from acknowledged jurisdiction, and the distinction is exactly what the Arguments section keeps in view.
  • The translation and text. The salutation and chs. 44, 47, 57 are quoted from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers); chs. 59 and 63, absent from the older single manuscript, are quoted from the complete text (New Advent’s ANF supplement and the Hoole translation respectively). Chapter numbers follow the standard division.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Clement also stands as a witness on the government of the church (the two-order ministry and apostolic succession), the ministerial priesthood (the divinely ordered, succeeding ministry that presents the church’s offering), and the Old Testament canon (his use of Wisdom and Judith).
