The Council of Nicaea, canon 6 (325)

Dates: 325 (the First Ecumenical Council, convened at Nicaea in Bithynia by the emperor Constantine; canon 6 among its twenty disciplinary canons) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest ecumenical council’s sixth canon confirms the “ancient customs” by which the great sees governed their surrounding regions: Alexandria over Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis, Antioch over its provinces, and — named only as the parallel that fixes the measure — Rome over its own. On the primacy timeline the canon is a counter-datum: three hundred bishops, legislating for the whole church, treat Rome not as the universal head above the rest but as one great see among co-equal metropolitans, its regional authority the same in kind as Alexandria’s and grounded in custom, not in a transmitted Petrine commission. What the canon regulates is the metropolitan system; what it presupposes is that even Rome’s reach is bounded and customary.

Claims & evidence

Canon 6: Rome named as one great see among the metropolitans

The canon opens by confirming the settled spheres of the three chief sees. Rome appears not as the rule but as the illustrative parallel — Alexandria has jurisdiction over its regions because the like is customary for Rome:

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

— The Canons of the 318 Holy Fathers Assembled… at Nice, Canon VI, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV).1 Two features are read against a jurisdictional primacy. First, Rome is parallel, not paramount: its authority over its own region is cited to measure Alexandria’s, the two set side by side as instances of one custom, with Antioch and “the other provinces” keeping their privileges on the same footing. Second, the ground given is “ancient customs” (ta archaia ethē) — the settled practice of the churches, confirmed by the council — not any commission of Peter to the Roman see. The canon knows a Roman sphere; it does not know a Roman supremacy over Alexandria, Antioch, and the rest.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline. Canon 6 is the first time an ecumenical council legislates on the standing of the great sees, and it does so on a model of plural, custom-based spheres. Roman primacy readings can point to it as evidence Rome held first rank (the canon assumes its regional authority as the known measure); anti-jurisdiction readings point to it as evidence that Rome’s rank was of the same kind as Alexandria’s and Antioch’s — a precedence of honour and a defined regional jurisdiction, not a universal one. The canon settles neither the honour-versus-jurisdiction question nor the source of Rome’s standing; that adjudication is weighed in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • What the canon does not say. It grounds the arrangement in custom, names no Petrine title for Rome, and gives Rome no appellate reach into Alexandria’s or Antioch’s affairs — each see keeps “its own privileges.” Rome’s later defenders would appeal to it for Rome’s primacy; Percival’s own note observes that when a pope later refused to acknowledge the honour granted Constantinople at the councils of 381 and 451, “he was in reality defending the principle laid down in the canon of Nice, that in such matters the ancient customs should continue” — a principle of settled regional custom, precisely what the canon confirms.
  • Companion conciliar data. The same through-line runs through Sardica (343), which grants Rome a limited role in bishops’ appeals; Constantinople (381), canon 3, which ranks Constantinople next after Rome “because Constantinople is New Rome”; and Chalcedon (451), canon 28, which grounds Rome’s privileges in its being “the royal city.” Read together, the councils frame Rome’s pre-eminence in terms of custom and civil status as much as apostolic right — the tension the timeline exists to display.
