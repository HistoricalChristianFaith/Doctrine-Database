The Council of Nicaea, canon 6 (325)

Dates: 325 (the First Ecumenical Council, convened at Nicaea in Bithynia by the emperor Constantine; canon 6 among its twenty disciplinary canons) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest ecumenical council’s sixth canon confirms the “ancient customs” by which the great sees governed their surrounding regions: Alexandria over Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis, Antioch over its provinces, and — named as the parallel for Alexandria — Rome over its own. The canon grounds the arrangement in custom and names no Petrine title for Rome. How it bears on the kind of primacy Rome held is disputed; the readings are set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?

Claims & evidence

Canon 6: Rome named beside Alexandria and Antioch

The canon opens by confirming the settled spheres of the three chief sees. Rome appears as the parallel: Alexandria has jurisdiction over its regions because the like is customary for Rome:

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

— The Canons of the 318 Holy Fathers Assembled… at Nice, Canon VI, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV).1 Two features of the canon are cited in the debate. Rome’s authority over its own region is cited to measure Alexandria’s, the two set side by side as instances of one custom, with Antioch and “the other provinces” keeping their privileges on the same footing; and the ground given is “ancient customs” (ta archaia ethē), the settled practice of the churches confirmed by the council.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Canon 6 is the earliest extant legislation of an ecumenical council on the standing of the great sees, and it is framed in terms of plural, custom-based spheres. The readings, with their proponents, are set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?
  • Custom as the ground. Later bishops of Rome appealed to the Nicene canons for Rome’s standing; Percival’s own note observes that when a pope later refused to acknowledge the honour granted Constantinople at the councils of 381 and 451, “he was in reality defending the principle laid down in the canon of Nice, that in such matters the ancient customs should continue” — the principle of settled regional custom that the canon confirms.
  • Companion conciliar data. The other conciliar witnesses on this timeline are Sardica (343), which grants Rome a limited role in bishops’ appeals; Constantinople (381), canon 3, which ranks Constantinople next after Rome “because Constantinople is New Rome”; and Chalcedon (451), canon 28, which gives Constantinople “equal privileges” with Rome. How the four councils bear on the kind of primacy Rome held is set out at the disputed reading.
