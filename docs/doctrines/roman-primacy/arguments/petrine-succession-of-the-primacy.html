Does the commission to Peter in Matthew 16 descend to his Roman successors as a standing jurisdiction?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — even granting that Peter is the rock and holds the keys, the leap to a transmissible office lodged at Rome is not in the text: Matthew names no successor, no see, and no Rome; the same binding-and-loosing is given verbatim to all the apostles (Matthew 18:18) and to all (John 20:23); and the strongest steelman — the keys as an Isaiah-22 stewardship-office — shows at most that such an office could pass on, never that it passed to a Roman bishop · advanced on this timeline by Leo the Great and, earlier, Stephen of Rome

Related arguments: the rock and the keys — see also Is the “rock” of Matthew 16:18 Peter, his confession, or Christ?

Across doctrines — the same binding-and-loosing keys (Matthew 16:19; 18:18; John 20:23) are pressed for rival conclusions elsewhere on this wiki. Here the grant of that power to all the apostles (Matthew 18:18; John 20:23) is the standing defeater against an exclusive Petrine-Roman jurisdiction; the identical power is read as a delegated priestly authority handed on through episcopal ordination, and as the keys of the whole gathered congregation. The exegetical fulcrum is the same in all three: to whom was the power given, and does it transmit? And the perpetuity half of the same verse — “the gates of hell shall not prevail against it” (Matthew 16:18) — is likewise pressed both here (as the church’s abiding Petrine office) and, in mirror image, for the free-church Baptist succession that runs around Rome; whether that clause promises any traceable visible institutional succession at all is weighed at “Does ‘the gates of hell shall not prevail’ promise an unbroken visible succession?” (assessment: weak).

The claim

…as that remains which Peter believed in Christ, so that remains which Christ instituted in Peter… the blessed Peter… has not abandoned the helm of the Church… [in the bishop of Rome] he may be recognized and honoured, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds… not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops.

— Leo the Great, Sermon III §II–IV; the sermon’s own headings read “From Christ and Through S. Peter the Priesthood is Handed on in Perpetuity” and “S. Peter’s Work is Still Carried Out by His Successors.”1 The claim is not merely that Matthew 16 honours Peter (weighed at the identity of the rock), but that it founds a perpetual office whose authority descends to the bishop of Rome as Peter’s heir — so that the pope exercises Peter’s own jurisdiction over the whole church. This is the inference the doctrine actually needs, and it is weighed here on its own.

Who advances it

The developed form is Leo’s (c. 450), the fullest patristic statement of a standing Petrine office lodged in the Roman see. Its earliest datable appearance in a jurisdictional claim is Stephen of Rome (c. 256), who — his opponents report — “boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter” in order to bind the other churches over rebaptism.2 Modern Catholic apologetics rests the reading above all on the Isaiah 22:22 background to the keys: the “key of the house of David” laid on Eliakim’s shoulder is the badge of a royal-steward office, and offices — Shebna gives way to Eliakim — have successors; so the keys handed to Peter institute a transmissible stewardship of Christ’s kingdom, not a personal honour that dies with the man.3

The evidence for

The keys evoke a transmissible office. The strongest case is the Isaiah 22 allusion, and it should be granted at full strength. Matthew 16:19 — keys, plus “open/shut” power rendered as binding and loosing — reproduces the language of Isaiah 22:22 so closely that the echo is hard to deny; and in Isaiah the key belongs to an office in the Davidic household, one that passes from a deposed steward (Shebna) to his replacement (Eliakim). If Jesus deliberately clothes Peter in the robe of the royal major-domo, the natural implication is a role that, like its OT type, outlives its first holder. To this the proponent adds the perpetuity note of v. 18 — “the gates of hell shall not prevail against it” — read as promising the church, and so its foundational office, an endurance across time; and the singular address (“to thee I will give”) as marking Peter out from the college even where the college later shares the power.3

And the see is Peter’s. If the office is transmissible and Peter ended his life as bishop of Rome, the succession runs through Rome by the ordinary rule that a see’s bishop inherits its founder’s charge — the step Stephen and Leo make explicit, and on which the whole Roman claim is built (the Roman-death tradition is traced at the apostle Peter).

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The Isaiah 22 allusion is real and is the argument’s one strong leg: the keys do carry the connotation of a stewardship-office, and a stewardship-office is, by its OT type, the kind of thing that can have successors. So far the reading is not fanciful; it identifies a genuine feature of the image. The perpetuity of the church in v. 18 is also fairly read.

Where it breaks: the text says nothing of successors, a see, or Rome. The Isaiah type establishes only that a keys-office could pass on; it cannot say to whom, and Matthew supplies no answer. There is no mention of a successor, no mention of a see, and above all no mention of Rome — the one thing the doctrine most needs is the one thing wholly absent from the proof-text. The allusion gets the proponent a transmissible stewardship in the abstract; lodging that stewardship in the Roman episcopate requires importing, from outside Matthew 16, both the tradition that Peter was bishop of Rome and the premise that a founder’s jurisdiction (not merely his memory) descends to a local see — neither of which the passage states or implies.3

The power is given to all the apostles. The singular address does not isolate a unique Petrine prerogative, because the identical binding-and-loosing is conferred on the whole body two chapters later — “Verily I say unto you [plural], Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth” (Matthew 18:18) — and again at John 20:23. This is not a modern debating point but the reading of the fathers themselves: even the Latin commentators who make Peter the rock grant that the keys were given to all — Rabanus Maurus, “this power of binding and loosing, though it seems given by the Lord to Peter alone, is indeed given also to the other Apostles, and is even now in the Bishops and Presbyters in every Church”; Theophylact, “even though the words ‘I will give unto thee’ were spoken to Peter alone, yet they were given to all the apostles.”4 A power the whole college holds cannot, without more, ground a jurisdiction of one member’s successors over the rest.

Tertullian saw the leap and denied it. The earliest surviving text to argue the point — Tertullian, On Modesty 21 (c. 210) — grants that the grant was Peter’s and precisely for that reason refuses to let it pass to a see: those who “presume that the power… has derived to you, that is, to every Church akin to Peter” subvert “the manifest intention of the Lord,” for the gift was conferred “personally upon Peter,” “not to the Church which consists of a number of bishops.” The transmission the doctrine asserts was contested from the first, on the proof-text’s own wording.5

Even friends of the Petrine reading stop short. Oscar Cullmann and D. A. Carson — who grant that Peter is the rock — deny that any Roman succession follows: the text speaks of Peter, not of a successor, and Peter’s foundational role is by nature unrepeatable. And the timeline itself shows the inference resisted wherever it was pressed as jurisdiction — Victor’s excommunication rebuffed (c. 190), Stephen’s Petrine claim refused by Cyprian and Firmilian (256), Nicaea ranking Rome as one great see among the metropolitans by “ancient custom” (325). The claim of descent is a fifth-century theory read back onto a first-century text, not a datum the text yields.3

Assessment

Weak. Judged on its best available case — the Isaiah 22 stewardship-office, granted at full strength — the succession reading does not hold. The Isaiah type shows only that a keys-office is the kind of thing that can have successors; it is silent on whether this one does, and utterly silent on Rome, which Matthew 16 never names. The gap between “Peter received the keys” and “the bishop of Rome wields them by succession” is bridged not by the text but by two imported premises — a Petrine Roman episcopate and the descent of jurisdiction to a see — while the same binding-and-loosing is handed to all the apostles (Matthew 18:18; John 20:23), the earliest exegete of the point (Tertullian) reads the grant as personal and non-transferable, and even modern defenders of the Petrine reading refuse the succession. This is not a question that turns on the supernatural — grant every miracle and providence, and the exegesis still under-determines the conclusion, which is a matter of what the text says and what the early church did. The office-type is a real observation; the descent-to-Rome is a conjecture beyond the evidence, and on the evidence it is weak. (That Peter himself was the church’s foundation-stone — the separable, and stronger, half of the proof-text — is weighed at the identity of the rock.)
