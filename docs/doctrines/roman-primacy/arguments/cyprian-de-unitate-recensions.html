Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

Disputed reading · on the Primacy of Rome · witness: Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

Related readings: Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Do the great councils show a Roman primacy of honour or of jurisdiction?

The passage

Chapter 4 of On the Unity of the Church (c. 251) survives in two forms. A second recension, long called the Primacy Text, carries three clauses absent from the received text. In the Ante-Nicene Fathers apparatus they read:

[after “Feed my sheep”:] Upon him, being one, He builds His Church, and commits His sheep to be fed.

[after “the beginning proceeds from unity”:] And the primacy is given to Peter, that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See; and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.

[and, further on:] Who deserts the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded, does he trust that he is in the Church?

— the Primacy-Text additions to On the Unity of the Church 4–5, source.1 The received text of the same chapter continues: “Assuredly the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.” Chapter 5 adds: “The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur).2

The question

Two questions are joined here: whether the Primacy-Text clauses are Cyprian’s own, and whether, with or without them, the chapter places in Peter’s see at Rome a primacy to which the other churches owe communion. Cyprian is the leading Latin bishop of the mid-third century, so the answer bears on what the Western church of his day held about Rome.

The clauses are Cyprian’s, and place the chair of Peter at Rome

Cyprian wrote the Primacy Text, and by “the chair of Peter” he meant the Roman see, communion with which marks membership in the Church.

Held by: John Chapman (Benedictine, Revue Bénédictine, 1902–03; Studies on the Early Papacy, 1928);3 Pierre Batiffol (Catholic, Primitive Catholicism, 1911);4 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, The Early Papacy, 1920);5 and the Abbé Freppel (Catholic, S. Cyprien, 1865).6

On this reading the Primacy-Text clauses are Cyprian’s own words and say what they appear to say. Freppel took the giving of the keys and the charge to feed the flock as “the charter of investiture of the papacy,” resting on this passage.6 Chapman set every phrase of the alternative passage beside Cyprian’s other writings to show how it is “saturated with Cyprianic expressions,” and concluded that the hypothesis “that St. Cyprian himself composed the alternative passage” accounts for all the evidence: “It is not a pro-Roman forgery.” He held that Cyprian introduced it “for the benefit of Maximus and Nicostratus,” the Roman confessors who had joined Novatian’s schism.3 Batiffol, accepting “with Harnack, Dom Chapman’s attribution of both text A and text B to St. Cyprian,” held the Primacy Text to be the first edition, written against Novatian’s schism at Rome, and the received text Cyprian’s later correction; the text is old, quoted by Pope Pelagius II towards the end of the sixth century.4 Batiffol paraphrases the Primacy Text’s question as Cyprian put it to the Roman schismatics: “Can the Roman who deserts that cathedra which is peculiarly that of Peter — that on which the Saviour founded the Church when He said Tu es Petrus — believe that he is in the Church? In other words, to abandon the cathedra of the bishop is everywhere an impiety but at Rome more so than anywhere else.”4 Cyprian’s letters use the same language of Rome. Writing to Cornelius of Rome in the same years, he complains of schismatics who dared “to set sail and to bear letters from schismatic and profane persons to the throne of Peter, and to the chief church whence priestly unity takes its source.”7 Fortescue writes of Cyprian: “Twice he identifies communion with the Pope with membership in the Church.”5 Chapman, who judged that Cyprian held no consistent theory of church government, found him “quite convinced that the Pope has some undefined sphere of authority,” pointing to Cyprian’s request that Stephen write to the bishops of Gaul so that the Novatianist Marcianus of Arles might be replaced.3 On this reading Cyprian, in both recensions, treats the chair of Peter as the Roman see, the “chief church” communion with which marks membership in the one Church.

The clauses are interpolated; Cyprian’s text makes every bishop heir of Peter’s chair

The Primacy-Text clauses are later additions; Cyprian’s own text makes Peter the beginning of one episcopate that every bishop holds, and the chair of Peter is that episcopate.

Held by: the editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers (vol. V);8 Edward White Benson (Anglican, Archbishop of Canterbury, Cyprian: His Life, His Times, His Work, 1897);6 and Hugo Koch (Cyprian und der römische Primat, 1910).3

On this reading the Primacy-Text clauses are not Cyprian’s. Benson writes: “The words are spurious. The history of their interpolation may be distinctly traced even now,” and calls it a history “which well may make it the most interesting of literary forgeries.” He traces their history through the printed editions, which first carried them in Paul Manutius’s Roman edition of 1563,4 and through the Gallican assembly’s appeal to the printed text in 1682.6 The Ante-Nicene editors judge the “primacy is given to Peter” clause, “as well as the one a few lines before,” to be “beyond all question spurious,” and the “chair of Peter” clause “undoubtedly spurious”: the treatise “has been subjected to falsifying interpolations, long since exposed and detected.”8 What Cyprian wrote is the received text, which grounds unity in Peter as its beginning and in the same breath makes the other apostles “the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power.” Koch, as Chapman quotes him, read Peter in the chapter as “not the principle, not the cause and centre, but merely the starting-point in time and the means of recognition of Church unity.”3 The Ante-Nicene editors read Cyprian’s praise of Rome in the same light. Of “the throne of Peter, and … the chief church whence priestly unity takes its source” they write: The Apostolic See of the West was necessarily all this in the eyes of an unambitious faithful Western co-bishop; but the letter itself proves that it was not the See of one who had any authority over or apart from his co-bishops. Let us not read into his expressions ideas which are an after-thought, and which conflict with the life and all the testimony of Cyprian.7 They gloss the image of Peter’s chair as Cyprian uses it: “i.e., the universal episcopate is the chair of Peter,” and call the maxim that follows, the one episcopate held whole by each bishop, “the essence of the treatise.”8 On this reading Cyprian’s own text grounds unity in Peter as the beginning of one episcopate that every bishop holds whole, and the chair of Peter is that universal episcopate rather than a see set over the others.

Peter the source of one episcopate that all bishops share; the chair of Peter is that episcopate

Peter’s primacy in Cyprian is the origin and figure of a single episcopate shared by every bishop, so that the chair of Peter belongs to the whole episcopate.

Held by: Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, St Sergius Institute, Paris; “The Church Which Presides in Love,” repr. 1992);9 Maurice Bévenot (Jesuit, Journal of Theological Studies, 1954), on the authorship of the two versions only.10

Bévenot holds both versions Cyprian’s own, reversing the order of composition Chapman had proposed;10 on this reading the Primacy-Text clauses, as Cyprian’s, are to be read whole. The sentence that gives Peter the primacy gives it “that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See,” and at once continues: “and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.” Peter’s primacy is the figure and origin of the Church’s oneness, the theme of a treatise written in the schisms of 251, and the episcopate it founds is shared: “the episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole.”2 Afanasiev states the same sense of the chair: “This Throne of Peter is held by the whole episcopate, so that every bishop is Peter’s successor, but only insofar as he is part of the episcopate,” and “every member of the one episcopate possesses the chair of Peter in common with his fellows.”9 Five years later, opening the Seventh Council of Carthage on 1 September 256, during his dispute with Stephen of Rome over rebaptism, he said: “For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop … has his own proper right of judgment.”11 Firmilian of Caesarea, writing to Cyprian in the same dispute, answered Stephen’s appeal to Peter: “while you think that all may be excommunicated by you, you have excommunicated yourself alone from all.”12 For Afanasiev every bishop holds the whole of the one episcopate in his own city, so that the chair of Peter is the one episcopate as such, founded on Peter and held in common by all its members.9
