Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as evidence for a Roman primacy of jurisdiction. Two questions are intertwined here and are best answered together. The textual one — is the “Primacy Text” recension of chapter 4 (“the primacy is given to Peter … one See”) authentic, or a later papal interpolation? — is now largely settled the way that helps the primacy reading: the strongest modern scholarship (Maurice Bévenot) holds both recensions Cyprian’s own, so the old Protestant “forgery” charge falls and the argument may be granted its best text. But granting the Primacy Text does not deliver the doctrinal conclusion. Even on that recension Cyprian makes Peter the source and figure of a unity in which “they are all shepherds” and the one Rock “is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent”; his settled ecclesiology — the episcopate “one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” — makes every bishop the heir of Peter’s chair; and within five years he acted the principle out, convening eighty-seven bishops on the ground that “no one of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops,” and refusing to let Stephen of Rome’s appeal to his “succession from Peter” bind the African churches. The Petrine imagery grounds episcopal collegiality, not Roman monarchy · the datum sits on this timeline at Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

The claim

Chapter 4 of On the Unity of the Church survives in two forms. The received text grounds the church’s unity in Christ’s singular commission to Peter but at once levels the apostles; a second recension, long called the Primacy Text, adds three clauses that press Peter’s singularity harder. In the Ante-Nicene Fathers apparatus the added clauses read, verbatim:

[after “Feed my sheep”:] Upon him, being one, He builds His Church, and commits His sheep to be fed.

[after “the beginning proceeds from unity”:] And the primacy is given to Peter, that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See; and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.

[and, further on:] Who deserts the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded, does he trust that he is in the Church?

— the Primacy-Text additions to On the Unity of the Church 4–5.1 The advocated reading takes these clauses at full weight: “the primacy is given to Peter,” “one See,” and “the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded” are read as Cyprian’s testimony that Christ lodged a governing primacy in Peter’s see — Rome — to which the other churches owe communion. This page weighs that specifically jurisdictional reading; the double datum and both texts in full are set out on the Cyprian detail page.

Who advances it

The Primacy Text has been a mainstay of Roman-Catholic controversial theology since the sixteenth century: Cyprian, the greatest Latin churchman of the age of the martyrs, apparently saying outright that “the primacy is given to Peter” and that to desert “the chair of Peter” is to leave the Church, is quoted as patristic proof that a Petrine primacy of government was believed in the third-century Church. Older Protestant controversialists answered the only way the passage seemed to allow — by denying the text: the Primacy-Text clauses were a Roman interpolation, smuggled into Cyprian to make him witness to a papacy he never taught, and the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors flatly print them as “beyond all question spurious.”2 So the argument has two moving parts, and each side historically staked its case on the textual one.

The evidence for

The strongest text may now be granted — the forgery charge has failed. The decisive twentieth-century work on Cyprian’s manuscripts, by the Jesuit patrologist Maurice Bévenot, overturned the old interpolation verdict: the two recensions are both Cyprian’s own, the Primacy Text an earlier draft that he himself later revised into the received form. On that finding — now the mainstream critical view — the primacy reading is entitled to its best text: “the primacy is given to Peter” is Cyprian’s own pen, not a papal gloss.3 The honest apologetic case does not need the interpolation dispute at all; it can concede the whole textual point and still press the doctrinal one.

Cyprian does exalt “the chair of Peter” and a “one See.” The language is real and it is emphatic: Christ built the Church on Peter “being one,” the primacy “is given to Peter,” there is “one Church of Christ and one See,” and to desert “the chair of Peter” is to forfeit the Church. No father before Cyprian concentrates the figure of unity so tightly on Peter, and Cyprian elsewhere calls Rome “the chief church whence priestly unity takes its source” (Epistle 54). That a later papalism should hear its own doctrine in these words is not a wild misreading; the words are there.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The textual leg holds, and in the primacy reading’s favour: Bévenot’s case that both recensions are Cyprianic is well-grounded and widely accepted, so the argument may be assessed on the Primacy Text without the distraction of a forgery charge. And Cyprian’s Petrine emphasis is genuine, not manufactured. The question is only whether that emphasis reaches the thing the doctrine needs — a primacy of jurisdiction, a standing right of Peter’s Roman successors to govern the other churches. On that it fails, and it fails on Cyprian’s own words and his own conduct.

Even the Primacy Text makes Peter the source, not the sovereign. Read the added clause whole and it collapses the monarchy it seems to grant: “the primacy is given to Peter, that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See; and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.” The primacy is given to Peter in order to exhibit the Church’s oneness — the whole point of the treatise, written against Novatian’s schism — and the very same sentence at once redistributes what it concentrated: all are shepherds, the one Rock is fed by all the apostles together. Bévenot’s own conclusion, from the scholar who rehabilitated the text, is that even the Primacy Text asserts Peter’s primacy as the figure and origin of a shared episcopate, not a jurisdiction of his Roman heirs. The recension that looks most like a papal proof-text, read to its end, is a statement of collegial unity.3

The apostles are made Peter’s equals in the same chapter. Whatever recension one reads, chapter 4 continues: “Assuredly the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.” Peter is first only as the point from which unity begins — a temporal and figurative priority (the beginning proceeds from unity), not a superior power, which the apostles are said expressly to share equally. A text that in one breath gives Peter the primacy and in the next declares the others “the same as was Peter” in honour and power is not teaching that Peter (or his see) rules the rest.4

For Cyprian every bishop sits in Peter’s chair — the episcopate is one, held whole by each. This is the load-bearing point, and it is Cyprian’s own gloss on his imagery. Chapter 5 draws the conclusion the whole treatise turns on: “The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur). Each bishop holds not a fraction but the whole episcopate in his own city; so “the chair of Peter” is the figure of that one episcopate as such — a chair every catholic bishop occupies — not the title-deed of the Roman see in particular. The ANF apparatus itself glosses the phrase exactly so: “the universal episcopate is the chair of Peter.” On this reading the Petrine language that a later papalism hears as founding Roman primacy grounds, for Cyprian, the equality and unity of all bishops — which is precisely why he could invoke Peter and yet deny that any bishop is a bishop over bishops.4

“No bishop of bishops” — Cyprian ruled out the jurisdiction reading in so many words. The decisive control on what Cyprian meant is what he did with it. Opening the Seventh Council of Carthage on 1 September 256 — eighty-seven bishops each casting his own sentence — he laid down that no see may bind another bishop’s judgment: “For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop … has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another.” This is the sharpest denial of a monarchical episcopate to survive from a bishop of Cyprian’s stature, and it was uttered against the Roman claim he was then resisting. A man who taught that Christ made Peter’s see the governing head of the churches could not have opened a council by denying that any bishop is a bishop of bishops.5

He rebuffed the “succession from Peter” when Rome pressed it as a claim to rule. The occasion of that council was the rebaptism controversy of 255–256, in which Stephen of Rome forbade the African and Eastern practice and threatened to break communion with the bishops who dissented — resting his ruling, his opponents report, expressly on his “succession from Peter.” Firmilian of Caesarea, answering for the East, turned the appeal to Peter against Stephen himself: he “boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter … while you think that all may be excommunicated by you, you have excommunicated yourself alone from all.” This is the crucial test case: when a bishop of Rome grounded a claim to overrule distant churches precisely on descent from Peter, Cyprian and the churches around him treated it as an overreach to resist, not a jurisdiction to obey — and no excommunication took effect. Cyprian’s “chair of Peter,” then, is demonstrably not a Roman prerogative to command; the one time the Roman prerogative was asserted on Petrine ground, he refused it.6

Assessment

Weak — as evidence for a Roman primacy of jurisdiction. Keep the two questions apart and both come out against the reading. The textual question is now settled in the argument’s favour and can be conceded outright: on Bévenot’s widely-accepted case both recensions are Cyprian’s own, so the Primacy Text is authentic and the old “forgery” charge is no defeater — the argument gets its best text. But the doctrinal question is not carried by that text. Even the Primacy Text makes the primacy given to Peter the means of showing the Church’s oneness, in a sentence that at once calls all the apostles shepherds and has the one Rock “fed by all … with unanimous consent”; the same chapter declares the other apostles “the same as was Peter … in honour and power”; the next chapter defines the episcopate as one, “held by each one for the whole,” making every bishop an heir of Peter’s chair; and Cyprian’s own conduct closes the case — “no bishop of bishops” at Carthage in 256, and a flat refusal to let Stephen’s “succession from Peter” bind the African churches. Cyprian’s Petrine imagery is real and load-bearing, but what it bears is episcopal collegiality — unity secured by the concord of equal bishops, each holding the whole chair of Peter — not a monarchy of the Roman see over the rest. The interpretation that reads a Roman primacy of jurisdiction out of De unitate 4 is a conjecture against the grain of the very treatise it quotes: weak. Whether Rome’s claim to rule descends from Peter at all is weighed under the Petrine succession of the primacy; the identity of the rock, under the rock of Matthew 16.
