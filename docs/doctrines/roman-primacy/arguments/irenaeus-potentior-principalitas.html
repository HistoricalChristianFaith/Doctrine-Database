Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas (Against Heresies III.3.2) assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

Disputed reading · on the Primacy of Rome · witness: Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Related readings: Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Does Cyprian’s “Primacy Text” teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Do the great councils show a Roman primacy of honour or of jurisdiction?

The passage

… that tradition derived from the apostles, of the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded and organized at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul … For it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its pre-eminent authority, that is, the faithful everywhere, inasmuch as the apostolical tradition has been preserved continuously by those [faithful men] who exist everywhere.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies III.3.2, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers).1 The Greek of the sentence is lost. The surviving Latin reads Ad hanc enim ecclesiam propter potiorem principalitatem necesse est omnem convenire ecclesiam, the manuscripts varying between potiorem and potentiorem; the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors, printing “pre-eminent authority,” add that “both the text and meaning have here given rise to much discussion” and that they are “far from sure that the rendering given above is correct, but … unable to think of anything better.”2

The question

Dominic Unger, surveying the literature in 1952, wrote that “perhaps no other text of this great apologist has received such varied interpretations,” and sorted the readings into two groups: “the one granting the Roman Church a primacy of moral power, of sovereignty, the other denying her this and granting her a primacy of honor only.”3 Other interpreters frame the sentence in terms that fit neither group. The question is what kind of standing the sentence gives the Roman church; it is among the earliest texts cited for Rome’s place among the churches.

An authority with which every church must agree

Every church must agree with Rome because of its superior authority, an authority to teach that these readers hold to be a primacy of jurisdiction.

Held by: Dominic J. Unger (Capuchin, “St. Irenaeus and the Roman Primacy,” Theological Studies 13, 1952), who calls it “the traditional Catholic interpretation”;3 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, The Early Papacy, 1920);4 the First Vatican Council; and Robert Bellarmine (Catholic, De controversiis).3

On this reading the sentence gives the reason for what precedes it. Irenaeus has offered two ways to find the apostolic tradition: count up the successions of all the churches, or point to the one church of Rome, “very great, very ancient, and universally known,” founded by Peter and Paul, whose succession list he alone supplies. The connective enim (“for”) explains why Rome by itself suffices: every church must agree with it, because of its potentior principalitas. Unger reads convenire ad as doctrinal and moral agreement, a sense he finds elsewhere in Irenaeus and which, he notes, many non-Catholic interpreters (among them Harnack) also accept. He takes potentiorem as “the key word” and principalitas as “a power to teach authentically and authoritatively,” so that the necessity is moral, not merely logical: Irenaeus himself draws the inference at III.4.1 that one “must” avoid the heretics and accept the tradition. The illustration that follows supports it, on Unger’s reading: the Roman church went to Corinth by a “most powerful letter” and Corinth “bowed to the Roman.” Rome’s place as the imperial city, he grants, explains why the primary see was set there, “but the primacy of that See was one of jurisdiction and came from God, independently of the central position of Rome.”3 Fortescue, rendering the clause “because of her mightier rule,” writes: “Taking the plain meaning of these words, we have that the Church of Rome has a mightier rule, and therefore every Church must agree with that of Rome (or go to Rome).”4 Bellarmine had read the passage the same way: all must come to this church “et ab ipsa tamquam a capite et fonte pendere” (that is, depend on it as on head and source). Unger’s conclusion states the reading: “it is morally necessary for every Church, that is, for all the faithful, to agree with this, the Roman Church, in which, as by an instrumental cause, the tradition of the Apostles has always been preserved by those who are everywhere.”3

A primacy of honour: Rome as the pre-eminent witness to apostolic tradition

Irenaeus holds up Rome as the most prominent and convenient witness to the apostolic tradition that every apostolic church preserves, without giving it authority over the others.

Held by: James F. McCue (Catholic, Theological Studies 25, 1964);5 H. Dressler (Catholic, New Catholic Encyclopedia, “Irenaeus, St.”);6 Christopher Wordsworth (Anglican, St. Hippolytus and the Church of Rome, 1853);2 and Adolf Harnack (1893).3

On this reading the sense comes from the frame of Against Heresies III.1–4. Irenaeus is answering Gnostics who claimed a secret tradition, and his reply is that the true tradition is public, traceable through the bishops of any apostolic church; he invokes Ephesus and Smyrna to the same end (III.3.4). Rome enters as the most convenient specimen. McCue takes principalitas as most likely rendering the Greek archē, “because of its more imposing foundation,” a reference back to the foundation by Peter and Paul; he finds “nothing in Irenaeus to warrant the conclusion that he recognized in the Roman Church a unique authority or a unique role in the Church.”5 Irenaeus names Peter and Paul together as founders and has the apostles commit the episcopate to Linus.7 The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors print, from Berington and Kirk, a “more literal” version in which every church must “resort” to Rome, where the faith of all is preserved by those who come from everywhere, so that Rome “was a mirror of the Catholic World … not the Sun, dispensing her own light to others, but the glass bringing their rays into a focus”; a strict rendering, they add, “leaves the principalitas to the city, not the Church.” Wordsworth conjectured that the lost Greek spoke of Rome’s greater antiquity.2 Nautin’s philological study finds it not certain that “this Church” means Rome, and Dressler judges that necesse est convenire is “not to be understood as a juridical obligation.”6 About a decade later Irenaeus admonished Victor of Rome not to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date.8 McCue states the reading’s positive sense: “Rome is being held up as a model or pattern of sound doctrine … In theory, any of the apostolic sees could serve as a doctrinal standard. Rome, because it is so impressively apostolic and because of its cosmopolitan character and its extensive dealings with others, is a most convenient standard.”5 On this reading Irenaeus honours Rome as the pre-eminent and most convenient witness to the apostolic tradition that every apostolic church holds.

One of several early indications of a primacy present from the first

Newman reads the sentence as one of several partial early signs which, read together, point to a Roman primacy present from the first and exercised more fully as need arose.

Held by: John Henry Newman (Catholic, An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine, 1845; rev. 1878).9

On this reading the question to put to the second-century texts is not whether they state the later primacy in full but whether they show the element from which it grew. Newman frames the question for the papal supremacy as a whole: “whether there was not from the first a certain element at work, or in existence, divinely sanctioned, which, for certain reasons, did not at once show itself upon the surface of ecclesiastical affairs, and of which events in the fourth century are the development.” Of the first age he writes: “A partial fulfilment, or at least indications of what was to be, there certainly were in the first age. Faint one by one, at least they are various, and are found in writers of many times and countries, and thereby illustrative of each other, and forming a body of proof.” Irenaeus stands in that series: he speaks of Rome as founded and established by Peter and Paul, “appeals to its tradition, not in contrast indeed, but in preference to that of other Churches,” and declares that every church “must resort” to Rome, or “must agree with it, propter potiorem principalitatem.”9 On this reading the sentence is one of several early, partial indications, read together with the others, of a Roman primacy present from the first and exercised more fully as the need arose.

The greatest authority of witness among the churches: a priority, not a power

Rome has the greatest authority of witness among the churches, which the others freely receive: a priority, distinct both from a power over them and from a precedence of honour.

Held by: Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, St Sergius Institute, Paris; “The Church Which Presides in Love,” repr. 1992).10

On this reading potentior principalitas names Rome’s priority among the churches, an authority of witness received freely by the others. Afanasiev holds that no philological analysis can recover the lost Greek, and reads the phrase from Irenaeus’s own picture of Rome as “the very great, very ancient church, known to all others”: “This church possesses the greatest authority among the churches; consequently it is the church with the priority. Surely, then, we could rightly say that the phrase potentior principalitas means priority.” Because it is the greatest priority, it “does not exclude the priority of other churches in the more limited circles of local churches,” such as Smyrna and Ephesus. Necesse “does not suggest any legal obligation”: “the duty of appealing, if there is disagreement, to the church which has the greatest authority.” Rome’s witness “was not a verdict backed by the force of law”; “It was a free act when the local churches followed Rome’s witness,” and that witness “had higher validity than any and every legal verdict.” He sets the phrase beside Ignatius’s: “Presiding in love in Ignatius corresponds with the potentior principalitas in Irenaeus,” and concludes that for Clement, Ignatius and Irenaeus “the priority enjoyed by the Roman Church was due to authority of witness: or, to use more modern language, it possessed priority of receptio.”10 On this reading Irenaeus attests the Roman church’s priority among all the local churches, an authority of witness that the churches received, distinct both from a primacy of jurisdiction and from a precedence of honour.
