Is the “rock” of Matthew 16:18 Peter, his confession, or Christ?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — on the grammar the rock is most probably Peter himself, a reading now held even by many non-Catholic exegetes; but the patristic tradition genuinely divides, a large and early strand reading the rock as Peter’s confession or as Christ, so the identity is settled by no consensus — and, either way, identifying the rock decides nothing about a Roman primacy, which is weighed separately · advanced on this timeline by Leo the Great, with the confession-reading defended by Chrysostom and others

Related arguments: the rock and the keys — see also Does the commission to Peter in Matthew 16 descend to his Roman successors as a standing jurisdiction?

The claim

…the blessed Peter persevering in the strength of the Rock, which he has received, has not abandoned the helm of the Church which he undertook… And so if anything is rightly done and rightly decreed by us… it is of his work and merits whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See.

— Leo the Great, Sermon III §II–III, reading Peter as “the Rock” on which Christ builds.1 The words of Matthew 16:18 are a play on a name: “Thou art Peter (Petros), and upon this rock (petra) I will build my church.” Three readings of “this rock” have contended since antiquity — that it is (a) Peter’s person, newly named; (b) Peter’s confession, “Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God” (v. 16); or (c) Christ himself, whom Peter had just confessed. This page weighs the identity question on its own; whether anything about a Roman primacy follows from it is a separate question, weighed at the succession argument.

Who advances it

The three readings do not line up neatly by confession. The Peter reading is the natural sense of the wordplay and is assumed by the developed papal theory (Leo; the medieval Latin glossators); but it is also the verdict of much modern non-Catholic scholarship on grammatical grounds — Oscar Cullmann, W. F. Albright and C. S. Mann, R. T. France, and D. A. Carson all identify the rock as Peter while denying that any papacy follows.2 The confession reading — the rock is the faith Peter voiced — is the dominant patristic strand: John Chrysostom (“on the faith of his confession”), Theodore of Mopsuestia (“this is not the property of Peter alone… upon this confession and faith”), Theophylact, Philoxenus of Mabbug, and, on the Latin side, Hilary in one register.3 The Christ reading is the mature Augustine’s stated preference — “the rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4), Peter named from it — which he leaves, with unusual modesty, for “the reader to choose”; Aquinas lists it among the licit expositions.4

The evidence for

The grammar favours Peter. The strongest case reads the rock as Peter himself, and it does not depend on Catholic premises. The demonstrative is emphatic and immediate — Jesus has just addressed Simon in the second person (“Thou art Peter”), and “this rock” most naturally points to the man just named, not past him to an abstraction or to the speaker. The Petros/petra difference, long pressed as a “small stone versus bedrock” distinction, is now widely judged to carry no such weight in first-century Greek: petra is feminine and cannot serve as a man’s name, so the masculine Petros is simply the form the name must take — “the reason for the different Greek form is simply that Peter, as a man, needs a masculine name” (R. T. France).2

The Aramaic collapses the distinction entirely. The saying was almost certainly spoken in Aramaic, where a single word — kêphā’ (Cephas) — does the work of both Greek terms: “Thou art Kepha, and on this kepha I will build.” Joseph Fitzmyer, weighing the Semitic substratum, concludes that the underlying pun makes Simon and the rock one thing; the Syriac Peshitta confirms the reading, rendering both Greek words by the one word kêphā’.5 On this ground the confession- and Christ-readings look like later theological refinements laid over a wordplay whose plain force is Simon = rock. Carson puts the modern judgement bluntly: were it not for reaction against Roman claims, “it is doubtful whether many” would read the rock as anyone but Peter.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a piece of grammar the Peter-reading is strong and, in modern exegesis, near-majority: the emphatic demonstrative, the name-play, the collapse of the Petros/petra distinction in Koine, and the Aramaic substratum all pull the same way, and a line of non-Catholic commentators grants it without embarrassment. A page that pretended the rock were obviously the confession or Christ would be reading against the grain of the Greek.

What the patristic record actually shows. The honest headline is that the fathers do not speak with one voice, and the papal reading cannot claim them as a body. The single most-attested ancient reading is the confession: Chrysostom, Theodore, Theophylact, and Philoxenus make the faith Peter voiced the foundation, and several say in so many words that the gift “is not the property of Peter alone” but “the common property of all believers.” The mature Augustine preferred Christ. Even the Latin fathers who make Peter the rock (Hilary, the Glossa, Rabanus) at once universalise the keys to all the apostles. So the appeal to a patristic consensus for Peter-as-rock-and-therefore-Rome is doubly misleading: the fathers divide on the rock, and those who grant Peter the rock do not grant Rome the keys.3

What is overstated. Two overreaches attach to this proof-text and belong on the proponents’ pages. First, to cite the fathers en masse for the Petrine reading — as the florilegia do, sometimes on the strength of a passage of “Cyril of Alexandria” that is widely regarded as spurious — misrepresents a divided tradition as a settled one.6 Second, and more basic, is the slide from “the rock is Peter” to “therefore the bishop of Rome.” Cullmann and Carson — who grant that Peter is the rock — deny that any transmissible Roman office follows: Peter’s role as rock is, in Cullmann’s words, historically unrepeatable, tied to his foundational confession, and the text says nothing of a successor. The identity of the rock and the descent of a jurisdiction are two questions, and settling the first leaves the second exactly where it was.2

Assessment

Contested. On the grammar the rock is most probably Peter himself — the wordplay, the Aramaic kepha, and the emphatic demonstrative make that the natural reading, and modern non-Catholic scholarship largely concedes it. But the question is contested rather than settled: the ancient church divided, with its largest strand reading the rock as Peter’s confession and a weighty voice (the later Augustine) reading it as Christ, so the interpretation is strong by the lights of modern philology and weak by the lights of patristic consensus — the defining shape of a contested reading. And the contest is, for the doctrine, curiously beside the point: even at its strongest the Petrine reading yields only that Simon Peter was the church’s foundation-stone, not that his office descends to a Roman see. That inference — the one the primacy actually needs — is weighed, and found much weaker, at the succession argument.
