Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth (c. 96) attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

Disputed reading · on the Primacy of Rome · witness: 1 Clement (c. 96)

Related readings: Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Does Cyprian’s “Primacy Text” teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Do the great councils show a Roman primacy of honour or of jurisdiction?

The passage

It is disgraceful, beloved, yea, highly disgraceful, and unworthy of your Christian profession, that such a thing should be heard of as that the most stedfast and ancient Church of the Corinthians should, on account of one or two persons, engage in sedition against its presbyters.

If, however, any shall disobey the words spoken by Him through us, let them know that they will involve themselves in transgression and serious danger…

— 1 Clement 47 and 59, source.1 Around 96 the church of Rome sent a long letter to Corinth, where a faction had deposed the presbyters. The letter declares that ministers who served blamelessly “cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry” (ch. 44), tells the ringleaders to “submit yourselves to the presbyters” (ch. 57), and asks the Corinthians to become “obedient to the things that have been written by us” (ch. 63).2

The question

That Rome wrote to Corinth about the deposed presbyters is not in question. What is disputed is what kind of act the letter records: an authority of the Roman church, or of its bishop, over another church; a standing of the Roman church among the churches that is not a power over them; or the admonition one church gave another. The answer bears on how early a Roman primacy can be traced in the surviving record.

The Roman church speaks with authority as a community

The letter shows the Roman church, speaking as a body, exercising a moral authority over Corinth, in which Schaff and Lightfoot see the origin of the Roman primacy.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church, vol. II);3 J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, The Apostolic Fathers, Part I, 1890).4

On this reading the letter shows a church acting with authority, and the authority is the Roman church’s as a community. Schaff states that the letter was sent “at its own impulse, and unasked,” and that its author “speaks in a tone of authority to a sister church of apostolic foundation, and thus reveals the easy and as yet innocent beginning of the papacy.”3 Lightfoot observes “the urgent and almost imperious tone which the Romans adopt in addressing their Corinthian brethren,” and writes: “It may perhaps seem strange to describe this noble remonstrance as the first step towards papal domination. And yet undoubtedly this is the case.” The step he describes belongs to the church, not to a bishop: “the Romans as a community remonstrate on terms of equality with the Corinthians on their irregularities, strong only in the righteousness of their cause,” and “the power of the bishop of Rome was built upon the power of the Church of Rome. It was originally a primacy, not of the episcopate, but of the church.” Reading Ignatius’s greeting to the same church, he calls it “a primacy not of the bishop but of the whole church, a primacy not of official authority but of practical goodness, backed however by the prestige and the advantages which were necessarily enjoyed by the church of the metropolis.” Of the letter’s prayer for the rulers under Domitian he asks: “Who would have grudged the Church of Rome her primacy, if she had always spoken thus?”4 On this reading 1 Clement records the Roman church, speaking as a body, exercising a moral authority over another church, a primacy of the church rather than of its bishop.

An exercise of the primacy of the Roman Church

Rome directs another church and is obeyed, which these readers take as the earliest extant exercise of the Roman primacy.

Held by: Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, The Early Papacy, 1920);5 Johannes Quasten (Catholic, Patrology, vol. I, 1950);6 Pope Benedict XVI (Catholic, General Audience of 7 March 2007).7

On this reading Rome treats the Corinthian schism as its own concern, names the remedy, and directs the guilty to yield, and the other church receives the direction. Fortescue writes: “Here we have a Bishop of Rome, the Church of Rome, if a man prefers to put it that way, assuming the right to send categorical orders to Christians in Greece,” and “Rome sends her legates to settle the dispute and enforce her commands”; he concludes that “The Pope’s writ carried and was obeyed in Greece within about sixty years from the death of our Lord.”5 Quasten finds in the letter “unequivocal proof” of the primacy of the Roman Church: “The Church of Rome speaks to the Church of Corinth as a superior speaks to a subject.” He grants that it contains “no categorical assertion of the primacy of the Roman See,” and points to the opening apology for the delay in writing, which shows that Rome regarded the Corinthian affair as its duty, and to the warning of chapter 59: “Such an authoritative tone cannot be adequately accounted for on the ground of the close cultural relations existing between Corinth and Rome.”6 Benedict XVI, citing Irenaeus’s report that “the Church in Rome dispatched a most powerful Letter to the Corinthians exhorting them to peace,” calls the letter “a first exercise of the Roman primacy after St Peter’s death,” reflecting “the concern of the Church of Rome which presides in charity over all the other Churches.”7 The letter’s standing at Corinth lasted: it was still read publicly there in worship some seventy years later.8 On this reading 1 Clement is the earliest extant exercise of the Roman primacy over another church.

A fraternal admonition from one church to another

One church exhorts another in a crisis, its authority resting on its argument and on the Roman community’s standing rather than on any office over Corinth.

Held by: James F. McCue (Catholic, Theological Studies 25, 1964), drawing on the Catholic scholars Adolf W. Ziegler (1958) and Otto Karrer.8

On this reading the letter is, in McCue’s words, “most naturally … an exhortation to repentance addressed by one community to another,” and McCue takes this to be Irenaeus’s own view of it. The obedience the letter asks is owed “to the things which God (or Christ) has spoken through us”: the writer “relies wholly upon the substance of what he says,” and “the tone is always one of exhortation rather than of command.” Chapter 56 presents such correction as mutual: “A reproof which we administer to one another is honorable and extremely helpful.” Churches of the period wrote such letters to one another, and McCue sets beside 1 Clement the letters of Dionysius of Corinth to Sparta, Athens, Nicomedia, Crete and Pontus, observing that it is “difficult to imagine that these letters could have been less imperative, less authoritarian than 1 Clement.” Ziegler holds that Rome’s solicitude “can have its origin in genuine charity and in the strong sense of solidarity that animated early Christianity”; Karrer writes that Clement acted “with authority and with love, ‘not as lording it over the clergy.’”8 The letter is corporate: it opens “The Church of God which sojourns at Rome, to the Church of God sojourning at Corinth,” and Clement’s name never appears in it, so McCue concludes that any authority it carries is “that of the Roman community, not of an individual within that community.” It entreats as it directs, speaking of “the supplication which we have made concerning peace and unity,” and the editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers note that “the Corinthians asked this of their brethren.”9 A decade later Ignatius of Antioch, writing to the same Roman church, speaks of it in the same register of entreaty: “I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you.”10 On this reading the letter is the counsel of one church to a sister church in crisis, carrying the weight of its argument and of the Roman community’s standing.

The witness of the church that holds priority in love, without power over others

The Roman church bears authoritative witness on events at Corinth, a priority of witness and love that is neither a power over other churches nor a precedence of honour.

Held by: Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, St Sergius Institute, Paris; “The Church Which Presides in Love,” repr. 1992).11

On this reading the letter is the earliest reliable evidence of the Roman church’s priority among the churches, and that priority is one of witness and love, not of power. Afanasiev calls the epistle “our earliest reliable evidence that the Church of Rome stood in an exceptional position of authority in this period.” It is written “in the name of the Roman Church,” church of God to church of God, and contains no claim “to exercise any power over the Church of Corinth.” He holds that the deposed presbyters applied to Rome so that Rome “might refuse receptio of what had taken place in the Church of Corinth,” and that Rome did so: “This was not Rome laying down the law, but the Church bearing witness about what had happened within the Church.” The letter, couched “in the form of an exhortation,” shows that Rome “was aware of the decisive weight, in the Church of Corinth’s eyes, that must attach to its witness”: “the Church of Rome, at the end of the first century, exhibits a marked sense of its own priority, in point of witness about events in other churches.” Such a church “holds a two-fold priority, of authority and love”; it cannot be said to have “power over the other churches,” and Afanasiev adds that “One cannot even say that the primacy was one of honor, for in ancient times the ideas of honor and power were closely associated.”11 On this reading 1 Clement attests a real Roman priority among the churches, exercised as authoritative witness within the communion of churches.
