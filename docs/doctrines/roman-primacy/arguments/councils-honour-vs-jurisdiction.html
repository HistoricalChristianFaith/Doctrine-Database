Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?

Disputed reading · on the Primacy of Rome · witnesses: Nicaea (325), Sardica (343), Constantinople (381), Chalcedon (451)

Related readings: Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? · Does Cyprian’s “Primacy Text” teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?

The passage

Nicaea’s sixth canon:

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

Constantinople’s third:

The Bishop of Constantinople, however, shall have the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome.

And Chalcedon’s twenty-eighth, which quotes the canon of 381:

For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges (isa presbeia) to the most holy throne of New Rome, justly judging that the city which is honoured with the Sovereignty and the Senate, and enjoys equal privileges with the old imperial Rome, should in ecclesiastical matters also be magnified as she is… and rank next after her.

— Nicaea canon 6, Constantinople canon 3, Chalcedon canon 28, source.1 Between them stands the Western Council of Sardica (343), whose third canon proposes: “let us, if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle, and let those who gave judgment write to Julius, the bishop of Rome, so that, if necessary, the case may be retried by the bishops of the neighbouring provinces.”2

The question

All four councils leave Rome first among the sees. What is disputed is the kind of primacy the canons record: a first rank of honour among sees ordered by custom and by the standing of their cities; a primacy that carries a jurisdictional role over the other churches; or a first place in the conciliar order of the sees, exercised within the synod of bishops. The Catholic and Orthodox members of the international theological dialogue stated the common ground and the dispute in 2007: both agree that Rome “occupied the first place in the taxis, and that the bishop of Rome was therefore the protos among the patriarchs,” and “disagree, however, on the interpretation of the historical evidence from this era regarding the prerogatives of the bishop of Rome as protos.”3 Metropolitan Hilarion of Volokolamsk (Moscow Patriarchate, 2014) calls the Ravenna statement “controversial” and holds that it “makes no mention of any differences in ecclesiology between the Orthodox and the Catholics; in this way it is misleading.” He also reports that Orthodox positions on universal primacy differ: Alexander Schmemann held that the Church as a universal organism “must have at her head a universal bishop as the focus of her unity and the organ of supreme power,” and Metropolitan Elpidophoros of Bursa criticized the Moscow Patriarchate’s 2013 document, given below as one Orthodox reading.4 These are the earliest extant conciliar texts that rank the great sees.

A primacy with a jurisdictional role, which the councils presuppose and serve

The councils assume a Roman primacy with real authority over other churches, which Rome’s bishops claimed on Peter’s behalf and which the councils sought to have confirm their acts.

Held by: John Chapman (Benedictine, Studies on the Early Papacy, 1928);5 Pope Zosimus (418), who cited the Sardican appeal canon as conciliar law;2 the Roman legate Philip at the Council of Ephesus (431);6 and Leo the Great (Letters CV and CVI, 451).7

On this reading the councils presuppose Rome’s first place and give it functions over other churches. Nicaea measures Alexandria’s authority by what “is customary for the Bishop of Rome”; Constantinople, raising a new see, still sets it “after the Bishop of Rome.” Sardica gives the bishop of Rome a defined place in the discipline of other churches, “to honour the memory of Peter”: he may order a deposed bishop’s case retried, no successor is to be installed “unless the bishop of Rome judge and render a decision as to this,” and he may send presbyters “invested with his authority” to sit as judges.2 Chapman holds that the council assumed the Pope’s “admitted right of hearing appeals at Rome in a Council called by himself,” and that the canons were “a proposal for the attribution to the Pope of most extraordinary powers” over and above it.5 At Ephesus in 431 the legate Philip declared that Peter “down even to to-day and forever both lives and judges in his successors,” and that Pope Celestine “according to due order, is his successor and holds his place.”6 Valentinian III’s rescript of 445 decrees that “whatever shall be sanctioned by the authority of the Apostolic See shall be law.”8 At Chalcedon the bishops acclaimed Leo’s Tome: “Peter has spoken through Leo.”9 The council’s synodal letter to Leo calls him “the mouthpiece unto all of the blessed Peter,” says that he presided over its bishops “as the head to the members,” and asks him to “honour our decision by your assent” on the canon about Constantinople.10 Leo read the conciliar order as Nicaea’s, “founded upon the decrees of the Spirit” and unalterable, and defended the ranks of Alexandria and Antioch on their Petrine links: Alexandria’s dignity “merited through S. Mark, the evangelist and disciple of the blessed Peter,” Antioch, where “first at the preaching of the blessed Apostle Peter the Christian name arose.”7 Chapman reads canon 28 as “passed subject to the approval of St. Leo,” who “instantly annulled the canon,” after which Anatolius of Constantinople “humbly begged the Pope’s forgiveness.”5 On this reading the councils order the great sees within a Roman primacy that they presuppose, that Rome’s bishops claimed on Peter’s behalf, and that the councils sought to have confirm their acts.

A primacy of honour among sees ranked by custom and civil standing

The canons rank the great sees by ancient custom and by the standing of their cities; Rome’s first place is a precedence of that kind, which councils confirm and can extend to other sees.

Held by: F. W. Puller (Anglican, The Primitive Saints and the See of Rome);5 John Johnson (Anglican);11 Tillemont, on the Sardican canons;2 and the African bishops at the Council of Carthage (419) and in their letter to Pope Celestine.2

On this reading the canons state their own grounds, and the grounds are custom and civic dignity. Nicaea confirms “the ancient customs” and names Rome as the parallel for Alexandria’s regional jurisdiction, with Antioch and “the other provinces” keeping “their privileges” on the same footing. Constantinople ranks its bishop second “because Constantinople is New Rome.” Chalcedon states the principle for the whole order: the fathers granted privileges to old Rome “because it was the royal city,” and gave “equal privileges” to New Rome “actuated by the same consideration,” placing a see without an apostolic founder above Alexandria and Antioch.1 The same council sent appeals against a metropolitan “to the Exarch of the Diocese, or to the throne of the Imperial City of Constantinople” (canons 9 and 17); Johnson commented that “here is a greater privilege given by a General Council to the see of Constantinople than ever was given by any council, even that of Sardica, to the bishop of Rome.”11 Sardica’s appeal right is a favour the council confers: on “if it seem good to your charity,” Tillemont comments, “This form is very strong to shew that it was a right which the Pope had not had hitherto,” and Peter de Marca, cited by Van Espen, holds that Hosius invoked Peter’s memory “that he might the more easily lead them to consent to this new privilege.” Richer held the provision temporary, a measure, on that view, for the rescue of Athanasius.2 Puller reads the canons as giving the Pope, “in certain rare cases … a very restricted right of determining whether there shall be a re-hearing,” proposed by Hosius “tentatively.”5 Sardica was a Western synod, the Eastern bishops having withdrawn. When Rome cited the appeal canon as Nicene, the African bishops sent to Alexandria and Constantinople for the Nicene canons, found it was Sardica’s, and asked Rome not to receive appeals from Africa, lest they “seem to introduce the smoky pride of the world into the Church of Christ.”2 On this reading Rome’s first place is a precedence among the great sees, set by ancient custom and by the rank of the imperial city, which councils confirm and can extend to other sees.

A first place in the conciliar order of the sees, exercised within synodality

In the Moscow Patriarchate’s position, the councils set an order (taxis) of the great sees in which Rome stood first: a primacy of honour within the synod of the patriarchates, different in kind from primacy at the regional level.

Held by: the Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church, in its document on primacy in the universal Church (26 December 2013), as presented by Metropolitan Hilarion (Alfeyev) of Volokolamsk (2014).4

On this reading the canons establish an order (taxis) of the great sees, and primacy differs in kind at each level of the Church. The canonical model is the thirty-fourth Apostolic Canon: “The bishops of every nation must acknowledge him who is first among them and account him as their head, and do nothing of consequence without his consent … But neither let him (who is the first) do anything without the consent of all; for so there will be unanimity.”12 As Hilarion presents the 2013 document, primacy at the regional level, “a matter of canonical convenience, is based on church canons, notably on the 34th Apostolic Canon,” while “At the universal level there is a primacy of honor which is based on the sacred diptychs, i.e. the official order of churches established by Ecumenical Councils.” For that universal primacy “there is neither canon nor patristic statement that would describe such a primacy, other than the canons that establish a taxis (order) for the five major patriarchates. This taxis implies that one would be first but gives no indication of his prerogatives over and above the remaining four patriarchs.” The document holds that “the functions of the primus on various levels are not identical and cannot be transferred from one level to another,” and that in the first millennium “the primacy of honor once belonged to the see of Rome.” Hilarion adds that “Byzantine sources speak rather of a pentarchy,” in which the five patriarchates’ “rights and privileges are equivalent.”4 On this reading the councils of 325–451 record Rome as first in a conciliar order of sees, a primacy of honour within the synodal communion of the patriarchates.
