Do the great councils show a Roman primacy of honour among equals, or of universal jurisdiction?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — for the reading that the great councils treat Rome’s primacy as a pre-eminence of honour and precedence among co-equal sees, ranked by ecclesiastical custom and civil status, rather than a constitutive universal jurisdiction. Read on their own terms, the conciliar canons rank the great sees the way they rank their cities: Nicaea (325) names Rome as one great metropolitan among Alexandria and Antioch, its sphere fixed by “ancient custom”; Constantinople (381) and Chalcedon (451) ground the whole order — Rome’s first place included — in the sees’ standing as imperial capitals (“because it was the royal city”), giving New Rome “equal privileges.” The jurisdiction reading’s best conciliar leg — the appeal-to-Rome of Sardica (343) — is real but bounded: it is a favour bestowed by the council (“if it seem good to your charity”), enacted by a one-party Western synod, and when Rome later pressed it as a canon of Nicaea the African church exposed the misattribution (the Apiarius affair). Rome kept a genuine first rank throughout; what the councils will not supply is a standing power to govern the other churches · the data sit on this timeline at Nicaea (325), Sardica (343), Constantinople (381), and Chalcedon (451)

The claim

The interpretation weighed here reads the conciliar record as a whole. Its steelman: across the four great fourth- and fifth-century councils the fathers legislate an order of precedence among the chief sees — Rome first, then (from 381) Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch — but they ground that order in custom and the civil dignity of the cities, not in a transmitted Petrine commission, and they bound each see’s authority to its own region. The load-bearing texts are four canons. Nicaea’s sixth:

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

Constantinople’s third:

The Bishop of Constantinople, however, shall have the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome.

And Chalcedon’s twenty-eighth, which quotes the canon of 381 and states the ground for both sees plainly:

For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges (isa presbeia) to the most holy throne of New Rome, justly judging that the city which is honoured with the Sovereignty and the Senate, and enjoys equal privileges with the old imperial Rome, should in ecclesiastical matters also be magnified as she is… and rank next after her.

— Nicaea canon 6, Constantinople canon 3, Chalcedon canon 28.1 The advocated reading takes these at face value: the councils rank Rome first, but as the elder capital and by custom, not as the chair of Peter; the primacy they concede is one of honour and precedence, and the parallel elevation of a confessedly non-apostolic see (Constantinople) on frankly civic grounds shows the fathers were not operating a doctrine of Petrine jurisdiction at all.

Who advances it

The reading is the ordinary conclusion of critical conciliar history — Eastern Orthodox and Protestant historians of the councils, and the editorial tradition of the standard English collection (the Percival volume of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers), who read the canons as ranking sees by ecclesiastical custom and civil status. It is also, at the decisive points, conceded from within: the fathers of Chalcedon who framed canon 28 said in so many words that even Rome’s privileges were granted “because it was the royal city,” and the African bishops of the Apiarius affair — the church of Augustine — treated Rome’s claim to a Nicene appellate right as a thing to be checked against the record, not obeyed on sight. The opposing reading, that the councils presuppose and confirm a Roman primacy of jurisdiction, is the Roman-Catholic case; this page weighs the specifically jurisdictional claim and folds its best evidence in below.

The evidence for

Nicaea 6 makes Rome one great see among co-equal metropolitans, grounded in custom. The first ecumenical council, legislating for the whole church, confirms the regional spheres of the chief sees and names Rome not as the rule above the rest but as the parallel that fixes the measure: Alexandria governs Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis “since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also,” and Antioch and the other provinces keep “their privileges” likewise. Rome’s authority is the same in kind as Alexandria’s and Antioch’s, its warrant is “ancient custom” rather than a Petrine commission, and it is bounded to its own suburbicarian sphere. Three hundred bishops treat the see of Rome as a great metropolitanate, not a universal monarchy.1

Constantinople 3 and Chalcedon 28 rank the sees by their cities — Rome included. The canon of 381 gives Constantinople “the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is New Rome”; Chalcedon takes that canon up, quotes it, and states the principle for the whole order — the fathers granted privileges “to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city,” and gave “equal privileges” to New Rome on “the same consideration.” This is the sharpest conciliar datum against the jurisdiction reading, and it cuts at the root: the councils ground Rome’s own pre-eminence in imperial status, and on that same principle raise a see with no apostolic pedigree at all to the second place, over the Petrine-and-Pauline claims of Alexandria and Antioch. A body that derived Rome’s primacy from Peter could not have written that Rome ranks first “because it was the royal city,” nor promoted Constantinople above the apostolic sees by the logic of the capital.1

The one appellate canon was Western and council-granted — and its later use as “Nicene” was exposed. The jurisdiction reading’s strongest conciliar text is Sardica (343), whose canons 3–5 let a deposed bishop take his cause to Julius of Rome, who may order a retrial and send legates. But the canon frames the right as a favour the council bestows — “let us, if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle” — not a prerogative Rome holds by divine descent; the ancient commentators noted that “if it pleases you” is “very strong to shew that it was a right which the Pope had not had hitherto.” And Sardica was a one-party Western synod whose canons never entered the Eastern collections. The exposure came in the fifth century: when Rome (Popes Zosimus, Boniface, Celestine) pressed this very appeal-right against the African churches as a canon of Nicaea, the African bishops, unable to find it in their Nicene copies, sent to Alexandria and Constantinople for authentic texts and demonstrated it was Sardican — copied after the Nicene canons under a shared heading — then wrote Rome that the appeal-right was not to be pressed, lest they “seem to introduce the smoky pride of the world into the Church of Christ.” The Apiarius affair (c. 418–424) is a documented case of a distant church testing a Roman jurisdictional claim against the conciliar record and refusing it.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The councils do concede Rome a real and consistent primacy — a first place among the sees, an honoured precedence that even the see-shuffling of 381 and 451 leaves at the top. The honour reading does not deny this; it is the reading. What the conciliar texts will not yield is the further thing the developed doctrine needs: a constitutive universal jurisdiction, a standing right of the Roman bishop to govern the other churches by Petrine descent. On that, the canons’ own stated grounds — custom (Nicaea) and civic dignity (Constantinople, Chalcedon) — tell against the reading, and the one appellate provision (Sardica) is bounded, Western, and was refused when pressed as universal law. The jurisdiction case must therefore rest its conciliar weight on data the honour reading can absorb; here they are, at their strongest.

The appeals of Sardica are a genuine primatial function — but a granted, not a native, one. It will not do to wave Sardica away: canons 3–5 really do give the bishop of Rome a defined role as a court of further instance, and Rome really did exercise it. The honest weight of it, though, is exactly what the canon says: the synod, “if it seem good to your charity,” chooses to route appeals through Rome “to honour the memory of Peter” — conferring a privilege, not recognizing a sovereignty the see already possessed. And the body conferring it was a Western council of Rome’s own party, its canons unknown to the East. A right the church can vote to Rome is a right the church could withhold; that is a primacy of honour operating, not a jurisdiction descending from Christ. The Apiarius sequel confirms the point historically: the moment the appeal-right was asserted as more — as ancient, Nicene, and binding — the African church exposed the overreach and declined it.2

“Peter has spoken through Leo” — assent to Rome’s teaching, after testing it, not submission to its rule. The jurisdiction reading’s other great conciliar prize is Chalcedon’s acclamation of Leo’s christological Tome: “Peter has spoken through Leo.” It is a real and striking honour to the Roman see. But the same council’s acts show the fathers received the Tome only after examining it against Cyril of Alexandria and satisfying themselves of its orthodoxy — some bishops raising objections that were debated and resolved before the acclamation — and that same council then enacted canon 28, grounding Rome’s primacy in its being the imperial city and giving New Rome equal privileges, over the open protest of Leo’s own legates. A council that submitted to Rome’s jurisdiction does not audit the pope’s doctrine against a third father before accepting it, and does not, in the same sitting, override his protest on the very question of his see’s standing. The acclamation honours the orthodoxy of Leo’s teaching; it is not a surrender of the council’s judgment to his office.3

Leo’s protest itself concedes the civic principle it fights. Rome did not accept canon 28: Leo refused to ratify it, resting the order of the sees on the “unalterable” Nicene canons and declaring the bishops’ assent “null and void.” This is often read as Rome asserting the Petrine principle against the civic one — and it is. But note what Leo defends and how: he fights to keep Alexandria and Antioch in their inherited second and third places precisely because their dignity is apostolic and ancient (Alexandria “through S. Mark, the evangelist and disciple of the blessed Peter,” Antioch where “the Christian name arose” at Peter’s preaching), and he cannot appeal to any conciliar canon that grounds Rome’s primacy in Peter — he must appeal to Nicaea’s custom-based order instead. The protest is evidence that by 451 Rome held a mature Petrine theory (traced on the Leo page); it is not evidence that the councils shared it. The collision is the whole datum: the council grounded even Rome’s pre-eminence in its city, and Rome had to reach outside the conciliar canons — to Nicene “custom” and to Peter — to resist it.4

Assessment

Sound — for the honour-among-equals reading; correspondingly weak for reading a universal jurisdiction out of the conciliar canons. Taken on their own terms, the councils rank the great sees by custom (Nicaea 6) and by the civil dignity of their cities (Constantinople 3, Chalcedon 28), grant Rome a genuine and consistent first place of honour, and — decisively — ground even that first place in Rome’s having been the imperial capital, on a principle that simultaneously raises the non-apostolic see of Constantinople above Alexandria and Antioch. That is not the legislation of a body that presupposes a Petrine monarchy. The jurisdiction reading’s real conciliar data are folded in and weighed, not waved away: the Sardican appeals are a genuine primatial function, but a favour the council granted “if it seem good to your charity,” enacted by a Western synod and refused when later pressed as Nicene law (the Apiarius exposure); and Chalcedon’s “Peter has spoken through Leo” is assent to the orthodoxy of Leo’s Tome, tested against Cyril before it was accepted, by a council that in the same sitting overrode Leo’s protest on the standing of his see. Rome’s own mature Petrine claim is real by 451 — but it appears in Leo’s protest against a council, not in the councils’ canons. The conciliar record is strong evidence for a Roman primacy of honour and precedence and weak evidence for one of jurisdiction: sound. The kindred sub-claims — whether 1 Clement’s intervention, Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas, or Cyprian’s De unitate 4 evidence jurisdiction — are weighed on their own pages and come out the same way; whether Rome’s claim to rule descends from Peter at all is weighed under the Petrine succession of the primacy.
