Leo the Great and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 440–461 (bishop of Rome; the primacy statements gathered here fall c. 445–451) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo the Great states the mature Petrine theory in its fullest patristic form: the bishop of Rome is Peter’s heir, in whom Peter himself still lives, speaks, and judges; the commission of Matthew 16 was given to Peter as a standing office that descends to the Roman see, so that its holder is “the primate of all bishops” whose care reaches the whole church. This is the strongest ancient case for a Roman primacy of jurisdiction — and the timeline sets it at full strength. The honest limits stand beside it: Leo’s jurisdiction in the West leaned on an imperial rescript (Valentinian III, 445); his Tome was received in the East for its orthodoxy (“Peter has spoken through Leo”), not by submission to his rule; and the same council that acclaimed the Tome grounded even Rome’s primacy in imperial status (canon 28) and overrode Leo’s protest.

Claims & evidence

Peter still lives and judges in his heir’s see

Preaching each year on the anniversary of his own elevation, Leo works out the theory that would define the Roman claim. What Christ instituted in Peter is permanent, and Peter — not merely his memory but his living authority — abides in the see:

And the strength of the foundation, on which the whole superstructure of the Church is reared, is not weakened by the weight of the temple that rests upon it. For the solidity of that faith which was praised in the chief of the Apostles is perpetual: and as that remains which Peter believed in Christ, so that remains which Christ instituted in Peter… The dispensation of Truth therefore abides, and the blessed Peter persevering in the strength of the Rock, which he has received, has not abandoned the helm of the Church, which he undertook… And so if anything is rightly done and rightly decreed by us… it is of his work and merits whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See.

— Leo, Sermon III (on the anniversary of his elevation), §§II–III, source (Feltoe translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XII).1 The consequence for the office is drawn out in the same sermon: the bishop of Rome is Peter’s heir, and the honour paid him on this day is paid to Peter in him:

…in my humble person he may be recognized and honoured, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds, together with the charge of the sheep commended to him, and whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir… [the bishops] transfer the chief honour of this service… to him whom they know to be not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops. When therefore we utter our exhortations in your ears, holy brethren, believe that he is speaking whose representative we are: because it is his warning that we give, nothing else but his teaching that we preach.

— Leo, Sermon III, §IV, source.1 This is the developed doctrine entire: Matthew 16 read not as a one-time praise of Peter but as the founding of a perpetual office; the Roman bishop as its heir, an “unworthy” vessel through whom Peter’s undiminished authority still acts; and a resulting claim to be “the primate of all bishops.”

Rome is “the head of the world” through Peter’s see

On the feast of Peter and Paul, Leo grounds Rome’s standing not in its empire but in the apostle buried there — the see, not the city, is what made Rome the world’s head:

These are they who promoted thee to such glory, that being made a holy nation, a chosen people, a priestly and royal state, and the head of the world through the blessed Peter’s holy See thou didst attain a wider sway by the worship of God than by earthly government.

— Leo, Sermon LXXXII (on the apostles Peter and Paul), §I, source (Feltoe translation, NPNF Series II, Vol. XII).2 In the same sermon Peter is “chief of the Apostolic band,” “appointed to the citadel of the Roman empire, that the light of Truth… might spread itself more effectively throughout the body of the world from the head itself” — Rome the head because Peter’s chair is there. (The sermon also treats Peter and Paul as “equal in their election,” the twin eyes of the body; the see, in Leo’s telling, is Peter’s.)

The Tome received at Chalcedon: doctrinal authority acknowledged

Leo’s claim was not mere rhetoric on his own day: in 449 his christological letter to Flavian of Constantinople — the Tome — defined the two natures of Christ against Eutyches, and when it was read to the Fourth Ecumenical Council at Chalcedon in 451 the assembled bishops acclaimed it:

Peter has spoken through Leo.

— acclamation of the fathers at the Council of Chalcedon, second session (10 October 451), on the reading of Leo’s Tome (Letter 28 to Flavian).3 Leo read the outcome as a vindication of his see: the heresy, he wrote to the emperor Marcian, had been overthrown “through the instrumentality of the Apostolic See.”4 This is the timeline’s clearest case of Rome’s doctrinal authority being received by the whole church — and it is worth stating at full strength before the qualification: the council did not adopt the Tome on Leo’s bare word. Its bishops examined it, tested it against Cyril of Alexandria, and had doubtful passages defended before assenting; the acclamation crowned a reception of the Tome’s orthodoxy, not a submission to Rome’s jurisdiction. On the very question of jurisdiction and rank the same council would rule against Leo (canon 28, below).

The imperial prop: Valentinian III’s rescript (445)

Leo’s jurisdiction in the West did not rest on the sermons alone. When he moved against Hilary of Arles for exceeding his authority in Gaul but lacked the power to enforce the sentence there, the emperor Valentinian III issued a rescript backing him — grounding the primacy in three props at once, Peter, the city, and a council:

Since, then, the primacy of the Apostolic See is established by the merit of St. Peter (who is the chief among the bishops), by the majesty of the city of Rome, and finally by the authority of a holy council, no one, without inexcusable presumption, may attempt anything against the authority of that see.

— Valentinian III, edict on the authority of the bishop of Rome (8 July 445), Robinson’s translation.5 The rescript decrees that the bishops “of the other provinces, shall attempt nothing counter to ancient custom without the authority of the venerable father of the Eternal City,” and that “whatever shall be sanctioned by the authority of the Apostolic See shall be law.” It is a genuine assertion of Roman jurisdiction — but it is an imperial one, effective in the Western empire, and it rests the primacy expressly on “the majesty of the city of Rome” alongside Peter: a civil prop under the Petrine claim, of exactly the kind the Eastern council would soon turn against Rome.

Notes

  • Why Leo is the climax of the timeline. Everything the earlier witnesses leave contestable — whether Matthew 16 founds an office, whether that office descends to Rome, whether the descent carries jurisdiction — Leo asserts outright and systematically. He is the first to state the whole theory in one voice: a perpetual Petrine office, the Roman bishop as its living heir, and a resulting primacy over “all bishops.” The wiki sets this out at full strength; it is the strongest patristic case for jurisdictional primacy, and the reader should meet it as such.
  • What the evidence does and does not settle. Sermon III is Leo’s claim about his own office, preached to his own congregation — powerful testimony to what a mid-fifth-century Roman bishop believed, but not an independent witness that the wider church conceded it. The three external supports each carry a limit. (1) The Tome’s reception acknowledged Rome’s doctrinal weight, but on examination and for its orthodoxy, not by jurisdictional submission. (2) Valentinian’s rescript is imperial backing in the West, and grounds the primacy partly in the city’s “majesty.” (3) At the same Chalcedon, canon 28 grounded even Rome’s first place in its having been “the royal city” and gave New Rome “equal privileges” — and when Leo protested and refused to ratify it, the protest was overridden in the East, which received canon 28 as law. See the Council of Chalcedon (canon 28) for the collision in full: the pope grounded Rome in Peter, the council in the city.
  • Two things granted together. Leo’s theory is the maturest form of the jurisdictional reading and the strongest datum for it; and the same decade shows that reading contested where it mattered — the Eastern church honouring Rome’s orthodoxy while declining its rule, and grounding the sees’ rank in their cities. Both stand on the timeline. Whether the early primacy is one of honour tracking apostolic and civic pre-eminence or of universal jurisdiction is weighed in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • Cross-links. The apostolic-succession machinery Leo’s claim runs on — ordination as the channel of Peter’s office — is traced under the ministerial priesthood; the tradition of Peter’s mission and death at Rome, on which Sermon LXXXII draws, sits on the apostle Peter. Cyprian’s conciliar-egalitarian “no bishop of bishops” (c. 256) is the sharpest ancient counter to Leo’s “primate of all bishops”; see Cyprian.
