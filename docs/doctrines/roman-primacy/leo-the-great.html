Leo the Great and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 440–461 (bishop of Rome; the primacy statements gathered here fall c. 445–451) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo the Great sets out a Petrine theory of the Roman see: the bishop of Rome is Peter’s heir, in whom Peter himself still lives, speaks, and judges; the commission of Matthew 16 was given to Peter as a standing office that descends to the Roman see, so that its holder is “the primate of all bishops” whose care reaches the whole church. In the same years the emperor Valentinian III issued a rescript (445) backing the Roman see’s authority in the West; Leo’s Tome was acclaimed at Chalcedon (“Peter has spoken through Leo”) after the bishops had examined it; and the same council’s canon 28, which Leo refused, ranked Constantinople next after Rome with “equal privileges.”

Claims & evidence

Peter living and judging in his heir’s see (Sermon III)

Preaching each year on the anniversary of his own elevation, Leo sets out his theory of the Roman see. What Christ instituted in Peter is permanent, and Peter — not merely his memory but his living authority — abides in the see:

And the strength of the foundation, on which the whole superstructure of the Church is reared, is not weakened by the weight of the temple that rests upon it. For the solidity of that faith which was praised in the chief of the Apostles is perpetual: and as that remains which Peter believed in Christ, so that remains which Christ instituted in Peter… The dispensation of Truth therefore abides, and the blessed Peter persevering in the strength of the Rock, which he has received, has not abandoned the helm of the Church, which he undertook… And so if anything is rightly done and rightly decreed by us… it is of his work and merits whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See.

— Leo, Sermon III (on the anniversary of his elevation), §§II–III, source (Feltoe translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XII).1 The consequence for the office is drawn out in the same sermon: the bishop of Rome is Peter’s heir, and the honour paid him on this day is paid to Peter in him:

…in my humble person he may be recognized and honoured, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds, together with the charge of the sheep commended to him, and whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir… [the bishops] transfer the chief honour of this service… to him whom they know to be not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops. When therefore we utter our exhortations in your ears, holy brethren, believe that he is speaking whose representative we are: because it is his warning that we give, nothing else but his teaching that we preach.

— Leo, Sermon III, §IV, source.1 In this sermon Matthew 16 is read not as a one-time praise of Peter but as the founding of a perpetual office; the Roman bishop as its heir, an “unworthy” vessel through whom Peter’s undiminished authority still acts; and a resulting claim to be “the primate of all bishops.”

Rome “the head of the world” through Peter’s see (Sermon LXXXII)

On the feast of Peter and Paul, Leo grounds Rome’s standing in the apostle buried there rather than in its empire:

These are they who promoted thee to such glory, that being made a holy nation, a chosen people, a priestly and royal state, and the head of the world through the blessed Peter’s holy See thou didst attain a wider sway by the worship of God than by earthly government.

— Leo, Sermon LXXXII (on the apostles Peter and Paul), §I, source (Feltoe translation, NPNF Series II, Vol. XII).2 In the same sermon Peter is “chief of the Apostolic band,” “appointed to the citadel of the Roman empire, that the light of Truth… might spread itself more effectively throughout the body of the world from the head itself” — Rome the head because Peter’s chair is there. (The sermon also treats Peter and Paul as “equal in their election,” the twin eyes of the body; the see, in Leo’s telling, is Peter’s.)

The Tome at Chalcedon

In 449 Leo’s christological letter to Flavian of Constantinople — the Tome — defined the two natures of Christ against Eutyches, and when it was read to the Fourth Ecumenical Council at Chalcedon in 451 the assembled bishops acclaimed it:

Peter has spoken through Leo.

— acclamation of the fathers at the Council of Chalcedon, second session (10 October 451), on the reading of Leo’s Tome (Letter 28 to Flavian).3 Leo read the outcome as a vindication of his see: the heresy, he wrote to the emperor Marcian, had been overthrown “through the instrumentality of the Apostolic See.”4 The council’s bishops examined the Tome, tested it against Cyril of Alexandria, and had doubtful passages defended before assenting. How the acclamation and the examination have been read, with the proponents of each reading, is set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction? On the rank of the sees the same council enacted canon 28, which Leo refused (see the Council of Chalcedon).

Valentinian III’s rescript (445)

When Leo moved against Hilary of Arles, whom he charged with exceeding his authority in Gaul, the emperor Valentinian III issued a rescript backing him, naming three grounds of the primacy at once: Peter, the city, and a council:

Since, then, the primacy of the Apostolic See is established by the merit of St. Peter (who is the chief among the bishops), by the majesty of the city of Rome, and finally by the authority of a holy council, no one, without inexcusable presumption, may attempt anything against the authority of that see.

— Valentinian III, edict on the authority of the bishop of Rome (8 July 445), Robinson’s translation.5 The rescript decrees that the bishops “of the other provinces, shall attempt nothing counter to ancient custom without the authority of the venerable father of the Eternal City,” and that “whatever shall be sanctioned by the authority of the Apostolic See shall be law.” The rescript is an imperial act, effective in the Western empire, and names “the majesty of the city of Rome” and “the authority of a holy council” alongside the merit of Peter.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Leo states that Matthew 16 founds an office, that the office descends to the bishop of Rome, and that it carries a primacy over “all bishops.” How the earlier witnesses on this timeline relate to it is disputed (see the disputed readings). His sermons are the earliest extant statement of the whole theory in one voice: a perpetual Petrine office, the Roman bishop as its living heir, and a resulting primacy over the episcopate.
  • Reception. Sermon III is Leo’s teaching about his own office, preached to his own congregation. Three events of the same years bear on its reception: (1) the acclamation of the Tome at Chalcedon, after the bishops’ examination of it; (2) Valentinian’s rescript in the West, which grounds the primacy in Peter’s merit, the city’s “majesty,” and a council; (3) Chalcedon’s canon 28, which gave New Rome “equal privileges” and which Leo refused. See the Council of Chalcedon (canon 28). How Leo’s claims relate to the conciliar record is set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?
  • Cross-links. The apostolic succession on which Leo’s claim draws — ordination as the channel of Peter’s office — is traced under the ministerial priesthood; the tradition of Peter’s mission and death at Rome, on which Sermon LXXXII draws, sits on the apostle Peter. For Cyprian’s “no bishop of bishops” (256), see Cyprian.
