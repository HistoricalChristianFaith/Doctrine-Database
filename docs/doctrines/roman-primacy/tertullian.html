Tertullian and the Power of the Keys

Dates: c. 210 (De Pudicitia / On Modesty, from Tertullian’s Montanist years, c. 208–212; De Praescriptione Haereticorum / The Prescription Against Heretics is earlier, c. 200) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155–c. 220) supplies the timeline’s earliest explicit denial that the Petrine commission of Matthew 16 passes as a standing jurisdiction to the bishop of Rome. In On Modesty (De Pudicitia), written after he had joined the rigorist Montanist (“New Prophecy”) movement and turned against the mainstream (“psychic”) church, he ridicules a Roman bishop’s edict absolving adultery and fornication — mocking its author as “Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops” — and argues at length (ch. 21) that the keys and the power of binding and loosing were given to Peter personally, or to spiritual men, and were not transmitted to “every Church akin to Peter,” least of all to “the Church which consists of a number of bishops.” This is placed on the timeline as a counter-datum: c. 210 the inference from the Matthew 16 keys to a Roman jurisdiction was already being contested head-on. The honest complication — kept in full view below — is that this is the polemical, schismatic Tertullian; the same author, a decade earlier and still in the catholic church, had appealed warmly to Rome’s double-apostolic pedigree (The Prescription Against Heretics 32, 36). Both are reported here; the shift is itself part of the evidence.

Related doctrines: the keys and binding-and-loosing of Matthew 16:19 / 18:18 that Tertullian confines to Peter are the founding proof-texts weighed in the summary’s Arguments section; Tertullian’s wider quarrel with the “psychic” church over who may forgive sins touches the ministerial priesthood.

Claims & evidence

The mocked edict: “Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops” (De Pudicitia 1)

Tertullian opens On Modesty by rounding on a recent edict — issued, on the usual identification, by Callistus of Rome (bishop c. 217–222; some scholars place the treatise a little earlier under Zephyrinus) — that offered church reconciliation to penitent adulterers and fornicators. Tertullian’s sarcasm fastens on the grandeur of the claim behind it:

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops — issues an edict: “I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication.” O edict, on which cannot be inscribed, “Good deed!” … But it is in the church that this (edict) is read, and in the church that it is pronounced; and (the church) is a virgin!

— On Modesty 1.1 The titles are thrown as taunts, not conceded as descriptions: Tertullian’s point is that no bishop — however styled “bishop of bishops” — has the power he is arrogating. The passage is doubly useful to the primacy timeline. It shows a Roman bishop, c. 210, evidently making a sweeping claim to remit even capital sins by his own sentence; and it shows that claim being met with open ridicule by a leading Latin churchman, who treats “bishop of bishops” as a pretension to be exposed rather than an office to be obeyed.

The keys were given to Peter personally, not to Rome’s see (De Pudicitia 21)

In the treatise’s theological core, Tertullian confronts the proof-text directly. If Rome grounds its power to bind and loose on what Christ said to Peter in Matthew 16, then (he argues) it has misread the plain grammar of the grant: every clause is singular, spoken to Peter:

If, because the Lord has said to Peter, “Upon this rock will I build My Church,” “to thee have I given the keys of the heavenly kingdom;” or, “Whatsoever thou shalt have bound or loosed in earth, shall be bound or loosed in the heavens,” you therefore presume that the power of binding and loosing has derived to you, that is, to every Church akin to Peter, what sort of man are you, subverting and wholly changing the manifest intention of the Lord, conferring (as that intention did) this (gift) personally upon Peter? “On thee,” He says, “will I build My Church;” and, “I will give to thee the keys,” not to the Church; and, “Whatsoever thou shalt have loosed or bound,” not what they shall have loosed or bound.

— On Modesty 21.2 He then makes the grant self-exhausting in Peter’s own acts: “In (Peter) himself the Church was reared … (Peter) himself essayed the key” — unlocking the kingdom to Israel at Pentecost and to the nations at the Jerusalem council. And he draws the conclusion the primacy reading cannot use: whatever power over sins the keys convey belongs, on the pattern of Peter, to spiritual men — a prophet or apostle of the Spirit — and precisely not to a college of bishops:

And accordingly “the Church,” it is true, will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops. For the right and arbitrament is the Lord’s, not the servant’s; God’s Himself, not the priest’s.

— On Modesty 21.3 This is the sharpest early statement of a reading the summary’s Arguments weigh in detail: that the Matthew 16 keys were a personal endowment of Peter, not a jurisdiction handed down through a see. What Tertullian denies is not that Peter received a unique key — he insists on it — but that Rome’s bishop is the standing heir of it.

The earlier Tertullian: Rome doubly apostolic (The Prescription Against Heretics 32, 36)

The counter-datum has its own counter-weight, and honesty requires it be shown. A decade before, while still in the catholic church and writing against the Gnostics, Tertullian had appealed to the apostolic churches — Rome chief among them — as living guarantors of the true tradition. He cites Rome’s succession from Peter as a model of authentic pedigree:

… as the church of Smyrna, which records that Polycarp was placed therein by John; as also the church of Rome, which makes Clement to have been ordained in like manner by Peter.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 32.4 And in ch. 36 he sends the inquirer to the apostolic sees whose thrones still stand, ending with Rome — the church that heard both Peter and Paul, and holds a doubled apostolic authority:

Since, moreover, you are close upon Italy, you have Rome, from which there comes even into our own hands the very authority (of apostles themselves). How happy is its church, on which apostles poured forth all their doctrine along with their blood! where Peter endures a passion like his Lord’s! where Paul wins his crown in a death like John’s …

— The Prescription Against Heretics 36.5 The pre-Montanist Tertullian thus honours Rome’s apostolic standing as fully as any early Western witness — but note what he honours it as: an apostolic church, one specimen (alongside Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus) of the many sees where the apostles’ own teaching can be checked, and grounded in Peter and Paul jointly. Even here the accent falls on Rome’s doctrinal reliability, not on a transmitted Petrine jurisdiction; and he attributes the founding not to a bishop-Peter but to Peter’s ordaining of Clement.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline: the earliest explicit resistance to the Matthew 16 inference. Where Victor (c. 190) supplies the earliest recorded attempt at Roman jurisdiction and its rebuff, Tertullian supplies the earliest recorded argument that the scriptural warrant for such jurisdiction fails: the keys were Peter’s personally, not the Roman see’s by succession. That is a datum whatever one makes of the man making it.
  • The Montanist and polemical setting — stated plainly. On Modesty is a partisan tract from Tertullian’s schismatic years: he now regards the mainstream (“psychic”) church as lax and himself as of the church “of the Spirit.” His argument that the power to forgive belongs to spiritual men, not to bishops, serves that rigorism — he wants to withhold reconciliation from grave sinners, not to redistribute papal power on principle. So his motive is contested and his conclusion self-serving. But the exegetical point stands independent of the motive: he reads the grammar of Matthew 16 as a personal grant to Peter, and a great many later interpreters who share none of his Montanism have read it the same way. The wiki reports the framing so the reader can weigh it; it does not discount the exegesis merely because a schismatic voiced it.
  • The shift from Prescription to Pudicitia is real — and cuts both ways. The younger, catholic Tertullian appealed to Rome’s apostolicity; the older, Montanist Tertullian mocks its bishop. A primacy reading can say the earlier praise is the “undistorted” Tertullian and the later scorn mere sectarian bitterness. A counter-reading can say the earlier praise was only ever of Rome as an apostolic witness (never as a jurisdiction), so that nothing in it was surrendered when he later denied the jurisdiction. Both are on the page; the point common to both is that even at his most Rome-friendly, Tertullian grounds Rome in Peter and Paul and in doctrinal fidelity, not in a transmitted Petrine office.
  • Which bishop? The edict of Pudicitia 1 is most commonly assigned to Callistus (c. 217–222), whose laxist penitential policy is independently attacked in the roughly contemporary Refutation of All Heresies ascribed to Hippolytus; a minority dates the treatise earlier and points to Zephyrinus (c. 199–217). Tertullian names no one; the identification is inferred from the policy described. Either way the datum — a Roman bishop claiming to remit capital sins and being mocked as “bishop of bishops” for it — is unaffected.
  • Provenance. Both works survive in Tertullian’s own Latin; quotations here follow the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation (Vol. III–IV, Roberts–Donaldson).
