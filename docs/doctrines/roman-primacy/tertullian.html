Tertullian and the Power of the Keys

Dates: c. 210 (De Pudicitia / On Modesty, from Tertullian’s Montanist years, c. 208–212; De Praescriptione Haereticorum / The Prescription Against Heretics is earlier, c. 200) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155–c. 220) supplies the earliest extant argument that the keys of Matthew 16 were given to Peter personally and did not pass to “every Church akin to Peter.” If the bishop he answers was the bishop of Rome, as Fortescue and Newman read him, it is also the earliest extant explicit denial that the Petrine commission passes to Rome’s bishop; Roger Pearse’s introduction to the treatise suggests “perhaps Agrippinus of Carthage.” In On Modesty (De Pudicitia), written after he had joined the rigorist Montanist (“New Prophecy”) movement and turned against the mainstream (“psychic”) church, he ridicules a bishop’s edict absolving adultery and fornication — mocking its author as “Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops” — and argues at length (ch. 21) that the keys and the power of binding and loosing were given to Peter personally, or to spiritual men, and were not transmitted to “every Church akin to Peter,” least of all to “the Church which consists of a number of bishops.” The same author, a decade earlier and before his Montanist period, had appealed warmly to Rome’s double-apostolic pedigree (The Prescription Against Heretics 32, 36). Both are reported here.

Related doctrines: the keys and binding-and-loosing of Matthew 16:19 / 18:18 that Tertullian confines to Peter are the founding proof-texts of the Roman claim traced on the primacy of Rome; Tertullian’s wider quarrel with the “psychic” church over who may forgive sins touches the ministerial priesthood.

Claims & evidence

The mocked edict: “Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops” (De Pudicitia 1)

Tertullian opens On Modesty by rounding on a recent edict — assigned to a bishop of Rome by Fortescue and Newman (Callistus, c. 217–222, and Zephyrinus being the candidates) and, tentatively, to Agrippinus of Carthage by Roger Pearse (see the Notes) — that offered church reconciliation to penitent adulterers and fornicators. Tertullian’s sarcasm fastens on the grandeur of the claim behind it:

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops — issues an edict: “I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication.” O edict, on which cannot be inscribed, “Good deed!” … But it is in the church that this (edict) is read, and in the church that it is pronounced; and (the church) is a virgin!

— On Modesty 1.1 Tertullian uses the titles in mockery; his point is that no bishop, however styled, has the power to remit these sins. The passage records a bishop, of Rome or of Carthage according to the identification adopted, claiming c. 210 to remit sins of adultery and fornication by edict, and a leading Latin writer ridiculing both the claim and the title “bishop of bishops.”

The keys as a personal grant to Peter (De Pudicitia 21)

In the treatise’s theological core, Tertullian confronts the proof-text directly. If the edict’s author grounds his power to bind and loose on what Christ said to Peter in Matthew 16, then (Tertullian argues) he has misread the plain grammar of the grant: every clause is singular, spoken to Peter:

If, because the Lord has said to Peter, “Upon this rock will I build My Church,” “to thee have I given the keys of the heavenly kingdom;” or, “Whatsoever thou shalt have bound or loosed in earth, shall be bound or loosed in the heavens,” you therefore presume that the power of binding and loosing has derived to you, that is, to every Church akin to Peter, what sort of man are you, subverting and wholly changing the manifest intention of the Lord, conferring (as that intention did) this (gift) personally upon Peter? “On thee,” He says, “will I build My Church;” and, “I will give to thee the keys,” not to the Church; and, “Whatsoever thou shalt have loosed or bound,” not what they shall have loosed or bound.

— On Modesty 21.2 He then makes the grant self-exhausting in Peter’s own acts: “In (Peter) himself the Church was reared … (Peter) himself essayed the key” — unlocking the kingdom to Israel at Pentecost and to the nations at the Jerusalem council. And he concludes that whatever power over sins the keys convey belongs, on the pattern of Peter, to spiritual men — a prophet or apostle of the Spirit — and precisely not to a college of bishops:

And accordingly “the Church,” it is true, will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops. For the right and arbitrament is the Lord’s, not the servant’s; God’s Himself, not the priest’s.

— On Modesty 21.3 Tertullian thus reads the Matthew 16 keys as a personal endowment of Peter, not a jurisdiction handed down through a see. He does not deny that Peter received a unique key — he insists on it — but denies that “every Church akin to Peter” inherits it.

The earlier Tertullian: Rome doubly apostolic (The Prescription Against Heretics 32, 36)

A decade before, before his Montanist period and writing against the Gnostics, Tertullian had appealed to the apostolic churches — Rome chief among them — as living guarantors of the true tradition. He cites Rome’s succession from Peter as a model of authentic pedigree:

… as the church of Smyrna, which records that Polycarp was placed therein by John; as also the church of Rome, which makes Clement to have been ordained in like manner by Peter.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 32.4 And in ch. 36 he sends the inquirer to the apostolic sees whose thrones still stand, ending with Rome — the church that heard both Peter and Paul, and holds a doubled apostolic authority:

Since, moreover, you are close upon Italy, you have Rome, from which there comes even into our own hands the very authority (of apostles themselves). How happy is its church, on which apostles poured forth all their doctrine along with their blood! where Peter endures a passion like his Lord’s! where Paul wins his crown in a death like John’s …

— The Prescription Against Heretics 36.5 Here Tertullian honours Rome among the apostolic churches (alongside Corinth, Philippi and Ephesus) where the apostles’ own teaching can be found, names Peter and Paul together, and credits Peter with ordaining Clement.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Where Victor (c. 190) supplies the earliest extant record of a Roman bishop acting against distant churches and of the resistance to it, Tertullian supplies the earliest extant argument that the keys were Peter’s personally and did not pass to “every Church akin to Peter”; whether the argument is aimed at the Roman see depends on the identification of the edict’s author.
  • The Montanist setting. On Modesty is a polemical tract from Tertullian’s Montanist years: he now regards the mainstream (“psychic”) church as lax and himself as of the church “of the Spirit.” His argument that the power to forgive belongs to spiritual men, not to bishops, serves that rigorism — he argues to withhold reconciliation from grave sinners. His reading of Matthew 16 as a personal grant to Peter is made in that setting.
  • From Prescription to Pudicitia. The younger Tertullian appealed to Rome’s apostolicity; the older, Montanist Tertullian mocks a bishop’s edict and denies that the keys pass to a church. In both works Rome is named with Peter and Paul.
  • Which bishop? Tertullian names no one; the identification is inferred from the policy described and from the titles he mocks. The edict has been assigned to a bishop of Rome: to Callistus (c. 217–222), whose penitential policy is independently attacked in the roughly contemporary Refutation of All Heresies ascribed to Hippolytus, or, on an earlier dating, to Zephyrinus (c. 199–217). Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, 1920) reads the edict as the Pope’s and holds that Tertullian’s “argument against the Pope further shows that the Pope claimed the authority of St. Peter as prince of the apostles”;6 John Henry Newman (Catholic, 1845) likewise writes that Tertullian, “in indignation and bitter mockery,” calls the Pope “the Pontifex Maximus, the Bishop of Bishops.”7 Roger Pearse assigns it, tentatively, to Agrippinus, bishop of Carthage: his introduction to the treatise at tertullian.org speaks of “an edict of a bishop, perhaps Agrippinus of Carthage,” and its bibliography lists G. Bardy, “L’édit d’Agrippinus” (1924), and A. Donini, “L’Editto di Agrippino” (1925) ⚠ unverified — these studies have not been consulted here.8 On any identification the passage records a bishop’s claim to remit these sins and Tertullian’s mockery of him as “bishop of bishops.”
  • Provenance. Both works survive in Tertullian’s own Latin; quotations here follow the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation (Vol. III–IV, Roberts–Donaldson).
