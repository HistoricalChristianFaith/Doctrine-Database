The First Council of Constantinople, canon 3 (381)

Dates: 381 (the Second Ecumenical Council, convened at Constantinople by the emperor Theodosius I; canon 3 among its canons) · Wikipedia
Summary: The second ecumenical council’s third canon gives the bishop of Constantinople “the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is New Rome.” An ecumenical council here gives a civic reason for a see’s rank: Constantinople ranks second because it is the new imperial capital. The canon states no reason for Rome’s own first place. Leo the Great, writing in 451, said the canon had never been brought to the knowledge of the Apostolic See.

Claims & evidence

Canon 3: Constantinople ranked second, “because Constantinople is New Rome”

The canon is a single sentence:

The Bishop of Constantinople, however, shall have the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome.

— Canons of the One Hundred and Fifty Fathers… at Constantinople, Canon III, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV).1 The canon gives Constantinople its new dignity on a political ground: it is “New Rome,” the reigning imperial city. The honour granted is a “prerogative of honour” (presbeia tēs timēs), a precedence that leaves Rome first.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Canon 3 is the earliest extant conciliar statement that ties a great see’s rank to the rank of its city. It leaves Rome first. How it bears on the kind of primacy Rome held is set out at the disputed reading, Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction?
  • Leo’s response (451). Leo the Great, writing to Anatolius of Constantinople after Chalcedon, defended the inherited ranks of Alexandria and Antioch against Constantinople’s elevation and called the canon “the written assent of certain bishops given, as you allege, 60 years ago, and never brought to the knowledge of the Apostolic See by your predecessors.”2 As Percival’s note frames the later Roman refusal of the parallel canon at Chalcedon, the pope “was in reality defending the principle laid down in the canon of Nice, that in such matters the ancient customs should continue”.
  • The sequel at Chalcedon. Seventy years later, Chalcedon’s canon 28 (451) took this canon up, quoted it, and stated a civic ground for both sees — the Fathers granted Rome its privileges “because it was the royal city,” and gave New Rome “equal privileges” on the same reasoning. Leo of Rome protested canon 28. How the two canons bear on the kind of primacy Rome held is set out at the disputed reading above; see also Nicaea’s custom-based spheres (325) and Sardica’s appeal-right (343).
