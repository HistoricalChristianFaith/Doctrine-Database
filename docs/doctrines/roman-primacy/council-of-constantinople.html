The First Council of Constantinople, canon 3 (381)

Dates: 381 (the Second Ecumenical Council, convened at Constantinople by the emperor Theodosius I; canon 3 among its canons) · Wikipedia
Summary: The second ecumenical council’s third canon gives the bishop of Constantinople “the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is New Rome.” On the primacy timeline it is a pointed datum: an ecumenical council frames the ranking of the great sees in civic terms — Constantinople ranks second precisely because it is the new imperial capital — and the reason it gives for Rome’s first place, by the same logic, is that Rome is the old imperial city, not that it is Peter’s see. Rome kept its precedence but never accepted the principle that assigned Constantinople the second place, since that principle grounded rank in political status rather than apostolic right.

Claims & evidence

Canon 3: Constantinople ranked second, “because Constantinople is New Rome”

The canon is a single sentence, and its logic is the whole point:

The Bishop of Constantinople, however, shall have the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome.

— Canons of the One Hundred and Fifty Fathers… at Constantinople, Canon III, source (Percival translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series II, Vol. XIV).1 Two things follow. First, the canon awards Constantinople its new dignity on a frankly political ground — it is “New Rome,” the reigning imperial city — and, in doing so, implies the same ground for Rome’s own primacy: the sees rank as their cities rank. Second, the honour granted is a “prerogative of honour” (presbeia tēs timēs), a precedence in dignity, expressly leaving Rome first — but first as the elder capital, not as the chair of Peter. The canon knows nothing of a Petrine grounding for the order it fixes.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline. Canon 3 is the clearest early conciliar statement that the rank of the great sees tracks the rank of their cities. It touches Rome only to leave it first; but the reason it gives — imperial status — is the reason a jurisdictional, Petrine reading of Rome’s primacy most needs to deny. The honour-versus-jurisdiction adjudication is weighed in the summary’s Arguments section, not settled here.
  • Rome’s response, and the principle at stake. Rome retained its first place but declined to receive canon 3 as law, because it disliked both the elevation of Constantinople over the apostolic sees of Alexandria and Antioch and the civic principle behind it. As Percival’s note frames the later Roman refusal of the parallel canon at Chalcedon, the pope “was in reality defending the principle laid down in the canon of Nice, that in such matters the ancient customs should continue” — i.e. custom and apostolicity, not the shifting dignity of imperial cities. That is precisely the collision the canon stages: the fathers rank by civil status; Rome would rank by apostolic right.
  • The sequel at Chalcedon. Seventy years later, Chalcedon’s canon 28 (451) took this canon up, quoted it, and made its civic logic explicit — the Fathers granted Rome its privileges “because it was the royal city,” and gave New Rome “equal privileges” on the same reasoning. That is when Leo of Rome protested outright. Read as a pair, 381 and 451 are the conciliar backbone of the argument that the fathers grounded Roman pre-eminence in imperial status; see also Nicaea’s custom-based spheres (325) and Sardica’s bounded appeal-right (343).
