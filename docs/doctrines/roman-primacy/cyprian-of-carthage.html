Cyprian of Carthage and the “Chair of Peter”

Dates: c. 251 (On the Unity of the Church) and 256 (the Seventh Council of Carthage) · Cyprian, c. 200–258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248 and martyr under Valerian, the leading Latin churchman of the mid-third century · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is the timeline’s pivotal double datum on the primacy question. He speaks of the church’s unity as founded on Peter and of “the chair of Peter” in language a later papalism would prize — and one recension of On the Unity of the Church 4 (the so-called Primacy Text) says outright that “the primacy is given to Peter.” Yet the same chapter, on its received text, at once levels the apostles (“the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter”), and Cyprian’s settled ecclesiology makes every bishop the heir of Peter’s chair: the episcopate is one, held whole by each bishop, so Peter’s chair grounds the unity of the episcopate, not a Roman monarchy over it. Five years later he acted on that principle against a bishop of Rome: convening eighty-seven African bishops he opened that “no one of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops,” and held that Stephen of Rome’s ruling on rebaptism did not bind the churches of Africa (the clash is told from Rome’s side on the Stephen of Rome page). This page keeps to the primacy question; Cyprian’s wider theology of church order — the one episcopate held in solidum, conciliar decision, the people’s part in election — is on his church-government page, and the priestly side of his ministry on his ministerial-priesthood page.

Related doctrines: the “rock” and chair of Peter of Matthew 16 are the founding proof-texts weighed in the summary’s Arguments section; the recension problem of De unitate 4 (Primacy Text vs. Received Text) and the honour-versus-jurisdiction reading of Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” are argued there in full. Cyprian’s collegial theology of the one episcopate is traced on the government of the church.

Claims & evidence

Unity founded on Peter — but the apostles made equal (On the Unity of the Church 4, received text)

Writing c. 251 against the schism of Novatian, Cyprian grounds the unity of the church in Christ’s singular commission to Peter. On the received text of chapter 4 the passage runs:

The Lord speaks to Peter, saying, “I say unto thee, that thou art Peter; and upon this rock I will build my Church … And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven …” And again to the same He says, after His resurrection, “Feed my sheep.” And although to all the apostles, after His resurrection, He gives an equal power, and says, “As the Father hath sent me, even so send I you: Receive ye the Holy Ghost …” yet, that He might set forth unity, He arranged by His authority the origin of that unity, as beginning from one. Assuredly the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.

— On the Unity of the Church 4.1 Read this way the Petrine emphasis is a figure of oneness, not of Roman supremacy: Christ began the church from a single man so that its unity should be visible, but “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power.” In the next chapter Cyprian draws the ecclesiological conclusion the whole treatise turns on — “The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur) — so that every bishop, not the bishop of Rome alone, holds the chair of Peter (see the fuller treatment on his church-government page).

The two recensions: the “Primacy Text” of chapter 4

Chapter 4 survives in two forms, and the difference bears directly on the primacy question. Alongside the received text above, a second recension — long called the Primacy Text (Textus receptus vs. Textus PT) — adds three clauses that press Peter’s singularity harder. In the Ante-Nicene Fathers apparatus they read, verbatim:

[after “Feed my sheep”:] Upon him, being one, He builds His Church, and commits His sheep to be fed.

[after “the beginning proceeds from unity”:] And the primacy is given to Peter, that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See; and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.

[and, further on:] Who deserts the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded, does he trust that he is in the Church?

— the Primacy-Text additions to On the Unity of the Church 4–5, as flagged in the ANF apparatus.2 Older editors (including the ANF translators) judged these clauses a later interpolation — “beyond all question spurious,” a Roman gloss smuggled into the text to make Cyprian testify to a papal primacy he never taught. Twentieth-century study reversed that verdict: Maurice Bévenot’s critical work argued that both recensions are Cyprian’s own — the Primacy Text an earlier draft that Cyprian himself later softened — and that even the Primacy Text asserts the primacy of Peter as the source and figure of the one episcopate (“they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent”), not a standing jurisdiction of Peter’s Roman successors over the other bishops. Which recension is authentic, and what the Primacy Text amounts to if it is Cyprianic, is weighed as its own question in the summary’s Arguments section; both texts are set out here so the reader can weigh them.

“No bishop of bishops” — the primacy denied in practice (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256)

Whatever the “chair of Peter” meant for Cyprian, it did not mean submission to Rome. In 255–256 he clashed head-on with Stephen of Rome, who forbade the African and Eastern practice of rebaptizing those who came from heretical communities and threatened to cut off from communion the bishops who disagreed — grounding his ruling on his succession from Peter. Cyprian answered by summoning a council. Opening the Seventh Council of Carthage on 1 September 256 — eighty-seven bishops each casting his own sentence — he laid down the principle that no see, Rome included, may bind another bishop’s judgment:

It remains, that upon this same matter each of us should bring forward what we think, judging no man, nor rejecting any one from the right of communion, if he should think differently from us. For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another. But let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ…

— Cyprian’s opening address to the Seventh Council of Carthage (the Sentences of the Eighty-Seven Bishops).3 It is the sharpest denial of a monarchical papacy to survive from a bishop of Cyprian’s stature, and it is aimed squarely at the Roman claim he was then resisting. The office of bishop is exalted; the relations between bishops are those of equal colleagues in one college, unity secured by concord and council rather than by subordination to a single see. The conciliar theology behind it — the one episcopate held in solidum — is developed on his church-government page.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline — the pivot the whole primacy debate turns on. Cyprian is claimed by every side, and honestly so. A primacy reading points to “the chair of Peter,” the unity-from-one argument, and (on one recension) “the primacy is given to Peter”; an anti-primacy reading points to “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter” and “no bishop of bishops.” Both are Cyprian. The wiki does not resolve him to one side on the timeline; it sets out the double datum and routes the adjudication to the Arguments.
  • For Cyprian every bishop sits in Peter’s chair. The load-bearing point, easily missed, is that Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” is not the Roman see in particular but the one episcopate as such. “The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole”: each bishop holds the whole chair of Peter in his own city. So the Petrine language that a later papalism reads as founding Roman primacy grounds, for Cyprian, the equality and unity of all bishops — which is exactly why he could invoke Peter and still deny that any bishop is a “bishop of bishops.”
  • The recension problem — stated without prejudice. The Primacy Text is neither self-evidently a papal forgery (the old view) nor self-evidently a proof of Roman jurisdiction (the apologetic use of it). Bévenot’s widely-accepted case is that Cyprian wrote both versions; even so, the Primacy Text speaks of Peter’s primacy as the origin of a unity in which “they are all shepherds” and the one Rock “is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent” — language of a shared episcopate, not of a monarchy. The authenticity-and-meaning question is argued on its own page in the Arguments section.
  • Provenance. Quotations follow the Roberts–Donaldson translation (Ante-Nicene Fathers, Vol. V) of Cyprian’s own Latin; the Primacy-Text clauses are quoted as the ANF apparatus preserves them.
