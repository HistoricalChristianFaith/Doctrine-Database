Cyprian of Carthage and the “Chair of Peter”

Dates: c. 251 (On the Unity of the Church) and 256 (the Seventh Council of Carthage) · Cyprian, c. 200–258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248 and martyr under Valerian, the leading Latin churchman of the mid-third century · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian supplies two sets of texts on the primacy question. He speaks of the church’s unity as founded on Peter and of “the chair of Peter,” and one recension of On the Unity of the Church 4 (the so-called Primacy Text) says outright that “the primacy is given to Peter.” The same chapter, on its received text, says that “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter,” and the next defines the episcopate as one, each part of it held “for the whole” by each bishop; how Peter’s chair relates to the Roman see is disputed. Five years later, in his dispute with Stephen of Rome over rebaptism, he convened eighty-seven African bishops, opened the council by saying that “no one of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops,” and held that Stephen’s ruling did not bind the churches of Africa (the clash is told from Rome’s side on the Stephen of Rome page). This page keeps to the primacy question; Cyprian’s wider theology of church order — the one episcopate held in solidum, conciliar decision, the people’s part in election — is on his church-government page, and the priestly side of his ministry on his ministerial-priesthood page.

Related doctrines: the “rock” and chair of Peter of Matthew 16 are the founding proof-texts of the Roman claim traced on the primacy of Rome; the recension problem of De unitate 4 (Primacy Text vs. Received Text) and the readings of Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” are set out at the disputed reading, Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? Cyprian’s collegial theology of the one episcopate is traced on the government of the church.

Claims & evidence

Unity founded on Peter (On the Unity of the Church 4, received text)

Writing c. 251, amid the schisms of that year (against Novatian’s party at Rome, on Pierre Batiffol’s reading; against that of Felicissimus at Carthage, on John Chapman’s), Cyprian grounds the unity of the church in Christ’s singular commission to Peter. On the received text of chapter 4 the passage runs:

The Lord speaks to Peter, saying, “I say unto thee, that thou art Peter; and upon this rock I will build my Church … And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven …” And again to the same He says, after His resurrection, “Feed my sheep.” And although to all the apostles, after His resurrection, He gives an equal power, and says, “As the Father hath sent me, even so send I you: Receive ye the Holy Ghost …” yet, that He might set forth unity, He arranged by His authority the origin of that unity, as beginning from one. Assuredly the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.

— On the Unity of the Church 4.1 On the received text, Christ began from one “that He might set forth unity,” and “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power.” In the next chapter Cyprian writes: “The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur); see the fuller treatment on his church-government page.

The two recensions: the “Primacy Text” of chapter 4

Chapter 4 survives in two forms, and the difference bears on the primacy question. Alongside the received text above, a second recension — long called the Primacy Text (abbreviated TR and PT) — adds three clauses that press Peter’s singularity harder. In the Ante-Nicene Fathers apparatus they read, verbatim:

[after “Feed my sheep”:] Upon him, being one, He builds His Church, and commits His sheep to be fed.

[after “the beginning proceeds from unity”:] And the primacy is given to Peter, that there might be shown one Church of Christ and one See; and they are all shepherds, and the Rock is one, which is fed by all the apostles with unanimous consent.

[and, further on:] Who deserts the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded, does he trust that he is in the Church?

— the Primacy-Text additions to On the Unity of the Church 4–5, as printed in the ANF apparatus.2 The ANF editors judged these clauses a later interpolation, “beyond all question spurious,” as did E. W. Benson (1897). John Chapman (1902–03, 1928) and Pierre Batiffol (1911) held both forms Cyprian’s own, the Primacy Text adapted to the schism at Rome, its “chair of Peter” applied to the Roman see. Maurice Bévenot (Jesuit, 1954) argued that both recensions are Cyprian’s own, reversing the order of composition Chapman had proposed; that the Primacy Text presents the primacy of Peter as the source and figure of the one episcopate is also attributed to him (⚠ unverified). Which recension is Cyprian’s, and what the Primacy Text teaches, is set out at the disputed reading, Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?; both texts are given here.

“No bishop of bishops” (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256)

In 255–256 Cyprian opposed Stephen of Rome, who forbade the African and Eastern practice of rebaptizing those who came from communities they held to be heretical and threatened to cut off from communion the bishops who disagreed — grounding his ruling, his opponents report, on his succession from Peter. Cyprian answered by summoning a council. Opening the Seventh Council of Carthage on 1 September 256 — eighty-seven bishops each casting his own sentence — he said:

It remains, that upon this same matter each of us should bring forward what we think, judging no man, nor rejecting any one from the right of communion, if he should think differently from us. For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another. But let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ…

— Cyprian’s opening address to the Seventh Council of Carthage (the Sentences of the Eighty-Seven Bishops).3 The address was given while Cyprian was resisting Stephen’s ruling, and it holds that each bishop has “his own proper right of judgment.” The conciliar theology behind it — the one episcopate held in solidum — is developed on his church-government page.

Notes

  • Reception and debate. Cyprian is cited on more than one side. Chapman, Batiffol and Adrian Fortescue (1920), who find a Petrine primacy at Rome in him, point to “the chair of Peter,” the unity-from-one argument, (on one recension) “the primacy is given to Peter,” and his letters to Cornelius; the ANF editors and Benson point to “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter” and “no bishop of bishops.” The ANF editors gloss the “chair of Peter” as “the universal episcopate”; Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, Paris) holds that for Cyprian “every member of the one episcopate possesses the chair of Peter in common with his fellows.”2 The readings, with their proponents, are set out at the disputed reading, Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction?
  • Provenance. Quotations follow the Roberts–Donaldson translation (Ante-Nicene Fathers, Vol. V) of Cyprian’s own Latin; the Primacy-Text clauses are quoted as the ANF apparatus preserves them.
