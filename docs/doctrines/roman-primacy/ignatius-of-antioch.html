Ignatius of Antioch and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 108 (the seven genuine letters written en route to martyrdom at Rome, c. 107–110) · Wikipedia
Summary: About a decade after 1 Clement, Ignatius — bishop of Antioch, writing under guard on the road to his death — addresses the Roman church in the most exalted salutation he gives any church: it is the one “which presides in the place of the region of the Romans” and “which presides over love” (Greek prokathēmenē tēs agapēs). The same short letter greets no bishop of Rome, alone among the seven, and in it Ignatius declines to command the Romans (“I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you”). Whether the salutation attests a primacy of jurisdiction, a primacy of honour, or a priority of the Roman church among the churches that is neither is disputed. This page reports the letter’s words and the readings of them; Ignatius’s witness to church order proper — the single presiding bishop of his other letters — is treated on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

The salutation: the church “which presides over love” (Romans, salutation)

Every one of Ignatius’s seven letters opens with an elaborate greeting, but none is heaped as high as the one to Rome. Where he salutes the other churches warmly, he salutes Rome with a cascade of superlatives and two verbs of presiding:

Ignatius, who is also called Theophorus, to the Church which has obtained mercy, through the majesty of the Most High Father, and Jesus Christ, His only-begotten Son; the Church which is beloved and enlightened by the will of Him that willeth all things which are according to the love of Jesus Christ our God, which also presides in the place of the region of the Romans, worthy of God, worthy of honour, worthy of the highest happiness, worthy of praise, worthy of obtaining her every desire, worthy of being deemed holy, and which presides over love, is named from Christ, and from the Father…

— Epistle to the Romans, salutation, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, genuine [shorter] recension; also at historicalchristian.faith).1 Two phrases carry the weight. The first is “presides in the place of the region of the Romans” (prokathēmenē … en topōi chōriou Rhōmaiōn). The second, “presides over love” (prokathēmenē tēs agapēs), is the Roman church presiding in, or over, love (agapē). Both are read differently by the scholars named in the Notes below.

Features of the letter cited in the debate

Two further features of the letter are cited in the debate.

(1) It greets no bishop of Rome. Ignatius’s other six letters are full of the office of the single presiding bishop, naming a sitting bishop in city after city and binding the churches to obey him (see his Government of the Church page). In the one letter to Rome no bishop of Rome is named, greeted, or commended to be obeyed. The only episcopal title in the whole letter is Ignatius’s own:

… that God has deemed me, the bishop of Syria, worthy to be sent for from the east unto the west.

— Epistle to the Romans, ch. 2.2 Readers differ on what the silence means. James McCue (Catholic, 1964) writes that “There is some reason to think that Rome did not have an Ignatian-type bishop until possibly the early second century”;3 J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, 1890) notes that “in the letter to the Church of Rome there is not the faintest allusion to the episcopal office from first to last” and reads the letter’s primacy as one “not of the bishop but of the whole church”;4 John Henry Newman (Catholic, 1845) grants that “St. Ignatius is silent in his Epistles on the subject of the Pope’s authority” and holds that “if in fact that authority could not be in active operation then, such silence is not so difficult to account for.”5 Nicholas Afanasiev (Orthodox theologian, Paris) notes that “Ignatius does not say anything about the Bishop of Rome” and holds that “We know that Clement was president of the Roman Church.”6

(2) Ignatius declines to command the Romans. Writing to the other churches he exhorts, directs, and lays down what they must do. To Rome he writes:

I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you. They were apostles; I am but a condemned man: they were free, while I am, even until now, a servant.

— Epistle to the Romans, ch. 4.7 Ignatius asks the Romans only not to intervene to save him from martyrdom, and does so by entreaty. In the same breath he names Peter and Paul as the two apostles who could command; Irenaeus (c. 180) names the same pair as the founders of the Roman church. Quasten reads the refusal to command as deference to a church that “has its authority from the Princes of the Apostles”; McCue notes that Ignatius shows similar deference to the Trallians and the Ephesians.3

Notes

  • Reception and debate. What Ignatius’s honour of Rome amounts to is disputed. Johannes Quasten (Catholic, Patrology, vol. I, 1950) calls the salutation “the earliest avowal of the Primacy of Rome that we possess from the pen of a non-Roman ecclesiastic,” and holds that the letter as a whole shows the honour paid Rome “founded not on the extent of her charitable influence but on her inherent right to universal ecclesiastical supremacy.” James F. McCue (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1964) reads “presides in the land of the Romans” as a regional authority like Antioch’s in Syria, and concludes that Ignatius did not recognize a universal Roman supremacy: “Nothing that he says justifies the conclusion that he saw in the successors of Peter the source of the world-wide unity of the Church.”3
  • Readings of “presides over love.” Adolf Harnack took prokathēmenē tēs agapēs to mean “pre-eminent in love”; F. X. Funk, as presented by Quasten, read it as “presiding over the bond of love,” the bond of love being “another way of saying ‘the Church universal’”; McCue proposes “presiding in the land of the Romans … guardian [there] of Christian faith and love,” and judges that “whatever version one adopts,” the phrase “provides a very uncertain foundation for generalizations about early second-century ecclesiology.”3 Lightfoot reads the two phrases together: the church “hath the presidency” in the region of the Romans, “But immediately afterwards the nature of this supremacy is defined. The presidency of this church is declared to be a presidency of love.”4 Afanasiev reads “presides in love” as presiding “in the concord based on love between all the local churches,” and “presides in the region of the Romans” as “the Church of Rome’s presidency among the local churches situated in Roman country”; the Roman church, he concludes, “indeed has the priority in the whole company of churches united by concord.”6
  • The middle recension only. The quotations above are from the seven letters of the “middle recension,” which the Roberts–Donaldson edition prints as genuine — not from the longer interpolated recension or the additional letters attributed to Ignatius, whose ecclesial language is fuller. That edition prints the short text first in each chapter, and it is that text quoted here.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Ignatius is a principal witness for the monarchical episcopate (one bishop over each church; no bishop of Rome is named in his letter to Rome), and appears also on the ministerial priesthood, the Real Presence, and the intermediate state timelines. The tradition of Peter’s own mission and death at Rome — on which the Roman claim later draws — is traced on the apostle Peter.
