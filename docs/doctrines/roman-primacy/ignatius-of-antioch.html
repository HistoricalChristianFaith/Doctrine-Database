Ignatius of Antioch and the Primacy of Rome

Dates: c. 108 (the seven genuine letters written en route to martyrdom at Rome, c. 107–110) · Wikipedia
Summary: About a decade after 1 Clement, Ignatius — bishop of Antioch, writing under guard on the road to his death — addresses the Roman church in the most exalted salutation he gives any church: it is the one “which presides in the place of the region of the Romans” and “which presides over love” (Greek prokathēmenē tēs agapēs). That striking honorific of Roman pre-eminence is the datum the primacy reading rests on here. But the same short letter carries two honest limits: alone among the seven it greets no bishop of Rome, and Ignatius pointedly declines to command the Romans (“I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you”) — so what the salutation honours may be a primacy of honour and love, not of jurisdiction, over a church that as yet shows no single ruling bishop. This page reports the datum and both limits; Ignatius’s witness to church order proper — the monarchical bishop he everywhere else exalts — is treated on his Government of the Church detail page, which this page does not repeat.

Claims & evidence

The salutation: the church “which presides over love” (Romans, salutation)

Every one of Ignatius’s seven letters opens with an elaborate greeting, but none is heaped as high as the one to Rome. Where he salutes the other churches warmly, he salutes Rome with a cascade of superlatives and two verbs of presiding:

Ignatius, who is also called Theophorus, to the Church which has obtained mercy, through the majesty of the Most High Father, and Jesus Christ, His only-begotten Son; the Church which is beloved and enlightened by the will of Him that willeth all things which are according to the love of Jesus Christ our God, which also presides in the place of the region of the Romans, worthy of God, worthy of honour, worthy of the highest happiness, worthy of praise, worthy of obtaining her every desire, worthy of being deemed holy, and which presides over love, is named from Christ, and from the Father…

— Epistle to the Romans, salutation, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, genuine [shorter] recension; also at historicalchristian.faith).1 Two phrases carry the weight. The first, “presides in the place of the region of the Romans” (prokathēmenē … en topōi chōriou Rhōmaiōn), locates the church at the head of its own region — a geographical or civic pre-eminence. The second, “presides over love” (prokathēmenē tēs agapēs), is the famous and much-disputed one: the Roman church presides in, or over, love (agapē) — read by later Roman apologists as a presidency over the whole community of charity that is the church, and by others as praise of Rome’s renowned generosity and pre-eminent standing, not a title of rule. Either way the register is honour paid, not obedience owed: Ignatius is greeting, not submitting to, the Roman church.

The counterweight inside the same letter: no bishop, no command

Two data sit in the very letter that carries the honorific, and both cut against reading a jurisdictional primacy off it.

(1) It greets no bishop of Rome. Ignatius is the great champion of the single presiding bishop — his other six letters are saturated with the office, naming a sitting bishop in city after city and binding the churches to obey him (see his Government of the Church page). Yet in the one letter to Rome no bishop of Rome is named, greeted, or commended to be obeyed. The only episcopal title in the whole letter is Ignatius’s own:

… that God has deemed me, the bishop of Syria, worthy to be sent for from the east unto the west.

— Epistle to the Romans, ch. 2.2 On the maximal reading this is an argument from silence and no more; but it is a pointed silence, given how loudly the same author exalts the monarchical bishop everywhere else. It is among the reasons many hold that Rome had not yet settled into a single ruling bishop (a monarchical episcopate) at the turn of the second century — the same picture 1 Clement gives a decade earlier, where the Roman church speaks corporately and no one bishop is named.

(2) Ignatius declines to command the Romans. Writing to the other churches he exhorts, directs, and lays down what they must do. To Rome he expressly refuses that posture:

I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you. They were apostles; I am but a condemned man: they were free, while I am, even until now, a servant.

— Epistle to the Romans, ch. 4.3 The remark is doubly telling. Ignatius handles Rome with deference — he asks the Romans only not to intervene to save him from martyrdom, and does so by entreaty, not decree. And in the same breath he names Peter and Paul as the two apostles who could command — the very pair later tradition makes the founders of the Roman see. That association of Rome with Peter and Paul is a genuine strand of the primacy dossier (it recurs in Irenaeus and beyond); but here it is invoked precisely to mark the distance between apostolic authority and Ignatius’s own, not to transfer that authority to a Roman bishop.

Notes

  • Honour or jurisdiction? That Ignatius honours Rome above the other churches is plain and is genuine early evidence of Roman pre-eminence — the prokathēmenē tēs agapēs is among the most quoted phrases in the whole debate. What the honour amounts to — a primacy of jurisdiction already in view, or a primacy of honour, love, and precedence — is the contested question, and it is not settled here. The letter’s own two limits (no bishop greeted, no command issued) pull toward the honour-and-love reading; the extravagance of the salutation is pressed the other way. The adjudication belongs to the summary’s Arguments section, not to this timeline block.
  • What “presides over love” does not obviously mean. The phrase is often quoted as if it plainly awarded Rome a presidency over the universal church (the “community of love”). It may; but the grammar leaves it open, and Ignatius uses agapē elsewhere for the local congregation and for the love-feast, not only for the church catholic. Reading a jurisdictional claim out of a two-word phrase in a salutation is exactly the sort of over-precise inference the Arguments section keeps in view; the honest report is that the phrase is honorific and striking, and genuinely ambiguous as to scope.
  • The middle recension only. The quotations above are from the seven genuine (“middle recension”) letters — not the later interpolated “long recension” nor the spurious epistles, which inflate Ignatius’s ecclesial language and cannot bear historical weight. The Roberts–Donaldson edition prints the genuine short text first in each chapter, and it is that text quoted here.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Ignatius is the hinge witness for the monarchical episcopate (one bishop over each church — the office conspicuously absent from his letter to Rome), and appears also on the ministerial priesthood, the Real Presence, and the intermediate state timelines. The tradition of Peter’s own mission and death at Rome — the ground on which the Roman claim is later built — is traced on the apostle Peter.
