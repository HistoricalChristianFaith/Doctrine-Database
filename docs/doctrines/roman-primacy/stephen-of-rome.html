Stephen of Rome and the Rebaptism Controversy

Dates: bishop of Rome c. 254–257 (the rebaptism controversy, 255–256) · Wikipedia
Summary: Stephen supplies the earliest extant report of a bishop of Rome grounding a binding claim over other churches explicitly on his succession from Peter. When the churches of Africa (under Cyprian) and the East (under Firmilian of Caesarea) held that converts from communities they counted heretical must be baptized afresh, Stephen forbade the practice, invoked the authority of the Roman see as Peter’s, and threatened to cut off from communion the bishops who disagreed. Cyprian answered by convening eighty-seven African bishops on the principle that “no one of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops”; Firmilian answered with a letter charging that Stephen, “who so boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter,” had by his own act “cut himself off” from the church’s unity. Stephen’s letters do not survive whole: Cyprian quotes one sentence of his ruling on rebaptism, and his claim to the succession from Peter reaches us only as reported by Firmilian.

Related doctrines: how Stephen’s Petrine claim bears on Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” is set out at the disputed reading, Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? The rebaptism dispute itself — who may confer valid baptism — touches baptism and the ministerial priesthood; Cyprian’s conciliar answer is on the government of the church.

Claims & evidence

Stephen’s claim: the succession from Peter, and the threat to cut off dissenters (255–256)

The dispute arose over a practical question — whether those baptized in communities regarded as heretical or schismatic were truly baptized, or must be baptized on entering the catholic communion. Africa and the East said they must; Stephen ruled that they must not, and pressed his ruling on the other churches under pain of broken communion. Stephen’s letters survive only in his opponents’ quotations. Cyprian, writing to Pompey, quotes the ruling Stephen sent to Africa: “If any one, therefore, come to you from any heresy whatever, let nothing be innovated (or done) which has not been handed down, to wit, that hands be imposed on him for repentance.”1 Firmilian of Caesarea charges Stephen with breaking a peace his predecessors had kept:

… on this account there is no departure at all from the peace and unity of the Catholic Church, such as Stephen has now dared to make; breaking the peace against you, which his predecessors have always kept with you in mutual love and honour.

— Firmilian, in the letter preserved among Cyprian’s correspondence.2 The ground Stephen claimed was Petrine. Firmilian twice fastens on it — that Stephen “boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter,” and again that he “announces that he holds by succession the throne of Peter”:

And in this respect I am justly indignant at this so open and manifest folly of Stephen, that he who so boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter, on whom the foundations of the Church were laid, should introduce many other rocks and establish new buildings of many churches…

— Firmilian.3 This is the earliest extant report of a bishop of Rome resting a claim over other churches expressly on his descent from Peter.

Cyprian’s answer: “no bishop of bishops” (Carthage 256)

Cyprian held that Stephen’s ruling did not bind the African churches and answered it in council — eighty-seven bishops, each casting his own sentence — opening with the words: “For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience.” That address, and the collegial theology behind it, are quoted and set in context on Cyprian’s page and his church-government page.4

Firmilian’s answer: “you have excommunicated yourself alone from all” (256)

Firmilian of Caesarea, writing to Cyprian, answered Stephen more sharply. He charges that in claiming Peter’s throne to found “many other rocks” — that is, to accept baptisms given by those he counted heretics — Stephen betrays the very unity he invokes: he “betrays and deserts unity,” so that “the truth of the Christian Rock is overshadowed, and in some measure abolished, by him.” And he turns Stephen’s threat of excommunication back on its author:

… how great sin have you heaped up for yourself, when you cut yourself off from so many flocks! For it is yourself that you have cut off. Do not deceive yourself, since he is really the schismatic who has made himself an apostate from the communion of ecclesiastical unity. For while you think that all may be excommunicated by you, you have excommunicated yourself alone from all.

— Firmilian.5 The ANF editors note that the letter is written “with a little more vehemence and acerbity than becomes a bishop.” Like Cyprian, Firmilian did not accept Stephen’s appeal to Peter as binding on his church. No excommunication of the African or Eastern churches is recorded as taking effect; the dispute lapsed at Stephen’s death in 257.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Where Victor (c. 190) attempted to excommunicate the churches of Asia over the Easter date, Stephen (256) attempted to overrule the churches of Africa and the East over rebaptism, and did so, his opponents report, on an explicitly Petrine ground — “the succession from Peter.” In both episodes a Roman bishop acts against churches beyond his province, other bishops oppose him, and the threatened sentence is not recorded as taking effect.
  • Stephen known through his opponents. None of Stephen’s letters survives whole. One sentence of his ruling is quoted by Cyprian (Epistle 73, to Pompey); everything else on this page is his position as reported by the bishops resisting it, one of whom (Firmilian) writes, in the ANF editors’ words, “with a little more vehemence and acerbity than becomes a bishop.” That he rested his ruling on succession from Peter and threatened communion over it is reported by Firmilian, writing to Cyprian.1
  • Later veneration. Stephen came to be honoured as a saint in the Roman tradition.
  • Later reception of the ruling on rebaptism. Vincent of Lérins, in the fifth century, takes Stephen’s stand against rebaptism as his example of resistance to innovation: Stephen, “Prelate of the Apostolic See, in conjunction indeed with his colleagues but yet himself the foremost, withstood it,” laying down in a letter to Africa the rule “Let there be no innovation—nothing but what has been handed down,” and “Antiquity was retained, novelty was rejected.” Vincent’s subject is the sacramental ruling; his account of the dispute names Agrippinus of Carthage as, in his words, “the first who held” that baptism ought to be repeated.6
  • Provenance. Quotations follow the Roberts–Donaldson translation (Ante-Nicene Fathers, Vol. V) of the letter of Firmilian preserved among Cyprian’s correspondence (Epistle 74 in that collection; Epistle 75 in the numbering of the Vienna edition, CSEL 3)7, and of Cyprian’s address to the Seventh Council of Carthage.
