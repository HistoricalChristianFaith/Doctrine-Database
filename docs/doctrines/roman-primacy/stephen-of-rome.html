Stephen of Rome and the Rebaptism Controversy

Dates: bishop of Rome c. 254–257 (the rebaptism controversy, 255–256) · Wikipedia
Summary: Stephen supplies the timeline’s first datum of a bishop of Rome grounding a binding claim over other churches explicitly on his succession from Peter. When the churches of Africa (under Cyprian) and the East (under Firmilian of Caesarea) held that converts from heretical communities must be baptized afresh, Stephen forbade the practice, invoked the authority of the Roman see as Peter’s, and threatened to cut off from communion the bishops who disagreed. It is the sharpest third-century assertion of Roman jurisdiction — and it was flatly rebuffed. Cyprian answered by convening eighty-seven African bishops on the principle that “no one of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops”; Firmilian answered with a letter of open indignation that Stephen, “who so boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter,” had by his own act “cut himself off” from the church’s unity. The honest complication, kept in view: Stephen’s own words do not survive — every line of his claim reaches us quoted, and hostilely, by the bishops he was trying to overrule. The episode is placed on the timeline as the counter-datum it is: a Roman bishop felt entitled to bind distant churches by Peter’s authority, and the wider church of his day did not concede the entitlement.

Related doctrines: whether Stephen’s Petrine claim evidences a primacy of jurisdiction or an overreach the church rejected is the through-line question weighed in the summary’s Arguments section. The rebaptism dispute itself — who may confer valid baptism — touches baptism and the ministerial priesthood; Cyprian’s conciliar answer is on the government of the church.

Claims & evidence

Stephen’s claim: the succession from Peter, and the threat to cut off dissenters (255–256)

The dispute arose over a practical question — whether those baptized in heretical or schismatic communities were truly baptized, or must be baptized on entering the catholic church. Africa and the East said they must; Stephen ruled that they must not, and pressed his ruling on the other churches under pain of broken communion. Because none of Stephen’s letters survive, his position is known through his opponents. Firmilian of Caesarea reports that Stephen made an unprecedented breach of peace:

… on this account there is no departure at all from the peace and unity of the Catholic Church, such as Stephen has now dared to make; breaking the peace against you, which his predecessors have always kept with you in mutual love and honour.

— Firmilian, in the letter preserved among Cyprian’s correspondence.1 The ground Stephen claimed was Petrine. Firmilian twice fastens on it — that Stephen “boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter,” and again that he “announces that he holds by succession the throne of Peter”:

And in this respect I am justly indignant at this so open and manifest folly of Stephen, that he who so boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter, on whom the foundations of the Church were laid, should introduce many other rocks and establish new buildings of many churches…

— Firmilian.2 This is the earliest surviving report of a bishop of Rome resting a claim to overrule other churches expressly on his descent from Peter — the exact inference the whole primacy debate turns on. Whether Stephen was asserting a jurisdiction the church already recognized or overreaching for one it did not is weighed in the summary’s Arguments.

The rebuff, part one: “no bishop of bishops” (Cyprian, Carthage 256)

Cyprian did not submit. He held that Stephen’s ruling had no force over the African churches and answered it in council — eighty-seven bishops, each casting his own sentence — opening with the principle that no bishop, Rome’s included, may bind another’s judgment: “For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience.” That address, and the collegial theology behind it, are quoted and set in context on Cyprian’s page and his church-government page.3

The rebuff, part two: Firmilian — “you have excommunicated yourself alone from all” (256)

Firmilian of Caesarea, writing to Cyprian, answered Stephen with far sharper heat. He charges that in claiming Peter’s throne to found “many other rocks” — that is, to validate heretical baptisms — Stephen betrays the very unity he invokes: he “betrays and deserts unity,” so that “the truth of the Christian Rock is overshadowed, and in some measure abolished, by him.” And he turns Stephen’s threat of excommunication back on its author:

… how great sin have you heaped up for yourself, when you cut yourself off from so many flocks! For it is yourself that you have cut off. Do not deceive yourself, since he is really the schismatic who has made himself an apostate from the communion of ecclesiastical unity. For while you think that all may be excommunicated by you, you have excommunicated yourself alone from all.

— Firmilian.4 The rhetoric is intemperate — the ANF editors note it is written “with a little more vehemence and acerbity than becomes a bishop” — but its logic is the same as Cyprian’s: an Eastern metropolitan, faced with a Roman bishop invoking Peter to bind him, does not treat the invocation as decisive but as a pretension to be resisted. Whatever Stephen’s threat, no excommunication of the African or Eastern churches took effect; the dispute lapsed, unresolved on Rome’s terms, at his death in 257.

Notes

  • Why this sits on the timeline — the assertion and its rebuff, together. Where Victor (c. 190) attempted to excommunicate the churches of Asia over the Easter date, Stephen (256) attempted to overrule the churches of Africa and the East over rebaptism, and did so on an explicitly Petrine ground — “the succession from Peter.” The pattern of the two episodes is identical and is itself the datum: a Roman bishop asserts authority beyond his province; the wider church rebuffs him; the threatened sentence does not stand. The primacy is being claimed earlier and more sharply than it is being conceded.
  • Stephen speaks only through hostile witnesses — stated plainly. Not one of Stephen’s own letters survives. Everything on this page is his claim as reported by the two bishops resisting it, one of whom (Firmilian) writes with open contempt. That cuts two ways. It means the exact words and tone of Stephen’s claim cannot be recovered, and a papal reading may fairly say his opponents blackened it. But it also means the substance — that Stephen rested his ruling on succession from Peter and threatened communion over it — is attested by hostile sources who had every reason to understate a Roman prerogative, not invent one; that they report it, and answer it as an overreach, is strong evidence the claim was really made and was not yet the settled law of the church.
  • Rome’s own tradition later canonized Stephen. The see whose jurisdiction Cyprian and Firmilian denied came in time to honour Stephen as a saint and, on the developed reading, as a witness to the very primacy they resisted. The timeline reports the third-century reality: the claim was made, and it was refused.
  • Provenance. Quotations follow the Roberts–Donaldson translation (Ante-Nicene Fathers, Vol. V) of the letter of Firmilian preserved among Cyprian’s correspondence (Epistle 74 in that collection; Cyprian’s Epistle 75 to Pompey in some numberings), and of Cyprian’s address to the Seventh Council of Carthage.
