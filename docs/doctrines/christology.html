The Person of Christ (one hypostasis, two natures)

After the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) had confessed the Son’s full deity, bishops and theologians of the fifth to eighth centuries debated how Godhead and manhood are joined in the one Jesus Christ: one person or two, one nature or two, and in what sense the Word “became flesh” (John 1:14). This timeline records the witnesses to that debate, earliest extant attestation first, and the councils that ruled on it, among them the Council of Chalcedon (451), whose Definition confesses “one and the same” Christ “in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably.” The churches that received Chalcedon and those that did not, today the Oriental Orthodox churches, read that Definition differently, and the readings are set out under Disputed readings.

The entries run from Apollinaris, who taught that the divine Word took the place of a human mind in Christ, through the exchange between Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, and Cyril, patriarch of Alexandria, the Council of Ephesus (431), Leo the Great’s Tome (449) and the Council of Chalcedon (451), to Severus of Antioch, who rejected Chalcedon, Leontius of Byzantium, the Second Council of Constantinople (553), Maximus the Confessor and the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) on Christ’s two wills, and John of Damascus’s Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (c. 743). Related timelines are the Trinity and Theotokos, which follows the same controversy through the title “Mother of God.”

Timeline

Apollinaris of Laodicea (c. 375)

Apollinaris, bishop of Laodicea and earlier an ally of Athanasius against the Arians, taught that in the incarnate Lord the divine Word took the place of the human rational soul or mind: the Word “has become flesh, not having taken upon himself a human mind that is changeable… but a mind that remains divine, an unchangeable and heavenly mind.”1 Gregory of Nazianzus answered him in his Letter 101 to Cledonius: “that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved,” so that, for Gregory, a Christ without a human mind does not save the human mind.2 The first canon of the Council of Constantinople (381) anathematized the heresy “of the Apollinarians.”3

Nestorius (c. 429)

Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople and formed in the Antiochene tradition, ranked the title Theotokos (“God-bearer”) below Christotokos, wary of a “confusion of natures” such as he attributed to Apollinaris: “I have no doubt that the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos, as the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels.”4 He taught that each nature keeps its own properties, “adored through an unconfused conjunction of the highest sort in the single person of the only-begotten,” and in his later self-defence wrote: “we have not two Christs or two sons… but one and the same.”5 The Council of Ephesus deposed him in 431; whether he held the Christology he was condemned for is a disputed reading on the Theotokos timeline, Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 430)

Cyril of Alexandria replied that the union is not a “conjunction” but a real becoming-one: the Word “united to himself hypostatically flesh enlivened by a rational soul, and so became man… two different natures come together to form a unity, and from both arose one Christ, one Son” — whence Mary is Theotokos, not because the Word took the origin of his being from her but because “there was born from her his holy body… with which the Word was hypostatically united.”6 He held that what is said of the man may be said of God: the one Christ “suffered in the flesh… freedom from suffering even thus is kept to Him in that He is conceived of as God,” “the flesh suffering this, not the Word apart and by Himself.”7 His Twelve Anathemas set these confessions out as tests. He also used the formula “one incarnate nature of the Word,” which he took to be Athanasius’s; the origin of the formula is discussed on the Apollinaris and Cyril detail pages.

The Council of Ephesus (431)

The Third Ecumenical Council (so counted by the churches that received it) read Cyril’s Second Letter to Nestorius as faithful to Nicaea, confessed Mary Theotokos, and deposed Nestorius, resolving “with many tears… that Nestorius be excluded from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion”; its seventh canon forbade framing any rival creed to Nicaea.8 The council’s decision is treated in full on the Theotokos timeline. In 433 the Alexandrian and Antiochene parties were reconciled in the Formula of Reunion, which Cyril accepted: “a union of two natures… one Christ, one Son, one Lord.”

Leo the Great (449)

Leo the Great, bishop of Rome, set out his teaching in the Tome to Flavian, written against the monk Eutyches, who had been condemned at Constantinople in 448. Leo wrote that “the properties of either nature and substance… came together in one person,” each keeping “its own proper character without loss” — “in the whole and perfect nature of true man was true God born.”9 The Tome also says: “each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh.”10 The Tome was not read at the second Council of Ephesus (449); at Chalcedon in 451 it was read and acclaimed.

The Council of Chalcedon (451)

The Council of Chalcedon, the Fourth Ecumenical Council in the reckoning of the churches that received it, received Cyril’s synodical letters and Leo’s Tome and issued a Definition that confesses “one and the same Jesus Christ… in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably… the peculiar property of each nature being preserved, and being united in one Person and subsistence… not separated or divided into two persons, but one and the same Son,” born “of the Virgin Mary, the Mother of God according to his manhood.”11 The churches that did not receive the Council, today the Oriental Orthodox churches (in Pius XII’s 1951 list, “in Egypt, Ethiopia, Syria, Armenia and elsewhere”), accept the first three councils only. Whether the Definition departed from Cyril’s Christology is a disputed reading; see Did the Council of Chalcedon (451) depart from Cyril of Alexandria’s Christology?

Severus of Antioch (c. 515)

Severus of Antioch, patriarch 512–518 and revered as a father by the churches that did not receive Chalcedon, held to Cyril’s “one incarnate nature of God the Word” and rejected the Definition’s “in two natures”: to say that “each of the natures performs its own acts” — for him the teaching of Leo’s Tome — is to “divide the one and only Christ into two natures.” He also wrote: “Flesh does not renounce its existence as flesh… nor has the Word departed from his nature… but the difference also is preserved.”12 His Philalethes answered a pro-Chalcedonian collection of Cyril extracts, arguing that it had cited Cyril selectively ⚠ unverified. How his charge against the Council has been read is set out in Disputed readings below.

Leontius of Byzantium (c. 540)

Leontius of Byzantium addressed an objection put to Chalcedon: if Christ’s human nature is real and complete, is it not its own person, so that “two natures” means two hypostases? The reply associated with him holds that the manhood is neither an independent hypostasis nor anhypostatos (without hypostasis at all), but enhypostatos, real and subsisting in the hypostasis of the Word. On this account the human nature has a real existence, not an illusory one, yet never stands alone.13 Whether Leontius himself used enhypostatos as the technical term later credited to him is discussed in the scholarship cited in the note.

The Second Council of Constantinople (553)

The Fifth Ecumenical Council in the Chalcedonian reckoning, convened by Justinian, condemned the “Three Chapters” (the person and writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia and writings of Theodoret and Ibas against Cyril) and made the theopaschite confession an anathema: “If anyone does not confess that our Lord Jesus Christ who was crucified in the flesh is true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity: let him be anathema.”14 John Behr (Orthodox, 1998) writes that Severus’ attack on the Chalcedonian theologians “tentatively trying to reclaim Cyril” prompted “what has since become known as ‘neo-Chalcedonian’ theology”; Donald Fairbairn (Protestant, 2022) writes that the reading of the councils in the light of Cyril “has usually been labeled Alexandrian or Neochalcedonian by its detractors, not its proponents.”15

Maximus the Confessor and the Third Council of Constantinople (c. 640–681)

Maximus the Confessor opposed Monothelitism, the teaching that the one Christ has a single will. He argued that a will belongs to a nature, so that two natures entail two wills: Christ “possesses as a human being a natural will, which is shaped, but not opposed, by his divine will.”16 He was condemned, his tongue and right hand were cut off, and he died in exile. The Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) defined that Christ has “two natural wills… not contrary the one to the other… but his human will follows, and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will.”17

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus set out the two-natures teaching of the councils in Book 3 of his An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith: a union of two perfect natures “by synthesis; that is, in subsistence, without change or confusion or alteration or difference or separation… one subsistence of the Son of God incarnate,” each nature keeping its own properties yet exchanging them (communicatio idiomatum) through “the interpenetration of the parts,” so that “the Lord of Glory is said to have been crucified, although His divine nature never endured the Cross.”18 He states the distinction associated with Leontius, that “the flesh of God the Word did not subsist as an independent subsistence… but… became rather a subsistence which subsisted in another,” and follows Maximus and the Third Council of Constantinople: “since, then, Christ has two natures, we hold that He has also two natural wills and two natural energies… wills and energies are faculties belonging to nature, not to subsistence.”19
