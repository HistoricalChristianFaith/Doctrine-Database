Saint George

Behind the dragon-slayer of the English coin and the Garter stands a soldier-martyr venerated at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine, attested from the fourth century by a dated inscription and by the pilgrims who visited his tomb, and of whose life no account survives older than an apocryphal Passion. The sources then carry a growing legend: an Acts in which George is tortured for seven years, dies three times and is three times raised, listed among the apocrypha in the Gelasian Decree (early 6th c.); a Byzantine warrior-saint’s cult that spread east and west; an Islamic reception in which George was identified, across the Near East, with al-Khiḍr, the deathless “Green One,” in shrines and festivals shared by Christians and Muslims; a dragon episode first attested many centuries after the martyr; the Golden Legend (c. 1260), to which Thurston credits the dragon-story’s wide diffusion; and George’s patronage of England and of the Order of the Garter. Like the Seven Sleepers, George is a holy figure honoured by both Christians and Muslims; like Saint Christopher, a saint whose legend far outgrew the record of his life.

This page is a history of the George tradition and of how the churches, and then Muslim devotion, came to hold it, set out earliest source first: the fourth-century cult at Lydda, the fifth-century Passion and its sixth-century rejection, the Eastern and Western liturgical cult, the identification with al-Khiḍr, the dragon episode (treated in full on its own page), the Golden Legend, and modern scholarship on the cult and the legend. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source, not by a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The cult of a martyr at Lydda (4th c.)

The earliest extant attestation of George is not a story but a cult. A martyr of that name was venerated at Lydda (Greek Diospolis, modern Lod) in Palestine, with a martyrium reputedly over his grave; the pilgrims Theodosius, Antoninus and Arculf (6th–8th c.) name Lydda as the seat of his veneration and the resting-place of his remains. A Greek building-inscription from Shaqqa (ancient Eaccaea) in the Hauran honours “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions”; reckoned on the era of Bostra its year 263 is AD 368, though Waddington preferred a local era because 368 is not a fifteenth indiction (so Delehaye). Of the man himself no account survives older than the Passion. Hippolyte Delehaye (1909) holds that the sanctuary rose over the tomb of a local martyr whose acts were never written or soon forgotten; Herbert Thurston (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) finds “no ground for doubting the historical existence of St. George,” while “no faith can be placed” in attempts to fill in his history; he writes that George “suffered at or near Lydda, also known as Diospolis, in Palestine, probably before the time of Constantine,” and notes that the version of the legend naming Diocletian as persecutor “is not primitive.” Wikipedia’s summary places the martyrdom around 303, in the Great Persecution under Diocletian. Whether the cult honoured a local martyr or the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia is disputed; see the identity question.1

The Passio Georgii: seven years of torture, three deaths (5th c.)

The apocryphal Passio Georgii (the “Pseudo-Pasicrates” text, ascribed to a servant who claimed to have witnessed the martyrdom) took shape by the later fifth century. In it George is tried by a persecuting king, Dadianus (Datianus / Dacianus), and tortured for seven years in a succession of torments; he dies three times in the course of it and is each time raised by the archangel Michael to be tortured afresh; a sorcerer named Athanasius is summoned against him and is himself converted. John Matzke (1902) and the text’s modern translators David Bryson and Roger Pearse (2020) read an Arian hand behind it, the villain “Athanasius” bearing the name of the anti-Arian bishop of Alexandria; Delehaye (1909) finds in it reminiscences of the historical George of Cappadocia, the Arian bishop of Alexandria lynched under Julian. It is the basis of the Syriac, Coptic and other oriental versions; the Decretum Gelasianum lists it among the apocrypha (see next entry).2

The Gelasian Decree lists the Passion among the apocrypha (late 5th / 6th c.)

The Decretum Gelasianum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis (attributed to Pope Gelasius in 495, as Thurston reports; a sixth-century document according to Bryson and Pearse) lists “the Passion of Georgius” among the apocrypha, under the heading of writings which “the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive,” and names it among passions “which appear to have been composed by heretics” and are therefore “not read in the holy Roman church”; in the same passage it declares that “we venerate together with the aforesaid church all the martyrs and their glorious sufferings, which are better known to God than to men.” Thurston (1909) reads the decree as including George among those saints “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose actions are only known to God.” Later Greek revisions reworked the Passion into a soberer form: Thurston holds that “this mitigated version has been derived from the more extravagant by a process of elimination and rationalization, not vice versa,” and Matzke distinguished the earlier “apocryphal version” from a later “canonical version” in which the king became Diocletian. The dragon episode is absent from both forms.3

The Eastern and Western cult: a warrior-saint spreads (6th–10th c.)

Devotion continued alongside the rejection of the Passion. In successive Greek revisions (Andrew of Crete and others reworked the Passion), George became one of the great military saints of Byzantium, paired with Theodore and Demetrius, invoked by soldiers, his churches multiplying from Palestine and Egypt across Anatolia and into the Latin West. His feast settled on 23 April. By the tenth century his life stood in the standard Greek menologia, and in the West Gregory of Tours already knew relics of “the blessed George.” The icons of this period show a standing or mounted, lance-bearing soldier, without a dragon.4

The Islamic reception: George and al-Khiḍr, the “Green One” (medieval Near East)

In the Near East George came to be identified, in popular devotion, with al-Khiḍr (“the Green One”), the deathless sage of Islamic lore, traditionally the mysterious servant of God who teaches Moses in Sūrat al-Kahf (18:60–82). Medieval Muslim writers on the holy places of Syria report the identification, gathered by Josef Meri (2002). Muslims, Christians, and sometimes Jews venerated a single figure at shared shrines: the monastery of al-Khader near Bethlehem, the church of St George at al-Salt in Jordan, sites around Mount Carmel and Antioch, where the same holy man is George to the Christian and Khiḍr/Khaḍir to the Muslim. In the Ottoman calendar St George’s Day ran together with the spring festival of Hıdırellez (Hızır + Ilyās/Elijah). After the Seven Sleepers, George is the second figure on these pages honoured by both Christians and Muslims. How the identification arose, and whether it continues the cult of the storm-god Baal, are disputed; see how the identification arose and the Baal-continuity question.5

The dragon episode (first attested 11th c.)

The episode now best known of St George, the princess, the lake-dragon and the mounted saint, is absent from the early sources. Christopher Walter (2003) dates its earliest narrative to an eleventh-century Georgian text and its earliest images to eleventh-century Cappadocia, the dragon-rescue having been told first of another warrior-saint, Theodore Tiro; Thurston (1909) had traced it no further back than the twelfth or thirteenth century. Charles Clermont-Ganneau (1876) linked it to the Perseus-and-Andromeda story, set by classical authors at Joppa, a few miles from George’s shrine at Lydda; Thurston rejected derivation from pagan sources and suggested an allegory of the tyrant Dadianus, whom the older text sometimes calls a dragon. Whether the dragon-fight is a historical event is a separate question with its own home on these pages: see “St George and the dragon: is the dragon-slaying a historical event?”6

The Golden Legend carries the story through the Latin West (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine gathered the dragon-tale into his Golden Legend (c. 1260), and through William Caxton’s 1483 printing it became the standard English story: the pond-dragon of Silene “in the province of Libya,” the king’s daughter led out by lot, the saint who wounds the beast and leads it tamed into the city on the maiden’s girdle, and the mass baptism, “well fifteen thousand men… without women and children.” In the prologue to the life Voragine records that George’s legend was “numbered among other scriptures apocryphal in the council of Nicene, because his martyrdom hath no certain relation”; the list of apocrypha that names the George Passion is the Decretum Gelasianum (see above). Thurston (1909) holds that the dragon episode “probably owes its wide diffusion” to the Golden Legend. George is patron of England, where the national synod of Oxford ordered his day kept in 1222, and principal patron of the Order of the Garter, founded by Edward III c. 1347; his patronages of Georgia, Aragon and Genoa are not yet sourced ⚠ unverified.7

Modern scholarship (20th c. → today)

Modern scholars have studied the cult and the development of the legend. Hippolyte Delehaye, in Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), analysed the George Passions and the warrior-saint pattern; Christopher Walter (“The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” 1995) traced the cult from Diospolis; Samantha Riches, St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (2000), mapped the martyr and the dragon-hero. On the Islamic side, Josef Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (2002), documented the George–al-Khiḍr shrines, Jens Kreinath’s fieldwork in the Antakya region documented their living use, and Robert D. Miller II’s Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr (2019) argued that the identification’s roots reach back to the Canaanite storm-god of the same “green” ground.8
