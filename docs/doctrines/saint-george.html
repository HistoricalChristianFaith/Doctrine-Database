Saint George

Behind the dragon-slayer of the English coin and the Garter stands a figure the early church could barely document: a soldier-martyr venerated at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine from the fourth century, of whom no trustworthy account survives — killed, most likely, in the Great Persecution (c. 303). What did survive was his legend, and it grew like few others: an Acts so extravagant — seven years of torture, three deaths, three resurrections — that Rome itself binned it as apocryphal in the Gelasian Decree (early 6th c.); a Byzantine warrior-saint’s cult that spread east and west; an Islamic reception in which George fused, across the Near East, with al-Khiḍr, the deathless “Green One,” in shrines and festivals shared by Christians and Muslims alike; a medieval dragon that was grafted on seven hundred years after the martyr; and, at last, the Golden Legend, which fixed the dragon-story for the Latin West and made George a patron of kingdoms. Like the Seven Sleepers, George is a holy life that crossed languages and religions — a second Christian–and–Islamic shared-veneration case; like Saint Christopher, a saint remembered chiefly through the accretions time hung on a name.

This page is not a life of George; it is a history of a legend and of how the churches — and then Islam — came to hold it, set out earliest source first: the fourth-century cult and the shadowy martyr beneath it, the wild fifth-century Passion and its sixth-century condemnation, the Eastern and Western liturgical cult, the Islamic al-Khiḍr identification, the eleventh-century dragon accretion (weighed in full on its own page), the Golden Legend, and the modern scholarship that sifts the historical core from the accretion. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The historical core: a martyr venerated at Lydda (4th c.)

The earliest firm trace of George is not a story but a cult. A martyr of that name was venerated at Lydda (Greek Diospolis, modern Lod) in Palestine from the fourth century, with a martyrium reputedly over his grave; a Greek building-inscription from Shaqqa (ancient Eaccaea) in the Hauran, dated to the 360s, already honours “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions” — among the oldest datable evidence of the cult. That places a venerated George within two or three generations of the persecution in which he is said to have died, and at a fixed devotional centre. Of the man himself nothing reliable survives: a Christian soldier martyred at Diospolis around 303, probably in the Great Persecution under Diocletian, is the modest and coherent historical residue — his cult ran far ahead of any trustworthy account of him.1

The extravagant Passion: seven years of torture, three deaths (5th c.)

Into that documentary vacuum poured one of the most lurid martyr-legends of late antiquity. The apocryphal Passio Georgii (the “Pseudo-Pasicrates” text, ascribed to a servant who claimed to have witnessed the martyrdom) took shape by the later fifth century. In it George is tried by a persecuting king — Dadianus (Datianus / Dacianus) — and tortured for seven years in ever more inventive ways, dies three times in the course of it, and is each time raised by the archangel Michael to be tortured afresh; a sorcerer named Athanasius is summoned against him and is himself converted. The extravagance is not incidental: some scholars trace an Arian coloring behind it, the martyr-George perhaps conflated with the historical George of Cappadocia (the Arian intruder-bishop of Alexandria lynched under Julian), which the villain “Athanasius” would fit. This is the text that made George famous — and that the church would soon disown.2

The Gelasian Decree bins the Passion as apocryphal (early 6th c.)

The Roman church’s own verdict came fast. The Decretum Gelasianum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis (early sixth century, traditionally ascribed to Pope Gelasius, d. 496) lists the Passio Georgii among the apocryphal writings the church “does not in any way receive” — a rejection of the fabulous Acts, never of the saint. It is the honest position that later hardened into the tradition that George is one of those martyrs “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose acts are known only to God.” George’s legend is, in this, an inversion of the usual pattern: for most saints the earliest life is the soberest and the marvels accrete later, but George’s original Passion was already so outlandish that it was condemned, and the later revisions toned it down — even as a wholly new marvel, the dragon, waited seven centuries in the wings.3

The Eastern and Western cult: a warrior-saint spreads (6th–10th c.)

Condemnation did nothing to check the devotion. Purged of its worst excesses in successive Greek revisions (Andrew of Crete and others reworked the Passion into a soberer form), George became one of the great military saints of Byzantium — paired with Theodore and Demetrius, invoked by soldiers, his churches multiplying from Palestine and Egypt across Anatolia and into the Latin West. His feast settled on 23 April. By the tenth century his life stood in the standard Greek menologia, and in the West his cult was long established — Gregory of Tours already knew relics of “the blessed George.” The mounted, lance-bearing soldier of the icons — not yet a dragon in sight — is the image the medieval world inherited.4

The Islamic reception: George and al-Khiḍr, the “Green One” (medieval Near East)

In the lands of his birth the cult crossed a religious frontier. Across the Near East George came to be identified, in living popular devotion, with al-Khiḍr (“the Green One”) — the deathless sage of Islamic lore, traditionally the mysterious servant of God who teaches Moses in Sūrat al-Kahf (18:60–82). Muslims, Christians, and sometimes Jews venerated a single figure at shared shrines — the monastery of al-Khader near Bethlehem, the church of St George at al-Salt in Jordan, sites around Mount Carmel and Antioch — where the same holy man is George to the Christian and Khiḍr/Khaḍir to the Muslim. In the Ottoman calendar St George’s Day fused with the spring festival of Hıdırellez (Hızır + Ilyās/Elijah). It is, after the Seven Sleepers, the wiki’s second and most vivid case of a holy figure venerated by both Christians and Muslims; whether the fusion is a genuine shared cult, and how deep its roots run, is weighed below.5

The dragon: a late graft (11th c.)

The episode that now is St George in the popular mind — the princess, the lake-dragon, the mounted saint — is the latest arrival of all. It is absent from every early source; it surfaces only in the eleventh century, first attached to another warrior-saint (Theodore Tiro) and only then transferred to George, and it is transparently the Christianized Perseus-and-Andromeda motif whose classical setting at Joppa lay a few miles from George’s shrine at Lydda. Whether the dragon-fight is a historical event is a separate question with its own home on this wiki, where it is rated weak as history (while the martyr beneath it is rated plausible): see “St George and the dragon: is the dragon-slaying a historical event?” This spine’s concern is only that the dragon is, in the life of the legend, a demonstrably medieval accretion — the last and most famous stone in a structure raised long after the man.6

The Golden Legend fixes the story for the Latin West (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine gathered the dragon-tale into his Golden Legend (c. 1260), and through William Caxton’s 1483 printing it became the standard English story: the pond-dragon of Silene “in the province of Libya,” the king’s daughter led out by lot, the saint who wounds the beast and leads it tamed into the city on the maiden’s girdle, and the mass baptism — “well fifteen thousand men… without women and children.” Voragine is candid about the material he is passing on: he closes the life by noting that George’s Acts were “numbered among other scriptures apocryphal … because his martyrdom hath no certain relation” — a confused but pointed echo of the very condemnation the church had pronounced seven centuries before. From the Golden Legend the dragon-slayer passed into heraldry, patronage, and pageant — George made patron of England (and of Georgia, Aragon, Genoa, and more), and titular of the Order of the Garter.7

Modern scholarship: the core sifted from the accretion (20th c. → today)

Critical study has worked to separate the datable cult from the legend piled on it. Hippolyte Delehaye, in Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), analysed the George Passions and the warrior-saint pattern; Christopher Walter (“The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” 1995) traced the cult from Diospolis; Samantha Riches, St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (2000), mapped the martyr beneath and the dragon after. On the Islamic side, Josef Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (2002), documented the George–al-Khiḍr shrines, and later fieldwork (Jens Kreinath in the Antakya region) and comparative studies (Robert D. Miller’s Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr) argued out how far the identification’s roots reach back — some to the Canaanite storm-god of the same “green” ground. Where the martyr is real, where the marvels are borrowed, and how one saint came to be shared across two religions is the settled work of this scholarship.8

Arguments

Claims adduced about the legend, weighed adversarially and cutting across the timeline above.

  • Are St George and al-Khiḍr venerated as a single figure in a genuine shared Christian–Muslim cult? — sound. That George and the Islamic al-Khiḍr were identified and venerated together across the medieval and modern Near East — at al-Khader near Bethlehem, at al-Salt, around Carmel and Antioch, and in the Ottoman spring festival of Hıdırellez — is well documented in the sources and in modern fieldwork; the fusion is real and living. What is more speculative, and kept distinct, is the direction and depth of the borrowing — the claim that George is a late overlay on a far older “Green One” (Elijah, the Canaanite Baal) reaches beyond what the evidence secures.
