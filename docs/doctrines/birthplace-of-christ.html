Where Was Christ Born? Cave, Stable, or House

The Gospels give almost nothing to picture. Luke says only that Mary wrapped the child and “laid him in a manger, because there was no room for them in the kataluma” — a word older English Bibles translate “inn”; Luke uses the same word for the “guest-chamber” of the Last Supper and a different word, pandocheion, for the inn of the Good Samaritan. Matthew, telling of the magi, has them come “into the house” and find the child there. Nowhere does the New Testament mention a cave, a stable, an ox and an ass, or an innkeeper turning the family away: the cave, the stable, the ox and the ass, and the innkeeper are all supplied by later reading.

Yet the oldest surviving descriptions, within a century of the Gospels, picture neither an inn nor a stable but a cave outside Bethlehem — and the cave became a shrine. This page traces the sources earliest-first: the second-century cave of the Protoevangelium and of Justin Martyr, the grotto shown and venerated in the time of Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome, the basilica raised over it, and the later Western picture of a stable behind a full inn. How these sources are read is disputed: whether the cave rests on a local memory of the place or on inference — from the cave-stables of the region, or from the “lofty cave of the strong rock” of Isaiah 33:16 that Justin links to the story — whether Jerome’s report of a pagan grove over the cave attests early Christian veneration, and whether the cave-birth was borrowed from the cult of Mithras. These questions are set out under Disputed readings.

Related doctrines: The nativity of Christ — see also who were the magi?

Timeline

The Gospel notice (Luke and Matthew) (c. 5 BC)

Luke alone reports the manger: Mary “brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn.”1 The word rendered “inn” is kataluma — the same term Luke uses for the furnished “guest-chamber” of the Last Supper (Luke 22:11), while for a wayside inn he uses a different word, pandocheion (Luke 10:34). Matthew, describing the magi’s arrival, says that they came “into the house” (oikia) and “saw the young child with Mary his mother.”2 Between them the Gospels give a manger, a crowded lodging, and a house — but no cave, no stable, and no innkeeper.

The Protoevangelium of James (c. 150)

The earliest surviving narrative to localize the birth places it in a cave. On the road to Bethlehem Mary’s labour comes upon her, and Joseph “found a cave there, and led her into it,” leaving his sons with her while he went to seek a midwife.3 When he returns with her, “a luminous cloud overshadowed the cave,” then “a great light shone in the cave, so that the eyes could not bear it,” and as it faded “the infant appeared.”4 There is no inn and no stable here — only a cave outside the town, and around it the miraculous light.

Justin Martyr (c. 160)

Justin, who was born in Palestine, tells Trypho that because Joseph “could not find a lodging in that village, he took up his quarters in a certain cave near the village; and while they were there Mary brought forth the Christ and placed Him in a manger.”5 He states the cave without argument and links it to prophecy — Isaiah’s promise that the righteous one “shall dwell in the lofty cave of the strong rock” foreshadowed, he says, the cave of the Nativity.6 In the same passage he notes that the followers of Mithras “call the place where those who believe in him are initiated a cave,” which he takes for a demonic counterfeit of the true one.7

Origen (c. 248)

Origen’s is the earliest extant notice of the cave as a place publicly shown. Writing Against Celsus at Caesarea in Palestine, he tells the skeptic that anyone wanting evidence beyond Micah and the Gospels should know that “there is shown at Bethlehem the cave where He was born, and the manger in the cave where He was wrapped in swaddling-clothes,” and that “this sight is greatly talked of in surrounding places, even among the enemies of the faith.”8 The cave is now a pointed-out place, spoken of, he says, among non-Christians — some eighty years before Constantine raised a basilica over it. Origen grounds Bethlehem on Micah 5:2 and does not cite the “lofty cave” of Isaiah that Justin had linked to the story.9

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 320)

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, gives two notices written before the basilica. In the Demonstratio Evangelica (The Proof of the Gospel), written before Constantine’s church was raised, he twice reports that the cave is shown to visitors: “all agree that Jesus Christ was born in Bethlehem, and a cave is shewn there by the inhabitants to those who come from abroad to see it,”10 and again that “to this day the inhabitants of the place, who have received the tradition from their fathers, confirm the truth of the story by shewing… the cave in which the Virgin bare and laid her infant.”11 These notices attest the showing of the cave a generation before the monument, and Eusebius presents it as a tradition the inhabitants received from their fathers. Two decades later, in the Life of Constantine, he describes Helena dedicating a church “at the grotto which had been the scene of the Saviour’s birth,” which the emperor adorned with “costly presents of silver and gold.”12

The Bordeaux Pilgrim (333)

The earliest extant record by a datable pilgrim names the site. The anonymous traveller from Bordeaux, whose 333 itinerary is the oldest surviving Christian travel diary of the Holy Land, notes that two miles from Rachel’s tomb lies “Bethlehem, where the Lord Jesus Christ was born; there a basilica was made by Constantine’s command.”13 Written only a few years after the church over the grotto was begun, this is the earliest extant firsthand notice that the basilica Eusebius describes was standing and drawing visitors. His clipped road-log names the birthplace and the church — but not, in so many words, a cave: he attests the place and the monument, not what was shown within.

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Catechizing the baptismal candidates of Jerusalem around 350 — a few miles from Bethlehem, and while Constantine’s new basilica stood over the grotto — Cyril numbers “the place of the manger, which received the Lord” among the witnesses that will one day “put to shame” those who deny Christ’s true birth, set alongside the shepherds, the angels, the Temple, Symeon, and Anna.14 What makes him distinctive is the local footing: he appeals to sites his hearers already know, reminding each candidate that, “being an inhabitant of Jerusalem,” he “knowest also beforehand” the places of the promise, and recalling that “a few years ago the place was woody.”15 He names the manger’s place, not — here — a cave: a Palestinian witness to the Bethlehem site, contemporary with the basilica.

Ephraem the Syrian (c. 360)

The Syriac tradition names the cave too. Ephraem, the hymn-writer of Nisibis and Edessa, names the cave and the manger together in a single line of his Hymns on the Nativity, setting each against the royal splendour it displaces: “A manger hast Thou inherited out of [all] his beds, a cave has come down to Thee out of all his palaces. Instead of his chariots a common ass’s colt…”16 Ephraem never saw Bethlehem; he attests not a shown grotto but the cave as a settled feature of the Nativity story already current in the Syriac-speaking church, meditated on for its paradox — the Most High of David’s line laid, of all places, in a cave and a feeding-trough. His witness shows the cave tradition running in Syriac as well as in Greek and Latin by the mid-fourth century, a strand apart from the Palestinian shown-site line of Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome.

Egeria (c. 384)

Egeria, a Western nun whose travel diary is the fullest firsthand account of fourth-century Holy-Land worship, attests a living cult at Bethlehem: the Epiphany octave was kept there “daily by the priests and by all the clergy there, and by the monks who are appointed in that place.”17 By her visit Bethlehem is no bare memorial but a functioning liturgical centre with resident clergy. Her detailed description of the Nativity station itself, however, does not survive: the sole medieval manuscript loses a leaf at exactly this point — the section headed “Night Station at Bethlehem” opens with the note “a leaf is wanting” — so whatever she wrote of the cave or grotto is lost with that folio and cannot be quoted as hers. What survives shows the fully organised cult of the 380s, not the shrine’s interior.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 386)

The Greek East speaks through Gregory of Nyssa, the Cappadocian bishop and brother of Basil, in a homily on the feast of the Nativity. He names both the cave and the manger and reads each as a figure. “When you see the cave in which the Master is born,” he tells his hearers, “understand the lightless and subterraneous life of mankind, into which He enters, appearing to those sitting in darkness and the shadow of death”; and “the manger is the abode of irrational animals, in which the Word is born… so that those irrational animals might partake of rational food and become rational.”18 Preaching in Cappadocia rather than at Bethlehem, Gregory is no shown-site witness like Origen or Eusebius; his sermon takes the cave-and-manger scene as given and reads it allegorically — the cave a figure of humanity’s darkness, the manger the beasts’ trough where the Bread of Life is laid, with the ox and the ass of Isaiah 1:3 read for the two peoples gathered to the crib. Like Ephraem, he holds the cave and the manger together, and takes both as given rather than arguing for either.

Jerome (c. 395)

Jerome settled at Bethlehem in 386 and lived beside the grotto for the rest of his life, treating it throughout as the real birthplace: in the Life of Paula he shows his patron entering “the cave where the Saviour was born” and beholding “the infant Lord… crying in the manger.”19 He adds that from Hadrian (c. 135) to Constantine a pagan grove sacred to Tammuz “that is of Adonis” overshadowed “the very cave where the infant Christ had uttered His earliest cry,” one of the pagan installations by which, he writes, persecutors sought to pollute the Christian holy places; whether the grove attests early veneration of the cave is disputed.20 Preaching on the Nativity he laments that the humble clay manger had been re-cased in silver: “more precious to me is the one that has been removed.”21 Jerome keeps the cave and rejects the apocryphal midwife. In Against Helvidius he rejects the miraculous midwife of the Protoevangelium tradition — “no midwife assisted at His birth” — as “the ravings of the apocryphal accounts,” citing Luke’s manger and inn.22

Sozomen (c. 445)

The fifth-century church historian Sozomen, writing at Constantinople around 445, locates the Bethlehem church by the cave. Recounting Helena’s Holy-Land foundations, he says she “erected two temples, the one at Bethlehem near the cave where Christ was born, the other on ridges of the Mount of Olives, whence He was taken up to heaven.”23 More than a century after Constantine, the grotto that Eusebius had reported as shown by the local inhabitants is the landmark by which the historian places Helena’s church.

The Syriac Cave of Treasures (c. 550)

A Syriac sacred history compiled around the sixth century — pseudonymously ascribed to Ephraem — names two caves, and keeps them apart. The book touches the birth-cave only in passing: the magi, it says, “went into the cave” where “they saw Joseph sitting in astonishment, and Mary in a state of wonderment,” and there “offered … unto Him, gold, and myrrh, and frankincense” — the same cave tradition that Ephraem attests, in a later Syriac text.24 Its titular Cave of Treasures is a different cave: the cave near Paradise where Adam “took … gold, and myrrh, and frankincense, and he placed them in the cave … and he called the cave … ‘CAVE OF TREASURES.’”25 Adam’s body and those very offerings are carried through the Flood in Noah’s ark and, generations on, deposited by Shem not at Bethlehem but at “Gâghûltâ (Golgotha), which is the centre of the earth.”26 Its translator E. A. Wallis Budge describes the two caves as linked through the three gifts: the gold, myrrh, and frankincense Adam laid up stand for the magi’s, so that Adam’s cave “symbolized … the cave in which the Magi presented their gifts to Christ.”27 In the book the birth-cave is the one the magi enter; Adam’s Cave of Treasures lies near Paradise, and the body laid in it ends at Golgotha; the magi’s gifts are what the two share.

The Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew (c. 600–650)

This Latin infancy gospel is late, but it reworks much older matter — the second-century Protoevangelium above all — and it sets the cave and a stable in one sequence. The birth still happens in a cave, dark until Mary enters and it “began to shine with as much brightness as if it were the sixth hour of the day.”28 It then adds a step not found in the earlier witnesses: “on the third day… the most blessed Mary went forth out of the cave, and entering a stable, placed the child in the stall, and the ox and the ass adored Him.”29 Mary moves from the cave of the apocryphal and Palestinian tradition to a stable with Luke’s manger. The ox and the ass — in none of the canonical accounts — come with prophecies attached: Isaiah 1:3 and the Septuagint reading of Habakkuk 3:2, “between two animals thou art made known.” As Justin had linked the cave to Isaiah 33:16, this text links the two beasts to Isaiah and Habakkuk.

The Western turn: from cave to stable, and the ‘inn’ (1223 and after)

The last turn is Western and devotional. At Christmas 1223, in a rocky hollow above Greccio in the Rieti valley, Francis of Assisi staged a living crib. His biographer Thomas of Celano, writing within a few years, reports the saint’s wish: “I want to do something that will recall the memory of that child who was born in Bethlehem, to see with bodily eyes the inconveniences of his infancy, how he lay in the manger, and how the ox and ass stood by.”30 A manger was filled with hay and a live ox and ass set beside it, Mass was sung over it, and — in Celano’s phrase — “a new Bethlehem, as it were, is made from Greccio.”31 Francis’s tableau placed the manger scene at the centre of Western devotion, and later Western Nativity art commonly set the scene in a wooden stable rather than a cave ⚠ unverified; Greccio’s own scene was set in a rocky hollow.

The English “inn” came by way of translation. Luke’s word is kataluma, which Todd Bolen (2006) reads as a guest room; the Latin Vulgate rendered it diversorio, “lodging.”32 When William Tyndale first Englished the New Testament from the Greek in 1526, he made it “the ynne” — “ther was no roume for them… in the ynne”33 — and Geneva and the King James Version kept his wording, so the English-speaking world inherited an inn. Bolen notes that Luke names no innkeeper and no refusal, only “no room.”34 Some modern versions read otherwise: the 2011 New International Version and the Christian Standard Bible both read “guest room.”35

Disputed readings

Questions about how the early witnesses to the Bethlehem cave have been read.

The authenticity of the cave-site

Two questions about whether the cave shown at Bethlehem was a remembered place: what the second-century and later notices of the cave rest on, and what Jerome’s report of a pagan grove over it shows.

  • Does the Bethlehem cave tradition rest on local memory, or on inference from cave-stables and Isaiah 33:16? — turns on Justin, Origen, and Eusebius, and on the Septuagint of Isaiah 33:16.
  • Does Jerome’s report of an Adonis grove over the Bethlehem cave attest early Christian veneration of the site? — turns on Letter 58 and which came first, the grove or the Christian site.

A further question concerns the cave and the cult of Mithras:

  • Was the Christian cave-birth borrowed from the cult of Mithras? — turns on Justin’s pairing of the two caves and the date of the Roman cult.
