John Cassian on friendship

Dates: c. 426 · Wikipedia
Summary: The monastic teacher John Cassian (c. 360–435) devoted the whole of his Conference 16 — the First Conference of Abbot Joseph, titled simply On Friendship (De Amicitia, c. 426) — to the subject, making it the great early treatment of friendship inside Christian monasticism. He takes over the classical thesis that true friendship rests on virtue and carries it further than any pagan had: of all the bonds that unite men — recommendation, mutual advantage, shared work, and blood — only one is indissoluble, the friendship grounded “simply… in similarity of virtue,” which “even death itself cannot part.” But Cassian, unlike Cicero, has watched such friendships fail among monks who began well, and so he asks the practical question the philosophers never pressed: what makes a friendship last? His answer is a monastic rule — six foundations crowned by the demand that a friend root out anger utterly and never prefer his own judgment — and a sober verdict: lasting friendship is possible “only among those who are perfect and of equal goodness.” He also, notably, defends ordered and preferential love — the special bond of a chosen companion — against the suspicion that a monk owes only an even charity to all.

Claims & evidence

The many kinds of friendship — all of which fail

There are many kinds of friendship and companionship which unite men in very different ways in the bonds of love… In the case of others some bargain or an agreement to give and take something has joined them in the bonds of love. Others a similarity and union of business or science or art or study has united in the chain of friendship… But there is another kind of love, where the union is from the instincts of nature and the laws of consanguinity… But we see that all these kinds of love of which we have spoken, as they are common both to the good and bad, and to beasts and serpents, certainly cannot last for ever. For often separation of place interrupts and breaks them off, as well as forgetfulness from lapse of time, and the transaction of affairs and business and words.

— Conference 16 (On Friendship), ch. II, source. This is Cassian’s sorting of the field. Bonds of utility, of bargain, of common pursuit, and of kinship all “unite men,” but every one of them is shared by the wicked and even the beasts — robbers “embrace and cherish the partners of their crimes” — and every one is dissolved by time, distance, or a change of interest. It is Aristotle’s point that friendships of utility and pleasure are “easily dissolved,” recast for the cloister: what cannot outlast the circumstance that made it was never the highest thing.

One friendship is indissoluble: the bond grounded in virtue alone

Among all these then there is one kind of love which is indissoluble, where the union is owing not to the favour of a recommendation, or some great kindness or gifts, or the reason of some bargain, or the necessities of nature, but simply to similarity of virtue. This, I say, is what is broken by no chances, what no interval of time or space can sever or destroy, and what even death itself cannot part.

— Conference 16, ch. III, source. This is the classical thesis at its purest — friendship rooted in virtus, reserved for the good — but pushed past where Cicero left it. Cicero had called friendship all but immortal in memory; Cassian says the true bond “even death itself cannot part,” because its ground is a shared holiness that death does not end. He states the rule twice for emphasis: this is “the sure and indissoluble union of friendship, where the tie consists only in likeness in goodness,” sealed with the psalm, “the Lord maketh men to be of one mind in an house.”

Why friendships begun in Christ still break: the failure of one party

But we have known many set on this purpose, who though they had been joined together in companionship out of their burning love for Christ, yet could not maintain it continually and unbrokenly, because although they relied on a good beginning for their friendship, yet they did not with one and the same zeal maintain the purpose on which they had entered… for it was not maintained by the goodness of both alike, but by the patience of the one party, and so although it is held to by the one with unwearied heroism, yet it is sure to be broken by the pettiness of the other.

— Conference 16, ch. III, source. This is the observation that makes Cassian’s treatment new. He has actually seen monastic friendships, entered “out of their burning love for Christ,” go cold — and he diagnoses why: a friendship carried by only one partner’s patience cannot hold, because “love can only continue undisturbed in those in whom there is but one purpose and mind to will and to refuse the same things.” Hence his repeated insistence that unity is “of spirit rather than of place”: shared walls do nothing for those “who differ in character and purpose,” and distance is no bar to those “who are grounded on equal goodness.”

Lasting friendship is possible only among the perfect and equal

For this reason we said that the full and perfect grace of friendship can only last among those who are perfect and of equal goodness, whose likemindedness and common purpose allows them either never, or at any rate hardly ever, to disagree, or to differ in those matters which concern their progress in the spiritual life.

— Conference 16, ch. V, source. Cassian restates, at the close of the Conference too, that “love cannot last firm and unbroken except among men of the same purpose and goodness” (ch. XXIV) and that “true harmony and undivided union can only exist among those whose life is pure” (ch. XXVIII). This is the Aristotelian–Ciceronian reservation of perfect friendship to the good, made stricter: not merely good men but the equally and perfectly committed. He is careful, though, that this is not a counsel of despair — “no one can start from perfection except one who has begun from the very foundation” — and so turns from the ideal to the practical rule for reaching it.

The six foundations of enduring friendship — and the rule against anger

The first foundation then, of true friendship consists in contempt for worldly substance and scorn for all things that we possess… The second is for each man so to prune his own wishes that he may not imagine himself to be a wise and experienced person, and so prefer his own opinions to those of his neighbour. The third is for him to recognize that everything, even what he deems useful and necessary, must come after the blessing of love and peace. The fourth for him to realize that he should never be angry for any reason good or bad. The fifth for him to try to cure any wrath which a brother may have conceived against him however unreasonably… The last is… that he should realize daily that he is to pass away from this world.

— Conference 16, ch. VI, source. Here is Cassian’s distinctive contribution: friendship reduced to a workable monastic discipline. Two of the six are the load-bearing ones. The demand that a man “never… prefer his own opinions” recurs as the treatise’s central maxim (see below); and the flat prohibition — “he should never be angry for any reason good or bad” — drives the long middle of the Conference, where Cassian dissects every disguise anger wears among monks: sullen silence, feigned patience, provocative gestures, even rage-fuelled fasting. Nothing is to be “put before love” and nothing “put below rage and anger” (ch. VII); one must cure a brother’s vexation as one’s own, since before the altar “when his brother is angry with him he cannot pray” (citing Matthew 5:23–24).

Never prefer your own judgment to your brother’s

…it was ordered by them almost with the force of a law, that neither of us should trust to his own judgments more than his brother’s, if he wanted never to be deceived by the craft of the devil.

— Conference 16, ch. X, source. This is the second pillar of Cassian’s rule and the one that most sharply distinguishes friendship from mere agreement. A thought may “shine as if with a light infused by the devil” and yet be false, for “Satan himself transforms himself into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14); the only safeguard is to submit one’s private discoveries to a friend’s or elder’s judgment and “hold that the better part of true discretion is to be found in the judgment of another rather than in his own” (ch. XI). Cassian grounds the humility in Paul, who “went up to Jerusalem… that he might in a secret discussion confer with his fellow-Apostles on the gospel” (ch. XII). Friendship here becomes an instrument of spiritual safety, not only of affection.

Love ordered, not levelled: the defence of a special affection

It is possible then for all to show that love which is called agápē… And this should be shown to all men in general to such an extent that we are actually commanded by our Lord to yield it to our enemies… But diáthesis, i.e., affection is shown to but a few and those who are united to us by kindred dispositions or by a tie of goodness… This is true love set in order, which, while it hates no one, yet loves some still more by reason of their deserving it, and which, while it loves all in general, singles out for itself some from those, whom it may embrace with a special affection.

— Conference 16, ch. XIV, source. This chapter is decisive for the later monastic debate over “particular friendships.” Far from condemning preferential love, Cassian defends it: charity (agape) is owed universally, even to enemies, but affection (diathesis) is rightly “shown to but a few,” and there are legitimate “grades of love.” His proof-texts are the loves of Scripture — Jacob who “loved Joseph with deeper affection” (Genesis 37:4) and, above all, the disciple “whom Jesus loved” (John 13:23): Christ “embraced all the other eleven… with His special love,” yet loved John with “a fuller and more abundant love… which his prerogative of virginity and the purity of his flesh bestowed upon him.” The singling-out “did not indicate any coldness in love for the rest.” So the “set in order love” of the bride in the Song (Canticles 2:4) means a love that loves all while loving some more — the very warrant later ascetic writers would forget when they banned the particular friend.

The stronger partner is the one who yields

But you must certainly know that in general he plays a stronger part who subjects his own will to his brother’s, than he who is found to be the more pertinacious in defending and clinging to his own decisions. For the former by bearing and putting up with his neighbour gains the character of being strong and vigorous, while the latter gains that of being weak and sickly.

— Conference 16, ch. XXIII, source. Cassian inverts the natural reckoning of strength. The one who cedes his will “fulfils the law of Christ” by bearing another’s burden (Galatians 6:2), and so is the true physician who, being himself whole, can heal the weak; the one who clings to his own judgment is the invalid. This is the practical shape of the anti-self-will rule: yielding is not defeat but the exercise of the very virtue on which the friendship rests.

Friendships born of conspiracy cannot last

…those who entered the bonds of friendship from a beginning of conspiracy, cannot possibly preserve their harmony unbroken; either because they tried to keep it not out of their desire for perfection nor because of the sway of Apostolic love, but out of earthly love… This opinion then of the most prudent men is most certainly established; viz., that true harmony and undivided union can only exist among those whose life is pure, and who are men of the same goodness and purpose.

— Conference 16, ch. XXVIII, source. Cassian ends where he began: a friendship grounded in anything but virtue — here, in a mutual “earthly love” or bargain-like pact — carries within it the seed of its own rupture, and “that most crafty foe of ours” hurries such friends to break their word. The Conference closes with the note that his talk “fired us with a more ardent desire to preserve the love of our fellowship as a lasting one.”

Notes

  • What Cassian keeps, and what he adds. From the classical tradition he keeps the whole skeleton: the ranking of friendships (Aristotle’s utility / pleasure / virtue survives as recommendation / bargain / common-pursuit / kinship versus the one bond of virtue), the reservation of true friendship to the good, and the friend as one who wills and refuses the same things. What he adds is monastic and practical: he has watched Christ-begun friendships fail, and so he supplies a rule — six foundations, crowned by the eradication of anger and the surrender of private judgment — aimed not at defining friendship but at making a real one last inside a community of ascetics. Where Ambrose Christianized Cicero’s theory, Cassian writes the Christianized practice.
  • On “particular friendships.” Later monastic legislation would grow suspicious of the exclusive friend, fearing the special attachment as a rival to the common charity. Cassian’s Conference 16 stands at the head of the tradition on the other side: it not only permits but theologically defends an ordered, preferential love (diathesis) atop universal charity (agape), citing Jacob and Joseph and the beloved disciple. Whether the monastic tradition on balance permitted or forbade particular friendships is weighed as an argument in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • Toward Aelred. Cassian’s wedding of Ciceronian amicitia to the cloister — and his passing citation that love “not only belongs to God but… is God” (1 John 4:16, ch. XIII) — is one of the streams that feeds the great medieval synthesis of Aelred of Rievaulx’s De Spiritali Amicitia, which dares the fuller formula “God is friendship.” Cassian sits on the timeline between Ambrose’s Christianized De Officiis and that later flowering.
