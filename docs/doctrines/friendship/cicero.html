Cicero on friendship

Dates: c. 44 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Cicero (106–43 BC) wrote his Laelius de Amicitia (“On Friendship”) in 44 BC, as the Roman republic collapsed around him. It is the classic Latin treatise on the subject and the direct source the Latin fathers — Ambrose above all, and later Aelred of Rievaulx — rework for the church. Cicero grounds true friendship in virtue, holds it possible only among the good, defines it as complete accord in things human and divine joined with goodwill and affection, ranks it above every external good, and calls the true friend a “second self.”

Claims & evidence

Friendship is possible only among good men

friendship can only exist between good men

— Laelius de Amicitia V, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh, source. As with Aristotle’s “perfect” friendship, the true thing is reserved for the virtuous: only the good can be trusted, be stable, and love the friend for his own sake rather than for advantage. Cicero is careful to mean by “good” not the philosophers’ unattainable ideal but men of tried integrity such as ordinary life actually produces.

The definition of friendship

a complete accord on all subjects human and divine, joined with mutual goodwill and affection

— Laelius de Amicitia VI, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh, source. This one sentence — omnium divinarum humanarumque rerum… consensio — is the most quoted definition of friendship in the whole tradition; when Augustine and Aelred want to say what friendship is, it is Cicero’s clause they qualify or Christianize.

Friendship ranks above every external good

With the exception of wisdom, I am inclined to think nothing better than this has been given to man.

— Laelius de Amicitia VI, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh, source. Cicero sets friendship above wealth, health, power, and honours, and returns to the point later with the image the tradition remembered: “you might just as well take the sun out of the sky as friendship from life; for the immortal gods have given us nothing better or more delightful” (De Amicitia XIII).

The friend as “a second self”

In the face of a true friend a man sees as it were a second self.

— Laelius de Amicitia VII, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh, source. This is Cicero’s Latin recasting of Aristotle’s heteros autos — the famous alter idem — the formula by which the true friend is a second self, absent yet present, so that (Cicero adds) the dead friend still lives to the one who loved him.

Nature gives friendship as the handmaid of virtue, not a partner in guilt

Nature has given us friendship as the handmaid of virtue, not as a partner in guilt.

— Laelius de Amicitia XXII, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh, source. Cicero’s answer to the hard case — may a friend do wrong for a friend’s sake? — is a firm no: nothing done against the state or against duty is excused by friendship, for friendship exists to serve virtue, never to shelter crime. This limit is exactly the one the fathers seize on: it lets them praise friendship without licensing partiality in sin, and it anticipates the biblical warning that “the friendship of the world is enmity with God” (James 4:4).

Notes

  • Cicero is the pipe through which the classical doctrine reaches the church. Ambrose’s De Officiis Ministrorum is a deliberate Christian rewriting of Cicero’s De Officiis, and its closing book on friendship reworks the De Amicitia — keeping the grounding in virtue, the “second self,” and the ban on friendship in wrongdoing, while adding John 15 and the Scriptural exemplars. Aelred of Rievaulx’s De Spiritali Amicitia is, by its own account, the De Amicitia read again with Christ as its centre.
  • The same Cicero appears on this wiki also as a witness on the parity of sins timeline, as the channel of the Stoic omnia peccata paria thesis to the Latin West — there a reporter of a doctrine he mocks, here an author whose own teaching the church adopts.
