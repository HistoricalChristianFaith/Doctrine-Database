Ambrose of Milan on friendship

Dates: c. 389 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose (c. 339–397), bishop of Milan, wrote his De Officiis Ministrorum (“On the Duties of the Clergy,” c. 389) as a deliberate Christian rewriting of Cicero’s De Officiis, down to the title and the three-book plan. Just as Cicero closed by moving from the useful to the honourable, Ambrose closes his third and last book with a treatment of friendship (amicitia) that openly reworks Cicero’s Laelius for a clergy: he keeps the classical frame — friendship grounded in virtue, the “second self,” the ban on wrongdoing for a friend’s sake, the candid rebuke — and adds God as a party to friendship. Faith outranks friendship; the pattern of friendship is Christ, who called servants friends and hid nothing from them; and the exemplars are now David and Jonathan, the Three in the furnace, Job and his friends, and Judas the traitor.

Claims & evidence

The book is Cicero’s De Officiis, rewritten for the church

Ambrose does not merely echo Cicero — he announces the imitation. His treatise takes Cicero’s title, his tripartite scheme (the honourable, the useful, and their apparent conflict), and some of his anecdotes, opening Book III with Scipio’s maxim (from Cicero’s own De Officiis III.1) that he was “never less at leisure than when at leisure.” Where Cicero built his ethics on Panaetius and the Stoa and illustrated it from Roman history, Ambrose replaces the philosophers’ examples with Scripture’s: as he says in closing, “almost all the examples drawn from our forefathers… are included within these three books.”
— On the Duties of the Clergy III.28.138 (and III.1), source. The closing chapters on friendship rework the Laelius de Amicitia with Scripture alongside it.

Friendship follows virtue, and is preferred to every external good (with Cicero)

For that commendable friendship which maintains virtue is to be preferred most certainly to wealth, or honours, or power. It is not wont to be preferred to virtue indeed, but to follow after it.

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.21.124, source. Here Ambrose follows Cicero: friendship ranks above wealth, honour, and power, yet never above virtue, which it serves rather than commands. His exemplars in the same breath — Esther risking death for her people, Jonathan braving his father’s wrath, Ahimelech dying rather than betray the fleeing David — make the point that “friendship must ever be combined with virtue.”

Nothing must be done against virtue — or against religion — for a friend’s sake

Nothing, then, must be set before virtue; and that it may never be set aside by the desire for friendship, Scripture also gives us a warning on the subject of friendship. There are, indeed, various questions raised among philosophers; for instance whether a man ought for the sake of a friend to plot against his country or not… Ought friendship to take a higher place than our religion, or our love for our fellow-citizens?

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.125–126, source. This is Cicero’s hard case — may one wrong the state for a friend? — and Ambrose gives Cicero’s answer (no). To the state and to virtue he adds religion: the limit on friendship is now also the “cause of God.” Scripture, he notes, “blames not witness given against a friend, but false witness”: one must never bear false witness for a friend, and where “the cause of God or of one’s country compels one to give witness,” friendship gives way (III.22.126).

The candour of the true friend: rebuke, not flattery

For rebukes are good, and often better than a silent friendship. Even if a friend thinks himself hurt, still rebuke him; and if the bitterness of the correction wounds his mind, still rebuke him and fear not. “The wounds of a friend are better than the kisses of flatterers.”

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.127, source. Frank correction between friends — a theme dear to Cicero, who devoted a long stretch of the Laelius to admonition and flattery — Ambrose anchors in the wisdom books (Proverbs 27:6). One must rebuke privately first, then, if unheeded, openly; friendship “ought to be steadfast” and is not to be changed “in childish fashion at some idle fancy.” He balances the counsel: the warning must not be “harsh” nor the rebuke “bitter,” for “as friendship ought to avoid flattery, so, too, ought it to be free from arrogance.”

The friend is a “second self” — and friendship is a virtue, not a source of gain

For what is a friend but a partner in love, to whom thou unitest and attachest thy soul, and with whom thou blendest so as to desire from being two to become one; to whom thou entrustest thyself as to a second self, from whom thou fearest nothing, and from whom thou demandest nothing dishonourable for the sake of thine own advantage. Friendship is not meant as a source of revenue… Friendship is a virtue, not a way of making money.

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.133, source. The “second self” (alter idem) is Cicero’s own phrase, itself a Latin rendering of Aristotle’s heteros autos; Ambrose keeps it intact, and with it the twin insistence that true friendship demands nothing dishonourable and seeks no profit. He presses the Ciceronian corollary that the poor befriend better than the rich: “the friendships of the poor are generally better than those of the rich, and often the rich are without friends… For true friendship cannot exist where there is lying flattery.”

No friendship with one unfaithful to God

This is the fruit of friendship; and so faith may not be put aside for the sake of friendship. He cannot be a friend to a man who has been unfaithful to God. Friendship is the guardian of pity and the teacher of equality, so as to make the superior equal to the inferior, and the inferior to the superior. For there can be no friendship between diverse characters…

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.132, source. Cicero had grounded friendship in virtus and reserved it for the good; Ambrose keeps “there can be no friendship between diverse characters” and adds that one unfaithful to God cannot be a friend. Whether this specifies Cicero’s “accord in things human and divine” or replaces virtue with faith as the criterion of friendship is disputed; see the interpretation page. The passage leads directly into his set-piece exhortation to keep the friendships “begun with your brethren, for nothing in the world is more beautiful than that,” sealed with David’s lament: “Saul and Jonathan were lovely and pleasant, inseparable in their life, in death they were not divided.”

The pattern of friendship is Christ, who called servants friends

God Himself makes us friends instead of servants, as He Himself says: “Ye are My friends if ye do whatsoever I command you.” He gave us a pattern of friendship to follow. We are to fulfil the wish of a friend, to unfold to him our secrets which we hold in our own hearts, and are not to disregard his confidences… A friend, then, if he is a true one, hides nothing; he pours forth his soul as the Lord Jesus poured forth the mysteries of His Father.

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.135, source. Reading John 15:15, Ambrose makes Christ the pattern of the friend: the mark of friendship — hiding nothing, entrusting one’s secrets — is shown in the Lord who “poured forth the mysteries of His Father.” He adds that friendship is “shared alike by angels and by men.” In Ambrose’s account the friend’s openness imitates Christ’s disclosure of his Father’s mysteries.

The traitor at the table of friendship

Hence in the traitor the Lord found this the worst point on which to condemn his treachery, namely, that he gave no sign of gratitude and had mingled the poison of malice at the table of friendship… An enemy can be avoided; a friend cannot, if he desires to lay a plot.

— On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.136, source. Where Cicero had treated the pain of a friendship gone wrong in general terms, Ambrose gives it a name and a face: Judas, who “mingled the poison of malice at the table of friendship.” The betrayal is the worse precisely because it exploits the openness friendship requires — the friend, unlike the enemy, has been let past all one’s guard. He closes the whole treatise on friendship’s power to heal: Job’s three friends, who had wronged him, are pardoned through his intercession, “so that the prayer of friendship might win remission of sins.”

Reception and debate

  • What Ambrose keeps from Cicero, and what he adds. He keeps the whole classical frame: friendship grounded in and subordinate to virtue, possible only among the like-charactered, ranked above every external good, marked by candour and free of flattery, the friend a “second self,” and never a shelter for wrongdoing. He adds three things not in Cicero: (1) fidelity to God as a condition of friendship (III.22.132); (2) Christ as the pattern of, and a party to, friendship — “God Himself makes us friends instead of servants” — friendship “shared alike by angels and by men”; and (3) a wholly biblical cast of exemplars (Jonathan, Ahimelech, Esther, the Three in the furnace, Job’s friends, and Judas) in place of Cicero’s Scipio and Laelius.
  • Ambrose quotes the wisdom-literature maxims on friendship: “A faithful friend is the medicine of life” (Sirach 6:16) and the “wounds of a friend” over a flatterer’s kisses (Proverbs 27:6). His readings of David and Jonathan, John 15, and the “faithful friend” of Sirach are gathered under the biblical foundation on the summary timeline.
  • Reading the sentence on the unfaithful. Ambrose’s “he cannot be a friend to a man who has been unfaithful to God” is the subject of an interpretation page, which sets out two readings: a development of the classical ideal, in which faith names the good the friends share (Carolinne White, 1992), and a change of criterion, in which true friendship is denied to any unbeliever however virtuous (no named proponent located yet).
  • Aelred of Rievaulx’s De Spiritali Amicitia (c. 1160) also rereads Cicero’s Laelius with Christ at the centre, and discusses the formula “God is friendship” (see his page).
