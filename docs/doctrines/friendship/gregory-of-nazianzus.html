Gregory of Nazianzus on friendship

Dates: c. 381 · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Ambrose and Cassian theorize friendship, Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390) supplies the tradition’s most celebrated portrait of a real one — his own bond with Basil the Great, told in the funeral oration he preached over Basil (Oration 43, c. 381). The two had met as students, and Gregory describes their years together at Athens as a single life led toward God: they knew only two roads, one to the church and one to the lecture-hall; they competed only in yielding first place to each other; they “seemed to have one soul, inhabiting two bodies.” It is the classical ideal of the two virtuous souls — Aristotle’s “another self,” Cicero’s “second self” — but re-founded on a shared ascent to God and a common name, “to be and to be called Christians.” This page is the lived exemplar the theoretical treatises describe. (Their friendship did not run wholly smooth: Basil’s forcing of the wretched see of Sasima on Gregory strained it badly — part of the honest picture, noted below.)

Claims & evidence

Athens made the friendship: “our unity of life and nature”

thence he was sent by God, and by his generous craving for culture, to Athens the home of letters. Athens, which has been to me, if to any one, a city truly of gold… For it brought me to know Basil more perfectly, though he had not been unknown to me before… here I am anxious, and seize this opportunity… to dwell a little upon the recital of the causes and circumstances which originated our friendship, or to speak more strictly, our unity of life and nature.

— Oration 43 (Funeral Oration on Basil), §14, source. Gregory pauses the encomium to speak, unusually, from his own experience, and the phrase he lands on says everything: not merely a “friendship” but a “unity of life and nature” (henosis). He likens their meeting to Saul who, “seeking his father’s asses, found a kingdom” — he came to Athens for letters and found Basil. Elsewhere he pictures the two as “two branches of some river-stream,” parted at the “common fountain of our fatherland” and “again united by the impulsion of God no less than by our own agreement” (§15).

Rivals only in virtue: “one soul, inhabiting two bodies”

Yet envy we knew not, and emulation was of service to us. We struggled, not each to gain the first place for himself, but to yield it to the other; for we made each other’s reputation to be our own. We seemed to have one soul, inhabiting two bodies… The sole business of both of us was virtue, and living for the hopes to come, having retired from this world, before our actual departure hence… as we sharpened upon each other our weapons of virtue.

— Oration 43, §20, source. This is the classical formula — Aristotle’s friend as “another self,” Cicero’s “second self,” the “single soul in two bodies” of the old proverb — but reworked in two ways. First, the only rivalry admitted is a competition to defer, each making the other’s honour his own. Second, the “sole business” of the friendship is virtue and the “hopes to come”: the two friends are whetstones, sharpening on each other “our weapons of virtue.” Friendship here is not merely a good of this life but an instrument of the ascent to God — the seam where the pagan ideal turns Christian.

Two roads and one name: friendship as a shared ascent to God

Two ways were known to us, the first of greater value, the second of smaller consequence: the one leading to our sacred buildings and the teachers there, the other to secular instructors. All others we left to those who would pursue them — to feasts, theatres, meetings, banquets… we had but one great business and name — to be and to be called Christians… [nothing] was of such consequence as was to us our rising to God, through the help of, and with each other.

— Oration 43, §21, source. This is the famous “two streets” passage — the two friends knew only the road to the church and the road to the school, leaving the theatres and banquets to others. What binds them is not a shared taste or advantage (Aristotle’s lower friendships of pleasure and use) but a shared end: a common name, Christian, and a mutual “rising to God, through the help of, and with each other.” Even the danger of pagan Athens — “richer in those evil riches, idols, than the rest of Greece” — only confirmed them in the faith. The friendship is the vehicle of the ascent, not its rival.

A pair more famous than Orestes and Pylades — and the pain of parting

Orestes and Pylades were in their eyes nothing to us… And it is no wonder that I gained here in some advantage from his friendship, and that, as in life he aided me in virtue, so since his departure he has contributed to my renown.

— Oration 43, §22, source. Gregory reaches for the great friendship-pairs of pagan legend — Orestes and Pylades, the twin sons of Molione — and declares them “nothing” beside his own bond with Basil: the Christian friendship outdoes the classical exemplars it inherits. And the debt runs past death: Basil “in life… aided me in virtue,” and “since his departure” still adds to Gregory’s renown — friendship as a good that death does not cancel. When they had at last to part at Athens, Gregory says, “it was like cutting one body into two, to the destruction of either part” (§24).

Notes

  • The lived exemplar beside the treatises. The friendship timeline runs mostly through theory — Aristotle’s and Cicero’s analyses, Ambrose’s Christianized De Officiis, Cassian’s monastic rule. Gregory supplies the missing thing: a first-person account of an actual Christian friendship, and one told with a rhetorician’s art. His picture supplies in narrative what the others argue — friendship as two virtuous souls made one, re-founded on a common faith and a shared ascent to God — and became the standing patristic reference for what such a friendship looks like.
  • What Gregory keeps from the classical ideal, and what he adds. He keeps the whole apparatus: the “one soul in two bodies,” the reservation of true friendship to those bent on virtue, the friend as a second self, even the appeal to the legendary friendship-pairs. What he adds is the third term the pagan ideal lacked — God as the shared end. The friends’ “sole business” is virtue and “the hopes to come”; their one “name” is Christian; their aim is a mutual “rising to God.” This is the same baptism of amicitia that Ambrose performs in theory, shown here as a lived reality.
  • The honest picture: the Sasima rupture. The oration is a eulogy and paints the friendship at its best; the historical bond was more troubled. About 372, Basil — now metropolitan of Caesarea and locked in a jurisdictional contest with Anthimus of Tyana — consecrated Gregory bishop of Sasima, a wretched little posting Gregory bitterly resented as a pawn’s move; he never really took up the see, and the episode strained the friendship for years. That later estrangement does not undo the portrait, but it keeps it from being a mere idyll: even the tradition’s model friendship knew grievance and distance.1
  • A candidate relationship-topic. The Basil–Gregory friendship is documented richly enough — on both sides, across letters and orations — that it could one day carry its own relationship timeline (as the Jerome–Augustine and Theophilus–Chrysostom pages do), told through its dated moments from Athens to Sasima to the funeral oration. For now it serves here as a single witness on the friendship timeline.
