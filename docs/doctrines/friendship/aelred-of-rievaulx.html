Aelred of Rievaulx on friendship

Dates: c. 1160 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Cistercian abbot Aelred of Rievaulx (1110–1167) wrote De Spirituali Amicitia (Spiritual Friendship), a three-book dialogue that, by its prologue’s own account, rereads Cicero’s Laelius de Amicitia in the light of Scripture, with Christ as friendship’s beginning, middle, and end. Aelred takes over Cicero’s very definition (“agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity”) and recalls in the prologue a youthful love of Cicero, but reroots the bond in Christ: friendship must “begin in Christ, continue with Christ, and be perfected by Christ,” and he discusses — through his interlocutor Ivo — the formula “God is friendship,” a rewriting of 1 John 4:16 that he grants with careful qualification. The dialogue opens: “You and I are here, and I hope that Christ is between us as a third.” He defends an ordered love — charity owed to all, even enemies, but friendship reserved for the few — and presents spiritual friendship as a path toward the love and knowledge of God.

Claims & evidence

The prologue: a boy’s love of Cicero, later reread in the light of Christ

At last a volume of Cicero’s On Friendship fell into my hands. Immediately it seemed to me both invaluable for the soundness of its views and attractive for the charm of its eloquence… I began to acquire a taste for the sacred Scriptures and found that the slight knowledge the world had transmitted to me was insipid by comparison. Then I remembered what I had read in Cicero about friendship, but to my surprise it did not taste the same to me. Even at that time, nothing not honeyed with the honey of the sweet name of Jesus, nothing not seasoned with the salt of the sacred Scriptures, wholly won my affection.

— Spiritual Friendship, Prologue 2, 4–5, trans. Lawrence C. Braceland (Cistercian Fathers 5). Aelred lays his whole project bare in the prologue. As a schoolboy he had loved Cicero’s Laelius and found in it “a model to which I could recall my quest for many loves and affections”; but on entering the monastery and steeping himself in Scripture, the pagan book had gone flat — nothing satisfied him now that was not “honeyed with the honey of the sweet name of Jesus” and “seasoned with the salt of the sacred Scriptures.” So he resolved to test Cicero against “the authority of the Scriptures” and “to write on spiritual friendship and to set down for myself rules for a pure and holy love.” On his own account, the treatise tests Cicero against Scripture.

“Christ is between us as a third”

You and I are here, and I hope that Christ is between us as a third. Now no one else is present to disturb the peace or to interrupt our friendly conversation. No voice, no noise invades our pleasant retreat. Yes, most beloved, open your heart now and pour whatever you please into the ears of a friend.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 1, Braceland trans.; Latin Ecce ego et tu, et spero quod tertius inter nos Christus sit. Where Cicero’s Laelius is a monologue delivered to sons-in-law, Aelred stages a real conversation between himself and the monk Ivo — and installs Christ as a third party actually present to it (the note glances at Matthew 18:20, “where two or three are gathered… there am I in the midst”). Cicero had made the friend “a second self”; Aelred keeps that, and adds that the friendship is a fellowship of three, whose centre is the Christ between the two friends.

Friendship must begin, continue, and be perfected in Christ

What statement about friendship can be more sublime, more true, more valuable than this: it has been proved that friendship must begin in Christ, continue with Christ, and be perfected by Christ.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 10, Braceland trans. This thesis stands at the head of the inquiry. Ivo has just insisted that he wants friendship “which should begin in Christ, be maintained according to Christ, and have its end and value referred to Christ,” adding that “Cicero was ignorant of the virtue of true friendship, since he was completely ignorant of Christ, who is the beginning and end of friendship” (I.8). Aelred agrees so warmly that he abandons the posture of teacher — “I will not so much teach you as confer with you” — because Ivo has “lit that brightest of lamps” that fixes the end of the question. For Aelred, Christ is not friendship’s mere occasion but its origin, sustaining principle, and goal.

Cicero’s definition, taken up and put to the test

Is Cicero’s definition not an adequate beginning for you? “Friendship is agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity.”

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 11 (quoting Cicero, De amicitia 6.20), Braceland trans. Aelred does not discard the classical definition — he begins from it, word for word. But he immediately submits it to a Christian cross-examination through Ivo, who objects that the definition “suited not only pagans and Jews but also unjust Christians” and confesses “my conviction that true friendship cannot exist between those who live without Christ” (I.16). Aelred neither simply accepts nor rejects Cicero: the definition may fail “either by defect or by excess,” and the treatise’s work is to find where it must be corrected and where it can be kept. Ambrose had reworked Cicero’s De Officiis in a similar way, in the closing book of a treatise rather than a dialogue.

Aelred’s own definition: many made one

Friendship is that virtue, therefore, through which by a covenant of sweetest love our very spirits are united, and from many are made one. Hence even the philosophers of this world placed friendship not among the accidents of mortal life but among the virtues that are eternal.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 21, Braceland trans. Having tested Cicero, Aelred offers his own formula — the “from many are made one” echoing the Acts 4:32 portrait of the first believers “of one heart and one soul,” which he cites as the true fulfilment of Cicero’s definition (I.28, 29). Friendship is a virtue, and therefore eternal: “a friend loves always” (Proverbs 17:17), so “a friendship that can end was never true” (quoting Jerome). “Though challenged, though injured, though tossed into the flames, though nailed to a cross, a friend loves always” (I.24).

Friendship distinguished from charity: an ordered, preferential love

Divine authority commands that many more be received to the clasp of charity than to the embrace of friendship. By the law of charity we are ordered to welcome into the bosom of love not only our friends but also our enemies. But we call friends only those to whom we have no qualm about entrusting our heart and all its contents, while these friends are bound to us in turn by the same inviolable law of loyalty and trustworthiness.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 32, Braceland trans. Ivo asks whether friendship and charity are then the same thing; Aelred answers, “On the contrary, the greatest distinction!” Charity is owed universally — to all, even enemies — but friendship is reserved for the few whom one may trust with the whole heart. Cassian had drawn a similar distinction (universal agape and a preferential diathesis). For Aelred a chosen, singular friendship is not a rival to charity, and spiritual friendship is the selective bond that charity alone does not name.

“God is friendship” — the formula and Aelred’s qualification

IVO. To what does this lead? Should I say of friendship what John, the friend of Jesus, said of charity, “God is friendship”?
AELRED. This is novel indeed and lacks the authority of the Scriptures. The rest of that verse about charity, however, I surely do not hesitate to attribute to friendship, because the one who remains in friendship remains in God, and God in him.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 69–70 (reworking 1 John 4:16, “God is love… he that abideth in love abideth in God, and God in him”), Braceland trans. Aelred frames the line with care. It is Ivo who proposes the substitution — “God is friendship” for John’s “God is charity” — and Aelred does not flatly endorse it: the equation is “novel… and lacks the authority of the Scriptures.” What he does grant, without hesitation, is the rest of the verse: whoever remains in friendship remains in God. He declines to name God as friendship, and grants that friendship is a place where one abides in God.

Reception and debate

  • Aelred and the earlier witnesses. Ambrose rewrote Cicero in the closing book of his De Officiis; Augustine wrote of the friend loved “in God” who cannot be lost; Cassian wrote a monastic rule of friendship. Aelred writes a sustained treatise that keeps Cicero’s form, definition, and vocabulary and roots friendship in Christ.
  • Cicero quoted and tested. Aelred quotes Cicero’s definition (6.20), his claim that friendship belongs among the eternal virtues (9.32), his “three or four pairs” of famed friends (4.15), and much else — and then tests each against Scripture, keeping some and correcting some. In the dialogue it is Ivo who says that Cicero “was ignorant of the virtue of true friendship, since he was completely ignorant of Christ” (I.8); Aelred also retains much of Cicero’s apparatus. Whether this amounts to the classical ideal carried over and transformed, or replaced by a different love, is disputed; see the interpretation page.
  • On preferential friendship. Like Cassian, Aelred holds charity for all and friendship for the few, and treats the singular friendship as what charity does not supply and as a path toward the love and knowledge of God.
  • Text and translation. Quotations follow Lawrence C. Braceland’s translation (Aelred of Rievaulx: Spiritual Friendship, Cistercian Fathers Series 5, ed. Marsha L. Dutton), cited by the paragraph numbering of that edition. The three-book plan is Aelred’s own: Book One on the nature and origin of friendship, Book Two on its fruit and excellence, Book Three on how and among whom it may be kept unbroken.
