Aelred of Rievaulx on friendship

Dates: c. 1160 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Cistercian abbot Aelred of Rievaulx (1110–1167) wrote the Christian classic on the subject — De Spirituali Amicitia (Spiritual Friendship), a three-book dialogue that is a deliberate, self-conscious rewriting of Cicero’s Laelius de Amicitia with Christ installed as friendship’s beginning, middle, and end. It is the culmination of the whole arc this timeline traces. Aelred takes over Cicero’s very definition (“agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity”) and structure (three books, a youthful love of Cicero recalled in the prologue), but reroots the bond in Christ: friendship must “begin in Christ, continue with Christ, and be perfected by Christ,” and he dares — through his interlocutor Ivo — the audacious formula “God is friendship,” a rewriting of 1 John 4:16 that he grants with careful qualification. His most famous line opens the dialogue: “You and I are here, and I hope that Christ is between us as a third.” Against the monastic suspicion of the exclusive or “particular” friend he defends an ordered love — charity owed to all, even enemies, but friendship reserved for the few — and makes spiritual friendship a genuine path toward the love and knowledge of God.

Claims & evidence

The prologue: a boy’s love of Cicero, later reread in the light of Christ

At last a volume of Cicero’s On Friendship fell into my hands. Immediately it seemed to me both invaluable for the soundness of its views and attractive for the charm of its eloquence… I began to acquire a taste for the sacred Scriptures and found that the slight knowledge the world had transmitted to me was insipid by comparison. Then I remembered what I had read in Cicero about friendship, but to my surprise it did not taste the same to me. Even at that time, nothing not honeyed with the honey of the sweet name of Jesus, nothing not seasoned with the salt of the sacred Scriptures, wholly won my affection.

— Spiritual Friendship, Prologue 2, 4–5, trans. Lawrence C. Braceland (Cistercian Fathers 5). Aelred lays his whole project bare in the prologue. As a schoolboy he had loved Cicero’s Laelius and found in it “a model to which I could recall my quest for many loves and affections”; but on entering the monastery and steeping himself in Scripture, the pagan book had gone flat — nothing satisfied him now that was not “honeyed with the honey of the sweet name of Jesus” and “seasoned with the salt of the sacred Scriptures.” So he resolved to test Cicero against “the authority of the Scriptures” and “to write on spiritual friendship and to set down for myself rules for a pure and holy love.” The whole treatise is that experiment: Cicero read back through Christ.

“Christ is between us as a third”

You and I are here, and I hope that Christ is between us as a third. Now no one else is present to disturb the peace or to interrupt our friendly conversation. No voice, no noise invades our pleasant retreat. Yes, most beloved, open your heart now and pour whatever you please into the ears of a friend.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 1, Braceland trans.; Latin Ecce ego et tu, et spero quod tertius inter nos Christus sit. The dialogue’s opening words are the sentence the whole later tradition remembers Aelred by. Where Cicero’s Laelius is a monologue delivered to sons-in-law, Aelred stages a real conversation between himself and the monk Ivo — and installs Christ as a third party actually present to it (the note glances at Matthew 18:20, “where two or three are gathered… there am I in the midst”). The pagan ideal had made the friend “a second self”; Aelred keeps that, but adds the term the pagan version could not have: the friendship is a fellowship of three, and its centre is not the two friends but the Christ between them.

Friendship must begin, continue, and be perfected in Christ

What statement about friendship can be more sublime, more true, more valuable than this: it has been proved that friendship must begin in Christ, continue with Christ, and be perfected by Christ.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 10, Braceland trans. This is the thesis of the whole work, placed at the head of the inquiry. Ivo has just insisted that he wants friendship “which should begin in Christ, be maintained according to Christ, and have its end and value referred to Christ,” adding that “Cicero was ignorant of the virtue of true friendship, since he was completely ignorant of Christ, who is the beginning and end of friendship” (I.8). Aelred agrees so warmly that he abandons the posture of teacher — “I will not so much teach you as confer with you” — because Ivo has “lit that brightest of lamps” that fixes the end of the question. Christ is not friendship’s mere occasion but its origin, sustaining principle, and goal.

Cicero’s definition, taken up and put to the test

Is Cicero’s definition not an adequate beginning for you? “Friendship is agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity.”

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 11 (quoting Cicero, De amicitia 6.20), Braceland trans. Aelred does not discard the classical definition — he begins from it, word for word. But he immediately submits it to a Christian cross-examination through Ivo, who objects that the definition “suited not only pagans and Jews but also unjust Christians” and confesses “my conviction that true friendship cannot exist between those who live without Christ” (I.16). Aelred neither simply accepts nor rejects Cicero: the definition may fail “either by defect or by excess,” and the treatise’s work is to find where it must be corrected and where it can be kept. This is the same conscious baptism of amicitia that Ambrose had begun with Cicero’s De Officiis — now carried through as a full dialogue.

Aelred’s own definition: many made one

Friendship is that virtue, therefore, through which by a covenant of sweetest love our very spirits are united, and from many are made one. Hence even the philosophers of this world placed friendship not among the accidents of mortal life but among the virtues that are eternal.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 21, Braceland trans. Having tested Cicero, Aelred offers his own formula — the “from many are made one” echoing the Acts 4:32 portrait of the first believers “of one heart and one soul,” which he cites as the true fulfilment of Cicero’s definition (I.28, 29). Friendship is a virtue, and therefore eternal: “a friend loves always” (Proverbs 17:17), so “a friendship that can end was never true” (quoting Jerome). “Though challenged, though injured, though tossed into the flames, though nailed to a cross, a friend loves always” (I.24).

Friendship distinguished from charity: an ordered, preferential love

Divine authority commands that many more be received to the clasp of charity than to the embrace of friendship. By the law of charity we are ordered to welcome into the bosom of love not only our friends but also our enemies. But we call friends only those to whom we have no qualm about entrusting our heart and all its contents, while these friends are bound to us in turn by the same inviolable law of loyalty and trustworthiness.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 32, Braceland trans. Ivo asks whether friendship and charity are then the same thing; Aelred answers, “On the contrary, the greatest distinction!” Charity is owed universally — to all, even enemies — but friendship is reserved for the few whom one may trust with the whole heart. This is the same “ordered love” that Cassian had defended (universal agape atop a preferential diathesis), and it places Aelred squarely against the later monastic suspicion of the exclusive or “particular” friend: for Aelred a chosen, singular friendship is not a rival to charity but its crown, and spiritual friendship is precisely the selective bond that charity alone does not name.

“God is friendship” — the audacious formula, carefully qualified

IVO. To what does this lead? Should I say of friendship what John, the friend of Jesus, said of charity, “God is friendship”?
AELRED. This is novel indeed and lacks the authority of the Scriptures. The rest of that verse about charity, however, I surely do not hesitate to attribute to friendship, because the one who remains in friendship remains in God, and God in him.

— Spiritual Friendship, Book One 69–70 (reworking 1 John 4:16, “God is love… he that abideth in love abideth in God, and God in him”), Braceland trans. This is the line most often quoted from Aelred, and it is worth noting how carefully he frames it. It is Ivo who floats the daring substitution — “God is friendship” for John’s “God is charity” — and Aelred does not flatly endorse it: the equation is “novel… and lacks the authority of the Scriptures.” What he does grant, without hesitation, is the rest of the verse: whoever remains in friendship remains in God. The half-step he refuses (naming God as friendship) and the half-step he takes (friendship as the place where one abides in God) together mark exactly how far the Christianization of amicitia can go — and the wiki weighs the “God is friendship” reading of 1 John 4:16 as an argument in the summary’s Arguments section.

Notes

  • The culmination of the arc. Aelred is the point the whole timeline has been climbing toward. What Ambrose did in a single closing book of his De Officiis — rewrite Cicero for the church — and what Augustine made existential (the friend loved “in God” who cannot be lost) and Cassian turned into a monastic rule, Aelred gathers into one sustained treatise that keeps Cicero’s form, definition, and vocabulary whole and reroots the entire thing in Christ. It is the most complete Christian synthesis of classical amicitia the tradition produced.
  • Cicero kept, not discarded. Aelred’s method is neither rejection nor mere baptism-by-label: he quotes Cicero’s definition (6.20), his claim that friendship belongs among the eternal virtues (9.32), his “three or four pairs” of famed friends (4.15), and much else — and then tests each against Scripture, keeping what survives and correcting what does not. The famous verdict is that Cicero “was ignorant of the virtue of true friendship, since he was completely ignorant of Christ” (I.8) — yet the pagan apparatus survives almost entire, now serving a friendship that ascends toward God.
  • On “particular friendships.” Like Cassian before him, Aelred stands on the permissive side of the long monastic debate: charity for all, friendship for the few. Against the fear that a chosen, exclusive attachment competes with the love owed to the whole community, he makes the singular friendship the very thing charity does not supply — and a genuine path toward the love and knowledge of God. Whether the tradition on balance permitted or forbade the particular friend is weighed as an argument in the summary’s Arguments section, for which Aelred is a central witness.
  • Text and translation. Quotations follow Lawrence C. Braceland’s translation (Aelred of Rievaulx: Spiritual Friendship, Cistercian Fathers Series 5, ed. Marsha L. Dutton), cited by the paragraph numbering of that edition. The Laelius-imitating three-book plan is Aelred’s own: Book One on the nature and origin of friendship, Book Two on its fruit and excellence, Book Three on how and among whom it may be kept unbroken.
