C. S. Lewis on friendship

Dates: c. 1960 (The Four Loves) · Wikipedia
Summary: C. S. Lewis (1898–1963), the Oxford and Cambridge scholar and lay Anglican apologist, is this timeline’s modern terminus. The chapter on Friendship (Philia) in The Four Loves (1960) is the twentieth century’s best-known reflection on the subject, and Lewis writes it as a self-conscious heir of the classical tradition — naming “that Philia which Aristotle classified among the virtues [and] that Amicitia on which Cicero wrote a book,” and taking up their high estimate against a modern world that, he argues, has half forgotten how to value friendship at all. His distinctive contributions are four: that friendship is the least natural and least necessary of the loves, and therefore the most freely chosen and (he thinks) the most spiritual; that it is born side by side over a shared vision (“Do you see the same truth?”) rather than face to face like eros; that it is therefore the least jealous of loves; and that it carries a real and specific danger — the friendly circle hardening into a proud coterie, a “pocket of potential resistance.” He closes, as Aelred had, by handing the whole thing back to Christ: the friends we think we chose were chosen for us.

Claims & evidence

The least natural of the loves

Friendship is — in a sense not at all derogatory to it — the least natural of loves; the least instinctive, organic, biological, gregarious and necessary… it is unnecessary. Like philosophy, like art, like the universe itself (for God did not need to create). It has no survival value; rather it is one of those things which give value to survival.

— The Four Loves (1960), “Friendship,” full text. This is Lewis’s opening move, and it inverts the modern instinct that only what is biologically needed is real. Precisely because our species does not need friendship to reproduce or survive, Lewis counts it the freest of the loves and the one that most resembles the love of God, who created out of no need. Where the ancients placed friendship at the summit because it was chosen and rational rather than driven by appetite, Lewis restates the same intuition for readers who no longer share it — the very fact that friendship is dispensable is what makes it exalted.

Born side by side, not face to face: “Do you see the same truth?”

Lovers are normally face to face, absorbed in each other; Friends, side by side, absorbed in some common interest… In this kind of love, as Emerson said, “Do you love me?” means “Do you see the same truth?” — Or at least, “Do you care about the same truth?” The man who agrees with us that some question, little regarded by others, is of great importance can be our Friend. He need not agree with us about the answer.

— The Four Loves, “Friendship,” full text. Lewis locates the birth of a friendship in a moment of shared recognition — “Friendship arises out of mere Companionship when two or more of the companions discover that they have in common some insight or interest or even taste which the others do not share and which, till that moment, each believed to be his own unique treasure (or burden),” whose “typical expression… would be something like, ‘What? You too? I thought I was the only one.’” Friendship is thus about something outside the two friends: it is “uninquisitive,” indifferent to a companion’s rank, income, or marriage, because the real question is not who the friend is but whether he sees the same truth. This is Lewis’s own rendering of the classical insight that friendship rests on a shared good — Aristotle’s “another self” who loves the same things, Cicero’s “agreement in things human and divine.”

The least jealous of loves

In this, Friendship exhibits a glorious “nearness by resemblance” to Heaven itself… the more we thus share the Heavenly Bread between us, the more we shall have. Hence true Friendship is the least jealous of loves. Two friends delight to be joined by a third, and three by a fourth, if only the newcomer is qualified to become a real friend. They can then say, as the blessed souls say in Dante, “Here comes one who will augment our loves.” For in this love “to divide is not to take away.”

— The Four Loves, “Friendship,” full text. Because friendship faces outward toward a shared vision rather than inward on the pair, a new friend does not divide the love but multiplies it — each friend draws out facets of the others that no one alone could call forth. This is the exact opposite of eros, which is by nature exclusive and jealous. Here Lewis rejoins the older tradition’s defence of an ordered, expandable love — the point Cassian and Aelred had made in defending “particular” friendships against the fear that a chosen bond robs the wider community: for Aelred too the coming of friends was a matter of “from many are made one.”

The moderns undervalue it

To the Ancients, Friendship seemed the happiest and most fully human of all loves; the crown of life and the school of virtue. The modern world, in comparison, ignores it… The first and most obvious answer is that few value it because few experience it.

— The Four Loves, “Friendship,” full text. Lewis makes the historical contrast explicit, and it is what earns him his place at the end of this timeline: he is consciously recovering a lost esteem. He names his forebears — “that Philia which Aristotle classified among the virtues [and] that Amicitia on which Cicero wrote a book” — and diagnoses the modern neglect as partly a failure of experience (few moderns have the deep friendships the ancients assumed) and partly a modern suspicion that any strong same-sex affection must be disguised eros, a suspicion he rebuts at length. Against it he restates the ancient verdict: friendship is not a consolation prize beside marriage and family but a love that, at its height, “is the happiest and most fully human of all.”

The danger: friendship as a “pocket of potential resistance”

Friendship (as the ancients saw) can be a school of virtue; but also (as they did not see) a school of vice. It is ambivalent. It makes good men better and bad men worse… Every real Friendship is a sort of secession, even a rebellion… In each knot of Friends there is a sectional “public opinion” which fortifies its members against the public opinion of the community in general. Each therefore is a pocket of potential resistance.

— The Four Loves, “Friendship,” full text. This is Lewis’s sharpest addition to the tradition, and the point at which he claims to correct the ancients. The very thing that makes friendship precious — its indifference to outside opinion, its shared secret vision — is also what makes it dangerous: the circle of friends can curdle into a proud coterie, deaf to just criticism, a “ mutual admiration society” that flatters its members into believing themselves an elite. This is the modern counterpart to the biblical warning the timeline began with (James’ “friendship of the world is enmity with God”) and to the fathers’ wariness of a fierce particular attachment: for Lewis the remedy is that friendship, like every natural love, must submit to and be governed by charity, or it becomes a “school of vice.”

“I have chosen you for one another”: friendship handed back to Christ

A secret Master of the Ceremonies has been at work. Christ, who said to the disciples “Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you,” can truly say to every group of Christian friends “You have not chosen one another but I have chosen you for one another.” The Friendship is not a reward for our discrimination and good taste in finding one another out. It is the instrument by which God reveals to each the beauties of all the others.

— The Four Loves, “Friendship,” full text. Lewis ends where Aelred had ended: with Christ as a third party to the friendship rather than its mere occasion. What looks like the freest and most chosen of loves — we think we picked our friends by taste and luck — is at bottom a gift; “for a Christian, there are no chances.” So the arc that began with Aristotle’s and Cicero’s two virtuous souls, and was rerouted through God by Augustine (“his friend in Thee”) and Aelred (“Christ… between us as a third”), reaches its modern restatement: even the choosing was Christ’s, and the friends are the instrument by which he shows each the beauty of the rest.

Notes

  • The modern terminus of the arc. Lewis is the timeline’s closing witness precisely because he is a self-conscious heir: he names Aristotle and Cicero, adopts their high estimate of friendship as a virtue, and, like Aelred, reroots the whole thing in Christ. His achievement is to make the classical case live again for a century that had stopped believing friendship was a serious love at all — showing that the “philia vs. agape” question and the ancient claim that friendship is a school of virtue are still live.
  • Where he departs from the ancients. Lewis explicitly claims to see something Aristotle and Cicero did not: that friendship is ambivalent — a school of vice as readily as of virtue, because its very indifference to outside opinion can harden a circle into a proud, closed coterie. This is his one genuine addition to the tradition, and it answers, from the natural side, the same danger the fathers had watched warily from the side of charity: a particular love that competes with the love owed to all.
  • Text and edition. Quotations are from the “Friendship” chapter of The Four Loves (Geoffrey Bles, 1960; Harcourt Brace, 1960), quoted briefly under fair use and checked against the online full text of that chapter. The book treats four loves in turn — Affection (storge), Friendship (philia), Eros, and Charity (agape) — and the argument of the Friendship chapter presupposes the closing chapter’s claim that every natural love must be taken up and ruled by the supernatural love of God.
