Augustine of Hippo on friendship

Dates: c. 397 (Confessions, c. 397–400) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, narrates in Book IV of his Confessions the death of an unnamed boyhood friend and the grief that followed, and explains that grief: he had loved a mortal as if he would never die, pouring out his soul upon the dust. From this he concludes that a love resting in a mortal as its final good is torn apart by the friend’s loss, and that only friendship in God — where the friend is loved in the One who cannot be lost — is secure. “Blessed be he who loveth Thee, and his friend in Thee.” His letters, written across the miles to men he sometimes had never met, describe friendships founded on a shared faith.

Claims & evidence

True friendship exists only where God binds the friends together

But he was not then my friend, nor, indeed, afterwards, as true friendship is; for true it is not but in such as Thou bindest together, cleaving unto Thee by that love which is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost, which is given unto us. But yet it was too sweet, being ripened by the fervour of similar studies… Thou removedst that man from this life when he had scarce completed one whole year of my friendship, sweet to me above all the sweetness of that my life.

— Confessions IV.4.7, source.1 Augustine states his thesis in the sentence that introduces the friend: however sweet, the bond of their shared studies was not friendship “as true friendship is,” because true friendship exists only in those whom God binds to one another by binding each to himself. The friend was a fellow Manichee, drawn by Augustine into “superstitious and pernicious fables”; on Augustine’s later account, a bond not formed by God was not friendship “as true friendship is.”

Grief for the friend: a “second self,” one soul in two bodies

For I was astonished that other mortals lived, since he whom I loved, as if he would never die, was dead; and I wondered still more that I, who was to him a second self, could live when he was dead. Well did one say of his friend, “Thou half of my soul,” for I felt that my soul and his soul were but one soul in two bodies; and, consequently, my life was a horror to me, because I would not live in half.

— Confessions IV.6.11, source.2 Here Augustine reaches for the very formulas of the classical ideal — the friend a “second self” (Cicero’s alter idem, Aristotle’s heteros autos), the “one soul in two bodies” of the old proverb — to describe his grief. Because he had made a mortal the whole of his soul, the mortal’s death left him only “half,” a living horror to himself. In the chapters that follow he judges such a love by the fact that its object “must die.” (In his later Retractations Augustine looks back on the “one soul in two bodies” sentence as a “light declamation” whose logic he no longer defends.)

Loving a mortal as if he would never die

For whence had that former sorrow so easily penetrated to the quick, but that I had poured out my soul upon the dust, in loving one who must die as if he were never to die?

— Confessions IV.8.13, source.3 On Augustine’s account the grief was so total not because the love was too great but because it was misplaced — poured out “upon the dust,” spent wholly on a perishable thing as though it were imperishable. Augustine does not conclude that one should love friends less; the same chapter contains his warmest praise of friendship’s ordinary joys — “to discourse and jest with them; to indulge in an interchange of kindnesses; to read together pleasant books… these… were so much fuel to melt our souls together, and out of many to make but one.” In his account the fault lies not in the loving but in where the love rests.

Loving the friend in God, who cannot be lost

Blessed be he who loveth Thee, and his friend in Thee, and his enemy for Thy sake. For he alone loses none dear to him to whom all are dear in Him who cannot be lost. And who is this but our God… None loseth Thee but he who leaveth Thee.

— Confessions IV.9.14, source.4 Augustine names God as the one in whom the friend is to be loved. To love a friend in God is to love him in the One “who cannot be lost,” so that death, which robbed the young Augustine of half his soul, can no longer take the friend away — “he alone loses none dear to him to whom all are dear in Him.” The same sentence extends to the enemy (“his enemy for Thy sake”). Whether this re-founds Cicero’s virtue-friendship or sets a different love in its place is disputed; see the interpretation page.

Love is not condemned, but love in God is to be preferred

If souls please thee, let them be loved in God; for they also are mutable, but in Him are they firmly established, else would they pass, and pass away. In Him, then, let them be beloved; and draw unto Him along with thee as many souls as thou canst.

— Confessions IV.12.18, source.5 The chapter heading in Pilkington’s translation reads “Love is not condemned, but love in God… is to be preferred.” Augustine’s text continues: friends are still to be loved, since loving them in God means drawing them with oneself toward him. What is refused is only the love that rests in the mutable creature as its final good; that love “justly shall it be embittered… because whatsoever cometh from Him is unjustly loved if He be forsaken for it.”

The letters: a Christian friendship enacted across the miles

O excellent man and excellent brother, there was a time when you were unknown to my mind… why then does a longing for your presence rack my inmost soul?… I grieve because I do not see you, and my grief itself comforts me… For if… I had come to know you as my brother and friend, and as one so eminent as a Christian, so noble as a man, how could you think that it would be no disappointment to me if I were not permitted to know your dwelling?

— Letter 27 (to Paulinus of Nola, a.d. 395), source.6 Augustine writes to Paulinus of Nola a friend he has never laid eyes on, having come to know his “inner man” through a single letter “which flows with milk and honey” — and the bond is founded on a shared faith: they are already “brother and friend” in Christ before they are acquaintances in the flesh. Augustine describes a love that binds two souls who share the same Lord even before they share a city, and that longs for the “heavenly Jerusalem” through the ache of the friend’s absence. The same warmth runs through his long friendship with Nebridius, whose questions and death fill the early letters and the ninth book of the Confessions.

Reception and debate

  • What Augustine keeps, and what he changes. Unlike Ambrose, whose De Officiis consciously rewrote Cicero and largely kept his frame, Augustine does not write a treatise on friendship — he tells a story and draws a conclusion from it. He keeps the classical vocabulary (the “second self,” “one soul in two bodies,” the sweetness of shared studies and shared laughter) and asks where such a love should rest: in his account a friendship loved as its own final good is exposed by grief. Ambrose makes fidelity to God a condition of friendship; Augustine’s emphasis is that friendship is secure only when the friend is loved in the One who cannot die.
  • Grief in Book IV and Book IX. Book IX, where Augustine weeps for his mother Monica and then examines the weeping, gives a second account of grief beside that of Book IV. His Retractations call the Book IV phrase (“one soul in two bodies”) a “light declamation.”
  • Augustine’s preference for “love mingled with severity” over “deceit with indulgence” in dealing with a friend (Letter 93) is quoted with Ambrose’s counsel of candid rebuke under the biblical foundation on the summary timeline.
  • Augustine’s texts are cited in both interpretation pages on this topic: whether Ambrose’s rerooting of friendship in faith develops the classical ideal or changes its criterion, and whether classical virtue-friendship survived its Christian reworking.
