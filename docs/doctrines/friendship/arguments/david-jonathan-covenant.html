Was the David–Jonathan bond a covenant friendship?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the language, the formal covenant apparatus, and an unbroken pre-modern reception all converge on covenant/loyalty friendship as the plain sense of the narrative; the only caveat is not to over-sanitize a warmly affectionate bond into a bloodless legal pact · advanced by John Chrysostom and the whole pre-modern tradition, and by modern scholarship (Thompson, Olyan, Zehnder)

Related arguments: the nature of the David–Jonathan bond — see also the David–Jonathan bond as a homoerotic relationship

The claim

I will now cite you from the Scriptures a wonderful instance of friendship. Jonathan, the son of Saul, loved David, and his soul was so knit to him, that David in mourning over him says, “Thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women.”… Such friendship I would wish all to entertain both towards the living and the dead.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on 2 Timothy 7, link.1 The Old Testament’s set-piece of the two knit souls (1 Samuel 18–20; 2 Samuel 1:26) is read on this view as Scripture’s own paradigm of covenant friendship — a bond of sworn loyalty and steadfast affection (ḥesed) in the Near-Eastern idiom, crowned by David’s lament that Jonathan’s love “surpassed the love of women.” The interpretation to weigh: is this the best account of the narrative — that its “love” is the covenant-loyalty vocabulary of the ancient world, its “covenant” a formal treaty renouncing a throne, and its praise a ranking of kinds of love — against the modern reading that finds a romance in it?

Who advances it

This is the mainstream reading, and it is also the only reading anyone gave the text for its first two millennia. The whole pre-modern tradition — Jewish and Christian — takes the bond as the exemplary friendship: Chrysostom holds it up as “a wonderful instance of friendship”; Ambrose makes Jonathan the model of the friend who “for his affection’s sake avoided neither his father’s displeasure nor the danger to his own safety”; Bede reads the covenant allegorically as Christ and the Church; Aelred of Rievaulx enshrines it in the medieval classic of spiritual friendship.2 Modern scholarship supplies the philological spine: J. A. Thompson showed that Hebrew ʾāhēb (“love”) carries political/covenant-loyalty force in the Deuteronomistic History and in Near-Eastern treaty usage; Saul M. Olyan reads 2 Samuel 1:26 through its surrounding covenant language; and Markus Zehnder’s long study is the standard defence of the friendship reading against the homoerotic one.3

The evidence for

The strong form of the covenant-friendship reading rests on four converging observations.

“Love” here is covenant-loyalty vocabulary. In the Deuteronomistic History and across the ancient Near East, “to love” is the standard word for treaty allegiance, not only for private affection. Hiram of Tyre “was ever a lover of David” in a plainly diplomatic relationship (1 Kings 5:1); “all Israel and Judah loved David” (1 Samuel 18:16) is a statement about their allegiance to him as leader. So when “Jonathan loved him as his own soul” (18:1, 3) and “Jonathan caused David to swear again, because he loved him” (20:17), the word is doing the work it does everywhere else in these books: it names sworn loyalty.4

The narrative is saturated with formal covenant apparatus. The two do not merely feel affection; they “cut a covenant” (kārat bərît) and renew it — 1 Samuel 18:3; 20:16; 23:18 — sworn under oath “in the name of the LORD” and binding their descendants (“The LORD be between me and thee, and between my seed and thy seed for ever,” 20:42). Jonathan later “strengthened [David’s] hand in God” and formally ceded the succession: “thou shalt be king over Israel, and I shall be next unto thee” (23:16–18). The relationship is structured, from first to last, as a treaty of loyalty.5

The robe and armor are dynastic investiture, not disrobing. When Jonathan “stripped himself of the robe that was upon him, and gave it to David, and his garments, even to his sword, and to his bow, and to his girdle” (18:4), the crown prince is divesting himself of the royal regalia and investing David — the same symbolic grammar as Saul’s torn robe standing for the torn-away kingdom (15:27–28) or Elijah’s mantle cast on Elisha. It enacts the abdication Jonathan states outright at 23:17.5

“Passing the love of women” compares kinds of love. David’s lament — “I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan… thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women” (2 Samuel 1:26) — calls Jonathan “brother,” a treaty term for a sworn ally, and ranks his covenant loyalty above the (politically contingent) love of the marriage alliances that had so far cost David dearly. It is a comparison of one kind of love with another, not a disclosure that this one was erotic. That the comparandum is “women” tells the other way, too: David is portrayed throughout as vigorously heterosexual (Michal, Abigail, and the rest; later Bathsheba), and Jonathan himself married and fathered Mephibosheth — neither man is drawn as anything but married.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. On the philology, the covenant apparatus, the investiture symbolism, and the reception, the covenant-friendship reading is the plain sense and the consensus. Its one live challenger — that the narrative is homoerotically charged — is weighed on its own page and comes out weak: every textual hook it presses (the “love” language, the disrobing, the parting kiss, Saul’s rebuke, 1:26) has a covenant/political sense that fits ancient usage better, and the reading has no support before the 1970s.

What is overstated or imprecise. The danger runs the other way here: a defensive reading can over-sanitize the bond into a bloodless legal pact, as though “covenant” drained it of feeling. It did not. The text is unusually warm — souls “knit” together, weeping at parting “until David exceeded” (20:41), a love publicly ranked above marriage. Even scholars who firmly reject the erotic reading grant a genuine affective intensity, and Martti Nissinen’s careful term for it — homosocial, an intense same-sex comradeship of the sort the ancient world narrated of its heroes (Gilgamesh and Enkidu, Achilles and Patroclus) without implying sex — marks the honest boundary: the bond is warmly, demonstratively affectionate, and it is covenant friendship, not romance.7 Reading it as a cold treaty and reading it as an affair are opposite failures of the same text.

Assessment

Sound. That the David–Jonathan bond is a covenant friendship — sworn loyalty and steadfast ḥesed in the Near-Eastern idiom, expressed with real and demonstrative affection — is the mainstream, best-supported reading, and the only one the text received for its first two thousand years. The “love” is the treaty-allegiance vocabulary of these books, the “covenant” is a formal oath renouncing a throne, the robe is investiture, and “passing the love of women” ranks kinds of love. The only correction the sound reading needs is against itself: it should not flatten a warmly affectionate friendship into a mere legal instrument. The rival homoerotic reading is weighed separately and does not hold.
