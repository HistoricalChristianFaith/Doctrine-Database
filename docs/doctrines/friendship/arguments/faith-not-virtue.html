Does Ambrose’s “no friend to one unfaithful to God” (De Officiis III.22.132) develop the classical ideal of friendship or change its criterion?

Disputed reading · on The Christian Theology of Friendship · witness: Ambrose of Milan

Related readings: Did classical virtue-friendship survive its Christian reworking, or was it superseded?

The passage

This is the fruit of friendship; and so faith may not be put aside for the sake of friendship. He cannot be a friend to a man who has been unfaithful to God… For there can be no friendship between diverse characters.

— Ambrose of Milan, On the Duties of the Clergy III.22.132 (c. 389), source.1

The question

Aristotle and Cicero ground true friendship in virtue and reserve it for the good; Ambrose keeps that frame but makes fidelity to God a condition of friendship, and Aelred of Rievaulx later writes that friendship must “begin in Christ.” Whether this specifies the good the classical friends were already to share, or replaces virtue with faith as the criterion and so confines true friendship to fellow believers, is disputed, and it bears on how the patristic reworking of amicitia is described.

A development: faith names the good the friends share

This reading holds that Ambrose fills in the classical ideal rather than replacing it: friends united in God are united in the good the philosophers prized, now named at its root.

Held by: Carolinne White (Christian Friendship in the Fourth Century, 1992).2

White frames her Ambrose chapter as the question “Ciceronian or Christian friendship?” and argues that the best of classical friendship was “transformed and found a secure purpose in Christianity.”2 The case begins with Cicero’s own definition: friendship is “a complete accord on all subjects human and divine”; “friendship can only exist between good men”; and nature gives it “as the handmaid of virtue, not as a partner in guilt.”3 Right relation to the divine is already one of the terms on which the friends must agree; to specify that the accord includes the true God fills in a term Cicero left open.

Aristotle’s ground carries over in the same way. Perfect friendship holds between men “good, and alike in virtue,” who love each other “qua good,” each finding in the other “another self.”4 If God is the source of the good, friends united in the love of God are united in that same thing. The structure stays whole in Ambrose: like still loves like (“there can be no friendship between diverse characters”), the friend is still a second self, and candour and the ban on wrongdoing survive. On this reading the sentence ranks faith above friendship, as its first clause says (“faith may not be put aside for the sake of friendship”).

The later tradition builds on the same pattern. Aelred holds that spiritual friendship is desired “for its own natural worth… so that its fruit and reward is nothing but itself,” and that “he that abides in friendship, abides in God,” so the friend is loved for himself and not as a means.5 Aquinas calls charity “the friendship of man for God,” founded on God’s communicating his happiness to us, and holds that sinners are to be loved out of charity “in respect of their nature.”6 Cassian owes universal agape to all, even enemies, while preferential affection (diathesis) is “shown to but a few.”7 A supernatural storey is laid over the classical one, and the classical friendship of the good stands beneath it as a real good.

A change of criterion: faith replaces virtue as the ground of friendship

This reading holds that Ambrose’s sentence makes faith, not virtue, the criterion: true friendship is no longer possible between any two good people as such, but only among the faithful.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case reads the sentence as excluding any unbeliever. “He cannot be a friend to a man who has been unfaithful to God” denies friendship to the unbeliever however virtuous, and the “diverse characters” who cannot be friends are now divided by faith, not only by character.1 For Aristotle and Cicero the bond rested on virtue, which the good pagan could possess; a criterion that excludes him measures friendship by a different standard.4

Other Christian witnesses on the timeline state the same criterion. Augustine writes of his boyhood friend that “he was not then my friend, nor, indeed, afterwards, as true friendship is; for true it is not but in such as Thou bindest together.”8 In Aelred’s dialogue Ivo holds that “Cicero was ignorant of the virtue of true friendship, since he was completely ignorant of Christ,” and confesses “my conviction that true friendship cannot exist between those who live without Christ.”9 On this reading the classical friendship of two good men, complete in their own virtue, is not filled in but redefined: the bond that Aristotle and Cicero located in virtue is located in faith, and the friendship they counted possible with any good man is reserved to those who share it.
