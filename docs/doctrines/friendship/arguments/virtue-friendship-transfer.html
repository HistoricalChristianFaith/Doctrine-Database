Did classical virtue-friendship survive its Christian reworking, or was it superseded?

Disputed reading · on The Christian Theology of Friendship · witnesses: Ambrose of Milan, Aelred of Rievaulx

Related readings: Does Ambrose’s “no friend to one unfaithful to God” develop the classical ideal of friendship or change its criterion?

The passage

a complete accord on all subjects human and divine, joined with mutual goodwill and affection

— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia VI, trans. E. S. Shuckburgh (44 BC), and, twelve centuries later:

Is Cicero’s definition not an adequate beginning for you? “Friendship is agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity.”

— Aelred of Rievaulx, Spiritual Friendship, Book One 11, trans. Lawrence C. Braceland (c. 1160).1

The question

For Aristotle and Cicero true friendship rests on shared virtue, is possible only among the good, and makes the friend “another self” (heteros autos, alter idem); Ambrose, Augustine, Cassian and Aelred take this material over and add God as a party to the bond, alongside a command to love even the enemy. Whether the Christian reworking carries the classical ideal forward in a transformed form, or sets a different kind of love in its place, is disputed, and it governs how the whole timeline of Christian writing on friendship is described. Gilbert Meilaender (Friendship, 1981) grants the tensions between philia and agape; rejects their opposition.2

Christian love is a different love from the classical preferential bond

This reading holds that the love the gospel commands is a different kind of love from the friendship the classical writers praised, so that classical philia is judged and relativized rather than continued.

Held by: Søren Kierkegaard (Works of Love, 1847);3 Anders Nygren (Agape and Eros, 1930s; Eng. trans. 1953).4

The case begins from the love-command. Neighbour-love is commanded, owed to everyone as such, and reaches precisely those who return nothing: “if ye love them which love you, what thank have ye? for sinners also love those that love them”; “love your enemies”.5 Classical friendship is defined by the opposite features. Aristotle reserves the perfect kind for the good who love each other for their goodness, and holds that “one cannot be a friend to many people in the sense of having friendship of the perfect type with them”; it is answered love, among the deserving, confined to the few.6

Kierkegaard draws the consequence. Preferential love, erotic love and friendship alike, is “another form of self-love”: the beloved whom one loves as oneself is “the other I,” whereas the neighbour is “the first you.” The classical praise of the friend as a second self is, on this account, the praise of a love that needs neighbour-love beneath it as its foundation and its judge. Kierkegaard does not ask that the beloved cease to be loved; his claim is that the love the gospel commands is another love than the one the poets and philosophers celebrated.3

Nygren gives the thesis its historical form. Agape and eros, with philia in its train, are two “fundamental motifs”: agape descends from God, unmotivated by the worth of its object, and creates value in what it loves; eros ascends, drawn by a value already seen. Where the two meet in Christian history, “when the Eros motif invades Christianity, it endeavours to drive out and supplant the Agape motif.” On this reading the patristic synthesis of friendship and charity is a meeting of two distinct loves, and the classical bond that the fathers rewrote belongs to the ascending motif, not to the love the gospel names.4

The classical ideal carried over and transformed

This reading holds that the Christian writers took the classical ideal up whole, transformed it around God, and kept it as a genuine form of Christian love.

Held by: Carolinne White (Christian Friendship in the Fourth Century, 1992);7 E. D. H. (Liz) Carmichael (Friendship: Interpreting Christian Love, 2004).8

White states the thesis: “The best of classical friendship was transformed and found a secure purpose in Christianity,” and frames her Ambrose chapter as the question “Ciceronian or Christian friendship?”7 Carmichael traces the language of friendship as a model of agape from Aristotle and Cicero through Ambrose, Augustine, Aelred and Aquinas, and argues that philia has “deep roots” in Christian thought.8

The case rests first on form. The two Christian treatises on friendship are rewritings of the classical ones: Ambrose takes the title and three-book plan of Cicero’s De Officiis and closes his third book on friendship, reworking the Laelius; Aelred writes a three-book dialogue that begins from Cicero’s definition word for word and recalls a boyhood love of the Laelius.9 The apparatus travels with the form: the “one soul in two bodies” in Gregory of Nazianzus and Augustine, Ambrose’s “second self” and his “there can be no friendship between diverse characters,” the owed rebuke, the ban on wrongdoing for a friend’s sake, and the ranking above wealth, honour and power.10

The transformation is real on this reading, and it is located in the ground of the bond. Aelred’s “Christ is between us as a third” makes God a party to the friendship, and Augustine judges that he had “poured out my soul upon the dust, in loving one who must die as if he were never to die,” so that only a friend loved “in God” is secure.11 Aquinas names charity “the friendship of man for God,” founded on God’s communicating his happiness to us, and holds that sinners are to be loved out of charity “in respect of their nature.”12 The universality of charity is met by an ordering of the loves: Cassian owes agape to all, even enemies, and reserves affection (diathesis) for “but a few,” as Jesus loved the beloved disciple; Aelred keeps charity for all and friendship for the trusted few.13 The classical bond thus keeps its place inside a wider order of love, carried up and reordered around a new centre.
