Does classical virtue-friendship survive the Christian transformation, or is it superseded?

The claim

The best of classical friendship was transformed and found a secure purpose in Christianity.

— Carolinne White, Christian Friendship in the Fourth Century, link.1 This is the interpretive question that ties the whole timeline together. For Aristotle and Cicero, true friendship is possible only among the virtuous and rests on shared virtue — the friend is “another self” (heteros autos), “a second self” (alter idem).2 When the fathers baptize amicitia — Ambrose, Cassian, Augustine, Aelred — does that core thesis survive intact (friendship still grounded in virtue, now specified as Christ-directed virtue — the continuity reading), or is it fundamentally transformed and superseded, because the ground shifts from human virtue to grace, with God as a third term the pagans lacked, and the “only among the good” exclusivity is broken open by a charity owed to all, even the enemy (the discontinuity reading)? The interpretation weighed here is the continuity reading in its strongest, transformed-yet-continuous form.

Who advances it

The continuity reading is the mainstream of the scholarship. Carolinne White’s Christian Friendship in the Fourth Century frames the whole patristic development as the classical ideal “transformed” rather than discarded, titling its Ambrose chapter with the very question — “Ciceronian or Christian friendship?”1 E. D. H. (Liz) Carmichael’s Friendship: Interpreting Christian Love traces the language of friendship as a model of agape across Aristotle, Cicero, Ambrose, Augustine, Aelred, and Aquinas, arguing that philia has “deep roots” in Christian thought and belongs to it as a genuine mode of Christian love, not a pagan survival to be expelled.3 Behind both stands the plain testimony of the tradition itself: its two great treatises on friendship — Ambrose’s De Officiis and Aelred’s De Spirituali Amicitia — are self-conscious rewritings of the pagan classics, which is not how a tradition treats a doctrine it means to supersede. The rival, strong-discontinuity reading — that Christian love stands against the classical bond — is genuinely advanced (Nygren, Kierkegaard) and is weighed on its own page; here it is folded in as the defeater it is.

The evidence for

The transformed-yet-continuous reading rests on four observations, and it is strong.

The fathers rewrote the classics; they did not repudiate them. This is the nearly decisive structural fact. Ambrose’s De Officiis Ministrorum takes Cicero’s De Officiis — same title, same three-book plan — and ends, as Cicero had, on friendship, reworking the Laelius for a clergy. Aelred’s De Spirituali Amicitia is a three-book dialogue that keeps Cicero’s very definition of friendship (“agreement in things human and divine, with good will and charity”) and opens on a boy’s remembered love of Cicero.4 A tradition that means to supersede a doctrine does not adopt its opponent’s treatise as the template it fulfils. The gesture is fulfilment, not replacement.

The whole classical apparatus is carried over intact. The praise-words and the working rules all survive the transfer: the “second self” and the “one soul in two bodies” run unbroken from Aristotle and Cicero through Gregory of Nazianzus (“one soul, inhabiting two bodies”) and Augustine (“my soul and his soul were but one soul in two bodies”); like still loves like (Ambrose keeps “there can be no friendship between diverse characters”); candour and rebuke are still owed; nothing dishonourable may still be done for a friend’s sake; and friendship is still ranked above every external good.5 What changes is not the structure but the ground in which it is rooted — and even there the classical ground is deepened rather than razed, since if God is the highest good, friends united in the love of God are united in precisely the thing Aristotle prized, named at its root (argued at length under grounding friendship in faith rather than virtue).

Grace perfects nature; it does not abolish it. The tradition builds a supernatural storey above the natural one without demolishing it. Aquinas makes it explicit: charity (caritas) is itself “a certain friendship of man for God,” a friendship founded on the shared life God communicates — which leaves the ordinary natural friendship of the virtuous standing as a real, if lower, good.6 On this reading the Christian reworking adds to the classical ideal rather than knocking it down — development in the strict sense: the same thing unfolded and elevated, not a different thing put in its place.

The exclusivity is ordered, not abolished. The one point where discontinuity presses hardest is the “only among the good” limit, seemingly broken by the command to love the enemy. But the tradition’s answer is an ordering of the loves, not a supersession: John Cassian owes universal agape to all, even enemies, while reserving preferential affection (diathesis) for the few, as Jesus loved the beloved disciple; Aelred keeps charity for all and friendship for the trusted few.7 Universal charity is laid over preferential friendship, not against it — so the classical bond keeps its place inside a wider order of love.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. All four observations stand, and together they settle the master question against supersession. The deliberate rewriting of the classical treatises is by itself hard to reconcile with any reading on which the classical ideal is abolished; the survival of the whole apparatus confirms it; and the “grace perfects nature” and “ordered loves” moves answer the two seams — the ground and the exclusivity — where transformation is real. The strong-discontinuity thesis in its advocated form — Anders Nygren’s claim that agape and eros (with philia in its train) are two irreconcilable “fundamental motifs,” so that the classical love must be driven out — forces the evidence: the incarnate exemplar of agape kept particular friends, the gospel names friendship the highest love and offers it to the creature, and the two-motif scheme has been widely judged to misread the patristic synthesis as decline. That thesis is weighed and rated weak in the friendship-and-charity group; its failure is the negative half of this page’s positive result.8

What “survives intact” understates. The continuity reading’s own favourite word, intact, is where it can overreach, and honesty requires naming the genuine residue of transformation it tends to paper over. Two things really are new. First, a third term: Aristotle’s perfect friendship held between two self-sufficient virtuous men, complete in themselves; the Christian version makes both parties dependent on God as a party to the bond — Aelred’s “Christ between us as a third” — which is a genuine structural addition, not a mere relabelling. Second, Augustine does not merely deepen Cicero’s autonomous virtue-friendship; he corrects it, judging a love that rests in the perishable creature as its final good to be an idolatry that death exposes — “I had poured out my soul upon the dust, in loving one who must die as if he were never to die.”9 That is a verdict on the classical ideal as defective without the third term, not simply its fulfilment. And the exclusivity is not only ordered but genuinely broken open: Aristotle held perfect friendship impossible with many and impossible toward the base, whereas charity reaches precisely the enemy who returns nothing.10 None of this vindicates supersession — the bond, the apparatus, and the preferential love all survive — but it does mean the accurate description is continuity through transformation, not preservation unchanged. Where a proponent presses “survives intact” to mean “nothing essential changed,” that is an overstatement, and it is exactly the seam at which the narrower question — whether rerooting the ground in faith deepens or narrows the ideal — comes out only plausible, not sound.

Assessment

Sound. On its best available form — the transformed-yet-continuous reading — the classical ideal of virtue-friendship is taken up and elevated by the Christian tradition, not superseded: the fathers rewrote Cicero rather than repudiating him, carried the entire classical apparatus (the second self, candour, the ban on wrongdoing, the ranking above external goods, like-loves-like) over intact, deepened its ground from virtue-in-general to the God in whom virtue is grounded, and ordered its exclusivity inside a wider charity rather than abolishing it. The rival, strong-discontinuity reading that Christian love stands against the classical bond is weak on its own best case and is rated so elsewhere on this wiki. What keeps the verdict from a flat “the classical ideal survives unchanged” is a real residue: a genuinely new third term (God/grace as a party to the friendship) and a genuine breaking-open of the “only among the good” limit by enemy-love, together with Augustine’s treatment of the autonomous classical ideal as needing correction, not just completion. So the finding is continuity through transformation — the same thing carried up and reordered around a new centre — which is the mainstream reading and holds up; only the word “intact,” pressed to mean “unchanged,” overstates it.
