Aristotle on friendship

Dates: c. 340 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Aristotle (384–322 BC) is not a Christian witness; he stands at the head of the timeline as a classical source on friendship (philia) whom later writers on this timeline name. Books VIII and IX of the Nicomachean Ethics set out the three kinds of friendship (utility, pleasure, virtue), single out the “perfect friendship” of the good who love each other for their own sake as the true and lasting kind, call the friend “another self,” and hold friendship necessary to the good life.

Claims & evidence

Friendship is necessary to a good life — no one would choose to live without friends

For without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods

— Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1, trans. W. D. Ross, source. Aristotle opens his treatment of philia by ranking it among the indispensable goods: even the prosperous and powerful need friends, and friendship “holds states together.” It is not an ornament of the good life but a condition of it.

The three kinds of friendship, and the “perfect” friendship of the good

Perfect friendship is the friendship of men who are good, and alike in virtue; for these wish well alike to each other qua good, and they are good themselves.

— Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3, trans. W. D. Ross, source. Aristotle distinguishes friendships by what is loved in the friend. Those grounded in utility or pleasure are friendships only incidentally — “those who love each other for their utility do not love each other for themselves but in virtue of some good which they get from each other” — and dissolve when the use or the pleasure ends. Only the third kind, grounded in the friend’s character, loves the friend for his own sake, and only it is stable, rare, and complete.

The friend as “another self”

as the virtuous man is to himself, he is to his friend also (for his friend is another self)

— Nicomachean Ethics IX.9, trans. W. D. Ross, source. The good man’s relation to his friend mirrors his settled relation to himself: he wishes his friend well for the friend’s own sake, shares his griefs and joys, and finds in him heteros autos, “another self.” Cicero renders the idea in Latin as alter idem, “a second self” (see Cicero). The phrase lets Aristotle argue that even the happy and self-sufficient man still needs friends.

Placement on the timeline

  • In Aristotle’s account philia binds equals in virtue, is fragile before betrayal, and reserves its highest form for the few good men. God as a party to friendship, the command to love the enemy, and the universality of charity do not appear in it; they enter with the Christian writers later on this timeline.
  • Cicero’s Laelius de Amicitia treats the same themes in Latin; Ambrose and Aelred rework Cicero’s treatise (see their pages). C. S. Lewis names both Aristotle and Cicero as his forebears (see his page).
