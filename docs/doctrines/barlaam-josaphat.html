Barlaam and Josaphat

Two figures kept in the calendars of both the Latin and the Greek churches — the hermit Barlaam and the Indian prince Josaphat, who renounced a throne for the ascetic life — are the heroes of a Christian romance that Édouard Laboulaye (1859), Felix Liebrecht (1860), and the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia identified as a Christianized version of the life of the Buddha, and whose hero’s name they and later writers derive from bodhisattva. The story travelled for a thousand years across languages and religions — through Sanskrit, Middle Persian, Arabic, Georgian, Greek, and Latin — until its two figures were commemorated as saints in the medieval West and East. This page is a history of a legend and of its reception, set out earliest source first: the Buddhist lives of the Buddha, the earliest extant Christian notices of the Buddha, the Manichaean and Arabic carriers, the Georgian book in which the tale is a Christian saint’s life, the Greek and Latin versions that spread it, the saints’-lives collections and calendars that entered its heroes among the saints, and the nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship that traced the chain back to India. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source, not by a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The Sanskrit life of the Buddha (1st–3rd c.)

At the head of the chain stand the Sanskrit lives of the Buddha — Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita (early 2nd c.) and the fuller legendary Lalitavistara (compiled c. 1st–3rd c.). They narrate the sheltered prince Siddhārtha, kept from all sight of suffering, who is confronted on his rare excursions by the “four sights” — an old man broken by age, a sick man, a corpse, and a serene wandering ascetic — and, awakened to the reality of death and the possibility of release, abandons palace and kingdom for the religious life. The same sequence — the cloistered prince, the encounters, the Great Renunciation — reappears a millennium later, half a world away, in the story of the prince Josaphat (see what the parallel shows).1

The earliest extant Christian notices of the Buddha: Clement and Jerome (c. 200 & c. 393)

Long before the legend reached them, Christian writers of the Greek and Latin world already knew that a teacher called the Buddha was worshipped in India. Clement of Alexandria, listing the “barbarian philosophers,” records that “some, too, of the Indians obey the precepts of Buddha, whom, on account of his extraordinary sanctity, they have raised to divine honours.”2 Two centuries later Jerome, arguing for virginity against Jovinian, reports the Indian tradition that “Budda, the founder of their religion, had his birth through the side of a virgin” — a report of the virgin-birth motif of the Buddha-legend in a Latin writer, centuries before the legend appears in Christian dress.3

The Manichaean bridge and the Arabic Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf (3rd–8th c.)

Jes P. Asmussen traces the road from the Buddha to the saint through the Manichaeans (how far the surviving fragments document that stage is disputed). Fragments of the tale survive in Manichaean texts from Turfan in Uighur, Parthian, and Persian — evidence, Asmussen concludes in the Encyclopædia Iranica, that “it was the Manicheans who transmitted this Indian tale to the West” — and Asmussen places the Middle Persian form Bōdisav of the Buddha’s title bodhisattva among them (de Blois treats that form as reconstructed). On his account, from the Manichaean Middle Persian the story passed into Arabic as the Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf (the Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf), in circulation in ʿAbbasid Baghdad by the eighth century and connected by the bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm with the translation circle of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ; a version also entered Shīʿite devotional literature. The Manichaeans’ founder Mani (d. 274/277), in the Šābuhragān he addressed to the Persian king Shapur I, presented himself as the last in a line of messengers in which the Buddha came first — wisdom “brought by the messenger, called Buddha, to India, in another by Zarādusht to Persia, in another by Jesus to the West,” and now, in the last age, through Mani in Babylonia. It is the same sect that held the young Augustine as a hearer for nine years before his conversion.4 In the Arabic transmission, on Asmussen’s account, a slip of the pen occurs: the initial b of Būdhāsaf (ـبـ) is miscopied as y (ـيـ), giving Yūdāsaf — the ancestor of the Georgian Iodasaph, the Greek Ioasaph, and the Latin Josaphat.5

The Georgian Balavariani: the tale as a Christian saint’s life (9th–10th c.; date disputed)

In the Georgian Balavariani the Indian prince Iodasaph is not only awakened to the vanity of the world but converted to Christianity by the ascetic Balahvar, and the whole is cast as a Christian saint’s life. David Marshall Lang, who translated the long recension (edited by Ilia Abuladze from a unique Jerusalem manuscript, Jer. 140), argued that the Georgian stage is the hinge on which the Buddhist tale turned Christian; Korneli Kekelidze posited an earlier, lost Syriac Christian version. The Georgian’s date is disputed: Lang, Kekelidze, and Abuladze independently placed it in the ninth or tenth century, and Shalva Nutsubidze (1956) argued for a seventh-century original by John Moschus.6

The Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph (attributed dates from c. 600 to before 1028)

The Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph is the version from which every later European retelling descends. Most Greek manuscripts credit it only to “John the Monk” of St Sabas, and its authorship is disputed. The Georgian Life of John and Euthymius (c. 1045) lists the Balahvar romance among the translations from Georgian into Greek of Euthymius the Athonite (Euthymius the Iberian), the Georgian monk of the Iviron monastery on Mount Athos who died in 1028, and Lang (1955) and Robert Volk’s critical edition (2006–2009), which classes the work among the spuria of John of Damascus, attribute it to him. The Roman Martyrology of Baronius and the standard editions long credited it to John of Damascus, an attribution Franz Dölger defended in 1953; Hermann Zotenberg (1886) assigned it to a Sabaite monk John of the early seventh century.7

The Latin West and the saints'-lives collections (1048 → c. 1260)

A Latin translation made in 1048 opened the legend to the Latin West, and every later European version derives, on N. H. Levine’s account, from either the Greek or that Latin. In the thirteenth century the tale entered the great reference-works of Christendom: Vincent of Beauvais gave it in full in his Speculum Historiale (c. 1250s), and Jacobus de Voragine included it, abridged, among the saints’ lives of the Legenda Aurea (Golden Legend, c. 1260) — the collection that made Barlaam and Josaphat a standard Western saint’s life, read across Europe. From there the story spread into the vernaculars — Rudolf von Ems’s Middle High German verse romance, the Old French poems of Gui de Cambrai and Chardri, an Old Norse Barlaams saga made for the Norwegian court, and versions in Middle English, Swedish, and Icelandic; its embedded parable of the three caskets reached Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice through Caxton’s English Golden Legend.8

Other branches of the legend: Hebrew, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Syriac (13th c. onward)

The westward Christian road — Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin → the European vernaculars — was not the tale’s only route. From the same Arabic Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf the story forked into at least three other branches. It entered Judaism directly: Abraham ibn Ḥisdai of Barcelona rendered the Arabic into the Hebrew Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir (“The Prince and the Hermit”) in the early thirteenth century, recasting the prince’s renunciation in a Jewish philosophical frame, on a road of its own apart from the Christian Greek line. A separate Christian Arabic recension was carried south into Ge’ez as the Ethiopic Baralām and Yěwāsěf. And off the Greek grew a further Christian sub-branch: F. C. Conybeare argued that the old Armenian version is an abridgement of a lost Syriac rendering of the Greek. The legend thus forked into another faith, down another continent, and along a second Christian road east.9

The calendars: veneration as saints (medieval)

Both came to be commemorated as saints by inclusion in the calendars. They stand in the Roman Martyrology on 27 November and in the Greek calendar on 26 August; the Russian and other Slavic churches keep “Ioasaph, prince of India, and Abenner the King, his father” on 19 November. Whether this standing amounts to canonization is disputed. The 1911 Britannica wrote that “thus unwittingly Gautama the Buddha has come to official recognition as a saint in two great branches of the Catholic Church.”10

The Buddhist origin recovered (1859–1860) and modern scholarship

The 1911 Britannica credits the historian of Portuguese India Diogo do Couto (1542–1616) with recognizing the identity of the stories of the Buddha and Josaphat, and gives the honour “in modern times” to the nineteenth-century scholars Édouard Laboulaye (1859) and Felix Liebrecht, “in 1860 putting it beyond dispute.” In 1913 the Catholic Encyclopedia stated: the story “is a Christianized version of one of the legends of Buddha, as even the name Josaphat would seem to show,” a corruption of Joasaph, itself from the Middle Persian Budasif (= Bodhisattva). David Marshall Lang (1966) set out the language-by-language route — Sanskrit → Manichaean Middle Persian → Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin — with earlier work by F. C. Conybeare (1896) and Ilia Abuladze’s edition of the Georgian; the Manichaean stage is disputed. Wilfred Cantwell Smith later took the legend as a case study in cross-traditional history.11

Disputed readings

Questions about how the sources for the legend, its names, and its saints have been read.

The transmission

Two questions about the road the legend travelled from Sanskrit through Middle Persian and Arabic to Georgian, Greek, and Latin: what lies behind the saints’ names, and what stood between the Indian source and the Arabic book.

  • From what do the names Josaphat and Barlaam derive? — turns on the Arabic Būdhāsaf/Yūdhāsaf and the origin of Bilawhar.
  • Did the tale reach the Arabic book through a Manichaean intermediary? — turns on the Turfan fragments and their date.

Further questions concern the plot, the dates and authorship of the Georgian and Greek versions, and the saints’ standing in the calendars:

  • What does the parallel between Josaphat’s story and the Buddha’s life show?
  • Who wrote the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph? — Euthymius the Athonite, John of Damascus, or an earlier Sabaite monk.
  • When was the Georgian Balavariani composed? — the seventh century or the ninth to tenth.
  • Were Barlaam and Josaphat canonized?
