Lex Orandi, Lex Credendi: The Law of Prayer and the Law of Belief

Lex orandi, lex credendi (“the law of praying, the law of believing”) is the shorthand for the idea that the Church’s worship is a rule for its faith: what the Church prays tells you what the Church believes. The words are a compression of one clause in a fifth-century anti-“semi-Pelagian” dossier, most probably compiled by Prosper of Aquitaine: ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, “so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” There the “law” was the intercession for all people that 1 Timothy 2:1 enjoins and that the whole Church obeys. The intercessions ask God to give faith to unbelievers, so the beginning of faith is grace.1 The argument from prayer is older than the formula. Cyprian had called the Lord’s Prayer the Christian’s law of prayer, and Augustine had written that “prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace.” In the East, Athanasius and Basil argued from the baptismal formula to the Spirit’s deity.2

This page is a reception-history timeline. The “belief that developed” is the adage itself: where it came from, how each era used it, and which way it runs. Its classic modern uses are Catholic dogmatic definitions that appealed to the Church’s feasts and prayers: the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950). Its most-discussed twist is a reversal. Pius XII’s Mediator Dei (1947) wrote that, taken absolutely and generally, “let the law of belief determine the law of prayer,” and later Roman documents repeat that inverted sense.3 Witnesses are set out earliest first, from the second- and third-century arguments from the rites, through the fourth-century arguments from baptism and doxology for the Trinity and the fifth-century formula, through its medieval career as a papal decree, to the modern scholarship that recovered its original sense. Contested questions are weighed on argument pages rather than settled in the timeline: who wrote the text, what Prosper meant by lex, which way the adage runs, and whether the rule was already at work when the first Christians began to worship Jesus.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons: the Eucharist confirms the faith (c. 180)

The earliest argument in this survey from the Church’s worship to its faith is Irenaeus’s, against Gnostics who despised matter. They too offered bread and cup as the Lord’s body and blood. Irenaeus answers that their own rite contradicts their doctrine. If the Father is not the Creator, offering Him bread is offering Him someone else’s property.4 And the flesh fed with the Lord’s body cannot simply perish: “Let them, therefore, either alter their opinion, or cease from offering … But our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion.”5 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the sentence to call the Eucharist “the sum and summary of our faith.”6 The rite here is the eucharistic offering, not the prayers of petition, and the sentence runs both ways: belief agrees with (consonans) the Eucharist, and the Eucharist confirms (confirmat) belief. Its force depends on the heretics’ sharing the rite.7

Tertullian: custom as law, and the rites as evidence (c. 207–213)

In The Chaplet (208–212), Tertullian lists customs that no Scripture commands: the renunciation and triple immersion at baptism, the anniversary offerings for the dead, the sign of the cross. “Tradition will be held forth to you as the originator of them, custom as their strengthener, and faith as their observer,” and “even in civil matters custom is accepted as law.” But there he argues from practice to practice, to defend a Christian soldier’s refusal of the laurel crown.8 Elsewhere he argues from the rites to doctrine. Against the modalist Praxeas (c. 213), Christians are immersed “not once only, but three times … at each several mention of Their names,” so there are three persons.9 The flesh is washed, anointed, signed and fed on Christ’s body and blood, so body and soul “cannot then be separated in their recompense.”10 And Marcion’s god still uses the Creator’s water, oil and bread in his sacraments.11 Yet custom answers to truth. “Our Lord Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom,” and the rule of faith, “this law of faith,” is “immoveable and irreformable,” while discipline admits correction.12

Cyprian of Carthage: the “law of prayer” and the baptismal question (252–256)

Cyprian calls the Lord’s Prayer a law. It is “public and common,” and “this law of prayer” (hanc orationis legem) the three youths kept in the furnace. From the law of the prayer each Christian learns “what he ought to be.”13 He reads “Thy will be done … in earth” as a prayer for “those who believe not,” a reading Augustine would later use.14 In the dispute over baptism by heretics (255–256) he argued from the rite to doctrine: “the very interrogation which is put in baptism is a witness of the truth.” Candidates are asked whether they believe in “remission of sins through the holy Church,” so sins are forgiven only in the Church, and schismatics who ask it “lie in their interrogatory.” There is not “one law of the Creed” (symboli lex) for them and for the Church.15 But custom alone did not bind him. Against Pope Stephen’s ruling, “let nothing be innovated … which has not been handed down,” Cyprian answered that “custom without truth is the antiquity of error.”16 The Council of Arles (314) ruled against the African practice by the rite as well: a convert from heresy was to be asked the creed, and one baptized in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit received only the laying on of hands.17

Athanasius of Alexandria: baptized “into Creator and creature” (c. 340–361)

In the fourth century the argument from the rites was turned to the Trinity. Athanasius asked the Arians why, if the Son is a creature, “a thing made [is] classed with the Maker in the consecration of all of us.” They “do not baptize into Father and Son, but into Creator and creature.”18 Yet the words alone could not settle the question, since the Arians used them too. A baptism needs, “with the Name, … also the right faith,” and Christ said “Teach” before “Baptize.”19 He argued from worship as well: “creature does not worship creature,” so the Son, who is worshipped, is no creature.20 In the Letters to Serapion (c. 359–361) he carried the baptismal argument over to the Spirit. If the Spirit were a creature, the “rite of initiation” of those who said so would be “not entirely into the Godhead.”21

Basil of Caesarea: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief” (373–375)

Basil’s On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) began with a prayer. He had led the people in a doxology to the Father “with the Son together with the Holy Ghost” and was accused of innovation.22 His answer set out an order: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.” The confession of faith is “the origin and parent of the doxology,” and the objectors must teach “either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed.”23 So for Basil the prayer follows the creed, and the creed follows the baptism Christ commanded. He also defended the doxology from unwritten apostolic tradition. Much of the rite has no written source: the sign of the cross, turning east to pray, the words of the eucharistic invocation, triple immersion, even the baptismal confession itself.24 And he cited the old hymn at the lighting of the lamps, in which the people sang, “We praise Father, Son, and God’s Holy Spirit.”25

Gregory of Nazianzus: “How can He deify me by Baptism?” (380–381)

At Constantinople Gregory of Nazianzus met an argument from prayer on the other side: “who in ancient or modern times ever worshipped the Spirit? Who ever prayed to Him?” He replied that Christians worship and pray in the Spirit, and that “the adoration of One is the adoration of the Three.”26 His own argument ran from the rite: “if He is not to be worshipped, how can He deify me by Baptism? But if He is to be worshipped, surely He is an Object of adoration, and if an Object of adoration He must be God.”27 To candidates for baptism in January 381 he said that if he “worshipped a creature, or were baptized into a creature,” he “should not be made divine.”28 Later that year the Council of Constantinople confessed the Spirit as the one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.”29

Augustine of Hippo: the argument from the Church’s prayer (412–429)

The argument Prosper would later compress into a formula is Augustine’s. In 414 he read the four kinds of prayer in 1 Timothy 2:1 as the parts of the eucharistic liturgy, which the whole Church offers “for all men,” persecutors included.30 In 416 he and four other African bishops wrote to Pope Innocent I against Pelagius that “prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace”: whoever prays “lead us not into temptation” asks God for the power to keep the commandment.31 About 427 he answered Vitalis of Carthage, who held that the first step of faith is ours. The priest at the altar bids the people pray that God convert unbelievers, give catechumens the desire for baptism, and keep the faithful to the end. Vitalis must either answer “Amen” or mock the Church’s prayers, for “God does what He commands that He be asked to do.”32 In The Gift of Perseverance (428/429), written for Prosper and Hilary, he made it a principle. The Church has “been born and grows” in these prayers, and so in this faith. If it has always prayed that unbelievers might believe, it has always believed faith to be God’s gift.33 He argued the same way from the baptismal rite. Infants are exorcised and breathed upon (exsufflation) to drive out the devil, so they must be under his power through original sin.34 Prosper’s chapters 8 and 9 restate these two arguments in the same order. What Augustine never wrote is the formula, with its legal word lex.

Prosper of Aquitaine, the Capitula, ch. 8 (c. 435–442)

In Rome, Prosper of Aquitaine, Augustine’s champion against the monks of Marseilles, compiled a short dossier of rulings on grace: the Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei, also called the Indiculus or the “Capitula Caelestini.” His opponents said they would follow only what the Apostolic See had taught. So chapters 1–6 excerpt Popes Innocent I and Zosimus, chapter 7 the Council of Carthage of 418, and the last two chapters argue from the liturgy.35 Chapter 8 turns from the papal decrees to “the sacred rites of the priestly supplications, which, handed down by the apostles, are celebrated uniformly throughout the whole world and in every catholic Church, ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi”: so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing. It then lists what the bishops ask, with the whole Church groaning with them. They pray that faith be given to unbelievers, that idolaters be freed from their errors, that the veil be taken from the hearts of the Jews, that heretics and schismatics return, that the lapsed receive penance, and that catechumens be brought to the font. God’s answering of these prayers shows that conversion is wholly His work. Chapter 9 argues the same way from the exorcisms and exsufflations every candidate for baptism undergoes, infant or adult.36 Prosper’s wording is lex supplicandi, not orandi. His verb statuat is strong: it means to fix or establish, not merely to express. The same argument, without the formula, fills The Call of All Nations I.12 (c. 450), where Paul’s command to pray for all is the lex supplicationis that every priest and believer keeps.37 The text travelled in Dionysius Exiguus’s collection of papal decretals (c. 500), appended to Pope Celestine I’s letter of 431 to the Gallic bishops under the heading “Decreta Coelestini papae.” That is why the adage was long cited as Celestine’s (see the authorship argument).38 Since Marc Cappuyns’s study of 1929 it has been assigned to Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death. Cappuyns dated it 435–442. Alexander Hwang has argued for 450–455.39

Quoted as Celestine’s: the Scythian monks (c. 520)

The earliest known quotation of chapter 8 already treats it as a pope’s. About 520 a group of Scythian monks in Rome, led by Peter the Deacon, wrote to Fulgentius of Ruspe and the other African bishops exiled in Sardinia, setting out their faith on the incarnation and on grace. To show that every good movement of the will comes from God, they first appeal to a prayer: Basil’s anaphora, “which almost the whole East uses,” asks God to make the wicked good. Then they cite Pope Innocent and the African councils, and then “the most blessed Celestine in his letter to the Gauls.” That is chapter 8’s list of the bishops’ intercessions, followed by its conclusion that the answered prayer shows conversion to be God’s work. They begin their quotation at “when the leaders of the holy peoples,” so the clause about the law of supplicating is not included.40 By then the dossier was read in Rome as part of Celestine’s letter. The attribution is weighed on its own argument page.

Hincmar of Reims: the law of prayer against Gottschalk (859–860)

The first writer found in this survey who puts the clause itself to work is Hincmar, archbishop of Reims. In his treatise on predestination for Charles the Bald (859–860), written against the “twofold” predestination of Gottschalk of Orbais, he quotes “let the law of supplicating establish the law of believing” eight times, always as a decree of Pope Celestine.41 He turns it on a new question. The law of supplicating is the Church’s prayer on the day of the crucifixion “for all men,” for every order and rank, “for schismatics and heretics, for Jews and pagans, so that no one may be thought excepted.” So “since supplication is made to God by the whole catholic Church for all, we believe that God wills all men to be saved.”42 He also widens what counts as the law of supplicating. Roman Mass prayers that call Christ’s sacrifice the taking away of “the offence of the whole world” prove that He died for all, and the Easter blessing of the font proves that the redemption reaches every baptized person. After each he adds Celestine’s rule.43

The Capitula in the canon-law collections (c. 1010–1095)

The chapters went on circulating as papal law. Burchard of Worms’s Decretum (1007–1015) and Ivo of Chartres’s Decretum (1090s) copy several of them as “the decrees of Pope Celestine.” The chapters on grace stand among the dogmatic canons, and chapter 9 among the canons on baptism, under the rubric “That all who are to be baptized must first be exorcized.”44 There the argument from the rite has become a rule for the rite. Chapter 8, with its clause on the law of supplicating, is not among the canons of Ivo’s Decretum.45 It travelled another way as well. A longer form of the De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus, a fifth-century handbook of doctrine by Gennadius of Marseilles that was long printed among Augustine’s works, took over chapters of the Capitula word for word. In Migne’s text chapter 8, clause and all, is its chapter 30.46

Bernard of Clairvaux: a feast refused (c. 1140)

When the cathedral of Lyons adopted a feast of Mary’s conception, Bernard wrote to rebuke its canons, and his letter argues from worship in both directions. The feasts the Church had received taught him what to believe: “These things the Church sings concerning her, and has taught me to repeat … what I have not received from the Church I confess I should with great difficulty admit.” From the feast of Mary’s birth he learned that she was holy before she was born.47 But the new feast was “a rite which the Church knows nothing of, and which reason does not prove, nor ancient tradition hand down to us.” The holiness of the conception it implied was in his judgement false, it was “a presumptuous novelty against the custom of the Church,” and “the authority of the Apostolic See ought first to have been consulted.”48 A received feast teaches; a new one must first be justified by the faith.

Thomas Aquinas: the custom of the Church (c. 1270)

Aquinas still cites the Capitula as Pope Celestine’s. From chapter 9, on the exorcisms before baptism, he concludes that the unbaptized are under the devil’s power through original sin.49 He argues from the feast of Mary’s birth as Bernard had: “the Church does not celebrate feasts except of those who are holy,” so she was sanctified in the womb. But the feast of her conception, which Rome “tolerates” in some churches, “does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception.”50 “The custom of the Church has very great authority,” more than “an Augustine or a Jerome,” because “the very doctrine of catholic doctors derives its authority from the Church.” In the rites of the Mass “the Church cannot err, since she is taught by the Holy Ghost.”51 For Aquinas the authority belongs to the Church’s received practice, not to any rite as such.

The slogan takes shape (by 1834)

The two-member slogan lex orandi, lex credendi is not Prosper’s wording. The earliest instance found in this survey is the English Catholic writer Kenelm Digby’s Mores Catholici (book V, 1834). Digby argued that the liturgy must be uniform and unchangeable “for the reason assigned by St. Augustin: ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi;’ for the prayers which the Church uses in the administration of her holy rites, are so many proofs of the respective doctrines on which they depend.” The slogan had already detached from its author, since Digby credits it to Augustine.52 A Latin treatise on the Church in Migne’s Theologiae cursus completus (vol. 4, c. 1837) calls it an axioma and lists the standard proofs drawn from it. The exorcisms before baptism prove original sin. The Gloria Patri closing every psalm proves the Trinity. The outward adoration of the Eucharist proves the real presence, all the Church’s prayers prove the necessity of grace, and the ancient prayers for the dead prove purgatory. The same treatise warns that it would be a grave error to apply the maxim to every breviary, every legend of a saint, or every prescribed prayer.53 By 1870 John Henry Newman could invoke it in passing. In the Grammar of Assent he notes that the Church’s dogmatic definitions of the Trinity never call it a “mystery,” “as if fulfilling the maxim, ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi.’”54 None of these writers treats the slogan as new, so it is probably older than 1834. Its earlier history has not yet been traced here.

George Tyrrell, Lex Orandi (1903)

The Irish Jesuit George Tyrrell, soon to be the best-known English-speaking Catholic Modernist, made the adage the title of a book: Lex Orandi; or, Prayer and Creed (1903), followed by Lex Credendi: A Sequel to Lex Orandi (1906). He denied that the maxim means “every popular devotion rests on a sound dogmatic basis,” or that “the most widely venerated relic is ipso facto genuine.” It refers to “the prayer and the belief of the universal Church,” and “prayer” there means the whole life of charity. On that basis he set out to show how “the Lex Credendi or rule of faith has been determined and must be interpreted by the Lex Orandi or rule of prayer.”55 Later commentators read Pius XII’s reversal of the adage in 1947 as aimed partly at this Modernist reading. For Tyrrell, the lex orandi was the changing religious consciousness of an age, and it prescribed the lex credendi.56

Modern scholarship recovers the original sense (1929 onward)

Twentieth-century scholarship reread the adage in its fifth-century setting. Marc Cappuyns (1929) settled the authorship on Prosper. Karl Federer’s Liturgie und Glaube (1950) gave the closest reading of the parallel passage in The Call of All Nations.57 Building on Federer, Paul De Clerck argued in 1978, and again in 2000, that Prosper’s lex is first of all the apostolic precept of 1 Timothy 2:1, not “the liturgy” in general. The Church obeys that precept so universally that “the biblical law has become a liturgical law.” On this reading, what settles the doctrinal question is three levels together: the precept, the Church’s universal obedience to it, and the wording of the prayers. No isolated liturgical text does it alone. De Clerck called the casual citation of any prayer, preface or hymn as “the lex orandi” a banalization of the adage. He also criticized its reversal and urged that the principle be applied only through comparative liturgy, across all the rites.58 O. T. I. Wright (2024) objects that Prosper’s “law” is no mere recommendation promoted to a law. He also argues that the narrow reading wrongly leaves out chapter 9’s argument from the baptismal rites.59

Arguments

Contested questions about the adage, each weighed adversarially on its own page.

Related arguments — the worship of Jesus

The adage is sometimes carried back to the origin of Christianity itself: the first Christians worshipped Jesus before the Church had any doctrine of his divinity, and the doctrine followed the worship. Two rival readings of the first-century evidence are weighed separately.

Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity? — plausible

Prayer, hymns and invocation addressed to Jesus go back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers (marana tha, 1 Cor 16:22). They came long before any defined doctrine of his divinity, and from c. 230 writers argued from the Church’s hymns to Christ’s deity. But the worship rested on the belief that God had raised and exalted Jesus, so it came before formulated doctrine, not before belief. It also left open which doctrine of the Son would follow.

Did the first Christians worship God through Jesus, rather than Jesus himself as God? — plausible

Dunn’s reading rightly notes that most early prayer runs to God through Jesus, that cultic service is kept for God, and that Origen still forbade prayer to the Son. But direct invocation, hymns, doxologies and the worship of the Lamb in Revelation 5 tell against its strong form. What that devotion implied is a judgement on which historians divide. Most lean toward Hurtado and Bauckham.

Other arguments

  • Did Pope Celestine I write the “Capitula Caelestini”? — weak (the attribution goes back to c. 500, when the dossier was copied as part of Celestine’s letter of 431. But the compiler speaks of Rome’s popes as someone else’s, the letter never mentions an appendix, and Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death, knows of no Celestinian ruling on the beginning of faith. The dossier’s Roman reception from c. 500 is real, but it does not make chapter 8’s argument from prayer a papal decree.)
