Lex Orandi, Lex Credendi: The Law of Prayer and the Law of Belief

Lex orandi, lex credendi (“the law of praying, the law of believing”) is the shorthand for the idea that the Church’s worship is a rule for its faith: what the Church prays tells you what the Church believes. The words are a compression of one clause in a fifth-century anti-“semi-Pelagian” dossier, most probably compiled by Prosper of Aquitaine: ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, “so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” There the “law” was the intercession for all people that 1 Timothy 2:1 enjoins and that the whole Church obeys. The intercessions ask God to give faith to unbelievers, so the beginning of faith is grace.1 The argument from prayer is older than the formula. Cyprian had called the Lord’s Prayer the Christian’s law of prayer, and Augustine had written that “prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace.” In the East, Athanasius and Basil argued from the baptismal formula to the Spirit’s deity.2

This page is a reception-history timeline. The “belief that developed” is the adage itself: where it came from, how each era used it, and which way it runs. Its classic modern uses are Catholic dogmatic definitions that appealed to the Church’s feasts and prayers: the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950). Its most-discussed twist is a reversal. Pius XII’s Mediator Dei (1947) wrote that, taken absolutely and generally, “let the law of belief determine the law of prayer,” and later Roman documents repeat that inverted sense.3 Witnesses are set out earliest first, from the fifth-century formula to the modern scholarship that recovered its original sense. Contested questions are weighed on argument pages rather than settled in the timeline: who wrote the text, what Prosper meant by lex, and which way the adage runs.

Timeline

Prosper of Aquitaine, the Capitula, ch. 8 (c. 435–442)

In Rome, Prosper of Aquitaine, Augustine’s champion against the monks of Marseilles, compiled a short dossier of rulings on grace: the Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei, also called the Indiculus or the “Capitula Caelestini.” His opponents said they would follow only what the Apostolic See had taught. So chapters 1–6 excerpt Popes Innocent I and Zosimus, chapter 7 the Council of Carthage of 418, and the last two chapters argue from the liturgy.4 Chapter 8 turns from the papal decrees to “the sacred rites of the priestly supplications, which, handed down by the apostles, are celebrated uniformly throughout the whole world and in every catholic Church, ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi”: so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing. It then lists what the bishops ask, with the whole Church groaning with them. They pray that faith be given to unbelievers, that idolaters be freed from their errors, that the veil be taken from the hearts of the Jews, that heretics and schismatics return, that the lapsed receive penance, and that catechumens be brought to the font. God’s answering of these prayers shows that conversion is wholly His work. Chapter 9 argues the same way from the exorcisms and exsufflations every candidate for baptism undergoes, infant or adult.5 Prosper’s wording is lex supplicandi, not orandi. His verb statuat is strong: it means to fix or establish, not merely to express. The same argument, without the formula, fills The Call of All Nations I.12 (c. 450), where Paul’s command to pray for all is the lex supplicationis that every priest and believer keeps.6 The text travelled in Dionysius Exiguus’s collection of papal decretals (c. 500), appended to Pope Celestine I’s letter of 431 to the Gallic bishops under the heading “Decreta Coelestini papae.” That is why the adage was long cited as Celestine’s (see the authorship argument).7 Since Marc Cappuyns’s study of 1929 it has been assigned to Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death. Cappuyns dated it 435–442. Alexander Hwang has argued for 450–455.8

Quoted as Celestine’s: the Scythian monks (c. 520)

The earliest known quotation of chapter 8 already treats it as a pope’s. About 520 a group of Scythian monks in Rome, led by Peter the Deacon, wrote to Fulgentius of Ruspe and the other African bishops exiled in Sardinia, setting out their faith on the incarnation and on grace. To show that every good movement of the will comes from God, they first appeal to a prayer: Basil’s anaphora, “which almost the whole East uses,” asks God to make the wicked good. Then they cite Pope Innocent and the African councils, and then “the most blessed Celestine in his letter to the Gauls.” That is chapter 8’s list of the bishops’ intercessions, followed by its conclusion that the answered prayer shows conversion to be God’s work. They begin their quotation at “when the leaders of the holy peoples,” so the clause about the law of supplicating is not included.9 By then the dossier was read in Rome as part of Celestine’s letter. The attribution is weighed on its own argument page.

The slogan takes shape (by 1834)

The two-member slogan lex orandi, lex credendi is not Prosper’s wording. The earliest instance found in this survey is the English Catholic writer Kenelm Digby’s Mores Catholici (book V, 1834). Digby argued that the liturgy must be uniform and unchangeable “for the reason assigned by St. Augustin: ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi;’ for the prayers which the Church uses in the administration of her holy rites, are so many proofs of the respective doctrines on which they depend.” The slogan had already detached from its author, since Digby credits it to Augustine.10 A Latin treatise on the Church in Migne’s Theologiae cursus completus (vol. 4, c. 1837) calls it an axioma and lists the standard proofs drawn from it. The exorcisms before baptism prove original sin. The Gloria Patri closing every psalm proves the Trinity. The outward adoration of the Eucharist proves the real presence, all the Church’s prayers prove the necessity of grace, and the ancient prayers for the dead prove purgatory. The same treatise warns that it would be a grave error to apply the maxim to every breviary, every legend of a saint, or every prescribed prayer.11 By 1870 John Henry Newman could invoke it in passing. In the Grammar of Assent he notes that the Church’s dogmatic definitions of the Trinity never call it a “mystery,” “as if fulfilling the maxim, ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi.’”12 None of these writers treats the slogan as new, so it is probably older than 1834. Its earlier history has not yet been traced here.

George Tyrrell, Lex Orandi (1903)

The Irish Jesuit George Tyrrell, soon to be the best-known English-speaking Catholic Modernist, made the adage the title of a book: Lex Orandi; or, Prayer and Creed (1903), followed by Lex Credendi: A Sequel to Lex Orandi (1906). He denied that the maxim means “every popular devotion rests on a sound dogmatic basis,” or that “the most widely venerated relic is ipso facto genuine.” It refers to “the prayer and the belief of the universal Church,” and “prayer” there means the whole life of charity. On that basis he set out to show how “the Lex Credendi or rule of faith has been determined and must be interpreted by the Lex Orandi or rule of prayer.”13 Later commentators read Pius XII’s reversal of the adage in 1947 as aimed partly at this Modernist reading. For Tyrrell, the lex orandi was the changing religious consciousness of an age, and it prescribed the lex credendi.14

Modern scholarship recovers the original sense (1929 onward)

Twentieth-century scholarship reread the adage in its fifth-century setting. Marc Cappuyns (1929) settled the authorship on Prosper. Karl Federer’s Liturgie und Glaube (1950) gave the closest reading of the parallel passage in The Call of All Nations.15 Building on Federer, Paul De Clerck argued in 1978, and again in 2000, that Prosper’s lex is first of all the apostolic precept of 1 Timothy 2:1, not “the liturgy” in general. The Church obeys that precept so universally that “the biblical law has become a liturgical law.” On this reading, what settles the doctrinal question is three levels together: the precept, the Church’s universal obedience to it, and the wording of the prayers. No isolated liturgical text does it alone. De Clerck called the casual citation of any prayer, preface or hymn as “the lex orandi” a banalization of the adage. He also criticized its reversal and urged that the principle be applied only through comparative liturgy, across all the rites.16 O. T. I. Wright (2024) objects that Prosper’s “law” is no mere recommendation promoted to a law. He also argues that the narrow reading wrongly leaves out chapter 9’s argument from the baptismal rites.17

Arguments

Contested questions about the adage, each weighed adversarially on its own page.

  • Did Pope Celestine I write the “Capitula Caelestini”? — weak (the attribution goes back to c. 500, when the dossier was copied as part of Celestine’s letter of 431. But the compiler speaks of Rome’s popes as someone else’s, the letter never mentions an appendix, and Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death, knows of no Celestinian ruling on the beginning of faith. The dossier’s Roman reception from c. 500 is real, but it does not make chapter 8’s argument from prayer a papal decree.)
