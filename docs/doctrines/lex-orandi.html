Lex Orandi, Lex Credendi: The Law of Prayer and the Law of Belief

Lex orandi, lex credendi (“the law of praying, the law of believing”) is the shorthand for the idea that the Church’s worship is a rule for its faith: what the Church prays tells you what the Church believes. The words are a compression of one clause in a fifth-century anti-“semi-Pelagian” dossier, most probably compiled by Prosper of Aquitaine: ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, “so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” There the “law” was the intercession for all people that 1 Timothy 2:1 enjoins and that the whole Church obeys. The intercessions ask God to give faith to unbelievers, so the beginning of faith is grace.1 The argument from prayer is older than the formula. Cyprian had called the Lord’s Prayer the Christian’s law of prayer, and Augustine had written that “prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace.” In the East, Athanasius and Basil argued from the baptismal formula to the Spirit’s deity.2

This page is a reception-history timeline. The “belief that developed” is the adage itself: where it came from, how each era used it, and which way it runs. Its classic modern uses are Catholic dogmatic definitions that appealed to the Church’s feasts and prayers: the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950). Whether those feasts actually led to the definitions is weighed on its own page. Its most-discussed twist is a reversal. Pius XII’s Mediator Dei (1947) wrote that, taken absolutely and generally, “let the law of belief determine the law of prayer,” and later Roman documents repeat that inverted sense.3 Witnesses are set out earliest first, from the second- and third-century arguments from the rites, through the fourth-century arguments from baptism and doxology for the Trinity and the fifth-century formula, through its medieval career as a papal decree and the Reformation, when revised belief rewrote the rite and Trent defended the Roman Canon, to its modern career: the nineteenth-century liturgical movement, the scholarship that recovered its original sense, the Orthodox, Catholic and Methodist “liturgical theology” that made worship a primary source of theology, and the Roman documents that turned it into a legal category, from Summorum Pontificum (2007) to Traditionis Custodes (2021). Contested questions are weighed on argument pages rather than settled in the timeline: who wrote the text, what Prosper meant by lex, which way the adage runs, whether the rule was already at work when the first Christians began to worship Jesus, and whether the Roman Rite now has one law of prayer or two.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons: the Eucharist confirms the faith (c. 180)

The earliest argument in this survey from the Church’s worship to its faith is Irenaeus’s, against Gnostics who despised matter. They too offered bread and cup as the Lord’s body and blood. Irenaeus answers that their own rite contradicts their doctrine. If the Father is not the Creator, offering Him bread is offering Him someone else’s property.4 And the flesh fed with the Lord’s body cannot simply perish: “Let them, therefore, either alter their opinion, or cease from offering … But our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion.”5 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the sentence to call the Eucharist “the sum and summary of our faith.”6 The rite here is the eucharistic offering, not the prayers of petition, and the sentence runs both ways: belief agrees with (consonans) the Eucharist, and the Eucharist confirms (confirmat) belief. Its force depends on the heretics’ sharing the rite.7

Tertullian: custom as law, and the rites as evidence (c. 207–213)

In The Chaplet (208–212), Tertullian lists customs that no Scripture commands: the renunciation and triple immersion at baptism, the anniversary offerings for the dead, the sign of the cross. “Tradition will be held forth to you as the originator of them, custom as their strengthener, and faith as their observer,” and “even in civil matters custom is accepted as law.” But there he argues from practice to practice, to defend a Christian soldier’s refusal of the laurel crown.8 Elsewhere he argues from the rites to doctrine. Against the modalist Praxeas (c. 213), Christians are immersed “not once only, but three times … at each several mention of Their names,” so there are three persons.9 The flesh is washed, anointed, signed and fed on Christ’s body and blood, so body and soul “cannot then be separated in their recompense.”10 And Marcion’s god still uses the Creator’s water, oil and bread in his sacraments.11 Yet custom answers to truth. “Our Lord Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom,” and the rule of faith, “this law of faith,” is “immoveable and irreformable,” while discipline admits correction.12

Cyprian of Carthage: the “law of prayer” and the baptismal question (252–256)

Cyprian calls the Lord’s Prayer a law. It is “public and common,” and “this law of prayer” (hanc orationis legem) the three youths kept in the furnace. From the law of the prayer each Christian learns “what he ought to be.”13 He reads “Thy will be done … in earth” as a prayer for “those who believe not,” a reading Augustine would later use.14 In the dispute over baptism by heretics (255–256) he argued from the rite to doctrine: “the very interrogation which is put in baptism is a witness of the truth.” Candidates are asked whether they believe in “remission of sins through the holy Church,” so sins are forgiven only in the Church, and schismatics who ask it “lie in their interrogatory.” There is not “one law of the Creed” (symboli lex) for them and for the Church.15 But custom alone did not bind him. Against Pope Stephen’s ruling, “let nothing be innovated … which has not been handed down,” Cyprian answered that “custom without truth is the antiquity of error.”16 The Council of Arles (314) ruled against the African practice by the rite as well: a convert from heresy was to be asked the creed, and one baptized in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit received only the laying on of hands.17

Athanasius of Alexandria: baptized “into Creator and creature” (c. 340–361)

In the fourth century the argument from the rites was turned to the Trinity. Athanasius asked the Arians why, if the Son is a creature, “a thing made [is] classed with the Maker in the consecration of all of us.” They “do not baptize into Father and Son, but into Creator and creature.”18 Yet the words alone could not settle the question, since the Arians used them too. A baptism needs, “with the Name, … also the right faith,” and Christ said “Teach” before “Baptize.”19 He argued from worship as well: “creature does not worship creature,” so the Son, who is worshipped, is no creature.20 In the Letters to Serapion (c. 359–361) he carried the baptismal argument over to the Spirit. If the Spirit were a creature, the “rite of initiation” of those who said so would be “not entirely into the Godhead.”21

Basil of Caesarea: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief” (373–375)

Basil’s On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) began with a prayer. He had led the people in a doxology to the Father “with the Son together with the Holy Ghost” and was accused of innovation.22 His answer set out an order: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.” The confession of faith is “the origin and parent of the doxology,” and the objectors must teach “either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed.”23 So for Basil the prayer follows the creed, and the creed follows the baptism Christ commanded. He also defended the doxology from unwritten apostolic tradition. Much of the rite has no written source: the sign of the cross, turning east to pray, the words of the eucharistic invocation, triple immersion, even the baptismal confession itself.24 And he cited the old hymn at the lighting of the lamps, in which the people sang, “We praise Father, Son, and God’s Holy Spirit.”25

Gregory of Nazianzus: “How can He deify me by Baptism?” (380–381)

At Constantinople Gregory of Nazianzus met an argument from prayer on the other side: “who in ancient or modern times ever worshipped the Spirit? Who ever prayed to Him?” He replied that Christians worship and pray in the Spirit, and that “the adoration of One is the adoration of the Three.”26 His own argument ran from the rite: “if He is not to be worshipped, how can He deify me by Baptism? But if He is to be worshipped, surely He is an Object of adoration, and if an Object of adoration He must be God.”27 To candidates for baptism in January 381 he said that if he “worshipped a creature, or were baptized into a creature,” he “should not be made divine.”28 Later that year the Council of Constantinople confessed the Spirit as the one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.”29

Augustine of Hippo: the argument from the Church’s prayer (412–429)

The argument Prosper would later compress into a formula is Augustine’s. In 414 he read the four kinds of prayer in 1 Timothy 2:1 as the parts of the eucharistic liturgy, which the whole Church offers “for all men,” persecutors included.30 In 416 he and four other African bishops wrote to Pope Innocent I against Pelagius that “prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace”: whoever prays “lead us not into temptation” asks God for the power to keep the commandment.31 About 427 he answered Vitalis of Carthage, who held that the first step of faith is ours. The priest at the altar bids the people pray that God convert unbelievers, give catechumens the desire for baptism, and keep the faithful to the end. Vitalis must either answer “Amen” or mock the Church’s prayers, for “God does what He commands that He be asked to do.”32 In The Gift of Perseverance (428/429), written for Prosper and Hilary, he made it a principle. The Church has “been born and grows” in these prayers, and so in this faith. If it has always prayed that unbelievers might believe, it has always believed faith to be God’s gift.33 He argued the same way from the baptismal rite. Infants are exorcised and breathed upon (exsufflation) to drive out the devil, so they must be under his power through original sin.34 Prosper’s chapters 8 and 9 restate these two arguments in the same order. What Augustine never wrote is the formula, with its legal word lex.

Prosper of Aquitaine, the Capitula, ch. 8 (c. 435–442)

In Rome, Prosper of Aquitaine, Augustine’s champion against the monks of Marseilles, compiled a short dossier of rulings on grace: the Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei, also called the Indiculus or the “Capitula Caelestini.” His opponents said they would follow only what the Apostolic See had taught. So chapters 1–6 excerpt Popes Innocent I and Zosimus, chapter 7 the Council of Carthage of 418, and the last two chapters argue from the liturgy.35 Chapter 8 turns from the papal decrees to “the sacred rites of the priestly supplications, which, handed down by the apostles, are celebrated uniformly throughout the whole world and in every catholic Church, ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi”: so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing. It then lists what the bishops ask, with the whole Church groaning with them. They pray that faith be given to unbelievers, that idolaters be freed from their errors, that the veil be taken from the hearts of the Jews, that heretics and schismatics return, that the lapsed receive penance, and that catechumens be brought to the font. God’s answering of these prayers shows that conversion is wholly His work. Chapter 9 argues the same way from the exorcisms and exsufflations every candidate for baptism undergoes, infant or adult.36 Prosper’s wording is lex supplicandi, not orandi. His verb statuat is strong: it means to fix or establish, not merely to express. The same argument, without the formula, fills The Call of All Nations I.12 (c. 450), where Paul’s command to pray for all is the lex supplicationis that every priest and believer keeps.37 The text travelled in Dionysius Exiguus’s collection of papal decretals (c. 500), appended to Pope Celestine I’s letter of 431 to the Gallic bishops under the heading “Decreta Coelestini papae.” That is why the adage was long cited as Celestine’s (see the authorship argument).38 Since Marc Cappuyns’s study of 1929 it has been assigned to Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death. Cappuyns dated it 435–442. Alexander Hwang has argued for 450–455.39

Quoted as Celestine’s: the Scythian monks (c. 520)

The earliest known quotation of chapter 8 already treats it as a pope’s. About 520 a group of Scythian monks in Rome, led by Peter the Deacon, wrote to Fulgentius of Ruspe and the other African bishops exiled in Sardinia, setting out their faith on the incarnation and on grace. To show that every good movement of the will comes from God, they first appeal to a prayer: Basil’s anaphora, “which almost the whole East uses,” asks God to make the wicked good. Then they cite Pope Innocent and the African councils, and then “the most blessed Celestine in his letter to the Gauls.” That is chapter 8’s list of the bishops’ intercessions, followed by its conclusion that the answered prayer shows conversion to be God’s work. They begin their quotation at “when the leaders of the holy peoples,” so the clause about the law of supplicating is not included.40 By then the dossier was read in Rome as part of Celestine’s letter. The attribution is weighed on its own argument page.

Hincmar of Reims: the law of prayer against Gottschalk (859–860)

The first writer found in this survey who puts the clause itself to work is Hincmar, archbishop of Reims. In his treatise on predestination for Charles the Bald (859–860), written against the “twofold” predestination of Gottschalk of Orbais, he quotes “let the law of supplicating establish the law of believing” eight times, always as a decree of Pope Celestine.41 He turns it on a new question. The law of supplicating is the Church’s prayer on the day of the crucifixion “for all men,” for every order and rank, “for schismatics and heretics, for Jews and pagans, so that no one may be thought excepted.” So “since supplication is made to God by the whole catholic Church for all, we believe that God wills all men to be saved.”42 He also widens what counts as the law of supplicating. Roman Mass prayers that call Christ’s sacrifice the taking away of “the offence of the whole world” prove that He died for all, and the Easter blessing of the font proves that the redemption reaches every baptized person. After each he adds Celestine’s rule.43

The Capitula in the canon-law collections (c. 1010–1095)

The chapters went on circulating as papal law. Burchard of Worms’s Decretum (1007–1015) and Ivo of Chartres’s Decretum (1090s) copy several of them as “the decrees of Pope Celestine.” The chapters on grace stand among the dogmatic canons, and chapter 9 among the canons on baptism, under the rubric “That all who are to be baptized must first be exorcized.”44 There the argument from the rite has become a rule for the rite. Chapter 8, with its clause on the law of supplicating, is not among the canons of Ivo’s Decretum.45 It travelled another way as well. A longer form of the De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus, a fifth-century handbook of doctrine by Gennadius of Marseilles that was long printed among Augustine’s works, took over chapters of the Capitula word for word. In Migne’s text chapter 8, clause and all, is its chapter 30.46

Bernard of Clairvaux: a feast refused (c. 1140)

When the cathedral of Lyons adopted a feast of Mary’s conception, Bernard wrote to rebuke its canons, and his letter argues from worship in both directions. The feasts the Church had received taught him what to believe: “These things the Church sings concerning her, and has taught me to repeat … what I have not received from the Church I confess I should with great difficulty admit.” From the feast of Mary’s birth he learned that she was holy before she was born.47 But the new feast was “a rite which the Church knows nothing of, and which reason does not prove, nor ancient tradition hand down to us.” The holiness of the conception it implied was in his judgement false, it was “a presumptuous novelty against the custom of the Church,” and “the authority of the Apostolic See ought first to have been consulted.”48 A received feast teaches; a new one must first be justified by the faith.

Thomas Aquinas: the custom of the Church (c. 1270)

Aquinas still cites the Capitula as Pope Celestine’s. From chapter 9, on the exorcisms before baptism, he concludes that the unbaptized are under the devil’s power through original sin.49 He argues from the feast of Mary’s birth as Bernard had: “the Church does not celebrate feasts except of those who are holy,” so she was sanctified in the womb. But the feast of her conception, which Rome “tolerates” in some churches, “does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception.”50 “The custom of the Church has very great authority,” more than “an Augustine or a Jerome,” because “the very doctrine of catholic doctors derives its authority from the Church.” In the rites of the Mass “the Church cannot err, since she is taught by the Holy Ghost.”51 For Aquinas the authority belongs to the Church’s received practice, not to any rite as such.

Martin Luther: the gospel judges the Canon (1523–1526)

The Reformers ran the argument the other way: a changed belief rewrote the prayer. Luther’s Formula Missae (1523) says he means not “to abolish the liturgical service of God completely, but rather to purify” it. He does not reargue “that the mass is neither a sacrifice nor a good work.” He takes it as proven and measures the rite by it.52 So at the offertory, where “almost everything smacks and savors of sacrifice,” he writes: “Let us, therefore, repudiate everything that smacks of sacrifice, together with the entire canon.” The Words of Institution, which the Roman rite said in a low voice, are to be sung so that the people can hear them.53 The Deutsche Messe (1526) condemns the papal services for having been “changed into laws, works and merits to the utter destruction of faith.” Any order, his own included, is to be “straightway abolished” when it “becomes an abuse,” since “the Orders must serve for the improvement of faith and love.”54 Lutheran and other liturgical historians describe the reform in just these terms. In Spinks’s words, Luther worked not with “a pruning-knife” but with “the Gospel.” Geoffrey Wainwright generalizes that Protestantism typically makes doctrine the norm for worship.55

Thomas Cranmer: the Prayer Book rewritten to its doctrine (1549–1552)

In England Cranmer worked in two stages. The 1549 Book of Common Prayer still asked that the bread and wine “maie be unto us the bodye and bloude” of Christ.56 His Defence of 1550 named “the roots of the weeds”: transubstantiation, the real presence “in the sacrament of the altar,” and the sacrifice offered by the priest. Leave them, and “they will overspread all the ground again with the old errors.”57 Stephen Gardiner had read the 1549 rite in a Catholic sense, and the 1552 Book, in Gregory Dix’s words, altered “every point in 1549 to which Gardiner had appealed.” Now the communicants were to “be partakers of his most blessed body and bloud.” The bread was given with “Take and eate this, in remembraunce that Christ dyed for thee, and feede on him in thy hearte by faythe.”58 The “Black Rubric” then fixed the meaning of kneeling: no adoration is due to the bread and wine, which “remayne styll in theyr verye naturall substaunces,” and Christ’s natural body is “in heaven and not here.”59 Which doctrine 1552 taught, Zwinglian (Dix), Calvinist, or Cranmer’s own, is still disputed. That a doctrine shaped it is not.60

The Council of Trent: the Canon “pure from every error” (1562)

The Council of Trent answered in Session 22 (17 September 1562). It first defined the Mass as a true and propitiatory sacrifice, not “a bare commemoration” (canon 3). Then it declared the Roman Canon “so pure from every error, that nothing is contained therein which does not in the highest degree savour of a certain holiness and piety,” being composed “out of the very words of the Lord, the traditions of the apostles, and the pious institutions also of holy pontiffs” (ch. 4).61 Canon 6 anathematized anyone who says “that the canon of the mass contains errors, and is therefore to be abrogated.” Canon 9 defended the low voice, the Latin and the mixed chalice.62 The ceremonies, chapter 5 says, lead the faithful “to the contemplation of those most sublime things which are hidden in this sacrifice.”63 Trent did not make the rite untouchable. The previous session had declared the Church’s power to “ordain, or change” the rites of the sacraments, “their substance being untouched.” Pius V’s Missal of 1570 then fixed the Roman text: “nothing must be added … nothing omitted from it.”64 For the Catholic liturgist Josef Jungmann, Luther cut the Canon because of a doctrine, and Trent secured “the foundations of Catholic liturgy” by defining the doctrine it carried.65

The Prayer Book as a standard of doctrine (1662 onward)

In England the rite written to express a doctrine became, in time, a source of it. The Act of Uniformity of 1662 bound every minister to use the services “in such order and forme as is menc[i]oned in the said Booke,” so that “every person within this Realme may certainely knowe the rule to which he is to conforme in Publique Worship.”66 The Church of England’s Canon A5 names the Book among the places where its doctrine is found: “In particular such doctrine is to be found in the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion, The Book of Common Prayer, and the Ordinal.” The Worship and Doctrine Measure of 1974 wrote that statement into law.67 The law also runs the other way. Any new service must be “neither contrary to, nor indicative of any departure from, the doctrine of the Church of England in any essential matter” (Measure s. 4; Canon B2).68 Anglican writers often say that their church has no confession but its liturgy. One reference work describes Anglicanism as “possessing neither a confession as a point of unity nor a centralized authority structure,” and so “following the principle lex orandi lex credendi.” Critics answer that raising the Prayer Book to an uncontestable authority makes it “a confessional document” after all.69

The slogan takes shape (by 1834)

The two-member slogan lex orandi, lex credendi is not Prosper’s wording. The earliest instance found in this survey is the English Catholic writer Kenelm Digby’s Mores Catholici (book V, 1834). Digby argued that the liturgy must be uniform and unchangeable “for the reason assigned by St. Augustin: ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi;’ for the prayers which the Church uses in the administration of her holy rites, are so many proofs of the respective doctrines on which they depend.” The slogan had already detached from its author, since Digby credits it to Augustine.70 A Latin treatise on the Church in Migne’s Theologiae cursus completus (vol. 4, c. 1837) calls it an axioma and lists the standard proofs drawn from it. The exorcisms before baptism prove original sin. The Gloria Patri closing every psalm proves the Trinity. The outward adoration of the Eucharist proves the real presence, all the Church’s prayers prove the necessity of grace, and the ancient prayers for the dead prove purgatory. The same treatise warns that it would be a grave error to apply the maxim to every breviary, every legend of a saint, or every prescribed prayer.71 By 1870 John Henry Newman could invoke it in passing. In the Grammar of Assent he notes that the Church’s dogmatic definitions of the Trinity never call it a “mystery,” “as if fulfilling the maxim, ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi.’”72 None of these writers treats the slogan as new, so it is probably older than 1834. Its earlier history has not yet been traced here.

Prosper Guéranger: “tradition at its highest degree of power” (1840–1847)

Dom Prosper Guéranger, who refounded Solesmes in 1833, opened his Institutions liturgiques (1840) with the adage, credited to Pope Celestine: “On connaît l’axiome : Legem credendi statuat lex supplicandi.” He glossed it in the line for which the book is remembered: in the liturgy “the Spirit who inspired the sacred Scriptures still speaks; the Liturgy is tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity.”73 He turned the adage into a theory of heresy. Every sectary who wants a new doctrine meets the liturgy, “tradition at its highest power,” and Lutheranism, Calvinism and Anglicanism needed only “the substitution of new books and new formulas for the old,” after which “the faith of the peoples was henceforth defenceless.”74 On that ground he campaigned, successfully, to replace the French dioceses’ own “neo-Gallican” liturgies with the Roman rite. When the bishop of Orléans proposed in 1847 to turn the formula round, Legem deprecandi statuat lex credendi, Guéranger refused: “The question here is not what Saint Celestine did not say, but what Saint Celestine did say.”75

George Tyrrell, Lex Orandi (1903)

The Irish Jesuit George Tyrrell, soon to be the best-known English-speaking Catholic Modernist, made the adage the title of a book: Lex Orandi; or, Prayer and Creed (1903), followed by Lex Credendi: A Sequel to Lex Orandi (1906). He denied that the maxim means “every popular devotion rests on a sound dogmatic basis,” or that “the most widely venerated relic is ipso facto genuine.” It refers to “the prayer and the belief of the universal Church,” and “prayer” there means the whole life of charity. On that basis he set out to show how “the Lex Credendi or rule of faith has been determined and must be interpreted by the Lex Orandi or rule of prayer.”76 Later commentators read Pius XII’s reversal of the adage in 1947 as aimed partly at this Modernist reading. For Tyrrell, the lex orandi was the changing religious consciousness of an age, and it prescribed the lex credendi.77

Pius XI, Divini cultus: the canon of faith in the liturgy (1928)

Pius XI’s apostolic constitution on liturgical music, Divini cultus sanctitatem (20 December 1928), quotes the adage. “There is thus a close connection between dogma and the sacred Liturgy,” and so “Pope Celestine I saw the standard of faith expressed in the sacred formulae of the Liturgy” (Caelestinus I fidei canonem expressum esse censebat in venerandis liturgiae formulis). The constitution then quotes chapter 8 of the Capitula as Celestine’s.78 The attribution is weighed at Did Pope Celestine I write the “Capitula Caelestini”? Seven years later, in a private audience with the liturgist Bernard Capelle, Pius was reported as calling the liturgy “the most important organ of the ordinary magisterium of the Church.”79

Modern scholarship recovers the original sense (1929 onward)

Twentieth-century scholarship reread the adage in its fifth-century setting. Marc Cappuyns (1929) settled the authorship on Prosper. Karl Federer’s Liturgie und Glaube (1950) gave the closest reading of the parallel passage in The Call of All Nations.80 Building on Federer, Paul De Clerck argued in 1978, and again in 2000, that Prosper’s lex is first of all the apostolic precept of 1 Timothy 2:1, not “the liturgy” in general. The Church obeys that precept so universally that “the biblical law has become a liturgical law.” On this reading, what settles the doctrinal question is three levels together: the precept, the Church’s universal obedience to it, and the wording of the prayers. No isolated liturgical text does it alone. De Clerck called the casual citation of any prayer, preface or hymn as “the lex orandi” a banalization of the adage. He also criticized its reversal and urged that the principle be applied only through comparative liturgy, across all the rites.81 O. T. I. Wright (2024) objects that Prosper’s “law” is no mere recommendation promoted to a law. He also argues that the narrow reading wrongly leaves out chapter 9’s argument from the baptismal rites.82 Whether the narrow reading holds is weighed at its own argument page.

Pius XII, Mediator Dei: the adage reversed (1947)

Pius XII’s encyclical on the liturgy rejects one reading of the adage and reverses another. It first rejects the view that the liturgy is “a kind of proving ground for the truths to be held of faith,” so that a doctrine is sound if its rites produce “fruits of piety and sanctity”: “Hence the epigram, ‘Lex orandi, lex credendi.’” It then grants Prosper’s sentence its place. Popes and councils have drawn arguments from the liturgy, and Pius IX did so in defining the Immaculate Conception. But the liturgy “does not decide or determine independently and of itself what is of Catholic faith,” and “if one desires to differentiate and describe the relationship between faith and the sacred liturgy in absolute and general terms, it is perfectly correct to say, ‘Lex credendi legem statuat supplicandi’ — let the rule of belief determine the rule of prayer.”83 The same encyclical gives the pope alone the right “to introduce and approve new rites, as also to modify those he judges to require modification,” and warns that ancient usage is not better “on the simple ground that it carries the savor and aroma of antiquity.”84 The inversion uses the same words Guéranger’s opponent had proposed a century before.

Alexander Schmemann: “liturgical theology” (1961–1972)

Alexander Schmemann, an Orthodox priest teaching in Paris and then New York, made worship the source of theology. His Introduction to Liturgical Theology (Russian 1961; English 1966) states that “the early Church firmly confessed the principle lex orandi lex est credendi.” The task of liturgical theology, he wrote, is to present the Church’s worship as “the ‘rule of prayer’ dwelling within the Church and determining her ‘rule of faith.’”85 An essay of the same year concludes: “The lex orandi must be recovered as the lex credendi.”86 His charge against “scholastic” theology, Eastern as well as Western, was that it had turned worship “from a source” into “an object” for doctrine to define. Yet he denied that the liturgy is one “authority” or locus theologicus to be ranked against others. It is, he wrote, “the ontological condition of theology” (⚠ unverified).87 His essays surveyed here do not name Mediator Dei. The contrast with its reversal is drawn by his readers.

The Second Vatican Council: the liturgy as “summit” and “font” (1963)

The council’s Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, Sacrosanctum Concilium, does not quote the adage. It calls the liturgy “the summit toward which the activity of the Church is directed” and “the font from which all her power flows,” and it holds “all lawfully acknowledged rites to be of equal right and dignity.”88 It also ordered the Roman rite revised. The rites of the Mass were to be “simplified, due care being taken to preserve their substance,” with “no innovations unless the good of the Church genuinely and certainly requires them,” and new forms should “grow organically from forms already existing.”89 Paul VI’s revised Missal followed in 1969–1970. In 1976 he said it was promulgated “to take the place of the old,” as Pius V’s had been after Trent.90

Geoffrey Wainwright, Doxology: “worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship” (1980)

The Methodist theologian Geoffrey Wainwright built a systematic theology around worship and gave two of its chapters the two halves of the adage as titles. The tag, he wrote, “may be construed in two ways.” The “more usual” reading makes prayer the norm for belief, but grammatically the reverse is “equally possible,” and the ambiguity “corresponds to a material interplay which in fact takes place between worship and doctrine in Christian practice: worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship.”91 He also described the confessional pattern. “Roman Catholicism characteristically appeals to existing liturgical practice for proof in matters of doctrine,” while “Protestantism characteristically emphasizes the primacy of doctrine over the liturgy.” Each, he added, knows “the reverse relationship” as well: “Catholicism also seeks to control the liturgy doctrinally.”92

Aidan Kavanagh: theologia prima (1984)

The Benedictine liturgist Aidan Kavanagh argued, in lectures given in 1981 and published as On Liturgical Theology, that the assembly’s worship is “theologia prima,” primary theology, and doctrine is secondary.93 He rested the adage’s direction on Prosper’s verb. Belief “does indeed shape and influence the law of worship,” but the maxim “says only that the latter constitutes or founds the former. To reverse this is to cancel out the meaning of the maxim in its original formulation.” On creeds and eucharistic theories he wrote: “Influenced by, yes. Constituted or produced by, no.”94 Making belief the standard for worship, he wrote, “makes a shambles of the dialectic of revelation.” By the book’s end he calls the two laws “subtly correlative, the first founding the second, the second affecting (although not founding) the first.”95

John Paul II and the Catechism: “the Church believes as she prays” (1988–1992)

Later Roman documents keep both of Mediator Dei’s rules. John Paul II’s letter for the twenty-fifth anniversary of Sacrosanctum Concilium (1988) makes fidelity to the rites “a requirement of the Lex orandi, which must always be in conformity with the Lex credendi.”96 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes Prosper by name. When the Church celebrates the sacraments “she confesses the faith received from the apostles — whence the ancient saying: lex orandi, lex credendi (or: legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi according to Prosper of Aquitaine [5th cent.]). The law of prayer is the law of faith: the Church believes as she prays. Liturgy is a constitutive element of the holy and living Tradition.” The next paragraph draws the consequence for authority: “Even the supreme authority in the Church may not change the liturgy arbitrarily, but only in the obedience of faith and with religious respect for the mystery of the liturgy.”97

Benedict XVI, Summorum Pontificum: two expressions of one lex orandi (2007)

Benedict XVI made the adage a legal category. Summorum Pontificum declared Paul VI’s Missal “the ordinary expression of the lex orandi” of the Latin Church and the 1962 Missal “an extraordinary expression of the same lex orandi,” adding that “these two expressions of the Church’s lex orandi will in no way lead to a division in the Church’s lex credendi … for they are two usages of the one Roman rite.” The 1962 Missal, it said, had been “never abrogated.”98 His letter to the bishops gave the reason in terms of the adage: “In the history of the liturgy there is growth and progress, but no rupture. What earlier generations held as sacred, remains sacred and great for us too, and it cannot be all of a sudden entirely forbidden or even considered harmful.”99

Francis, Traditionis Custodes: “the unique expression of the lex orandi” (2021)

Francis reversed the 2007 settlement in the same vocabulary. Traditionis Custodes declares that the liturgical books of Paul VI and John Paul II “are the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite” (Latin unica expressio, “the only expression”), and abrogates the earlier norms.100 His letter to the bishops reported that a consultation of the bishops had found the 1962 Missal often used to reject “not only … the liturgical reform, but … the Vatican Council II itself,” and it cited Pius V, who had established “a single Missale Romanum” for the Latin Church.101 In 2022 Francis called the question “primarily ecclesiological”: one cannot accept the Council “and at the same time not accept the liturgical reform born out of Sacrosanctum Concilium.”102 Supporters, such as the liturgist Andrea Grillo, called Benedict’s “two forms of one rite” a sophism. Critics, such as Peter Kwasniewski and Joseph Shaw, answered that the older books are part of the Roman Rite’s law of prayer and that the article contradicts itself.103 The two positions are weighed under Related arguments — the Roman Rite’s lex orandi.

Arguments

Contested questions about the adage, each weighed adversarially on its own page.

Related arguments — the worship of Jesus

The adage is sometimes carried back to the origin of Christianity itself: the first Christians worshipped Jesus before the Church had any doctrine of his divinity, and the doctrine followed the worship. Two rival readings of the first-century evidence are weighed separately.

Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity? — plausible

Prayer, hymns and invocation addressed to Jesus go back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers (marana tha, 1 Cor 16:22). They came long before any defined doctrine of his divinity, and from c. 230 writers argued from the Church’s hymns to Christ’s deity. But the worship rested on the belief that God had raised and exalted Jesus, so it came before formulated doctrine, not before belief. It also left open which doctrine of the Son would follow.

Did the first Christians worship God through Jesus, rather than Jesus himself as God? — plausible

Dunn’s reading rightly notes that most early prayer runs to God through Jesus, that cultic service is kept for God, and that Origen still forbade prayer to the Son. But direct invocation, hymns, doxologies and the worship of the Lamb in Revelation 5 tell against its strong form. What that devotion implied is a judgement on which historians divide. Most lean toward Hurtado and Bauckham.

Related arguments — the Roman Rite’s lex orandi

Since 2007 the adage has been used as a legal category: Benedict XVI named two Missals as expressions of one Roman lex orandi, and Francis in 2021 named one. Each position has its own advocates, and the two are weighed separately.

Are the post-conciliar books the only expression of the Roman Rite’s lex orandi? — contested

As law it stands on firm Catholic ground. Pius V and Paul VI each imposed one Missal, and Roman teaching since Mediator Dei reserves the liturgy to papal authority. As a reading of the adage it is newer. Prosper’s law of prayer was what the whole Church had received and prayed, not the book currently prescribed, and the older Missal is still celebrated with permission. Whether the two Missals carry one faith or two visions of the Church is the real dispute.

Did the older Roman Missal remain an expression of the Church’s lex orandi? — contested

Its core is secure: no Roman document calls the 1962 Missal erroneous, it has been celebrated with permission without a break, and Prosper’s sense of the adage favours it. Benedict’s judgement that it was “never juridically abrogated” is defensible but runs against Paul VI’s stated intention. The strong claim that what was once sacred cannot be withdrawn conflicts with the authority over the rites that the same tradition records.

Other arguments

  • Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption? — plausible (both feasts came first, and both bulls argue from them; for the Immaculate Conception the doctrine was first argued in the feast’s defence. But neither feast fixed its own meaning. The Conception feast was kept by those who denied the doctrine, and its object was settled by Scotist theology and papal decree. The Assumption’s content came into the feast from the apocryphal Transitus, and Pius XII denied that the liturgy “engenders” the faith. The feasts set the question and spread the belief, but did not decide it.)
  • Did Prosper’s “law of supplicating” mean the apostle’s command to pray for all, not the liturgy at large? — sound (in its best form. The Call of All Nations calls the command of 1 Timothy 2:1 a “law of supplication,” the Capitula echo the verse’s obsecrationes, and Ambrosiaster and Augustine had already read it as the Church’s rule of prayer. Prosper’s marks of apostolic origin, universality and uniformity pick out particular rites, not the liturgy as a whole. But he does not confine the argument to the intercessions, since chapter 9 argues the same way from the baptismal exorcisms, and he does not set Scripture against the rite. The practice is the precept carried out.)
  • Did Pope Celestine I write the “Capitula Caelestini”? — weak (the attribution goes back to c. 500, when the dossier was copied as part of Celestine’s letter of 431. But the compiler speaks of Rome’s popes as someone else’s, the letter never mentions an appendix, and Prosper, writing after Celestine’s death, knows of no Celestinian ruling on the beginning of faith. The dossier’s Roman reception from c. 500 is real, but it does not make chapter 8’s argument from prayer a papal decree.)
