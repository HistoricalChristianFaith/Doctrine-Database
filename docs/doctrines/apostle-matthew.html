Matthew

Matthew is one of the Twelve, named in all four apostle-lists, whose call from a toll-booth the Gospels tell three times; from the second century on he is named as the author of the first Gospel, “composed in the Hebrew tongue.” Two questions attach to him in the sources. The first is his name: the man Matthew’s Gospel calls “Matthew” at the receipt of custom is named Levi in the parallel scene in Mark and Luke; Jerome writes “Matthew, also called Levi,” while Heracleon, quoted by Clement of Alexandria, lists the two names separately. The second is his book: Papias’s notice that “Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language” is followed by statements in Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome that Matthew wrote first in Hebrew, and modern writers dispute what Papias meant. For his life after Pentecost the sources name Ethiopia, Parthia and Persia, and differ on whether he died a martyr. This page sets out what was recorded about Matthew, earliest extant source first: the Gospel notices, Papias and the patristic chain on the Hebrew Gospel, Heracleon’s list in Clement, Jerome’s Latin summary, the mission-traditions, the Golden Legend’s account of Ethiopia, and modern scholarship. It is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on Matthew. Two disputed questions, the Hebrew Gospel and the Ethiopian martyrdom, are set out with the named holders of each reading under Disputed readings. For the shared ladder of sources on the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names Matthew in all four lists of the Twelve, and tells one story about him: his call. As Jesus “passed forth from thence, he saw a man, named Matthew, sitting at the receipt of custom: and he saith unto him, Follow me. And he arose, and followed him” — a tax-collector, a publican, left his toll-booth at a word. Matthew’s own Gospel alone adds the detail that he was a publican when it lists him among the Twelve: “Matthew the publican.” Mark and Luke tell a parallel call-scene — a toll-booth, the same words, the rising and following — in which the man is named Levi, “Levi the son of Alphaeus” in Mark, a “publican, named Levi” in Luke, who then makes Jesus “a great feast in his own house.” Whether Matthew and Levi are the same man is taken up by the sources below. The New Testament records nothing of his life after the call.1

Papias on the Hebrew oracles, and the patristic chain (c. 125, reported c. 325)

The earliest word about Matthew’s book is a single sentence of Papias of Hierapolis (c. 125), preserved by Eusebius: “So then Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able.” Later writers state that Matthew wrote in Hebrew. Irenaeus (c. 180): “Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect.” Origen (c. 230, via Eusebius): the first Gospel “was written by Matthew, who was once a publican, but afterwards an apostle… for the converts from Judaism, and published in the Hebrew language.” Eusebius himself: Matthew, “who had at first preached to the Hebrews, when he was about to go to other peoples, committed his Gospel to writing in his native tongue.” Eusebius also reports that Pantaenus, travelling to “India” about 190, found there the Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew, left by the apostle Bartholomew. What Papias meant by the “oracles,” and whether a Hebrew Matthew stands behind the Greek Gospel, is disputed; see Was Matthew’s Gospel first written in Hebrew?2

Clement of Alexandria quotes Heracleon on confession (c. 200)

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200), discussing what it means to “confess” Christ, quotes the Valentinian Heracleon on confession “with the voice, and before the authorities”: “neither will this utterance be found to be spoken universally; for all the saved have confessed with the confession made by the voice, and departed. Of whom are Matthew, Philip, Thomas, Levi, and many others.” Heracleon names Matthew among examples in a discussion of confession; whether this bears on his death is disputed (see Was Matthew martyred in Ethiopia?). The list also names “Matthew” and “Levi” separately.3

Jerome, On Illustrious Men (393)

Jerome, opening his catalogue of Christian writers, gives a compact Latin summary on Matthew. He identifies the apostle with Levi: “Matthew, also called Levi, apostle and aforetimes publican, composed a gospel of Christ at first published in Judea in Hebrew for the sake of those of the circumcision who believed, but this was afterwards translated into Greek, though by what author is uncertain.” Jerome adds that “the Hebrew itself has been preserved until the present day” in the library at Caesarea, and that he had had “the opportunity of having the volume described” to him by the Nazarenes of Beroea, who used it. In the preceding chapter he says he had translated the Gospel according to the Hebrews into Greek and Latin; J. E. Jacquier (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) notes that it is not known for certain that Jerome did not mistake that gospel for the Hebrew Matthew. Jerome thus states both that Matthew is Levi and that his Gospel was first written in Hebrew.4

The mission-field and the apostle-lists (dates uncertain)

The sources differ on where Matthew went after Judea. The line that reached the Greek historians sends him to Ethiopia: Rufinus and Socrates Scholasticus, expanding Eusebius’s scheme of the apostles’ allotted regions, assign “Ethiopia” to Matthew (“Matthew was allotted Ethiopia,” in Socrates), and Ethiopia is the setting of the Golden Legend’s account below. The apostle-lists — Greek and Latin catalogues of where each apostle preached and died — send him variously to Parthia and Persia, to Macedonia, to the cannibal “man-eaters” on the Black Sea, dying variously by the sword, by fire, or in peace at Hierapolis ⚠ unverified.5

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend gives a full account of Matthew in Ethiopia: the apostle confounds two sorcerers, Zaroes and Arphaxat, and their two dragons; he raises the son of King Egippus and converts the land; he consecrates the king’s daughter Iphigenia and her virgins to God; and when the next king, Hirtacus, wishes to marry her and is refused, he has Matthew struck down at the altar. Matthew’s emblems in Western art include the angel or winged man, his Gospel-symbol from the four living creatures ⚠ unverified, and the sword or spear, which is associated with this account ⚠ unverified. The Golden Legend was compiled about twelve centuries after the events it narrates; whether its martyrdom is Matthew’s death is disputed (see Was Matthew martyred in Ethiopia?).6

Modern scholarship

Modern writers have taken up the same three questions. On the man: R. T. France (The Gospel of Matthew, 2007), on Matthew 9:9, and Jerome (393) identify Matthew with the Levi of Mark and Luke; Heracleon’s list (c. 200) names them separately. On the book: W. D. Davies and D. C. Allison (1988) and France hold that the Greek Matthew was composed in Greek, using the Greek Mark and the Greek Old Testament; J. E. Jacquier (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) held that Matthew wrote in Aramaic and that the Greek Gospel is a free translation whose translator used Mark. The meaning of Papias’s “oracles” (logia) — the canonical Gospel, a sayings-collection, or a collection of messianic proof-texts — is discussed by Jacquier, who calls the question “still unsolved.” On his death: Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015) ranks the evidence for Matthew’s martyrdom among the least secure of the apostles’.7

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on the language of Matthew’s Gospel and on how he died.

  • Was Matthew’s Gospel first written in Hebrew? — Papias’s “oracles” and the patristic chain beside the Greek text.
  • Was Matthew martyred in Ethiopia? — the Golden Legend beside Heracleon’s list in Clement.
