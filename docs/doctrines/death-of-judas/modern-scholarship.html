Modern scholarship on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 1933–present (the modern critical treatment, from Kirsopp Lake’s 1933 “Note IV” onward)
Summary: The Apostolic Constitutions, Apollinaris, the Latin Acts texts, Oecumenius, Theophylact and Euthymius combined the two notices, and Theophylact added Papias to the sequence. Modern commentators divide: Kirsopp Lake (1933) reads Matthew, Acts, and Papias as developments of one loose traitor-death tradition, without direct literary dependence, each shaped by a scriptural template for the ruin of a traitor; C. K. Barrett, Ernst Haenchen, Hans Conzelmann, Richard I. Pervo, Joseph A. Fitzmyer, Arie W. Zwiep and Jesse E. Robertson read the notices as independent accounts not to be combined; Darrell L. Bock, Craig S. Keener and I. Howard Marshall read the notices as reconcilable.

Claims & evidence

Kirsopp Lake (1933): one loose traitor-death tradition, each account shaped by a scriptural template

The modern discussion begins with Kirsopp Lake’s “Note IV. The Death of Judas,” an appended study in The Beginnings of Christianity V. Lake takes the divergence as evidence against a single recoverable event:

From the complete contradiction between the three narratives, which do not fully agree in any point and differ sharply on most, it is clear that we have not much real recollection of fact. The question is whether we can trace any of the literary sources of the traditions.

He then traces each account to an Old Testament or apocryphal precedent: “The account in Matthew is surely not independent of the LXX story of the death of Ahithophel, who betrayed David” (2 Samuel 17:23, where Ahithophel too “went to his house… and hanged himself”); “The account in Acts is clearly influenced by Wisdom iv. 17 ff.” — singling out the two words “rhēxei… prēneis” of Wisdom 4:19 that recur in Acts 1:18; and “the account in Papias seems to be connected with the account in 2 Macc. ix. 7-18,” the swelling, worm-ridden death of Antiochus Epiphanes. But he stops short of claiming direct literary borrowing:

But attempts to prove any conscious literary dependence of Matthew, Acts, or Papias on any one source, such as, for instance, the story of Ahikar, are to be deprecated. The truth probably is that there was a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence. One writer put in one detail, the next added another, until finally nearly all had been incorporated.

— Kirsopp Lake, “Note IV. The Death of Judas,” in The Beginnings of Christianity V (Macmillan, 1933), 22–30. Lake also describes the Latin harmony as a textual development: the Old Latin gloss collum sibi alligavit (“he bound his own neck”), in the African Latin text Augustine quotes, is “an attempt to harmonize Acts with Matthew”; Jerome knew it in the shorter form suspensus, and the Vulgate reads suspensus crepuit medius (“and being hanged he burst in the middle”) — “a clear case of the well-known double process in textual history: first, a gloss is added to explain a difficult text; secondly, the gloss takes the place of the difficulty, which is left out altogether.”

Commentators who read the notices as independent: “no harmonization… is possible”

Several later commentators on Acts likewise read the notices as independent and decline to combine them. C. K. Barrett writes — “no harmonization of these accounts is possible” — and treats Matthew and Acts as rival popular etiologies of a real Jerusalem place-name.

no harmonization of these accounts is possible

— C. K. Barrett, The Acts of the Apostles I (ICC; T&T Clark, 1994), 93. The same view is taken by Ernst Haenchen (The Acts of the Apostles, Blackwell, 1971, 159–163), Hans Conzelmann (Acts of the Apostles, Hermeneia; Fortress, 1987, 11), Richard I. Pervo (Acts, Hermeneia; Fortress, 2009, 50), and Joseph A. Fitzmyer, whose The Acts of the Apostles (Anchor Bible 31; Doubleday, 1998), on 1:18–19, treats Matthew’s and Acts’ notices as two independent traditions attaching a death-story to the field called Akeldama, differing on why it bears that name — Matthew from the priests’ blood-money, Acts from Judas’s own blood. Arie W. Zwiep, in a monograph on the passage, warns specifically against the harmonizing reflex: conflating the two accounts “into a third, harmonized version” yields a narrative “neither story was ever meant” to give — Arie W. Zwiep, Judas and the Choice of Matthias (WUNT 2/187; Mohr Siebeck, 2004), 17 (publisher).

Jesse E. Robertson (2012): the death-notices as characterization, not chronicle

A book-length study of the question, Jesse E. Robertson’s The Death of Judas, reframes it: read against the ancient rhetorical handbooks, a death-account is not primarily a chronicle of what happened but a portrait of what the man was — so the three divergent deaths are three characterizations, each fitted to its author’s plot and theology, not three reports to be reconciled. His abstract states the thesis:

Three different accounts of the death of Judas Iscariot are preserved in Matthew 27:3–10, Acts 1:18–20, and fragments of Papias. The present study will argue that in the milieu of the ancient Mediterranean such death-accounts would have conveyed to the authorial audience particular character traits of Judas through established conventions… In Matthew, Judas is depicted as a traitor of a Davidic king and a failure as a disciple. In Acts, the portrait of Judas presents him as an apostate apostle and a defeated enemy of God’s people. In Papias, Judas is characterized as a greedy and intemperate miscreant who plots against a righteous benefactor.

— Jesse E. Robertson, The Death of Judas: The Characterization of Judas Iscariot in Three Early Christian Accounts of His Death (New Testament Monographs 40; Sheffield Phoenix, 2012), abstract (Baylor Ph.D. dissertation, 2009). On Robertson’s reading the hanging, the headlong fall and bursting, and the swelling each do the same rhetorical work by different means: they mark Judas as the impious traitor whose end fits his crime. Where Lake spoke of a “loose tradition,” Robertson describes a rhetorical convention.

Commentators who read the notices as reconcilable

Other commentators read the two accounts as combinable. Darrell L. Bock answers Barrett directly — against the claim that harmonization is impossible he holds that “things are not so clear” and that the differing details are “reconcilable,” the hanging and the fall being successive moments and Acts’ “acquired” (ektēsato) leaving room for the priests’ purchase with Judas’s money.

things are not so clear… reconcilable

— Darrell L. Bock, Acts (BECNT; Baker Academic, 2007), on 1:18–19. Craig S. Keener (Acts, NCBC; Cambridge, 2020, 117–18) and I. Howard Marshall (The Acts of the Apostles, TNTC; Eerdmans, 1980, 65) likewise treat the notices as compatible reports of one end, Marshall allowing that Acts may be recording the story as it was told in Jerusalem.

Akeldama as a place-name: Bart Ehrman

Both texts tie the betrayal-money to a field near Jerusalem known as the “field of blood” (Aramaic Akeldama). Bart Ehrman, who reads the death-notices as irreconcilable — “the descriptions of Judas’s death in these two accounts are at odds with one [another], even though there are, at the same time, some striking similarities” — takes Akeldama to be a genuine, pre-Christian Jerusalem place-name that both traditions are trying to explain.

— Bart D. Ehrman, “The Death of Judas in the NT” (The Bart Ehrman Blog).
