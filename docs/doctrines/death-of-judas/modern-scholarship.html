Modern scholarship on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 1933–present (the modern critical treatment, from Kirsopp Lake’s 1933 “Note IV” onward)
Summary: Where the ancient and medieval readers harmonized the two notices — and even spliced in Papias — modern criticism reopens the question. The dominant line reads Matthew, Acts, and Papias as independent traditions of a death whose manner was never remembered in common, each shaped by a scriptural template for the ruin of a traitor; a minority of commentators still judge the notices reconcilable. Nearly all agree on a bedrock historical core.

Claims & evidence

Kirsopp Lake (1933): three independent traditions, each shaped by a scriptural template

The modern discussion begins with Kirsopp Lake’s “Note IV. The Death of Judas,” an appended study in The Beginnings of Christianity V. Lake takes the divergence as evidence against a single recoverable event:

From the complete contradiction between the three narratives, which do not fully agree in any point and differ sharply on most, it is clear that we have not much real recollection of fact. The question is whether we can trace any of the literary sources of the traditions.

He then traces each account to an Old Testament or apocryphal precedent: “The account in Matthew is surely not independent of the LXX story of the death of Ahithophel, who betrayed David” (2 Samuel 17:23, where Ahithophel too “went to his house… and hanged himself”); “The account in Acts is clearly influenced by Wisdom iv. 17 ff.” — singling out the two words “rhēxei… prēneis” of Wisdom 4:19 that recur in Acts 1:18; and “the account in Papias seems to be connected with the account in 2 Macc. ix. 7-18,” the swelling, worm-ridden death of Antiochus Epiphanes. But he stops short of claiming direct literary borrowing:

But attempts to prove any conscious literary dependence of Matthew, Acts, or Papias on any one source, such as, for instance, the story of Ahikar, are to be deprecated. The truth probably is that there was a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence. One writer put in one detail, the next added another, until finally nearly all had been incorporated.

— Kirsopp Lake, “Note IV. The Death of Judas,” in The Beginnings of Christianity V (Macmillan, 1933), 22–30. Lake also identifies the Latin harmony as a textual accident, not a reading: the Old Latin gloss et suspensus crepuit medius (“and being hanged he burst in the middle”), which the Vulgate then absorbed, is “a clear case of the well-known double process in textual history: first, a gloss is added to explain a difficult text; secondly, the gloss takes the place of the difficulty, which is left out altogether” — the fusion the fourth-century Latin text already shows.

The decliners: “no harmonization… is possible”

The mainstream of critical commentary on Acts follows Lake in reading the notices as independent and declining to combine them. C. K. Barrett puts it flatly — “no harmonization of these accounts is possible” — and treats Matthew and Acts as rival popular etiologies of a real Jerusalem place-name.

no harmonization of these accounts is possible

— C. K. Barrett, The Acts of the Apostles I (ICC; T&T Clark, 1994), 93. The same judgement runs through Ernst Haenchen (The Acts of the Apostles, Blackwell, 1971, 159–163), Hans Conzelmann (Acts of the Apostles, Hermeneia; Fortress, 1987, 11), Richard I. Pervo (Acts, Hermeneia; Fortress, 2009, 50), and Joseph A. Fitzmyer, whose The Acts of the Apostles (Anchor Bible 31; Doubleday, 1998), on 1:18–19, treats Matthew’s and Acts’ notices as two independent traditions attaching a death-story to the field called Akeldama, differing on why it bears that name — Matthew from the priests’ blood-money, Acts from Judas’s own blood. Arie W. Zwiep, in the fullest monograph on the passage, warns specifically against the harmonizing reflex: conflating the two accounts “into a third, harmonized version” yields a narrative “neither story was ever meant” to give — Arie W. Zwiep, Judas and the Choice of Matthias (WUNT 2/187; Mohr Siebeck, 2004), 17 (publisher).

Jesse E. Robertson (2012): the death-notices as characterization, not chronicle

The one book-length treatment of exactly this question, Jesse E. Robertson’s The Death of Judas, reframes it: read against the ancient rhetorical handbooks, a death-account is not primarily a chronicle of what happened but a portrait of what the man was — so the three divergent deaths are three characterizations, each fitted to its author’s plot and theology, not three reports to be reconciled. His abstract states the thesis:

Three different accounts of the death of Judas Iscariot are preserved in Matthew 27:3–10, Acts 1:18–20, and fragments of Papias. The present study will argue that in the milieu of the ancient Mediterranean such death-accounts would have conveyed to the authorial audience particular character traits of Judas through established conventions… In Matthew, Judas is depicted as a traitor of a Davidic king and a failure as a disciple. In Acts, the portrait of Judas presents him as an apostate apostle and a defeated enemy of God’s people. In Papias, Judas is characterized as a greedy and intemperate miscreant who plots against a righteous benefactor.

— Jesse E. Robertson, The Death of Judas: The Characterization of Judas Iscariot in Three Early Christian Accounts of His Death (New Testament Monographs 40; Sheffield Phoenix, 2012), abstract (Baylor Ph.D. dissertation, 2009). On Robertson’s reading the details modern apologetics scrambles to reconcile — the hanging, the headlong fall and bursting, the grotesque swelling — are each doing the same rhetorical work by different means: they mark Judas as the archetype of the impious traitor whose end fits his crime, the very “loose tradition” Lake had posited, now given a rhetorical rather than a merely folkloric mechanism.

The allowers: the notices are reconcilable

A minority of recent commentators judge the two accounts combinable. Darrell L. Bock answers Barrett directly — against the claim that harmonization is impossible he holds that “things are not so clear” and that the differing details are “reconcilable,” the hanging and the fall being successive moments and Acts’ “acquired” (ektēsato) leaving room for the priests’ purchase with Judas’s money.

things are not so clear… reconcilable

— Darrell L. Bock, Acts (BECNT; Baker Academic, 2007), on 1:18–19. Craig S. Keener (Acts, NCBC; Cambridge, 2020, 117–18) and I. Howard Marshall (The Acts of the Apostles, TNTC; Eerdmans, 1980, 65) likewise treat the notices as compatible reports of one end, Marshall allowing that Acts may be recording the story as it was told in Jerusalem. The steelman of this position — which does not rest on the popular props (a decomposing body, a cliff above the Hinnom) — is weighed on its own argument page (assessment: plausible).

The agreed historical core

Across the divide, decliners and allowers alike grant a bedrock both texts share and neither invents: that Judas betrayed Jesus, that the betrayal-money was tied to a field near Jerusalem known as the “field of blood” (Aramaic Akeldama), and that Judas died violently and soon after. Even Bart Ehrman, who reads the death-notices as irreconcilable — “the descriptions of Judas’s death in these two accounts are at odds with one [another], even though there are, at the same time, some striking similarities” — takes Akeldama to be a genuine, pre-Christian Jerusalem place-name that both traditions are trying to explain.

— Bart D. Ehrman, “The Death of Judas in the NT” (The Bart Ehrman Blog). It is this floor — a real traitor, a real field, a real violent death — that survives whichever way the manner-of-death question is decided.
