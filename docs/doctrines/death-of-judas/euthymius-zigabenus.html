Euthymius Zigabenus on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 1118 (his Commentary on the Four Gospels, on Matthew, written under Alexius I Comnenus and largely epitomized from earlier Greek exegesis) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Comnenian-era Greek exegete gives, within his commentary on Matthew, the same failed-hanging reconciliation his contemporary Theophylact states from Acts: Judas hanged himself but was cut down alive before he choked, then lingered, swelled, and burst as Acts reports. He grounds the whole reading in Chrysostom and, unlike Theophylact, does not reach for Papias.

Claims & evidence

“To hang himself with a noose was of despair” — the failed hanging joins Matthew to Acts

Euthymius comments verse by verse on Matthew 27:3–10 under the running heading De Iudae poenitentia (“On the repentance of Judas”). On the hanging (27:5) he draws the standard Byzantine distinction between Judas’s remorse and true repentance, then splices in the Acts account by way of a hanging that did not kill:

Peccatum quidem coram omnibus confiteri, ac proiicere argenteos, resipiscentiae fuerunt: laqueo vero se suspendere, desperationis. Agnouit siquidem et poenituit eum, ac confessus est: veniam autem ab eo qui hanc dare poterat, non requisiuit… Quum ergo ad hunc clementissimum confugere oporteret, ille ad mortem potius confugit, vt a desperata tristique vita citius liberaretur. Et neque id continuo assequutus est, prout cupiebat: agnitus enim a quibusdam depositus est ne praefocaretur: deinde postquam in secreto quodam loco, modico vixisset tempore, praeceps factus siue praecipitatus, inflatus, diruptus ac diffissus est medius: et effusa sunt omnia viscera eius, sicut ait liber Actorum.

— Euthymius Zigabenus, Commentary on Matthew, on 27:5 (Hentenius’s Latin, facing the Greek, in Matthaei’s edition). In English: “To confess the sin before all, and to throw down the silver, were acts of repentance; but to hang himself with a noose, of despair. For he acknowledged his sin, and repented, and confessed it; yet he did not seek pardon from the one able to give it… So when he ought to have fled to this most merciful one, he fled rather to death, that he might the sooner be freed from his despairing and grievous life. And not even this did he obtain at once, as he wished: for being recognized by some he was taken down, that he should not be choked; then, after he had lived a little while in some hidden place, having pitched forward, swollen, he burst asunder and was split in the middle, and all his bowels gushed out, as the book of the Acts says.”1

The mechanism is Theophylact’s exactly — not Oecumenius’s snapped rope but a failed hanging, the noose interrupted by bystanders who take Judas down before he strangles — so that Acts’ bursting can follow as the sequel. Euthymius even carries over Acts’ own participle: his Judas becomes praeceps / pronus (Greek prēnēs, “prostrate, headlong”) before he swells, the very word whose sense is weighed on the manner-of-death argument page.

The field was bought before the death, not after — on Chrysostom’s authority

Having joined the two death-notices, Euthymius turns to the purchase of the field and corrects a rival ordering, again leaning on Chrysostom:

Quodsi etiam in eo scriptum est, quod praedium comparauit de mercede iniquitatis, non tamen hoc fuit post strangulationem, vt quidam putant. Id enim declarans Chrysostomus, agrum figuli fuisse dicit praedium illud.

— on 27:7–8: “And if it is also written [in Acts] that he acquired a field with the wages of iniquity, yet this was not after the strangling, as some suppose. For Chrysostom, explaining this, says that field was the potter’s field.” The move harmonizes Matthew’s priests-buy-the-field with Acts’ ektēsato by making them one transaction and setting it before, not after, Judas’s end — the reading weighed on the field-of-blood argument page.1

Notes

  • A second Comnenian witness to the failed-hanging harmony. Euthymius (c. 1118) and Theophylact (c. 1107) are near-contemporaries of the same Byzantine exegetical line, and give the same reconciliation — Judas cut down alive, then swelling and bursting per Acts. The difference is where each says it: Theophylact reconciles at Acts 1:18 (he wrote a commentary on Acts); Euthymius, whose Gospel commentary stops at the four Evangelists, has to reach across from Matthew to Acts to complete the harmony, and does so unprompted — a sign of how settled the joined death had become.
  • He does not cite Papias. Where Theophylact fills out the manner of death by transcribing Papias’s swelling fragment at length, Euthymius keeps only the swelling and bursting and credits them to Acts itself, naming no source but Chrysostom. His account is thus the more restrained form of the same tradition — the harmony without the marvel — and his value here, as elsewhere, is as a careful transmitter of the earlier Greek fathers rather than an independent source.
  • A witness elsewhere on this wiki. The same Commentary on the Four Gospels supplies Euthymius’s notes on the Simeon legend (Luke 2:25) and on the woman-taken-in-adultery obelus (John 7:53); he is best known for the latter, his report that “the accurate copies” of John lacked the pericope.
  • Whether the two notices genuinely describe one death, and whether the field-purchase and the field’s name can be read as one, is weighed on the manner-of-death and field-of-blood argument pages.
