Aceldama on the ground: is the field of blood a real, locatable site, and does the archaeology fit?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — there really is a single, continuously identified spot — the southern slope of the Hinnom valley, below Mount Zion — that has borne the name Aceldama from the fourth century to the present, and it is a genuine ancient burial ground: three Second Temple rock-cut tombs were excavated there in 1989, which fits Matthew’s “to bury strangers in” well. That much is close to settled. What the material record does not confirm is the two most distinctive things the Gospels say about the plot: no potter’s kiln or clay-working installation has ever been dug there (the “potter’s field” rests on Matthew’s phrase and on travellers’ reports of red clay, not on excavation), and a burial ground is equally consistent with the minority reading that the Aramaic name meant “field of sleep” — a cemetery — and never referred to blood at all. So the identification is strong; the “potter’s field” and “blood” specifics are the genuinely open parts.

Related arguments: Matthew’s priests and Acts’ Judas: one purchase, one name — the textual counterpart to this page (does Acts describe the same field, transaction, and name as Matthew?), where the instability of the Aramaic behind “Akeldama” is also weighed.

The question

[Matthew:] And they took counsel, and bought with them the potter’s field, to bury strangers in. Wherefore that field was called, The field of blood, unto this day… [Acts:] insomuch as that field is called in their proper tongue, Aceldama, that is to say, The field of blood.

— Matthew 27:7–8 and Acts 1:19. Set aside the well-worn question of whether the two texts contradict each other (weighed on the companion page) and ask a different one: is there a real place answering to these notices, and does the ground itself — a burial field, a source of potter’s clay — match what the evangelists describe? The reading weighed here is that Aceldama is a genuine, locatable Jerusalem site whose material record fits the Gospel descriptions.1

What can be said for it

The identification is early, and it is continuous. Unlike many Gospel toponyms, Aceldama is not a spot that pilgrims had to reinvent; the tradition points, without a serious break, at one strip of ground. Eusebius names it in the Onomasticon about 325 as “still pointed out” at Jerusalem — though his Greek places it on the north of Mount Zion, and Jerome’s Latin version silently corrects the bearing to the south: Acheldama, ager sanguinis, qui hodieque monstratur in Aelia ad australem plagam montis Sion, “the field of blood, still shown at Aelia on the southern side of Mount Zion.” The southern reading is the one that matches the ground, and it holds thereafter. Around 570 the pilgrim conventionally called Antoninus of Piacenza finds the field in use exactly as Matthew describes it — a burial ground for foreigners:

As we went out from Siloam we came into the field that was purchased for the price of the Lord, which is called Aceldama, that is, ‘Field of Blood,’ in which they bury pilgrims. There are cells of the servants of God among those graves, many virtuous men, and apples and vines throughout the area of those tombs.

From there the thread runs unbroken: the Greek Orthodox monastery of St. Onuphrius stands on the slope, a Crusader-era charnel house (partly rock-cut, partly masonry) received the bones of Christian pilgrims down into the modern period, and the site is shown as Aceldama to this day. A named place, held to one location across some thirteen centuries, is unusually good attestation for a Gospel site.2

The ground is a real ancient cemetery. This is the part the spade has actually confirmed. In 1989 Gideon Avni and Zvi Greenhut excavated three large rock-cut burial caves on the slope where the Hinnom meets the Kidron — one of Jerusalem’s principal burial grounds — and published them as The Akeldama Tombs. The caves are Second Temple in origin, with documented reuse in the Roman and Byzantine periods, and yielded ossuaries (some inscribed), pottery, glass, jewellery, coins and a sarcophagus. Whatever else is disputed, the central material claim — that this was a burial ground in use in the first century — is established, and it answers precisely to the one function Matthew assigns the plot: a field “to bury strangers in.”3

The topography fits the potter, too — and it reinforces Matthew’s Jeremiah imagery. The Hinnom’s southern slope was a clay source: nineteenth-century travellers reported red clay still being dug there, and the ground was long associated with potters’ work. That is the same corner of Jerusalem in which Jeremiah stages his potter oracles — the potter’s house of Jeremiah 18 and the smashed earthen bottle carried out “unto the valley of the son of Hinnom, which is by the entry of the east gate” (the Potsherd Gate) of Jeremiah 19. So Matthew’s “potter’s field” and his crediting of the naming-oracle to Jeremiah are not free-floating: they land on real Jerusalem geography, where a working clay-ground, a burial place, and Jeremiah’s potter all sit together at the mouth of the Hinnom.4

What weighs against it

The “potter’s field” is not archaeologically confirmed. This is the load-bearing caveat. The 1989 excavation found tombs, not a workshop: no kiln, no clay-working installation, no potter’s debris was reported on the ground itself. The clay connection rests on Matthew’s phrase, on the general geology of the Hinnom, and on the observations of nineteenth-century travellers — an inference, not an excavated fact. The material record confirms a cemetery; it does not confirm a pottery. It is entirely possible that the plot was a spent clay-pit turned burial ground, exactly as the name and the geology suggest — but that is a reconstruction the digging has not, so far, borne out.5

And a cemetery does not, by itself, confirm the name means “blood.” The distinctive descriptor in both Gospels is blood — but the Aramaic behind “Akeldama” is unstable. The best-attested Greek form, Hakeldamach, carries a final chi that on its face does not yield “field of blood” at all; the standard defence treats it as a transliteration artefact, but August Klostermann proposed in 1883 that the consonant is radical — Aramaic dmk/demach, “to sleep,” used of rest in the grave — so that Akeldama would be the “field of sleep,” i.e. a cemetery. Here the archaeology cuts against the reading it seemed to help: a burial ground fits “field of sleep” at least as neatly as “field of blood.” The excavated tombs are therefore neutral between the two etymologies; they corroborate that the plot was a graveyard, not that its name ever referred to blood. The proposal is a minority one, rejected by the standard reference works, but it is enough to show that the material record settles the function of the site without settling the meaning of its name.6

Identification is not the same as vindication of the story. The tradition converged early on one slope — but not perfectly: Eusebius’s “north” against Jerome’s “south” is a reminder that even a well-attested site can drift in the reporting. More importantly, what a spade can establish is a first-century burial ground of the right kind in the right place; it cannot reach behind that to Judas, the betrayal-money, or the naming. That a real, fitting site exists raises the plausibility of the narrative attached to it, but does not prove the attachment — possibility is not the same as demonstration. The honest yield of the ground is a genuine strangers’ cemetery on the southern Hinnom slope, continuously called Aceldama, whose two signature labels — “potter’s” and “blood” — are precisely the parts the evidence leaves open.7

Assessment

Plausible. The claim divides cleanly, and the two halves land differently. The identification — that there is a real, locatable Jerusalem site answering to the notices — is close to settled: a single strip on the southern slope of the Hinnom has been called Aceldama from Eusebius and Jerome through the Piacenza pilgrim and the medieval charnel house to the present, and the 1989 excavation confirms it was a genuine Second Temple burial ground, matching Matthew’s “to bury strangers in.” That is a stronger material footing than most Gospel sites enjoy.

What holds the compound reading short of probable is that the two most distinctive descriptions are exactly the ones the ground does not corroborate. No potter’s kiln or clay-works has been excavated, so the “potter’s field” remains an inference from the name and the geology; and a cemetery is neutral between “field of blood” and the minority “field of sleep,” so the archaeology confirms the site’s function without confirming that its name meant blood at all. The verdict is therefore split by design: the site is real and its use as a burial ground is well-grounded (that part is near-settled); the “potter’s” and “blood” specifics are plausible but unproven, and one of them may not be what the name ever meant.
