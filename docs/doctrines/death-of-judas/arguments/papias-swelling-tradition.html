Does Papias’s swollen Judas (Fragment III) preserve a tradition of Judas’s death independent of Acts 1:18?

Disputed reading · on the death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · witness: Papias of Hierapolis

The passage

Judas walked about in this world a sad example of impiety; for his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.

— Papias of Hierapolis, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1, tr. Roberts–Donaldson), preserved in Apollinaris of Laodicea. A longer form, with eyes swollen past a physician’s dioptra and a death on a deserted estate, is transmitted by Theophylact of Ohrid; both forms are set out on the Papias detail page.1

The question

Papias wrote his five-book Exposition of the Oracles of the Lord c. 110, and his notice describes neither Matthew’s hanging nor Acts’s headlong bursting but a swelling and a crushing. The dispute is over what the notice is: a report of Judas’s end handed down from the apostolic generation, one of several death-stories fitted to the traitor’s offence, or an expansion of the Acts account. It bears on whether the manner of Judas’s death was transmitted outside the two canonical notices.

The fragment survives only in later quotation: through Apollinaris of Laodicea (c. 382), the Greek catenae on Acts and Matthew (in a shorter and a longer form), and Byzantine commentators such as Theophylact.2 Ancient judgements of its author differ: Irenaeus, as Eusebius quotes him, called Papias “an ancient man who was a hearer of John,” while Eusebius (c. 325), who had his five books, wrote that “he appears to have been of very limited understanding, as one can see from his discourses.”3

A report of Judas’s end from a disciple of John

On this reading Papias records what became of Judas between the hanging Matthew reports and the bursting Acts reports, on the authority of the generation that heard the apostles.

Held by: Apollinaris of Laodicea (catena on Acts, c. 382); Theophylact of Ohrid (Commentary on Acts, c. 1107).4

Papias stated that he gathered what he could from those who had heard the apostles’ disciples, valuing “what came from the living and abiding voice” over books.3 His notice is the oldest post-canonical account of how Judas died, older than the Latin harmonizers and the whole Greek commentary tradition on Acts. It is also a distinct account: a living man so swollen that he could not pass where a wagon passes, crushed at last, a cause of death neither canonical notice gives. On this reading that distinctness marks a separate line of report rather than a retelling of either canonical text. Apollinaris reads the fragment together with Matthew: Judas “did not die by hanging, but lived on, having been cut down before he was suffocated,” and Papias supplies the death that followed. Theophylact gives the same sequence and names his source: “Judas did not die in the noose, but lived on even after, since he was taken down before he strangled to death. Papias, a disciple of John, relates this more clearly in the fourth book of the Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord.” He then transcribes the longer form, with the sightless eyes and the estate that “remains deserted.” On this reading Matthew, Acts and Papias are three partial reports of one end: the failed hanging, a lingering swollen life, and the death in which “his bowels gushed out,” the last preserved by a disciple of John who had it from the living voice.

One of three traitor-deaths shaped by scriptural precedent

On this reading the hanging, the bursting and the swelling are three developments of a loose tradition about how a traitor ought to die, each drawn from a different precedent and each portraying Judas’s character.

Held by: Kirsopp Lake (The Beginnings of Christianity V, 1933); Jesse E. Robertson (The Death of Judas, 2012).5

Lake read the three narratives together and judged that “we have not much real recollection of fact”; the question for him was which literary precedents shaped each. He derived Matthew’s hanging from the death of Ahithophel, Acts’s bursting from Wisdom 4:19, and held that “the account in Papias seems to be connected with the account in 2 Macc. ix. 7-18,” the swelling, worm-ridden death of Antiochus Epiphanes. The same type recurs in Herod Agrippa “eaten of worms” (Acts 12:23) and the traitor Nadan of the Ahiqar legend. Lake declined to make any one account copy any one source: “there was a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence. One writer put in one detail, the next added another, until finally nearly all had been incorporated.”6 Robertson, in a book-length study of the three accounts, reads them against the ancient rhetorical handbooks: such death-accounts “would have conveyed to the authorial audience particular character traits of Judas through established conventions.” Matthew’s Judas is a traitor of a Davidic king, Acts’s an apostate apostle; “In Papias, Judas is characterized as a greedy and intemperate miscreant who plots against a righteous benefactor.” Candida Moss reports Robertson as noting that Papias “follows an emerging literary trope about the retributive and punitive deaths of bad rulers.”5 On this reading Papias’s swelling stands beside the hanging and the bursting as a third portrait, each fitting the traitor’s end to his offence.

An expansion of the Acts account, picturing greed

On this reading Papias develops the canonical notices, Acts 1:18 above all, and gives Judas the swollen body that ancient medicine and moral writing attached to greed.

Held by: Candida R. Moss (“A Note on the Death of Judas in Papias,” New Testament Studies, 2019).7

Moss places the fragment in a second-century process: “By the second century, Christians had begun to expand upon and develop the Lukan and Matthean traditions about the death of Judas.” In Papias, “the already graphic scene in Acts 1.18, in which Judas’ bowels burst out of his body, gains greater definition.” The short fragment ends on the phrase it shares with Acts 1:18, “his bowels gushed out” (exechythē panta ta splanchna autou), and the swelling supplies a cause for that bursting. Other elements, in Moss’s words, “seem reliant upon now-canonical traditions”: the wagon-way the bloated Judas cannot pass recalls the camel and the needle’s eye, and his death at home in a notorious place gestures toward the Field of Blood. The longer form in the catenae and Theophylact adds the eyes, the stench and the worms.8 Moss reads the body through ancient biographical and medical literature “in which edema and parasitic infections are a consequence of greed.” Dropsy, in which the sufferer swells and thirsts for more, was used of insatiable avarice, and Papias, of all Judas’s faults, “focuses on Judas’ financial gain, and indicts him for greed”: “the moral diagnosis is one of greed.” His condition is “the outward manifestation of an internal corruption,” and his death “serves as a pedagogical example for other Christians about the dangers of greed.”7 On this reading the swollen Judas is the body of the man who sold his master for silver, drawn onto the bursting Acts reports.
