Papias’s swollen Judas: an independent early tradition?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the swelling-and-crushing account is genuinely a third version of Judas’s death and genuinely early (c. 110), and that much is worth something: it shows the manner of the traitor’s end was not fixed in common memory a generation after Acts. But as an independent tradition of real source-value — a separate memory of how Judas actually died — it does not hold. It keeps the exact words of Acts (“his bowels gushed out”), it follows the fixed literary shape of the tyrant’s grotesque judgment-death, and it survives only through late catena-quotation that goes on piling horror on horror.

Related arguments: the accounts of Judas’s end — see also Matthew’s hanging and Acts’ bursting: one death, told twice

The claim

Judas walked about in this world a sad example of impiety; for his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.

— Papias of Hierapolis, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1, tr. Roberts–Donaldson), preserved in Apollinaris of Laodicea. The reading weighed here is not the wording of the fragment but the use commonly made of it: that Papias — a hearer of the elders, writing within living memory of the apostolic age — hands down a distinct and independent report of Judas’s death, neither Matthew’s hanging nor Acts’ simple fall, and so an early witness of genuine value for what actually became of the traitor. Papias is set out in full on his detail page; this page asks only what his account is worth.1

Who advances it

The claim is a standing one rather than one modern writer’s. The older editors of the apostolic fathers printed the fragment as a serious early notice, and Papias’s antiquity has always tempted readers to treat his swollen Judas as a third strand of memory beside the two canonical ones — the natural inference from a source that predates every surviving commentator on the crux. Against that stands the recent study of the fragment, above all Candida Moss (2019), which reads it not as an independent memory but as a development downstream of Acts, shaped by the ancient conviction that a body reveals a soul: Judas’s grotesque swelling is the somatic signature of his greed. Between the two lies the honest question of this page — early, yes, but early evidence of what?2

The evidence for

It is genuinely early. Papias wrote his five-book Exposition of the Oracles of the Lord about 110, perhaps earlier; he is quoted for the Judas notice within a generation or two of Acts, and he claimed to gather what he could from those who had heard the apostles’ own disciples, preferring “the living and abiding voice” to books. Whatever its worth, his account is the oldest post-canonical word on how Judas died, older than the Latin harmonizers, older than the whole Greek commentary tradition on Acts. An early source is not thereby a true one — but its date is a real point in its favour, and cannot be waved away.3

It really is a third account, not Acts retold. Papias does not narrate a hanging (Matthew) or a prostrate, ruptured corpse (Acts). He narrates a living man so monstrously bloated that he could not pass down his own road, crushed at last by a passing wagon — a cause of death the New Testament nowhere gives. Whoever coined it was not simply copying either Gospel. That there were, by Papias’s day, at least three ways of telling the traitor’s end is not in dispute; it is the one solid thing the fragment establishes, and it tells against any picture in which the manner of Judas’s death was firmly and singly remembered.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The two claims above are secure: the fragment is early, and it is a distinct account. An honest verdict has to grant both, and neither can be dismissed by calling the story “legendary” and moving on. The question is the further step — from early and distinct to independent and historically valuable — and it is there that the case fails.

It is not clearly independent of Acts. The one phrase Papias shares word-for-word with the New Testament is the decisive detail of Acts 1:18 — “his bowels gushed out” (exechythē panta ta splanchna autou). A swelling that ends by bursting the bowels looks less like a rival memory than like an elaboration built onto Acts: keep Acts’ ruptured entrails, supply a lurid new cause for them. The swelling is not a substitute for Acts’ bursting but a run-up to it. That is the ordinary direction of legendary growth — a spare notice acquiring a vivid mechanism — and it is exactly what the later transmission goes on doing: Theophylact’s fuller form adds eyes swollen past a physician’s probe, a stench, worms, and a deserted estate, none of it in the short fragment. A tradition still visibly accreting horrors in the twelfth century is not obviously preserving a first-century memory in the second.4

Its shape is a fixed literary type, not a coroner’s report. The grossly swollen or worm-eaten death is, in Jewish and early Christian writing, the standard end of the impious persecutor and the greedy traitor. Antiochus IV in 2 Maccabees 9 rots alive, his flesh falling away, worms swarming, the stench unbearable to his own army; Herod Agrippa in Acts 12:23 is “eaten of worms” for usurping God’s glory; the traitor Nadan in the Ahiqar legend swells up and bursts. Kirsopp Lake, cataloguing the three Judas-deaths, derived Papias’s swelling precisely from the Antiochus of 2 Maccabees, as he derived Matthew’s hanging from Ahithophel and Acts’ bursting from Wisdom 4:19 — “a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence.” Moss sharpens the point to Judas in particular: in ancient medicine and moral physiognomy, dropsy — the body swelling with fluid while the victim thirsts for more — was the emblem of insatiable greed, so that the swollen Judas enacts the sin of the man who sold his Lord for silver. Read so, the fragment is a moral picture, not an autopsy: its details are supplied by what the death of such a man ought to look like.5

The source is fragile, and its author was known for credulity. The death-notice survives only at several removes — through Apollinaris of Laodicea, the Greek catenae on Acts and Matthew (which carry it in a shorter and a longer form), and Byzantine commentators such as Theophylact — so its original wording and setting are not fully recoverable. And the writer himself is the one Eusebius singled out as “a man of very small intelligence,” who took the church’s figurative sayings for literal marvels. That caution bears directly on a grotesque wonder-tale of this kind. None of this makes the story false; it makes it a poor candidate for an independent historical memory.6

Assessment

Weak. Two things are true and worth keeping: Papias’s account is genuinely early, and it is genuinely a third version of Judas’s death, distinct from both Gospels. From those alone it does real work — it shows that within a generation or two of Acts the manner of the traitor’s end was already told several ways, which is a live consideration wherever the two canonical notices are read as one remembered event. But the reading this page weighs asks for more: that the fragment be an independent tradition of source-value, a separate memory of how Judas actually died. On its best case it is not. Its one verbatim contact with the New Testament is the “bowels gushed out” of Acts 1:18, which points to elaboration rather than independence; its whole shape is the stock grotesque death of the impious traitor, its swelling the ancient emblem of the very greed Judas embodied; and it reaches us only through late, still-growing catena-quotation from a writer his own earliest reader thought credulous about marvels. The date is real and the distinctness is real; the historical source-value is not demonstrated, and the weight of the evidence is that the swelling is a moral and literary development downstream of Acts. This is not a verdict reached by ruling the marvellous out in advance — a swollen death is not impossible; it is a verdict on attestation, wording, and genre, which is where such a claim has to be weighed.
