Theophylact of Ohrid on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 1050–after 1107 (his Commentary on Acts, largely epitomized from earlier Greek exegesis) · Wikipedia
Summary: The medieval Byzantine coda to the crux. Theophylact takes the two death-notices as one death without argument — Judas hanged himself but was cut down before he strangled and lived on — and, to fill out the manner of his end, quotes Papias’s swelling fragment at length, splicing all three accounts into a single lurid whole.

Claims & evidence

“Judas did not die in the noose, but lived on”

Observe: he elaborated not on the transgression, but on the punishment for it, because Judas did not die in the noose, but lived on even after, since he was taken down before he strangled to death. Papias, a disciple of John, relates this more clearly in the fourth book of the Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord.

— Theophylact, Commentary on Acts, on 1:18. Where Oecumenius joined the notices by a snapped rope, Theophylact joins them by a failed hanging: the noose did not finish Judas, who was cut down alive, so that Peter in Acts can speak of what befell him afterward. He then reaches back past both Gospels to Papias for the “punishment” itself — the swelling, the sightless eyes, the death on the deserted estate — transcribing the fragment (quoted in full on the Papias page).

Notes

  • The three accounts become one story. Theophylact is the clearest instance of the harmonizing instinct running to completion: Matthew’s hanging is retained but rendered non-fatal, Acts’ bursting is the sequel, and Papias’s swelling supplies the drawn-out death in between. What began as three divergent notices is now a single continuous biography of the traitor’s end — the fullest medieval expression of the reconciling tradition, and the point at which its harmony is purchased by absorbing every variant rather than choosing among them.
  • A witness elsewhere on this wiki. Theophylact appears also as the medieval coda on the perpetual-virginity timeline; his commentaries largely epitomize Chrysostom and the earlier Greek fathers, so his value here is as a transmitter who preserves older material (notably the long Papias fragment) rather than an independent source.
  • Whether the notices genuinely describe one death, and whether Papias is an independent memory or a later embellishment downstream of Acts, is weighed on the manner-of-death argument page.
