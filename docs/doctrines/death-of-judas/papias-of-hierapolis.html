Papias of Hierapolis on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 60–130 (his Exposition of the Oracles of the Lord, five books, survives only in fragments; the Judas notice is quoted c. 110) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest post-apostolic witness to the crux, and the one who deepens rather than settles it. Papias reports neither Matthew’s hanging nor Acts’ simple bursting but a third manner of death — a monstrous bodily swelling ending in Judas being crushed — so that within a generation or two of the Gospels the story of the traitor’s end is already told three ways.

Claims & evidence

Judas swollen monstrous, then crushed and burst

The fragment, preserved by Apollinaris of Laodicea and printed among the remains of Papias, reads:

Judas walked about in this world a sad example of impiety; for his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.

— Papias, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1, tr. Roberts–Donaldson). Papias keeps the “bowels gushed out” of Acts 1:18 but supplies a cause the New Testament never gives — a swelling and a crushing, not a hanging and not a fall.

The fuller form transmitted by Theophylact

The Byzantine commentator Theophylact of Ohrid, quoting “Papias, a disciple of John… in the fourth book of the Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord,” preserves a longer version of the same fragment:

Judas presented a great example of impiety in this world, whose body swelled to such a degree that he could not pass where a wagon could drive through, and not only could he himself not pass, but not even his head alone. The lids of his eyes, they say, swelled so much that he could not see the light at all, and his eyes themselves could not be seen even by means of a medical dioptra, so deep were they from the outer surface… After great sufferings and torments he died, they say, on his own estate; and that estate remains deserted…

— Papias, as quoted in Theophylact, Commentary on Acts, on 1:18. The swollen eyes, the eyes past a physician’s probing, the deserted estate — details the terse Acts notice does not carry — show the tradition already accreting the somatic marks that ancient medicine read as the signature of greed.

Notes

  • A third account, not Acts retold. Papias is sometimes read as harmonizing Acts, but he reports a distinct manner of death — a swelling and a crushing that neither Matthew (hanging) nor Acts (a prostrate, burst body) contains. His value on this timeline is as the earliest evidence that the manner of the traitor’s death was not fixed in common memory, so that reconcilers thereafter had three notices to fit, not two.
  • Early, but fragmentary and secondhand. Papias wrote within living memory of the apostolic age, which lends his notice weight; but the death-fragment survives only through later quotation (Apollinaris, Theophylact, and the catenae), so its exact original wording and setting are not fully recoverable. Eusebius, who knew Papias’s five books, thought him a man “of very limited understanding” and credulous about marvels — a caution that applies here too.
  • Papias also appears on this wiki as a witness on the writing-on-the-ground timeline and as a source on several apostle pages. The relation of the three death-accounts is weighed on the manner-of-death argument page.
