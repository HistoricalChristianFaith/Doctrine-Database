Papias of Hierapolis on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 60–130 (his Exposition of the Oracles of the Lord, five books, survives only in fragments; the Exposition is dated c. 110, and the Judas notice survives in later quotation) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant post-canonical account of Judas’s death. Papias reports neither Matthew’s hanging nor Acts’ simple bursting but a third manner of death — a monstrous bodily swelling ending in Judas being crushed — so that by c. 110 the traitor’s end is extant in three forms.

Claims & evidence

Judas swollen monstrous, then crushed and burst

The fragment, preserved by Apollinaris of Laodicea and printed among the remains of Papias, reads:

Judas walked about in this world a sad example of impiety; for his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.

— Papias, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1, tr. Roberts–Donaldson). Papias keeps the “bowels gushed out” of Acts 1:18 but supplies a cause the New Testament never gives — a swelling and a crushing, not a hanging and not a fall.

The fuller form transmitted by Theophylact

The Byzantine commentator Theophylact of Ohrid, quoting “Papias, a disciple of John… in the fourth book of the Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord,” preserves a longer version of the same fragment:

Judas presented a great example of impiety in this world, whose body swelled to such a degree that he could not pass where a wagon could drive through, and not only could he himself not pass, but not even his head alone. The lids of his eyes, they say, swelled so much that he could not see the light at all, and his eyes themselves could not be seen even by means of a medical dioptra, so deep were they from the outer surface… After great sufferings and torments he died, they say, on his own estate; and that estate remains deserted…

— Papias, as quoted in Theophylact, Commentary on Acts, on 1:18. The swollen eyelids, the eyes past a physician’s probing, and the deserted estate are not in the Acts notice. Candida Moss (2019) reads the swelling against ancient biographical and medical literature “in which edema and parasitic infections are a consequence of greed” (“A Note on the Death of Judas in Papias,” New Testament Studies 65, abstract).

Notes

  • Relation to Matthew and Acts. Papias reports a distinct manner of death — a swelling and a crushing that neither Matthew (hanging) nor Acts (a prostrate, burst body) contains, while sharing Acts’ “bowels gushed out.” Later reconcilers, Apollinaris of Laodicea and Theophylact, worked his account into the sequence with Matthew and Acts.
  • Date and transmission. Papias’s Exposition is dated c. 110; Papias, as Eusebius quotes him, stated that he questioned those who had followed the elders about what the Lord’s disciples had said. The death-fragment survives only through later quotation (Apollinaris, Theophylact, and the catenae), so its exact original wording and setting are not fully recoverable. Irenaeus, as Eusebius quotes him, called Papias “a hearer of John”; Eusebius, who knew Papias’s five books, wrote that “he appears to have been of very limited understanding” (Church History 3.39.1, 4, 13).
  • Papias also appears as a witness on the writing-on-the-ground timeline and as a source on several apostle pages. How this swelling account has been read — as a report from a disciple of John, as one of three traitor-deaths shaped by scriptural precedent, or as an expansion of Acts — is set out on its interpretation page.
