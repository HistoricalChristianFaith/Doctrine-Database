Augustine of Hippo and the fourth-century harmonizers on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 354–430 (the Latin Acts text he quotes is set out c. 404 in Contra Felicem Manichaeum) · Wikipedia
Summary: By the end of the fourth century the two death-notices were being told as one sequence. The copy of Acts that Augustine quotes carries the hanging inside the verse itself, so that the text before him reads Judas bound his own neck, was cast down on his face, and burst.

Claims & evidence

The Latin text of Acts that Augustine quotes

Quoting Acts 1:18 against the Manichaean Felix, Augustine cites a form of the verse expanded well beyond the Greek:

collum sibi alligavit, et deiectus in faciem, disruptus est medius, et effusa sunt omnia viscera eius

— the Old Latin Acts of Augustine, Contra Felicem Manichaeum I.4: “he bound his own neck, and cast down on his face, burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out.” The Greek of Acts has no hanging; the Latin text before Augustine has Matthew’s hanging (“he bound his own neck”) inside the Acts verse. Jerome’s Vulgate reads, more briefly, et suspensus crepuit medius, “and being hanged he burst in the middle.” Kirsopp Lake (1933) described the Old Latin reading as a gloss that came to take the place of the difficulty it explained (see the modern-scholarship page).

Notes

  • Other fourth-century witnesses. About 380 the Apostolic Constitutions (VII.2) tell the story as a single unbroken sequence, citing Matthew and Acts together — Judas “repented, and hanged himself, and burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out.” About the same date Apollinaris of Laodicea, quoting Papias, offers a worked reconciliation with a different mechanism: Judas “did not die by hanging, but lived on, having been cut down before he was suffocated” — the hanging attempt survived, the swelling and crushing supplying the actual death. Two ways of joining the notices are thus extant from the late fourth century: one sentence that runs them together (the Latin text, the Constitutions), and a sequence in which the hanging fails and a later death follows (Apollinaris on Papias).
  • A joined text and an argument. Augustine quotes a text in which the two accounts are joined, not an argument that they can be. An explicit argument, naming a broken rope and stating that “there is no contradiction,” is extant in the Greek commentary on Acts under the name of Oecumenius; Theophylact gives a failed hanging instead.
  • Modern commentators who read the notices as one death, and those who read them as independent traditions, are set out on the modern-scholarship page.
