Augustine of Hippo and the fourth-century harmonizers on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 354–430 (the Latin Acts text he quotes is set out c. 404 in Contra Felicem Manichaeum) · Wikipedia
Summary: By the end of the fourth century the two death-notices were already being told as one sequence, without a sense that anything needed smoothing. Augustine is the clearest Latin witness to how far the harmony had gone: the copy of Acts he quotes has absorbed the hanging into the verse itself, so that the text before him reads Judas bound his own neck, was cast down on his face, and burst.

Claims & evidence

The Latin text of Acts had already fused the hanging and the fall

Quoting Acts 1:18 against the Manichaean Felix, Augustine cites a form of the verse expanded well beyond the Greek:

collum sibi alligavit, et deiectus in faciem, disruptus est medius, et effusa sunt omnia viscera eius

— the Old Latin Acts of Augustine, Contra Felicem Manichaeum I.4: “he bound his own neck, and cast down on his face, burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out.” Where the Greek of Acts says only that Judas became prostrate and burst, the Latin text before Augustine has already written Matthew’s hanging (“he bound his own neck”) into the Acts verse — the harmony made not by argument but by transmission. Jerome’s Vulgate fixed the same move more briefly: et suspensus crepuit medius, “and being hanged he burst in the middle.”

Notes

  • The other fourth-century harmonizers. Augustine is representative, not alone. About 380 the Apostolic Constitutions (VII.2) tell the story as a single unbroken sequence, citing Matthew and Acts together — Judas “repented, and hanged himself, and burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out.” About the same date Apollinaris of Laodicea, quoting Papias, offers a worked reconciliation with a different mechanism: Judas “did not die by hanging, but lived on, having been cut down before he was suffocated” — the hanging attempt survived, the swelling and crushing supplying the actual death. The fourth century thus holds two reconciling strategies side by side: fuse the notices into one sentence (the Latin text, the Constitutions), or space them in time so the hanging fails and something else kills him (Apollinaris on Papias).
  • A harmony by text, not yet by exegesis. What Augustine attests is not an argument that the accounts can be reconciled but a text in which they already have been — the interpretive question buried inside the wording a Latin reader received. The explicit exegetical form, naming the snapped rope and stating outright that “there is no contradiction,” arrives with the Greek commentary tradition: see Oecumenius and Theophylact.
  • Whether these notices truly describe one death, or two independent traditions of a death whose manner was not remembered in common, is weighed on the manner-of-death argument page.
