Oecumenius on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 550 (the Greek catena on Acts transmitted under his name) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Greek commentary on Acts carried under the name of Oecumenius states the reconciliation that will become the standard one in its explicit, exegetical form: the two accounts do not conflict because the rope broke — Judas hanged himself, the cord gave way, and he pitched forward onto the ground and burst.

Claims & evidence

“There is no contradiction. For both happened.”

And when they say that he used a rope and hung himself, the sacred Gospels also record that if he now fell forward and everything that follows, there is no contradiction. For both happened: the cord by which the hanging occurred broke, and therefore he was carried forward onto the ground and had fallen.

— Oecumenius, Commentary on Acts, on 1:16–20. This is the first surviving statement of the harmony as a mechanism rather than a fused text: Matthew reports the hanging, Acts the aftermath, and a broken cord joins them. The commentator adds that Acts’ placing the notice “some days later” is no obstacle, since it merely confirms that the death followed “immediately after the betrayal.”

Notes

  • The Latin parallel: Bede. In the West the Venerable Bede’s Commentary on Acts (c. 709–716), on 1:18, takes the fused death for granted — “the noose’s knot strangled the throat from which the voice of betrayal had emerged” — and reads Judas perishing “in the midst of the air” as fitting, “according to the example of Ahithophel and Absalom who persecuted King David.” The typological reflex — Judas as a second Ahithophel, the counsellor who betrayed David and “hanged himself” — is exactly the scriptural template modern critics point to (see the modern-scholarship page).
  • Dating. The catena on Acts attributed to “Oecumenius” is conventionally placed in the sixth century; the name also attaches to a later (tenth-century) catena on the Catholic Epistles, so the ascription is loose. Either way this witness precedes the fuller Byzantine restatement in Theophylact, c. 1107.
  • The soundness of the rope-broke harmony — and the objection that Acts’ wording is shaped by a scriptural pattern for the ruin of the wicked rather than reporting mechanics — is weighed on the manner-of-death argument page.
