Oecumenius on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 550 (the Greek catena on Acts transmitted under his name) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Greek commentary on Acts carried under the name of Oecumenius states a reconciliation in explicit, exegetical form: the two accounts do not conflict because the rope broke — Judas hanged himself, the cord gave way, and he pitched forward onto the ground and burst.

Claims & evidence

“There is no contradiction. For both happened.”

And when they say that he used a rope and hung himself, the sacred Gospels also record that if he now fell forward and everything that follows, there is no contradiction. For both happened: the cord by which the hanging occurred broke, and therefore he was carried forward onto the ground and had fallen.

— Oecumenius, Commentary on Acts, on 1:16–20. The commentary gives a broken cord as the mechanism joining the two notices: on this account Matthew reports the hanging, Acts the aftermath, and a broken cord joins them. The commentator adds that Acts’ placing the notice “some days later” is no obstacle, since it merely confirms that the death followed “immediately after the betrayal.”

Notes

  • The Latin parallel: Bede. In the West the Venerable Bede’s Commentary on Acts (c. 709–716), on 1:18, reads the death as one event — “the noose’s knot strangled the throat from which the voice of betrayal had emerged” — and reads Judas perishing “in the midst of the air” as fitting, “according to the example of Ahithophel and Absalom who persecuted King David.” Ahithophel is the counsellor who betrayed David and “hanged himself”; Kirsopp Lake (1933) derived Matthew’s hanging from the same story (see the modern-scholarship page).
  • Dating. The catena on Acts attributed to “Oecumenius” is conventionally placed in the sixth century; the name also attaches to a later (tenth-century) catena on the Catholic Epistles. Candida Moss (2019) dates “Oecumenius’ commentary on Acts” to the “late tenth century.” On either dating it precedes Theophylact, c. 1107.1
  • Modern commentators who read the notices as reconcilable, and those who read Acts’ wording as shaped by a scriptural pattern for the ruin of the wicked (Lake, on Wisdom 4:19), are set out on the modern-scholarship page.
