The Tomb of the Virgin (Church of the Sepulchre of St. Mary)

At the foot of the Mount of Olives, in the Kidron valley a stone’s throw from the garden of Gethsemane, a sunken church holds a rock-cut tomb-chamber venerated for some fifteen centuries as the burial place of the Virgin Mary — the spot from which, in the tradition of the churches that venerate it, her body was taken up to heaven. This page traces how that identification is attested: a location the New Testament does not mention, then an apocryphal burial-scene, a basilica, an empty tomb retold as an episode of a church council, a Crusader crypt that Saladin left standing, and a first-century Jewish tomb excavated in 1972 — alongside a modern rival, the “House of the Virgin” near Ephesus, which places Mary’s last days elsewhere.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who held a doctrine, but a chronicle of how one site-identification formed and was disputed. Its “witnesses” are therefore datable sources and events — an apocryphal Dormition narrative, a pilgrim’s road-log, a council-story embedded in a homily, a Frankish bishop’s travel-report, a Crusader queen’s burial, a Franciscan excavation, a German nun’s visions, and modern monographs — ordered earliest-source-first, each footnoted. The doctrine associated with the site — whether Mary was assumed body and soul into heaven — has its own timeline; this page follows the place.

Related doctrines: The Virgin Mary — see also the perpetual virginity of Mary · Mary as the New Eve · Theotokos (Mother of God) · the Immaculate Conception · the Assumption / Dormition

Timeline

The Gospels and Acts do not record Mary’s end (1st century)

The last the New Testament shows of Mary, she is among the disciples at prayer in Jerusalem: “these all continued with one accord in prayer and supplication, with the women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with his brethren.”1 From the cross Jesus had entrusted her to the beloved disciple — “Behold thy mother! And from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home”2 — the text on which the Ephesus tradition rests in part, its holders reasoning that John, who went to Ephesus, took Mary with him.3 No book of the New Testament records her death, a burial, a grave, or a translation.

The Dormition apocrypha bury Mary at Gethsemane (4th–6th centuries)

The earliest extant localization of the tomb is in narrative texts. The earliest surviving narratives of Mary’s end — the Liber Requiei (“Book of Mary’s Repose,” the oldest recoverable Dormition text, its underlying tradition reaching back into the fourth century)4 and the Syriac Six Books apocryphon5 — tell of the apostles gathered at her deathbed and her burial, and they are the earliest extant texts to localize the grave. On Stephen J. Shoemaker’s reconstruction the many Dormition narratives “come into sight at approximately the same time in the late fifth and early sixth centuries,” carrying with them a Palestinian cult that fixed Mary’s tomb in the Kidron valley beneath the Mount of Olives — the valley of Josaphat, at Gethsemane.6 Lino Cignelli (Franciscan biblical scholar, 2005) traces the narratives instead to “a single primitive document, a Judaeo-Christian prototype, clearly written within the mother church of Jerusalem some time during the second century, and, in all probability, composed for liturgical use right at the Tomb of Our Lady.”7 The Gelasian Decree (c. 494) listed a Transitus S. Mariae among the books “not received.”6 The place these narratives name is the place where the tomb has been venerated since.

A basilica in the valley: the Piacenza Pilgrim sees “a basilica of Saint Mary” (c. 570)

The earliest extant eyewitness report of a building at the site is that of the anonymous pilgrim from Piacenza (c. 570), who descends the Mount of Olives “into the valley of Gethsemane… And in that valley is a basilica of Saint Mary, where they say her house was, in which also she was taken up from her body.”8 A church on an octagonal footing over the tomb is attributed by the Wikipedia and Madain Project articles on the tomb to Patriarch Juvenal of Jerusalem, during the Emperor Marcian’s rule; an ascription to the empresses Pulcheria or Eudocia is reported as a later tradition. Jerome Murphy-O’Connor (2008), as cited in the Wikipedia article on the tomb, writes that a church there is mentioned only in the late sixth century.9 The pilgrim’s phrase names the site both as Mary’s reputed house and as the place of her being “taken up from her body.”

Arculf: the round two-storey church and the empty sepulchre (c. 680)

The fullest early description comes from the Frankish bishop Arculf, whose Holy-Land travels were written down at Iona by Abbot Adomnán in De locis sanctis (c. 680s). Arculf saw in the valley of Josaphat a church “built with wondrous roundness” in two storeys; in the lower church, “in the eastern portion of it is an altar, and at the right-hand side of the altar is the empty stone sepulchre of the holy Mary, where she was once laid to rest.” He then records: “But how, or when, or by what persons her holy remains were removed from this sepulchre, or where she awaits the resurrection, no one, as it is said, can know for certain.”10 His report describes a sunken, round tomb-church, the form the present crypt preserves, and records the sepulchre as venerated empty.

John of Damascus and the Euthymiac History: the empty-tomb report set at Chalcedon (c. 730; the scene set in 451)

A Greek account ties the Gethsemane tomb to a named moment of church history. John of Damascus, preaching at Gethsemane (c. 730), asks over the grave, “Why do you seek in the tomb one who has been assumed to the heavenly courts?”11 Appended to his second Dormition homily is an excerpt from the Euthymiac History, which reports that at the Council of Chalcedon (451) the Emperor Marcian and Empress Pulcheria asked Juvenal of Jerusalem for Mary’s bodily relics, and were told that Mary was buried in Gethsemane, her tomb later opened at the coming of an apostle who had arrived late and found empty — only the grave-clothes remaining, which were sent to Constantinople.12 Of the late apostle’s name, the Wikipedia article on the History, citing Phil Booth (2015), reports: “Although often called Thomas, that name does not appear to have been in the Euthymiac History.” The History is dated c. 550–750 by the Clavis Historicorum Antiquitatis Posterioris (2018) and by Shoemaker (2002), and as early as the early sixth century by Basile Lourié (2007); the Wikipedia article on the tomb ascribes it, probably, to Cyril of Scythopolis. The excerpt in John’s homily, the article on the History reports, “is usually regarded as an interpolation, although every surviving copy of the sermon includes it.”12 (Whether this chain of tradition reaches back to an apostolic memory is a question recorded among the Assumption timeline’s disputed readings.)

Ruin, Crusader crypt, and Saladin’s sparing of it (11th–12th centuries)

The upper fabric was destroyed and rebuilt more than once while the sunken tomb-chamber endured. The Byzantine church was damaged in the Persian invasion of 614 and the predecessor structure demolished under the Caliph al-Hakim (1009); in 1130 the Crusaders raised a new upper church and a Benedictine abbey, “St Mary of the Valley of Jehoshaphat,” on the staircase of which Queen Melisende of Jerusalem (d. 1161) and her mother were buried.13 When Saladin took Jerusalem in 1187 he pulled down the Crusaders’ upper church for its masonry but left the underground crypt and its rock-cut tomb-aedicule standing. The Wikipedia article on the tomb reports, in a sentence it marks as needing a citation, that through the church’s repeated destruction and rebuilding in later centuries the crypt was left untouched because, for Muslims, it is the burial place of the mother of the prophet ʿĪsā ⚠ unverified. The surviving Crusader stair of some forty-seven steps still descends to the tomb, held today under the Status Quo by the Greek Orthodox and Armenian Apostolic churches.13 Here the building changed hands and shapes over the centuries while the rock-cut tomb stayed in place.

The rival tradition: Ephesus and the “House of the Virgin” (19th century)

A second site associated with Mary’s last days lies above Ephesus. The German Augustinian nun Anne Catherine Emmerich (1774–1824) described, in visions recorded and published by Clemens Brentano in 1852, a house the Apostle John had built for Mary at Ephesus, where she lived to the end of her life; guided by those descriptions, the Abbé Julien Gouyet located ruins on a hill above Ephesus in 1881, and in 1891 the Lazarists Poulin and Jung found the building again and promoted it as the “House of the Virgin” (Meryém Ana Evi). Leo XIII blessed its first pilgrimage (1896), Pius XII named it a Holy Place (1951), and popes have since visited it; the Catholic Church has not pronounced on the house’s authenticity.14 On the age of the Ephesus tradition, See the Holy Land, reporting the Ephesus claim as one that “the Virgin Mary died and was buried in the city of Ephesus,” quotes the Catholic Encyclopedia as stating that “There was never any tradition connecting Mary’s death and burial with the city of Ephesus”;3 the Wikipedia article on the house reports that the discovery “revived and strengthened a Christian tradition dating from the 12th century, ‘the tradition of Ephesus’.” The same article reports that when Emmerich’s beatification case was submitted to the Vatican in 1892, experts in Germany comparing Brentano’s notes with his books found “various apocryphal biblical sources, maps and travel guides among his papers, which could have been used to enhance Emmerich’s narrations”; her cause was advanced on her holiness rather than on the visions ⚠ unverified.14

The spade: Bagatti’s excavation of a first-century Jewish tomb (1972)

After floods in 1972 stripped the overburden, the Franciscan archaeologist Bellarmino Bagatti (with Emmanuele Testa, and published with Michele Piccirillo) excavated the tomb-complex and concluded that the venerated chamber is part of a first-century Jewish rock-cut necropolis of the Second Temple period, its burials of the kokhim (loculi) and arcosolia type — the central tomb, he argued, an inner chamber later isolated from the surrounding graves by cutting away the rock and enclosing it in an aedicule.15 See the Holy Land summarizes his conclusion as being “that the place where Mary had been buried was clearly located in a cemetery used during the first century.”15 The Wikipedia article on the tomb notes that Bagatti’s findings “have not yet been subject to peer review by the wider archaeological community.”9

Modern studies of the site and its cult (1993, 2002)

Joan E. Taylor’s Christians and the Holy Places (1993) argues that the whole apparatus of venerated Holy Land sites — the Tomb of the Virgin among them — is a fourth-century-and-later Christian growth, not a continuous memory from the first century, so that on her reading the site attests the age of the cult, not of the burial.16 Stephen J. Shoemaker’s Ancient Traditions of the Virgin Mary’s Dormition and Assumption (2002) traces the Gethsemane tomb through the Palestinian Marian cult and the Dormition narratives that first localized it.6
