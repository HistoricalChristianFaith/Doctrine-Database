Augustine on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 415 (Augustine of Hippo, 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gives the classic Latin synthesis — the proud, envious fallen angel choosing the serpent as his mouthpiece — and reads Ezekiel 28 and Isaiah 14 of the devil, while soberly flagging the limits of the Isaiah reading.

Claims & evidence

The proud, envious fallen angel chose the serpent as his mouthpiece

But after that proud and therefore envious angel (of whose fall I have said as much as I was able in the eleventh and twelfth books of this work…), preferring to rule with a kind of pomp of empire rather than to be another's subject, fell from the spiritual Paradise, and essaying to insinuate his persuasive guile into the mind of man, whose unfallen condition provoked him to envy now that himself was fallen, he chose the serpent as his mouthpiece in that bodily Paradise… And this animal being subdued to his wicked ends by the presence and superior force of his angelic nature, he abused as his instrument, and first tried his deceit upon the woman…

— City of God XIV.11, source. The serpent is "this animal… abused as his instrument"; the fall and the envy belong to the angel, not the beast.

Ezekiel 28: "the devil was for a time without sin"

They have no reply to the witness of the prophets… where Ezekiel says, "You were in Eden, the garden of God; every precious stone was your covering." These texts indicate that the devil was for a time without sin.

— City of God XI.15, source. Augustine uses "You were in Eden, the garden of God" to establish that the devil was created good and fell — the anti-dualist point again.

Isaiah 14 read of the devil — but not every line fits him

what is said in Isaiah, "How he is fallen from heaven, Lucifer, son of the morning!" and the other statements in that context that speak of the king of Babylon are of course to be understood of the devil. However, the statement that is made in the same place, "He that sent orders to all nations is crushed on the earth," does not altogether fitly apply to the head himself.

— On Christian Doctrine III.37, source. Augustine reads "Lucifer" of the devil yet concedes that not every clause "altogether fitly" applies to the fallen spirit rather than the human king — an early flag of the very tension the modern argument pages weigh.

Notes

  • Augustine's "mouthpiece / instrument" is the mainstream patristic settlement: a real animal, a real fallen angel behind it — short of the modern claim that the serpent-figure itself is a rebel member of God's divine council.
