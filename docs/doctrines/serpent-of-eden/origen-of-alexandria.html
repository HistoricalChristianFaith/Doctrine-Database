Origen on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 248 (Origen of Alexandria, c. 184–253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reads Isaiah 14's Lucifer and Ezekiel 28's cherub as a "superior power" fallen from a former happiness, and argues that neither oracle can refer to a mere man.

Claims & evidence

Isaiah 14: "fallen from heaven, who formerly was Lucifer"

Most evidently by these words is he shown to have fallen from heaven, who formerly was Lucifer, and who used to arise in the morning. For if, as some think, he was a nature of darkness, how is Lucifer said to have existed before? Or how could he arise in the morning, who had in himself nothing of the light?… For at one time he was light.

— On First Principles (De Principiis) I.5.5, tr. Frederick Crombie (ANF 4), source. Origen's argument is partly anti-dualist: "at one time he was light," created good, not a being of darkness by nature.

Ezekiel 28: "some superior power which had fallen away… formerly holy and happy"

but as the second is manifestly of such a kind as cannot be at all understood of a man, but of some superior power which had fallen away from a higher position, and had been reduced to a lower and worse condition… We have shown, then, that what we have quoted regarding the prince of Tyre from the prophet Ezekiel refers to an adverse power, and by it it is most clearly proved that that power was formerly holy and happy; from which state of happiness it fell from the time that iniquity was found in it, and was hurled to the earth, and was not such by nature and creation.

— On First Principles (De Principiis) I.5.4, tr. Crombie (ANF 4), source. The one placed "among the delights of paradise" and "in the holy mount of God" cannot, for Origen, be the human prince of Tyre.

The Isaiah oracle cannot be a mere human king

And how could we possibly accept, as spoken of a man, what is related in many passages of Scripture, and especially in Isaiah, regarding Nebuchadnezzar? For he is not a man who is said to have “fallen from heaven,” or who was “Lucifer,” or who “arose in the morning.”

— On First Principles (De Principiis) IV.3.9, tr. Crombie (ANF 4), source. Origen names Nebuchadnezzar as the subject of the Isaiah passage and argues that its language cannot fit a human being.
