Origen on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 248 (Origen of Alexandria, c. 184–253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Supplies the locus classicus for reading the serpent's fall out of the prophets — Isaiah 14's Lucifer and Ezekiel 28's cherub as a "higher power" fallen from an original blessedness, and expressly denied to be a mere man.

Claims & evidence

Isaiah 14: "he who formerly was Lucifer… has fallen from heaven"

It is most clearly proved by these words that he who formerly was Lucifer and who "arose in the morning" has fallen from heaven. For if, as some suppose, he was a being of darkness, why is he said to have formerly been Lucifer or lightbearer? Or how could he "rise in the morning" who had in him no light at all?… So he was light once…

— On First Principles (De Principiis) I.5.5, source. Origen's argument is partly anti-dualist: the fallen power was "light once," created good, not a being of darkness by nature.

Ezekiel 28: "some higher power that fell… originally holy and blessed"

But since the second is most evidently of such a kind that it cannot possibly refer to a man but must be understood of some higher power that fell from higher places and was cast down to lower and worse ones… These statements, therefore, from the prophet Ezekiel concerning the prince of Tyre must relate, as we have shown, to an adverse power, and they prove in the clearest manner that this power was originally holy and blessed, and that he fell from this state of blessedness and was cast down into the earth "from the time that iniquity was found in him."

— On First Principles (De Principiis) I.5.4, source. The cherub "in the delights of the paradise of God… upon the holy mount of God" cannot, for Origen, be the human prince of Tyre.

The Isaiah oracle cannot be a mere human king

How can we possibly suppose that what is said in many places by Scripture, especially in Isaiah, about Nebuchadnezzar is said about a human being? For no human being is said to have "fallen from heaven" or to have been "Lucifer" or the one who "arose every morning."

— On First Principles (De Principiis) IV.3.9, source. Origen knows the plain historical referent and argues past it — the very move the modern serpent's-fall argument pages weigh.

Notes

  • Origen's interest is as much the origin of evil (the rebel was created good and fell by will) as the identity of the serpent; this is the framework the later tradition — Augustine, Jerome, Gregory — inherits and refines.
