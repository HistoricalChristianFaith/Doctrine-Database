Justin Martyr on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 160 (Justin Martyr, c. 100–165) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ties the names together — the serpent is the devil is Satan (etymologizing Satanas from sata, "apostate," and nas, "serpent") — and already places the serpent among the fallen heavenly "princes" of Psalm 82.

Claims & evidence

The serpent is the devil is Satan — the etymology

Or He meant the devil by the lion roaring against Him: whom Moses calls the serpent, but in Job and Zechariah he is called the devil, and by Jesus is addressed as Satan, showing that a compounded name was acquired by him from the deeds which he performed. For sata in the Jewish and Syrian tongue means apostate; and nas is the word from which he is called by interpretation the serpent, i.e., according to the interpretation of the Hebrew term, from both of which there arises the single word Satanas.… For as he had deceived Adam, so he hoped that he might contrive some mischief against Christ also.

— Dialogue with Trypho CIII, source. The one Moses calls "the serpent" is, for Justin, the single adversary the whole Bible names variously — and the very name "Satan" encodes his apostasy and his serpent-form.

The serpent among the fallen heavenly "princes"

…and the fall of one of the princes, i.e., of him who was called the serpent, who fell with a great overthrow, because he deceived Eve.

— Dialogue with Trypho CXXIV, source. Reading Psalm 82's "fall like one of the princes," Justin treats the serpent as one of the heavenly princes who fell — a striking early anticipation of the divine-being reading, even though (on the Divine Council timeline) he reads that Psalm's "gods" as men.

Notes

  • Justin is thus an early bridge between "the serpent is Satan" and "the serpent is a fallen heavenly prince." His fuller reading of Psalm 82's elohim as human beings is treated on his Divine Council page.
