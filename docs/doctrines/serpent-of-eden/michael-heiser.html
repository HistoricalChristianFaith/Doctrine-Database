Michael Heiser on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 2015 (Michael S. Heiser, 1963–2023) · Wikipedia
Summary: Revived and radicalized the ancient reading — the nachash a rebel divine being of the council, Eden the divine abode, Ezekiel 28 the guardian cherub's fall — while cautioning it is not a "Satan" tale but the fall of a rebel council-member.

Claims & evidence

The nachash is a divine being, in the divine council's abode

I believe the nachash of Genesis 3 was a divine being. The New Testament makes this explicitly clear (Rev. 12:9; 20:2).

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), ch. 11, source. Heiser argues the setting decides it: "the nachash is to be viewed differently than Balaam's donkey for a simple contextual reason: the incident with the nachash takes place in the divine council abode." Eden is God's dwelling among the heavenly host, so the intruder is a rebel council-member, not a snake.

The word-play: serpent, diviner, "shining one"

the lemma nachash would have (intentionally so) brought to mind other elements of the cognitive framework of the original readers: the dispensing of divine knowledge (the verb form) and luminescence (nachash is of the same root as nechoshet ["copper, bronze"])

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), ch. 11, source. Hence the "shining one" overtone; but Heiser stresses that "serpent" remains a fine translation and his point is that the figure "is not a member of the animal kingdom."

Ezekiel 28: the guardian cherub on the mountain of God

the "stones of fire" are on the holy mountain of God, a reference to the Garden of Eden.

— "The Stones of Fire in Ezekiel 28", source. Heiser reads the anointed guardian cherub as the same Edenic rebel, while cautioning the claim is "not that Ezekiel 28 draws upon a tale about a being called Satan… Rather… a divine being who rebelled."
