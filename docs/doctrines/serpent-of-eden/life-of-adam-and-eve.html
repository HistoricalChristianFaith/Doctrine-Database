The Life of Adam and Eve on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 1st c. AD (Jewish pseudepigraphon; the Latin Vita Adae et Evae and the Greek Apocalypse of Moses) · Wikipedia
Summary: Makes the serpent the instrument of a fallen heavenly being whose sin is pride and envy — cast out of his glory for refusing to worship the newly-made Adam, then using the serpent as his "vessel."

Claims & evidence

The devil cast out of heaven for refusing to worship Adam, out of envy

O Adam! all my hostility, envy, and sorrow is for thee, since it is for thee that I have been expelled from my glory, which I possessed in the heavens in the midst of the angels and for thee was I cast out in the earth.… And with guile I cheated thy wife and caused thee to be expelled through her (doing) from thy joy and luxury, as I have been driven out of my glory.

— Life of Adam and Eve (Vita Adae et Evae) 12, 16, tr. R. H. Charles (1913), source. The devil's own boast in the intervening chapter — "I will set my seat above the stars of heaven and will be like the Highest" (ch. 15) — is the same overreach Isaiah 14 puts in Lucifer's mouth.

The serpent made the devil's mouthpiece

And the devil saith to him: "Fear not, only be my vessel and I will speak through thy mouth words to deceive him."

— Apocalypse of Moses 16, tr. R. H. Charles (1913), source. The serpent is a vehicle; the tempter is the fallen being who speaks through it.

Notes

  • This is the fullest early narrative of the motive — envy at Adam's dignity — that Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Augustine will echo. It keeps the beast and the fallen spirit distinct, the pattern the patristic mainstream follows.
