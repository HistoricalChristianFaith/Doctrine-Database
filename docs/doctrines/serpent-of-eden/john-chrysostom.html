John Chrysostom on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 388 (John Chrysostom, c. 347–407; the Homilies on Genesis preached at Antioch) · Wikipedia
Summary: The mainstream literal-serpent foil — the serpent is emphatically a real beast, an "irrational animal" the devil "employed like some convenient instrument," and Chrysostom pointedly rejects that the animal had any power of speech of its own. The tempter is the fallen devil; the snake is only his tool. Nothing in the reptile is a divine being.

Claims & evidence

The serpent is an "instrument," the devil the agent

See the evil spirit's envy and devious scheming… The author of evil, accordingly, seeing an angel who happened to live on earth, was consumed by envy, since he himself had once enjoyed a place among the powers above but had been cast down from that pinnacle on account of his depravity of will and excess of wickedness… What did he do? He discovered this wild animal, namely, the serpent… He made use of this creature like some instrument and through it inveigled that naive and weaker vessel, namely, woman, into his deception by means of conversation.

— Homilies on Genesis 16, on Genesis 3:1, tr. Robert C. Hill (Fathers of the Church). Chrysostom keeps two agents strictly apart: the fallen devil — "cast down from that pinnacle," an apostate of the "powers above" — and the serpent, a mere "wild animal" and "creature" used "like some instrument." The rebel spirit is the one who fell from heaven; the beast is only his vehicle.

The animals did not share the power of speech — "perish the thought"

But perhaps in this case some may raise a difficulty and seek to find out if the wild animals also shared the power of speech. Not so—perish the thought; rather, people, following Scripture, need to consider the fact that the words came from the devil, who was spurred on to this deception by his own ill will, while this wild animal he employed like some convenient instrument… So he employs this irrational animal for laying his plan.

— Homilies on Genesis 16, on Genesis 3:1. This is the sharpest edge of the literal reading against any account that would locate the marvel in the serpent: the beast has no voice of its own ("perish the thought"), it is an "irrational animal," and every word is the devil's, spoken through it. The snake is not an intelligent, still less a divine, being.

The visible serpent points past itself to the invisible devil

…the good God, too, sentenced this creature to an eternal punishment, when like some sword, he served the purpose of the devil's villainy, so that we might reason from this evident and visible punishment to the depths of dishonor in which the devil also found himself. After all, if this creature who played the part of an instrument suffered such frustration, what kind of punishment is it likely that the devil received?

…if this is the story of the serpent that is visible, much more should the words be understood to refer also to the serpent perceptible only to the mind.

— Homilies on Genesis 17, on Genesis 3:14. Chrysostom's own two-level scheme runs the opposite way to the "shining one" reading. There is a "serpent that is visible" — the corporeal beast, "like some sword" in the devil's hand — and behind it "the serpent perceptible only to the mind," the devil himself. The identity of the tempter with the fallen spirit is affirmed; but it is reached by looking past the animal, never by reading the animal as anything more than a real reptile "cursed beyond all the beasts."

Notes

  • Chrysostom holds the two great commitments of the ancient tradition — a real snake, and a fallen supernatural tempter behind it — but he draws the line exactly where the modern nachash-as-divine-being reading crosses it. For Chrysostom the divine-being language belongs to the devil (who "had been cast down from heaven to earth for his envy and overweening arrogance"), never to the serpent, which stays a beast to be trodden underfoot.
  • He is thus a clean, datable foil for the timeline's contested question: the serpent-as-supernatural-tempter is secure and near-universal, but Chrysostom is a leading witness for the mainstream reading that the serpent itself is an ordinary animal — the Satan-identification attaching to the spirit that used it, not to the reptile.
