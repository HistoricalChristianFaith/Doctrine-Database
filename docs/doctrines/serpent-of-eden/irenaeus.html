Irenaeus on the Serpent of Eden

Dates: c. 180 (Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 130–202) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gives the fullest early account, fusing the apostate angel, envy as motive, and the serpent as the carrier of the hidden Slanderer.

Claims & evidence

The envious apostate angel, and the serpent that "carried and conveyed" him

This commandment the man kept not, but was disobedient to God, being led astray by the angel who, for the great gifts of God which He had given to man, was envious and jealous of him, and both brought himself to nought and made man sinful, persuading him to disobey the commandment of God. So the angel, becoming by his falsehood the author and originator of sin, himself was struck down, having offended against God, and man he caused to be cast out from Paradise. And, because through the guidance of his disposition he apostatized and departed from God, he was called Satan, according to the Hebrew word; that is, Apostate: … Now God cursed the serpent which carried and conveyed the Slanderer; and this malediction came on the beast himself and on the angel hidden and concealed in him, even on Satan…

— The Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 16, source. The motive is envy at "the great gifts of God" given to man — the same envy the Life of Adam and Eve dramatizes — and the curse falls both on "the beast himself" and on "the angel hidden and concealed in him."

Notes

  • Like Ephrem and Augustine after him, Irenaeus keeps the animal distinct from the hidden fallen angel — the serpent "carries and conveys" a being that is not itself a snake.
