The Islamic elaboration and the identity debate

Dates: Wahb ibn Munabbih (d. c. 732) → al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) and later · Encyclopaedia Iranica
Summary: Because the Qur’an gives only the epithet, the commentators had to say who Dhū’l-Qarnayn was. The mainstream took him for Alexander; a Yemeni counter-tradition, uneasy at a pagan Greek, made him a pious South-Arabian king instead — but gave him a biography borrowed from the Alexander Romance.

Claims & evidence

The mainstream: Dhū’l-Qarnayn is Alexander (Iskandar)

The dominant line of classical exegesis, al-Ṭabarī foremost, identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn with Alexander the Great — the identification the surrounding legend everywhere implies, and the one modern scholarship confirms on the strength of the Syriac-Legend parallels. This is the reading assumed by the whole Gog-and-Magog material that the commentators wove around the passage.

— Encyclopaedia of the Qur’ān (Leiden: Brill), s.vv. “Alexander” and “Ḏū l-Qarnayn”; for the Persian and Islamic reception, Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Alexander the Great.”

“The two horns” read as the two extremities

Where the epithet itself was concerned, al-Ṭabarī offered a reading that sidestepped the pagan iconography: the “two horns” are the two ends of the world — east and west, the setting and the rising of the sun — that Dhū’l-Qarnayn reached in his journeys. Other traditions took the horns literally, or as two locks of hair, or as a sign of rule over two ages or two peoples.

The South-Arabian counter-tradition: al-Ṣaʿb the Himyarite

Not all were content to see a Qur’anic near-prophet in Alexander — a pagan, and the pupil of Aristotle. A Yemeni counter-tradition, traced to the traditionist Wahb ibn Munabbih and reported through Ibn Hishām (with support claimed from Ibn ʿAbbās and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār), identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn instead with a pre-Islamic Himyarite king, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid. The argument was onomastic — “Dhū” is a characteristic South-Arabian name-element, and pre-Islamic poets were said to call al-Ṣaʿb “Dhū’l-Qarnayn.” But the life they gave him — prophetic dreams, a summons to meet al-Khiḍr, world-conquest, the walling-off of Gog and Magog, death at Babylon — is transparently the Alexander Romance reassigned to a Yemeni hero, part of a broader South-Arabian appropriation of prestige-legends.

— Wikipedia, “Ṣaʿb Dhū Marāthid” (with the underlying Yemeni-tradition scholarship footnoted); and “Alexander the Great in Islamic tradition.”

Other assimilations

The exegetical tradition floated further candidates — the Parthian “Kisrūnis,” the Lakhmid king al-Mundhir ibn Imruʾ al-Qays — each an attempt to attach the anonymous epithet to a known figure. None displaced the Alexander identification, which remained both the majority classical view and the scholarly one.
