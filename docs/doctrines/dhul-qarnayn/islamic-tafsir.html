The Islamic elaboration and the identity debate

Dates: Wahb ibn Munabbih (d. c. 732) → al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) and later · Encyclopaedia Iranica
Summary: Because the Qur’an gives only the epithet, the commentators had to say who Dhū’l-Qarnayn was. The dominant line took him for Alexander; a Yemeni tradition made him a pious South-Arabian king instead, with a biography that parallels the Alexander Romance.

Claims & evidence

The dominant classical reading: Dhū’l-Qarnayn is Alexander (Iskandar)

The dominant line of classical exegesis, al-Ṭabarī foremost, identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn with Alexander the Great, the identification also made by modern scholars from Nöldeke to van Bladel on the strength of the Syriac-Legend parallels. This is the reading assumed by the whole Gog-and-Magog material that the commentators wove around the passage.

— Encyclopaedia of the Qur’ān (Leiden: Brill), s.vv. “Alexander” and “Ḏū l-Qarnayn”; for the Persian and Islamic reception, Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Alexander the Great.”

“The two horns” read as the two extremities

Where the epithet itself was concerned, al-Ṭabarī offered this reading: the “two horns” are the two ends of the world — east and west, the setting and the rising of the sun — that Dhū’l-Qarnayn reached in his journeys. Other traditions took the horns literally, or as two locks of hair, or as a sign of rule over two ages or two peoples.

The South-Arabian tradition: al-Ṣaʿb the Himyarite

A Yemeni tradition, traced to the traditionist Wahb ibn Munabbih and reported through Ibn Hishām (with support claimed from Ibn ʿAbbās and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār), identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn instead with a pre-Islamic Himyarite king, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid. The argument was onomastic — “Dhū” is a characteristic South-Arabian name-element, and pre-Islamic poets were said to call al-Ṣaʿb “Dhū’l-Qarnayn.” The life the tradition gives him — prophetic dreams, a summons to meet al-Khiḍr, world-conquest, the walling-off of Gog and Magog, death at Babylon — parallels the Alexander Romance episode by episode, and the sources below set it among a wider South-Arabian adoption of prestige-legends.

— Wikipedia, “Ṣaʿb Dhū Marāthid” (with the underlying Yemeni-tradition scholarship footnoted); and “Alexander the Great in Islamic tradition.”

Other assimilations

The exegetical tradition floated further candidates — the Parthian “Kisrūnis,” the Lakhmid king al-Mundhir ibn Imruʾ al-Qays — each attaching the epithet to a known figure.

Notes

  • The ascription to Wahb. Tilman Nagel doubts that Wahb’s text included the South-Arabian identification, noting that al-Ṭabarī drew on Wahb’s Alexander story yet gives no Himyarite one; it may be Ibn Hishām’s ascription (Wikipedia, “Ṣaʿb Dhū Marāthid,” citing Nagel).
  • al-Khiḍr recurs here. The al-Ṣaʿb biography’s “summons to meet al-Khiḍr” is the same “Green One” the commentators place in the sura’s third narrative, Moses’ quest to the junction of the two seas (18:60–82), whose reviving-fish / water-of-life frame Friedländer, Wensinck and van Bladel connect with the Alexander legend and Wheeler does not: Does the al-Kahf Moses–al-Khiḍr pericope draw on the Alexander water-of-life legend? The same figure thus appears both in the al-Ṣaʿb biography and in the exegesis of the Moses pericope.
  • The identity question is a disputed reading: Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn Alexander the Great? The commentators also split over 18:86 — whether the sun literally sets in the muddy/hot spring, or only appears to (al-Khaṭṭābī, al-Rāzī, the Jalālayn, and Ibn Kathīr read it perspectivally, against the more literal early reports): Does Qur’an 18:86 assert that the sun literally sets in a “spring of dark mud”?
