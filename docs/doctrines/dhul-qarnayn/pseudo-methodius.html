The apocalyptic afterlife: Pseudo-Methodius, the Last Roman Emperor, and the release of Gog and Magog

Dates: Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius c. 690–692; the Islamic malāḥim/fitan tradition from the later 7th c. onward; Sallām al-Tarjumān’s quest c. 842–845 · GEDSH, “Methodius, Apocalypse of Pseudo-”
Summary: The sealed gate outlived every text that carried it. Where the earlier witnesses trace how the Alexander legend reached the Qur’an, this page traces the motif forward: the Syriac Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius made the breaking of Alexander’s gate a herald of the Last Days and gave medieval Christendom its Last Roman Emperor; Islamic eschatology made the release of Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj one of the great signs of the Hour. One sealed prophecy became a load-bearing element of both Christian and Muslim end-times expectation.

Claims & evidence

The Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (Syriac, c. 690–692)

Within a generation of the Arab conquests an anonymous Syriac-Orthodox monk wrote a work of consolation and prophecy under the borrowed name of Methodius of Olympus (a martyr-bishop dead c. 311, and so no possible author — hence “Pseudo-Methodius”). It survives in Syriac and was composed about 690–692, during the reign of the caliph ʿAbd al-Malik — a moment of fiscal pressure and mass Christian apostasy that the text answers with the promise of deliverance. Its preface sets the seer’s vision on “the mountain of Senegar” (Sinjar, in northern Mesopotamia); whether that names the real place of composition is now questioned. The book was translated into Greek almost at once, and thence into Latin by “Peter the Monk” (Petrus Monachus) in the early 8th century; more than two hundred Latin manuscripts survive, and it became one of the most-copied and most-influential apocalypses of the entire Middle Ages.

— G. J. Cameron & L. I. Conrad (eds.), The Byzantine and Early Islamic Near East I (Princeton: Darwin Press, 1992), 149–187; overview at the Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage, “Methodius, Apocalypse of Pseudo-.” English translation: B. Garstad, Apocalypse (of Pseudo-Methodius); An Alexandrian World Chronicle, Dumbarton Oaks Medieval Library 14 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard Univ. Press, 2012).

Alexander’s gate as a sign of the End

Pseudo-Methodius takes the sealed prophecy of the Syriac Christian Legend and turns it into a hinge of world history. Alexander, it recounts, shut the twenty-two unclean nations of Gog and Magog behind a gate in the northern mountains. At the appointed time God will command that gate to open; the penned nations will burst out and devastate the earth — and their release is set among the very last events before the end, exactly as the Legend’s written prophecy foretold. The motif that in the Qur’an is a barrier standing “until the promise of my Lord comes” is here spelled out as an eschatological timetable.

— Garstad, Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (2012), §§ 8–13; E. van Donzel & A. Schmidt, Gog and Magog in Early Eastern Christian and Islamic Sources (Leiden: Brill, 2010).

The Last Roman Emperor

Pseudo-Methodius’s most consequential invention is the figure of the Last Roman Emperor (the “King of the Greeks/Romans”), unknown before it and canonical after. The schema runs: the emperor rouses in wrath and crushes the Ishmaelites (the Arabs); a golden age of peace follows; then Gog and Magog break out through Alexander’s opened gate and are destroyed by God; the emperor journeys to Jerusalem, ascends Golgotha, lays his crown upon the Holy Cross, stretches out his hands, and surrenders the kingdom to God as he dies; the Cross and crown are taken up to heaven; with the “restrainer” (the katechon of 2 Thessalonians 2) thus removed, the Antichrist is at last revealed. The breaking of Alexander’s gate is not a stray marvel here but a fixed station on that road to the end. Through the Latin translation this scheme shaped Adso of Montier-en-Der, the crusade preaching, and Latin eschatology for the next eight centuries.

— Reinink, “Pseudo-Methodius: A Concept of History” (1992); Garstad, Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (2012); overview at Wikipedia, “Last Roman Emperor” and “Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius.”

Islamic eschatology: the release of Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj as a sign of the Hour

On the Muslim side the same barrier became one of the major signs of the Hour (ashrāṭ al-sāʿa). The Qur’an itself already points that way: Dhū’l-Qarnayn’s wall will be levelled “when the promise of my Lord comes” (18:98), and 21:96 foretells “when Gog and Magog are let loose and they swarm down from every height.” The hadith literature develops this into a vivid end-times sequence:

  • The Prophet warns that “a hole has been opened today in the barrier of Gog and Magog” — he made a ring with thumb and forefinger — a gap already widening toward the end (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3346; also 7135; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2880).
  • The penned nations dig at the barrier each day until they nearly break through, then say “we shall finish tomorrow” and it is restored overnight — until, at the last, they add “if God wills” (in shāʾ Allāh) and break out (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4080; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3153).
  • In the long hadith of al-Nawwās b. Samʿān their release is tied to the return of Jesus (ʿĪsā): the believers take refuge on Mount al-Ṭūr while Gog and Magog swarm the earth — the first of them drinking Lake Tiberias dry — until God sends al-naghaf (worms) into their necks and destroys them (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2937).

This matter was gathered and elaborated in the malāḥim (apocalyptic battles) and fitan (tribulations) literature — the earliest surviving Muslim apocalyptic compilation being the Kitāb al-Fitan of Nuʿaym ibn Ḥammād al-Marwazī (d. 843).

— Qur’an 18:98–99 and 21:96 (tr. Sahih International); the hadith at the sunnah.com links above; on the genre, Nuʿaym ibn Ḥammād, Kitāb al-Fitan, tr. D. Cook, The Book of Tribulations: The Syrian Muslim Apocalyptic Tradition (Edinburgh Univ. Press, 2017).

Sallām the Interpreter’s quest for the gate (c. 842–845)

The expectation was concrete enough to launch an expedition. After a dream, the Abbasid caliph al-Wāthiq (r. 842–847) sent Sallām al-Tarjumān (“the Interpreter”) north and east from Sāmarrāʾ to find Alexander’s barrier against Gog and Magog and report on its condition. The account — preserved by the geographer Ibn Khurradādhbih in his Kitāb al-Masālik wa’l-mamālik — has Sallām reach a great iron gate on the far northeastern frontier (its identification wandering, in the tradition, all the way to the Chinese frontier) and find it intact. It is the centerpiece of van Donzel & Schmidt’s study, and a measure of how far the sealed-gate motif had migrated from the Caucasus of the Syriac Legend.

— E. van Donzel & A. Schmidt (with C. Ott), Gog and Magog in Early Eastern Christian and Islamic Sources: Sallam’s Quest for Alexander’s Wall (Leiden: Brill, 2010); full text.
