The Greek Alexander Romance of Pseudo-Callisthenes

Dates: c. 3rd c. AD (oldest surviving Greek recension, α) · overview
Summary: The fountainhead of the whole tradition: a legendary Greek biography of Alexander, falsely ascribed to his court historian Callisthenes, from which every later branch — Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopic, Latin, Persian — descends. It is not yet the apocalyptic Alexander of the Gog-and-Magog wall; that grows later, in Christian hands.

Claims & evidence

A legend, not a history — and not by Callisthenes

The Alexander Romance is an ancient Greek novelistic life of Alexander, a farrago of authentic tradition, folklore, invented letters, and marvels. It circulated under the name of Callisthenes, Alexander’s actual campaign historian — but Callisthenes was executed by Alexander in 327 BC, before the king’s own death, and so cannot have written a biography that runs to Alexander’s end. The attribution is spurious, and scholarship calls the unknown author Pseudo-Callisthenes.

— Attalus, “The Alexander Romance” (recensions and transmission).

Date and recensions

The work is composite: some of its materials are early Hellenistic, others late imperial. Its oldest surviving Greek form, the α (alpha) recension, is generally dated to about the third century AD and survives in a single eleventh-century manuscript; later Greek recensions (β, γ, and others) expand and rework it. Estimates of the underlying materials range widely — Richard Stoneman would carry some elements back toward the third century BC, Wilhelm Kroll placed the compilation as late as the fourth century AD — but the α recension is the standard anchor for “the Greek Romance.”

— K. Nawotka, The Alexander Romance by Ps.-Callisthenes: A Historical Commentary (Brill, 2017), reviewed in Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2018.04.47.

The fountainhead of a millennium of retellings

From Pseudo-Callisthenes the tale branched into nearly every literature of the medieval world — the Armenian and Ethiopic versions, the Latin of Julius Valerius and (via the Archpriest Leo) the Historia de preliis, the Persian Iskandarnāma tradition, and, decisive for our purposes, the Syriac. It is the reason a single figure could be recognisably “the same” from Greek paganism to Islamic scripture.

What is not yet here: the wall against Gog and Magog

The feature that would matter most downstream is absent from the earliest Greek form. The eschatological iron gate that pens the unclean nations Gog and Magog in the north — the heart of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode — is a later apocalyptic, Christianizing graft onto the legend, prominent only in the derivative traditions (the Syriac Legend, the Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius, the Ethiopic). The Greek Romance gives the world-conqueror and his marvellous journeys; Syriac Christianity gives him the wall and the prophecy.

— E. van Donzel & A. Schmidt, Gog and Magog in Early Eastern Christian and Islamic Sources (Leiden: Brill, 2010), ch. 1.
