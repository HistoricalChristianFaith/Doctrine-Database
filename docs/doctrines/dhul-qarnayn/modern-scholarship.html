Modern scholarship: the chain traced, the dating argued

Dates: Nöldeke (1890) → Reinink (1985–2002) → van Bladel (2008) → Tesei (2023) · Nöldeke 1890
Summary: A century and a half of philology established that the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn retells the Syriac Alexander Legend, and argued out when the Legend was written — a debate still live, but one that leaves the dependence itself intact.

Claims & evidence

Nöldeke (1890): the source identified

The year after Budge’s 1889 edition put the Syriac texts in reach, Theodor Nöldeke devoted a study to the Alexanderroman and, in a few decisive pages, first argued that the Syriac Alexander Legend is the source of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode (18:83–102). He placed the Legend’s origin in north Mesopotamia, about 629–630 AD, just after Heraclius’s victory over Persia.

— T. Nöldeke, Beiträge zur Geschichte des Alexanderromans (Vienna: Denkschriften der kaiserlichen Akademie der Wissenschaften, Bd. 38, 1890).

Reinink (1985–2002): the Legend as Heraclian propaganda

G. J. Reinink turned Nöldeke’s dating into a full historical thesis: the Legend is a piece of Heraclian propaganda, composed by a Mesopotamian Christian shortly after the emperor’s triumph over Khosrow II, using the Alexander-figure and its vaticinium ex eventu to proclaim a Christian Roman world-empire on the eve of the end — and, plausibly, to draw Syrian Miaphysites back toward Constantinople. The 629/630 anchor rests on the internal prophecy, whose “prediction” runs out just then.

— G. J. Reinink, “Die Entstehung der syrischen Alexanderlegende als politisch-religiöse Propagandaschrift für Herakleios’ Kirchenpolitik” (1985), and “Heraclius, the New Alexander” (2002); see the Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage, “Alexander Cycle.”

Van Bladel (2008): the renewed comparison

Kevin van Bladel renewed Nöldeke’s thesis with a systematic, point-by-point comparison, concluding that “almost every element of this short Qur’anic tale finds a more explicit and detailed counterpart in the Syriac Alexander Legend,” and that the Qur’anic passage “is a retelling of the story found in this particular Syriac text.” He weighed and rejected the alternatives — the Syriac deriving from the Qur’an, or both from a lost common source — and adopted the c. 630 dating as “not seriously disputed.”

“… the Qur’an 18:83–102 is a retelling of the story found in this particular Syriac text.”

— K. van Bladel, “The Alexander Legend in the Qur’ān 18:83–102,” in G. S. Reynolds (ed.), The Qur’ān in Its Historical Context (London: Routledge, 2008), 175–203, full text.

Tesei (2023): the dating re-opened

Tommaso Tesei’s monograph re-dates the core Legend to the reign of Justinian (527–565), roughly a century before the Heraclian consensus, arguing that a later redactor interpolated the short end-times prophecy under Heraclius about 629–630. This revises Reinink’s “born-as-propaganda” framework — and Tesei’s own earlier position — but, as van Bladel notes in praising the book, it leaves the Legend→Qur’an relationship standing: an earlier Legend still predates the Qur’an. Current opinion thus spans the classic 629–630 (Nöldeke, Reinink, van Bladel) and Tesei’s Justinianic core with a Heraclian graft — a genuine, respectable dispute.

— T. Tesei, The Syriac Legend of Alexander’s Gate: Apocalypticism at the Crossroads of Byzantium and Iran (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2023), introduction; review at Bibliographia Iranica.
