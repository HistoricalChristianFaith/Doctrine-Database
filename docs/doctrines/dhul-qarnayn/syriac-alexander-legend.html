The Syriac Alexander tradition: the Romance, the Christian Legend, and the Poem

Dates: Syriac Romance c. 6th–7th c.; the Christian Legend (Neṣḥānā) c. 629–630 (Nöldeke/Reinink) or mid-6th c. (Tesei); the verse Poem soon after · Budge 1889
Summary: In the Christian East the conqueror becomes a monotheist and an apocalyptic prophet: the short prose Christian Legend concerning Alexander is the earliest extant text in which the wall against Gog and Magog and the end-times prophecy appear together. Nöldeke, Reinink and van Bladel identify it as the source of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode.

Claims & evidence

Three Syriac texts, one edition

The Syriac Alexander material was edited and translated in a single Victorian volume by E. A. Wallis Budge in 1889. It contains, among other pieces, (1) a long Syriac Alexander Romance, a rendering of Pseudo-Callisthenes into Syriac (perhaps by way of a lost Middle Persian intermediary), of uncertain but roughly sixth-to-seventh-century date; (2) the short prose Christian Legend concerning Alexander — the Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs, “the Glorious Deeds of Alexander”; and (3) a verse Alexander Poem, a mēmrā once ascribed to Jacob of Serugh.

— E. A. Wallis Budge, The History of Alexander the Great, being the Syriac Version of the Pseudo-Callisthenes (Cambridge, 1889), full text (the Christian Legend at pp. 144–158).

The Christian Legend: Alexander as a monotheist prophet

In the Neṣḥānā Alexander is no longer the son of Ammon but a servant of the one God. He vows his kingdom to God, asks to be shown the ends of the earth, and sets out: to the fetid (foul) sea at the place where the sun sets, and to the place where the sun rises; then to a mountain pass on the northern frontier. There, to shut out the Huns and the unclean nations of Gog and Magog, he builds a great gate of iron and bronze, and seals it with a written prophecy: the barrier will hold until an appointed time, when God will open it and the penned nations will pour out before the end of the world — and, before that, a Roman (Byzantine) world-empire will triumph over the Persians and hand its kingdom to the Messiah.

— “A Christian Legend concerning Alexander,” tr. Budge, in The History of Alexander the Great (1889), pp. 144–158.

Dating: Heraclian propaganda — or older?

Theodor Nöldeke (1890) placed the Legend in north Mesopotamia about 629–630 AD, and G. J. Reinink built this into a full thesis: the Legend is a piece of Heraclian propaganda, composed just after the emperor Heraclius’s victory over the Sasanian Khosrow II (628), using the Alexander-figure and its vaticinium ex eventu to legitimate a Christian Roman world-empire on the eve of the end. Kevin van Bladel adopted this dating; Tommaso Tesei (2023) has since argued that the core Legend belongs to the reign of Justinian (527–565), with the Heraclian-era prophecy interpolated by a later redactor. The dispute is set out under modern scholarship.

— T. Nöldeke, Beiträge zur Geschichte des Alexanderromans (Vienna, 1890); G. J. Reinink, “Heraclius, the New Alexander” (2002); T. Tesei, The Syriac Legend of Alexander’s Gate (Oxford, 2023).

The verse Alexander Poem (Pseudo-Jacob)

Close on the prose Legend follows a metrical homily on Alexander, a mēmrā long attributed to Jacob of Serugh (d. 521), who died about a century before the early-630s date, shortly after the Legend, that its editor G. J. Reinink assigns to the poem; Reinink and others speak of “Pseudo-Jacob.” The poem adds distinctive matter of its own — Alexander’s journey into the Land of Darkness in quest of the Water of Life, in which an old man tells the cook to wash a salted fish at every spring until, where it revives, he finds the water of immortality. Friedländer, Wensinck and van Bladel connect that reviving-fish episode with the sura’s third narrative, Moses’ quest to the junction of the two seas and his meeting with al-Khiḍr, and Wheeler disputes the connection: Does the al-Kahf Moses–al-Khiḍr pericope draw on the Alexander water-of-life legend? Its date is tied to the Legend’s.

— G. J. Reinink, Das syrische Alexanderlied. Die drei Rezensionen, CSCO 454–455 (1983); overview at the Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage, “Alexander Cycle.”
