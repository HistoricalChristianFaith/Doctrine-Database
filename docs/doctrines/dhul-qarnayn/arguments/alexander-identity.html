Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the two-horned world-ruler of Sūrat al-Kahf is, on a scholarly near-consensus, the legendary Alexander of the Syriac Alexander Legend: the epithet, the journeys, the world-rule and the Gog-and-Magog wall are all Alexander’s, and the classical Muslim mainstream (al-Ṭabarī) agreed. The rival South-Arabian identification arose from discomfort at a pagan identity, not from independent evidence · advanced by mainstream scholarship on the Qur’an’s narrative sources.

Related arguments: the Alexander connection — see also Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

The claim

The figure the Qur’an calls Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the two-horned one” (18:83–101), is Alexander the Great — not the historian’s Macedonian king, but the fabulous Alexander of the Alexander Romance as remade by Syriac Christianity. The identification is both the majority verdict of the classical Muslim commentators and the settled position of modern scholarship.

Who advances it

The Alexander identification is the mainstream classical reading — al-Ṭabarī and the bulk of the tafsīr tradition — and the near-unanimous modern one, from Theodor Nöldeke (1890) through G. J. Reinink to Kevin van Bladel. It follows directly from the recognition that the Qur’anic episode retells the Syriac Alexander Legend.

The evidence for

The two horns. The epithet answers to Alexander’s own iconography: on Hellenistic coinage he wears the ram-horns of Zeus-Ammon, and the Syriac Legend itself has God set horns on Alexander’s head. The “possessor of the two horns” is a title made for him.1

The whole story is Alexander’s. The world-dominion “upon the earth,” the journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun, the people at the mountain pass, and above all the iron-and-copper wall against Gog and Magog are the defining episodes of the Syriac Legend of Alexander. A figure who does all that Alexander does, in Alexander’s order, is Alexander.

The commentators said so. The dominant classical exegesis identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn with Iskandar (Alexander) outright; the identification is not a modern imposition on an innocent text but the reading the Muslim tradition itself mostly gave.

Adversarial assessment

The South-Arabian alternative. A Yemeni counter-tradition (Wahb ibn Munabbih via Ibn Hishām) made Dhū’l-Qarnayn a pre-Islamic Himyarite king, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid, arguing that “Dhū” is a South-Arabian name-element. But this reading is secondary in every sense: it arose from unease at identifying a Qur’anic near-prophet with a pagan Greek, the pupil of Aristotle; the biography it assigns al-Ṣaʿb (prophetic dreams, al-Khiḍr, world-conquest, the wall, death at Babylon) is itself lifted from the Alexander Romance and reattached to a Yemeni hero; and at least one strand of scholarship doubts the identification is even authentically Wahb’s. It is an apologetic substitution, not an independent line of evidence.2

The one distinction worth keeping. “Dhū’l-Qarnayn is Alexander” is a claim about the figure’s literary source, and at that level it is secure. It is not the claim that the Qur’an endorses the historical Macedonian as a saint or prophet — the text asserts no such thing; it takes over a story, not a canonization. Conversely, the later Muslim discomfort was aimed at exactly that further claim, which the text never makes. Keeping the two apart dissolves most of the traditional objection: one can grant the Alexander of the legend as the source without importing the Alexander of history as an object of veneration.

Assessment

Sound. On the epithet, the episodes, and the order, the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn is the Alexander of the Syriac Legend — the majority classical reading and the modern near-consensus alike. The South-Arabian alternative is a later, evidence-poor substitution driven by theological discomfort, and it borrows Alexander’s own biography even as it denies him the name. The only care needed is to keep the secure claim (literary source) apart from a claim the text never makes (endorsement of the historical king).
