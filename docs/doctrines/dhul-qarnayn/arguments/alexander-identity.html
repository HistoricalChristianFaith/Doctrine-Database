Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101

Related readings: Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

The passage

“And they ask you, [O Muḥammad], about Dhū’l-Qarnayn. Say, ‘I will recite to you about him a report.’” (v. 83)

“Indeed We established him upon the earth, and We gave him to everything a way.” (v. 84)

— Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–84 (tr. Sahih International).1

The question

The Qur’an gives the figure only an epithet, “the possessor of the two horns,” and the commentators differed over whom it names. E. A. Wallis Budge (1889) summed up the range: “Some say that the ‘two horned’ mentioned in the Koran is Alexander; while others say that a contemporary of Abraham is meant, who was king of Persia, and others that he was a king of Yemen.”2 The exegetical tradition also proposed the Parthian “Kisrūnis” and the Lakhmid king al-Mundhir ibn Imruʾ al-Qays.3 The two identifications set out below are the Alexander (Iskandar) reading and the South-Arabian one.

A South-Arabian king of Himyar, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid

Dhū’l-Qarnayn is a pious pre-Islamic king of Himyar in South Arabia.

Held by: Wahb ibn Munabbih (d. c. 732), as reported through Ibn Hishām, with support claimed from Ibn ʿAbbās and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār.3

This Yemeni tradition reads the epithet onomastically. “Dhū” is a characteristic South-Arabian name-element, borne by the Himyarite kings, and pre-Islamic poets were said to have called al-Ṣaʿb “Dhū’l-Qarnayn.” On this reading the God-favoured ruler whom God “established upon the earth” (18:84) is a monotheist Arabian king, a figure fitted to the near-prophetic standing the Qur’an gives him, rather than a pagan Greek and the pupil of Aristotle. The tradition gives al-Ṣaʿb a full life: prophetic dreams, a summons to meet al-Khiḍr, conquests reaching to the ends of the earth, the walling-off of Gog and Magog, and death at Babylon. The Qur’anic episodes of the journeys and the barrier are thus the deeds of a king of Arabia’s own past, and the title the poets gave him is the title by which the Qur’an names him.

Alexander (Iskandar), the hero of the Alexander legend

Dhū’l-Qarnayn is Alexander the Great: the legendary Alexander of the Alexander Romance as remade in the Syriac Christian Legend.

Held by: al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) and the dominant line of classical tafsīr; Theodor Nöldeke (1890); G. J. Reinink; Kevin van Bladel (2008).4

The dominant classical exegesis identified Dhū’l-Qarnayn with Iskandar outright; al-Ṭabarī explained the “two horns” as the two extremities of the world, east and west, that he reached. Modern holders trace the epithet to Alexander’s own iconography: on Hellenistic coinage he wears the ram-horns of Zeus-Ammon, and in the Syriac Legend Alexander prays, “thou hast made me horns upon my head,” and the Lord tells him, “I have made horns of iron to grow on thy head that thou mayest thrust down the kingdoms of the earth with them.” Budge’s note on the passage calls “Possessor of two horns” “a well-known name of Alexander.”5 The episodes follow Alexander’s order in the Syriac Legend: dominion over the earth, the journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun, the people at the mountain pass, and the iron-and-copper wall against Gog and Magog. The life given to al-Ṣaʿb, from the meeting with al-Khiḍr to the death at Babylon, is the life of the Alexander Romance.3 On this reading the identification concerns the figure’s literary source: the Qur’an’s two-horned world-ruler is the Alexander of the legend, recognised as such by the commentators who first named him and by the modern philologists who traced the story to its Syriac form.
