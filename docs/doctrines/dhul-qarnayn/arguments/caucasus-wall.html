Does Alexander’s Gog-and-Magog barrier reflect a real Caucasus/Sasanian frontier wall?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Syriac Christian Legend concerning Alexander

The passage

Now there was a road across it by which great merchants entered the inner countries, and by it did Alexander encamp.

… And he made a lower threshold from mountain to mountain, the length of which was twelve cubits; and he hammered it into the rocks of the mountains, and it was fixed with brass and iron.

— “A Christian Legend concerning Alexander,” tr. E. A. Wallis Budge, The History of Alexander the Great (Cambridge, 1889), pp. 144–158.1

The question

The Legend sets Alexander’s gate of iron and bronze at a merchant-road pass between two mountains, against “the Huns” and Gog and Magog; the Qur’an places Dhū’l-Qarnayn’s barrier “between two mountains” (18:93). The question is whether this barrier reflects knowledge of the real fortified passes and walls of the Caucasus–Caspian frontier, or is a legendary motif that later writers attached to one site after another.

A memory of the real Caucasus–Caspian frontier

The legendary barrier reflects the real fortified passes and Sasanian walls of the northern frontier against the steppe nomads.

Held by: Josephus (c. 75) and Procopius (c. 550), on Alexander’s gates at the Caspian passes; E. A. Wallis Budge (1889), for Derbent; Emeri van Donzel & Andrea Schmidt (2010), most consistently at the Dariel Pass.2

Josephus (Jewish War 7.7.4) reports that the king of Hyrcania “was master of that passage which king Alexander shut up with iron gates,” through which the Alans raided Media, and elsewhere equates the raiding Scythians with the biblical Magog. Procopius (Wars 1.10) says of the pass beyond Iberia that “Alexander, the son of Philip, constructed gates in the aforesaid place and established a fortress there,” a fort fought over in his own lifetime by the Huns, the emperor Anastasius and the Sasanian Kavad I.3 The frontier had walls of this shape. At Derbent (Persian dar-band, “barred gate”; Arabic Bāb al-Abwāb) Khosrow I (r. 531–579) raised twin stone walls from the mountain citadel into the Caspian, and the tradition reports that he “hanged iron gates at the entrance”; east of the Caspian the Wall of Gorgan, dated by excavation and OSL to the fifth–sixth century, ran ~195 km against the Hephthalite Huns.4 The Legend’s merchant-road defile between facing mountains is a Dariel-type pass, and the nations it shuts out are first of all “the Huns,” the foe the sixth-century walls were built against; Budge noted that the gate “was probably made at the Pass of Derbend.”1 News of these passes reached Syriac Christendom through the Byzantine–Sasanian wars: the Sasanians demanded Byzantine subsidies for garrisoning them, and the 562 treaty bound Persia to keep the Huns and Alans out of Darband and the Dariel Pass.5 On this reading the Legend clothed in Alexander’s name a frontier its first audience knew from treaty and war, a substrate van Donzel and Schmidt make central to their study.

A migrating motif, attached to many sites in turn

Alexander’s gate is a legendary motif that writers located at one site after another, from the Caspian Gates near Rhagae to the Great Wall of China.

Held by: Andrew Runni Anderson (1932), on the motif’s migration.6

Anderson’s Alexander’s Gate, Gog and Magog and the Inclosed Nations traces the gate across its sites. The original “Caspian Gates” were the pass near Rhagae (near Tehran) that the historical Alexander crossed; later writers transferred the name across the Caspian to the Caucasus, where it moved between Dariel and Derbent under one label, and the wall was then placed at Gorgan and at length on the Great Wall of China. The peoples penned behind it were re-identified over the centuries as Scythians, Huns, Khazars, Mongols and the Ten Lost Tribes. Islamic tradition, for its part, equated Derbent’s Bāb al-Abwāb with the wall of Dhū’l-Qarnayn. In the ninth century the caliph al-Wāthiq sent Sallām the Interpreter to find Alexander’s wall; as van Donzel and Schmidt recount his journey, he tried the Caucasus, found it unconvincing, and travelled thousands of miles east to identify the barrier with a Chinese frontier gate near Dunhuang. On this reading the barrier of the legend is a motif carried from frontier to frontier, located wherever each age placed the edge of the known world.7
