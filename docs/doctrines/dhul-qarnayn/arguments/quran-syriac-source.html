Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the Syriac Alexander Legend supplies a more explicit counterpart to almost every element of the short Qur’anic tale, in the same order; it circulated in the Syriac-Christian milieu the Qur’an drew on and predates it on any current dating. The passage “is a retelling” of the Legend (van Bladel), a mainstream verdict; the live disputes touch mechanism and date, not the fact of dependence · advanced by mainstream scholarship on the Qur’an’s narrative sources.

Related arguments: the Alexander connection — see also Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great?

The claim

The account of Dhū’l-Qarnayn in Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101 is a retelling of the Syriac Christian Legend concerning Alexander (the Neṣḥānā). The two are not independent stories that happen to resemble each other; they are one story, received into the Qur’an from the Syriac-Christian tradition and reshaped to the Qur’an’s own purposes — exactly as the Companions of the Cave, earlier in the same sura, retell the Christian Seven Sleepers.

Who advances it

This is the settled position of scholarship on the Qur’an’s narrative sources, first argued by Theodor Nöldeke (1890), developed by G. J. Reinink, and renewed with a full comparison by Kevin van Bladel (2008), whose verdict is that the Qur’anic passage “is a retelling of the story found in this particular Syriac text.” It follows from the chronology and from a dense structural correspondence.

The evidence for

A point-by-point correspondence. Van Bladel finds that “almost every element of this short Qur’anic tale finds a more explicit and detailed counterpart in the Syriac Alexander Legend,” and in the same order: God grants the hero dominion over the whole earth; he journeys west to fetid/deadly waters and a people to be judged; he journeys east to the sunrise over a people with no shelter; he builds a wall of iron and brass against Gog and Magog; and the tale closes on an end-times prophecy of nations surging out at the appointed time. The Qur’an’s “spring of dark mud” (18:86), its “sheets of iron… molten copper” (18:96), and its barrier that holds “until the promise of my Lord comes” (18:98) each answer to a specific feature of the Legend.1

Priority in time. The Legend is fixed in writing before the Qur’an on every current dating — whether Nöldeke and Reinink’s c. 629–630 or Tommaso Tesei’s Justinianic core (mid-6th c.) with a later prophecy. The older text cannot depend on the younger.

The right milieu. The Legend belonged to the Syriac-speaking Christianity of the Near East — the very environment from which the Qur’an demonstrably drew a great deal of its biblical and para-biblical narrative material, including the Seven Sleepers a few verses earlier in the same chapter.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Both the fact of a common origin and the direction of dependence are secure. The chronology rules out Syriac borrowing from Islam; the dense, ordered, specific correspondence rules out coincidence; the shared milieu supplies the channel. Van Bladel explicitly weighed and rejected the two escape routes — the Syriac deriving from the Qur’an, or both from a lost common source — and concluded the Qur’anic account must draw on the Syriac account, directly or by oral report.

What is genuinely open — and what it does not touch. Two live questions remain, but neither unsettles the dependence. First, the date of the Legend: Tesei (2023) re-dates the core a century earlier than the Heraclian consensus, with a Heraclian-era prophecy interpolated. That reorganizes the Legend’s own history — and van Bladel grants it forces a reconsideration of the work — but an earlier Legend still predates the Qur’an, so the dependence stands either way. Second, the mechanism: “drew on” should not be flattened into “copied a manuscript.” The Qur’an engages a living legend, which is why it strips the Christian frame (no Roman empire, no Messiah), abstracts the wall into a sign of the Hour, and tells the tale allusively as already known. These are the marks of a story received and re-told, not transcribed.2

One thing this does not establish. As with the Seven Sleepers, this argument shows only that the two accounts are one story; it says nothing about whether that story is historically true. To argue from shared origin to shared truth (or shared falsehood) would be a category error.

Assessment

Sound. The Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend are the same tale, with the dependence running from the earlier Syriac tradition to the later Qur’anic retelling — secured by priority in time, the shared Syriac-Christian milieu, and a point-by-point common structure. The qualifications are about mechanism (a living legend re-told, not a manuscript copied) and date (the Legend’s own composition, still debated), neither of which reaches the fact of dependence. Whether the events occurred is a separate question the argument does not address.
