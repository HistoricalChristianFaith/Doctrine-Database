Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101

Related readings: Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great? · When was the Syriac Alexander Legend (the Neṣḥānā) composed? · see also, on the same sura, Did the Qur’an’s Companions of the Cave draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend?

The passage

“They said, ‘O Dhul-Qarnayn, indeed Gog and Magog are [great] corrupters in the land. So may we assign for you an expenditure that you might make between us and them a barrier?’” (v. 94)

“‘Bring me bars of iron’ - until, when he had leveled [them] between the two mountain walls, he said, ‘Blow [with bellows],’ until when he had made it [like] fire, he said, ‘Bring me, that I may pour over it molten copper.’” (v. 96)

“‘This is a mercy from my Lord; but when the promise of my Lord comes [i.e., approaches], He will make it level, and ever is the promise of my Lord true.’” (v. 98)

— Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:94–98 (tr. Sahih International).1

The question

The Syriac Christian Legend concerning Alexander (the Neṣḥānā) tells of a God-favoured Alexander who journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun and builds a gate of iron and bronze against the Huns and Gog and Magog, sealed with an end-times prophecy. The question is whether the Qur’anic account derives from that Syriac tradition or whether the resemblance has another explanation; it concerns the literary relation of the two texts, not whether the events they narrate took place.

A retelling of the Syriac Legend

The Qur’anic account is the Syriac Legend’s story, received from Syriac-speaking Christianity and retold for the Qur’an’s own purposes.

Held by: Theodor Nöldeke (1890); G. J. Reinink; Kevin van Bladel (2008).2

Van Bladel finds that “almost every element of this short Qur’anic tale finds a more explicit and detailed counterpart in the Syriac Alexander Legend,” and in the same order: God grants the hero dominion over the earth; he journeys west to fetid waters and a people to be judged; he journeys east to the sunrise over a people without shelter; he builds a wall of iron and brass against Gog and Magog; and the tale closes on a prophecy of nations surging out at the appointed time. The “spring of dark mud” (18:86), the “sheets of iron… molten copper” (18:96) and the barrier that holds “until the promise of my Lord comes” (18:98) each answer to a feature of the Legend. The Legend was written before the Qur’an on every current dating, whether Nöldeke and Reinink’s c. 629–630 or Tommaso Tesei’s mid-sixth-century core with a later prophecy. It belonged to the Syriac-speaking Christianity from which, on this reading, the Qur’an drew other narrative matter, including the Companions of the Cave a few verses earlier. Van Bladel concludes that the Qur’anic passage “is a retelling of the story found in this particular Syriac text,” received directly or by oral report. Holders of this reading take the Qur’an’s allusive telling, as of a story already known, to mark a living legend retold: the Christian frame (the Roman empire, the Messiah) drops away, and the wall becomes a sign of the Hour.3

An account independent of the Syriac Legend

The two texts share their story for another reason: either the Syriac Legend took up the Qur’anic account, or both descend from an older common source.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

These are the two alternatives to dependence that van Bladel (2008) sets out. On the first, the Syriac Legend in the form that survives would have taken shape after, and under the influence of, the Qur’anic narrative. On the second, both would draw on an earlier account of a God-guided king, his journeys to the ends of the earth and his gate against the northern nations, which would explain the shared order of episodes without either text depending on the other. The Qur’an itself presents its account as “a report” recited in answer to a question its hearers put about him (18:83).1,3
