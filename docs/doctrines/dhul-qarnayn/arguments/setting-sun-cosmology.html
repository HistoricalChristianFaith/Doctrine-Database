Does Qur’an 18:86 assert that the sun literally sets in a “spring of dark mud”?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:86

Related readings: Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

The passage

“Until, when he reached the setting of the sun, he found it [as if] setting in a spring of dark mud, and he found near it a people. Allah said, ‘O Dhū’l-Qarnayn, either you punish [them] or else adopt among them [a way of] goodness.’” (v. 86)

— Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:86 (tr. Sahih International; the Arabic is wajadahā taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾatin).1

The question

The question is whether the verse states that the sun goes down into a spring at the western edge of the earth, as part of a cosmology of a flat earth ringed by the world-ocean, or reports what Dhū’l-Qarnayn saw from the world’s western edge. The key word is read ḥamiʾa (“muddy,” the Ḥafṣ text) or ḥāmiya (“hot,” the Shuʿba/Ibn ʿĀmir/Ḥamza/Kisāʾī line); al-Ṭabarī harmonizes the two, a spring being possibly “both hot and muddy.”2

A concrete setting-place, in an inherited late-antique cosmology

The verse pictures the sun setting at a real place, a spring at the western edge of the world, as the cosmology of its age and of its source did.

Held by: Ibn ʿAbbās; Muqātil ibn Sulaymān (d. 767); Tommaso Tesei (2015); Angelika Neuwirth (Encyclopaedia of the Qur’ān).3

The passage retells the Syriac Alexander Legend, whose geography is literal: Alexander comes to “the foetid sea, Okeyanos (the Ocean), which surrounds all creation,” and the sun “enters the window of heaven,” where “he bows down and makes obeisance before God his Creator.” The Qur’an’s spring of dark mud is, on this reading, the heir of that fetid sea.4 The earliest exegesis heard a concrete place. Muqātil, the oldest surviving attributed tafsīr, reports that the sun is hotter at rising than at setting. Al-Ṭabarī preserves the exchange in which Muʿāwiya recited ḥāmiya and Ibn ʿAbbās corrected him to ḥamiʾa, and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, asked to decide, said the sun sets in mud; he also cites verses Ibn Isḥāq ascribes to a pre-Islamic poet, that Dhū’l-Qarnayn saw “the setting of the sun… in a pool of black and fetid slime.” The Abū Dharr hadith has the Prophet teach that at sunset the sun “prostrates beneath the Throne.”2 Tesei argues that late antiquity carried two cosmologies, the Greco-Roman sphere and “the ancient Semitic tradition, which pictured the earth as a flat disk encircled by waters and surmounted by a dome-like sky,” and that the Qur’an draws on both; Neuwirth writes that “only al-Ṭabarī… presents an interpretation in accordance with the qur’ānic evidence, the image of a world swimming in an ocean.”5 On this reading the verse names the spring’s quality and places a people beside it, the marks of a locale.

The traveller’s view of the sunset

The verse reports how the sunset appeared to Dhū’l-Qarnayn, not where the sun actually goes.

Held by: al-Khaṭṭābī (d. 998); al-Rāzī (d. 1210); the Tafsīr al-Jalālayn; Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373).6

The verb is wajada, “he found” or “perceived”: the clause is grammatically the traveller’s observation. Al-Khaṭṭābī compared the observer on a coast, to whom the sun appears to enter the water; al-Rāzī took wajada as perception by the eye. The Jalālayn says the setting is described “as seen from the perspective of the eye, for otherwise [the sun] is far larger than this world,” and Ibn Kathīr writes that “he saw the sun as if it were setting in the ocean… but in fact it never leaves its path.”6
