Does the al-Kahf Moses–al-Khiḍr pericope (Qur’an 18:60–82) draw on the Alexander water-of-life legend?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Syriac verse Alexander Poem

Related readings: Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

The passage

And [mention] when Moses said to his boy [i.e., servant], “I will not cease [traveling] until I reach the junction of the two seas or continue for a long period.” (v. 60)

But when they reached the junction between them, they forgot their fish, and it took its course into the sea, slipping away. (v. 61)

And they found a servant from among Our servants [i.e., al-Khiḍr] to whom We had given mercy from Us and had taught him from Us a [certain] knowledge. (v. 65)

— Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:60–65 (tr. Sahih International).1

The question

Between the Companions of the Cave (18:9–26) and Dhū’l-Qarnayn (18:83–101), Sūrat al-Kahf tells of Moses’ journey with his servant to “the junction of the two seas” (majmaʿ al-baḥrayn) and his meeting with a “servant” of God whom the commentators name al-Khiḍr, “the Green One” (18:60–82). In the Alexander legend, Alexander quests for the spring of immortality and his cook washes a fish that comes back to life and escapes into the water. The question is whether the Qur’anic pericope, especially its opening frame (18:60–65), draws on that episode; it concerns literary relations, not whether the events occurred.

A retelling of the Alexander water-of-life episode

The Moses pericope draws on the Alexander water-of-life legend, with Moses in Alexander’s place and his servant in the cook’s.

Held by: Israel Friedländer (1913); A. J. Wensinck (Encyclopaedia of Islam), on the fish-and-servant borrowing; Kevin van Bladel (2008), who grants the parallel and holds a common source rather than borrowing; Gabriel Said Reynolds (2010).2

The pericope’s opening is a linked sequence: a quest to the waters at the world’s edge, a fish that goes into the sea at the junction, and a figure of hidden knowledge found there. Each answers to the Alexander episode. Friedländer read the whole Moses–al-Khiḍr complex as a reflex of the Alexander Romance; Wensinck accepted the fish-and-servant borrowing and argued that the Arabic fatā used of Moses’ servant fits the Alexander cook. The Syriac verse Alexander Poem (Pseudo-Jacob) has an old man instruct the cook to wash a salted fish at every spring, promising that where it revives he will find the water of life; the Islamic story as the commentators tell it has God tell Moses to carry a salted fish as a sign, and where it comes alive and slips into the sea he will find the servant of God. The reviving-fish scene stands in the Greek β-recension of the Romance (c. 300–550) and in pre-Qur’anic Syriac material.3 Van Bladel judges the traces in the Moses pericope and the Alexander Poem so alike that a single common source is likely, reached through “a poorly documented, probably oral, late tradition of the Gilgamesh story,” the tale of Gilgamesh’s quest for the plant of rejuvenation and his meeting with the immortal Utnapishtim (Tablet XI).4 A few verses later the sura retells the Syriac Alexander Legend in the Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode. On this reading the back half of the chapter draws twice on the Alexander cycle, and the medieval exegetes al-Rāzī and al-Suyūṭī had already linked Elijah, al-Khiḍr, Alexander and Dhū’l-Qarnayn.2

A Qur’anic story read into the Alexander recensions

The pericope is a Qur’anic story; later Alexander recensions took it up as Muslim commentators told it.

Held by: Brannon M. Wheeler (1998; expanded 2002).5

Wheeler builds his case on the diversity of the Alexander recensions, not all of which carry an immortality-quest; on the provenance and dating of the texts alleged as sources; and on the fish’s role in Q 18:61 and 18:63, which differs from its role in the Romance. On his reading the Qur’an’s verses say that the fish “took its course into the sea, slipping away,” and not that it was dead or that it revived at a water of life; that revival comes from the commentators. The guide is in the Qur’an “a servant from among Our servants”; his identification as al-Khiḍr rests on a report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and on the exegetes, who also gave “the Green One” his own water-of-life legends. On this reading the closest parallels lie between Alexander texts and the early Islamic exegesis of 18:60–65, and the Alexander recensions that carry them took up the Qur’anic story as that exegesis told it.1,5
