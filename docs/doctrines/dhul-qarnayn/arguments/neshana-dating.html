When was the Syriac Alexander Legend (the Neṣḥānā) composed — and did it originate as Heraclian propaganda?

Disputed reading · on Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend · witness: the Syriac Christian Legend concerning Alexander

Related readings: Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

The passage

Alexander, having built his gate against the Huns, has an inscription cut upon it:

Also I have written that, at the conclusion of eight hundred and twenty-six years, the Huns shall go forth by the narrow which goes forth opposite Haloras, whence the Tigris forth like the stream which turns a mill, and they shall take captive the nations, and shall cut off the roads, and shall make the earth tremble by their going forth. And again I have written and made known and prophesied that it shall come to pass, at the conclusion of nine hundred and forty years.... another king, when the world shall come to an end by the command of God the ruler of creation.

— “A Christian Legend concerning Alexander,” tr. E. A. Wallis Budge, The History of Alexander the Great (Cambridge, 1889), p. 154, online text.1

The question

The prophecy names two Seleucid-era (Anno Graecorum) dates, 826 AG (= 514/515 AD) and 940 AG (= 628/629 AD). Nöldeke dated the whole Legend to the years just after Heraclius’s victory over Persia; Reinink and van Bladel read it, at that date, as Heraclian propaganda; Shoemaker, Tesei and Debié hold that an older sixth-century text was updated with the 940 AG prophecy at that time.2,4

Composed c. 629–630 as Heraclian propaganda

The Legend was written in north Mesopotamia just after Heraclius’s triumph over Khosrow II, to proclaim a Christian Roman world-empire on the eve of the end.

Held by: Theodor Nöldeke (1890), on the c. 629–630 date; G. J. Reinink (1985, 2002); Kevin van Bladel (2008).3

A vaticinium ex eventu is dated by where its accurate foresight stops, and here it stops at 940 AG. Van Bladel ties that year to the close of the Byzantine–Sasanian war: the Persian nobles deposed Khosrow II in February 628, Persia withdrew in 629, and in 630 Heraclius restored the True Cross to Jerusalem. The prophecy’s sequel, in which “the kingdom of the Greeks” takes up a hammer of iron and a hammer of brass and the power of the other kingdoms melts away until none can “stand before the kingdom of the Romans,” reads on this view as that victory set in prophecy. Reinink turned Nöldeke’s dating into a full historical thesis, the Legend as “politico-religious propaganda” for Heraclius’s church policy: a Christian-Roman cosmocrator ideology that would rally the Syriac churches, including the Miaphysites the emperor was courting through the Monoenergist union, behind Constantinople on the threshold of the Last Days. The Legend’s Alexander, a monotheist king who vows his realm to God and receives dominion over the whole earth, fits that program. The 940 AG date is present in the transmitted text, and no copy of the Legend survives without it; van Bladel adopted the c. 630 anchor as “not seriously disputed.” On this reading the Legend was a topical tract of its Heraclian moment from the start.1,2

A sixth-century core, updated under Heraclius

The Legend was first written under Justinian (527–565), and a Heraclian-era redactor added the 940 AG prophecy.

Held by: Stephen Shoemaker (2018); Tommaso Tesei (2023); Muriel Debié (2024).4

Shoemaker judged that the Legend “in its current form almost certainly updates an older version… composed in the early sixth century.” Tesei builds the case from the body of the narrative rather than from the prophecy. The first date, 826 AG (514/515), commemorates the Sabir Hun incursion of the early sixth century. The Alexander–Tubarlaq material, in which the Persian king Tubarlaq gathers allied kings and armies against Alexander, answers on his reading to sixth-century concerns, including a critique of Justinian’s accommodations with the Sasanians, and sits more naturally in Justinian’s reign than in Heraclius’s. The 940 AG prophecy is on this account a later layer, written by a hand that updated an existing apocalyptic story at the moment of Heraclius’s victory. Tesei’s monograph revises both Reinink’s framework and his own earlier position, and Debié likewise dates the ur-text to the sixth century. On this reading the Legend was an older story of Alexander’s gate that circulated for a century before it was given its Heraclian form.4
