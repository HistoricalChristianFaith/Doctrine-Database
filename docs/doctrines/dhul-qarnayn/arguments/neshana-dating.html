When was the Syriac Alexander Legend (the Neṣḥānā) composed — and did it originate as Heraclian propaganda?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the classic thesis dates the Christian Legend concerning Alexander (the Neṣḥānā) to c. 629–630 and reads it as Heraclian war-propaganda, its internal “prophecy” running out precisely at the emperor’s victory over Persia. Tommaso Tesei (2023) re-dates the core to the reign of Justinian (527–565), with the end-times prophecy interpolated later — unseating the “born-as-propaganda” reading. Each case is strong by its own lights and neither has closed the question; the dispute is genuine and undecided. It does not touch the Legend→Qur’an dependence, which survives either dating · weighed against the scholarship on the Legend’s composition.

The claim

The interpretation weighed here is the one long treated as consensus: that the Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs was composed in north Mesopotamia about 629–630 AD as a piece of Heraclian propaganda — written just after the emperor’s triumph over Khosrow II to proclaim a Christian Roman world-empire on the eve of the end (and, plausibly, to draw Syrian Miaphysites back toward Constantinople). On this reading the text was born a topical tract of its Heraclian moment, not an older story merely re-copied. That is the claim rated below; its principal rival — a Justinianic core with a later Heraclian graft — is the defeater it must survive.

Who advances it

Theodor Nöldeke (1890) first placed the Legend in north Mesopotamia c. 629–630 and named it the source of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode. G. J. Reinink (1985, 2002) turned that dating into a full historical thesis — the Legend as “politico-religious propaganda” for Heraclius’s church policy — and Kevin van Bladel (2008) adopted the c. 630 anchor as “not seriously disputed.” The re-dating that now contests it is Tommaso Tesei’s (2023), anticipated by Stephen Shoemaker and joined since by Muriel Debié (2024), all of whom argue for a sixth-century ur-text.1

The evidence for

The dated prophecy runs out in 628–629. Once Alexander’s gate is built, the Legend has him seal it with a prophecy naming two Seleucid-era (Anno Graecorum) dates on which the penned nations will break through: 826 AG (= 514/515 AD) and 940 AG (= 628/629 AD). A vaticinium ex eventu — a “prophecy” written after the fact — is dated by where its accurate foresight stops, and here it stops at 940 AG. That year is the exact hinge of Heraclius’s war: the Persian nobles deposed Khosrow II in February 628, Persia sued for peace and withdrew in 629, and in 630 Heraclius restored the True Cross to Jerusalem. The prophecy’s Roman world-triumph after a final agony reads as this victory monumentalized.2

A coherent political motive. Reinink situates the tract in Heraclius’s post-war religious politics: a Christian-Roman cosmocrator ideology that would rally the Syriac churches — including the Miaphysites the emperor was courting through the Monoenergist union — behind Constantinople on the threshold of the Last Days. The Legend’s Alexander, a monotheist king who vows his realm to God and to whom God grants dominion over the whole earth, fits that program precisely.

It is a textual anchor, not a guess. The 940 AG date is present in the transmitted text; the Heraclian reading rests on a datum the manuscripts actually carry, not on a reconstructed lost recension.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The 940 AG prophecy is a real feature of the text and does point, with unusual precision, at 628–629. Whoever wrote those verses wrote them in or just after the Heraclian victory; on that much the two camps agree. The Heraclian reading also supplies a fully worked political Sitz im Leben that the bare prophecy alone would not.

The challenge Tesei presses. Tesei (2023) argues the core Legend was composed a century earlier, under Justinian (527–565), and that the 940 AG prophecy is a later interpolation by a Heraclian-era redactor updating an existing text. His case is not the prophecy but the rest of the narrative: the Alexander–Tubarlaq material, he contends, answers to sixth-century concerns — the Sabir Hun incursion the first date (826 AG = 514/515) already commemorates, and a critique of Justinian’s accommodations with the Sasanians, mocked through the Persian king Tubarlaq — and reads awkwardly as a product of Heraclius’s reign. Shoemaker had already judged that the Legend “in its current form almost certainly updates an older version… composed in the early sixth century,” and Debié (2024) likewise dates the ur-text to the sixth century. If they are right, the text was not born Heraclian propaganda; it was an older apocalyptic story that a Heraclian hand retrofitted.3

Where the challenge is itself exposed. The interpolation thesis has no manuscript witness: no copy of the Legend survives without the 940 AG prophecy, so the “core minus the graft” is a reconstruction argued from narrative fit, not a text anyone has held in hand. Reinink and van Bladel could reply that a unified composition around 629 explains the same data without positing an unattested earlier layer — and that the Sabir date (826 AG) is exactly the kind of earlier landmark a single late author would supply to lend his ex eventu prophecy depth. The dispute turns on how much weight the Tubarlaq/Justinian reading can bear against the plainer force of the 940 AG anchor — a question of literary-historical judgement on which competent Syriacists now genuinely divide.

Assessment

Contested. The Heraclian-propaganda dating rests on a solid textual anchor — the 940 AG prophecy, whose foresight stops precisely at 628–629 — and a coherent political motive, and it long stood as consensus. Tesei’s Justinianic re-dating, seconded by Shoemaker and Debié, is a respectable and now-influential challenge that would reduce the Heraclian layer to an interpolation and dissolve the “born-as-propaganda” reading; but it rests on narrative fit rather than any manuscript lacking the prophecy, so it has not closed the case either. Neither side has won: the question of when the Legend was written, and whether it originated as Heraclian propaganda, is genuinely undecided — strong by each discipline’s lights, without a clear victor. What both sides affirm, and what this dispute leaves wholly intact, is that the Legend on any of these datings predates the Qur’an, so the dependence of Sūrat al-Kahf on it stands regardless.
