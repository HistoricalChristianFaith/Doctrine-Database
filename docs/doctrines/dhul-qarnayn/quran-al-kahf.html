The Qur’an: Dhū’l-Qarnayn, the two-horned one

Dates: early 7th c. (Sūrat al-Kahf) · Qur’an 18:83–101
Summary: A few verses after the Companions of the Cave, the same sura tells of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the possessor of the two horns” — a God-favoured world-ruler who journeys to the sun’s setting and rising and builds an iron-and-copper barrier against Gog and Magog. It is the Syriac Alexander Legend, told allusively as a sign already known.

Claims & evidence

The passage (18:83–101)

The Qur’an tells the tale in answer to a question put to the Prophet, as a report of a figure the hearers already know. The essential verses (tr. Sahih International):

“And they ask you, [O Muḥammad], about Dhū’l-Qarnayn. Say, ‘I will recite to you about him a report.’” (v. 83)

“Indeed We established him upon the earth, and We gave him to everything a way.” (v. 84)

“Until, when he reached the setting of the sun, he found it [as if] setting in a spring of dark mud, and he found near it a people. Allah said, ‘O Dhū’l-Qarnayn, either you punish [them] or else adopt among them [a way of] goodness.’” (v. 86)

“Until, when he came to the rising of the sun, he found it rising on a people for whom We had not made against it any shield.” (v. 90)

“Until, when he reached [a pass] between two mountains, he found beside them a people who could hardly understand [his] speech.” (v. 93)

“They said, ‘O Dhū’l-Qarnayn, indeed Gog and Magog are [great] corrupters in the land. So may we assign for you an expenditure that you might make between us and them a barrier?’” (v. 94)

“‘Bring me sheets of iron’ — until, when he had leveled [them] between the two mountain walls, he said, ‘Blow [with bellows],’ until when he had made it [like] fire, he said, ‘Bring me, that I may pour over it molten copper.’” (v. 96)

“So Gog and Magog were unable to pass over it, nor were they able [to effect] in it any penetration.” (v. 97)

“‘This is a mercy from my Lord; but when the promise of my Lord comes, He will make it level, and ever is the promise of my Lord true.’” (v. 98)

“And We will leave them that day surging over each other, and [then] the Horn will be blown, and We will assemble them in [one] assembly.” (v. 99)

— Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101 (tr. Sahih International).

“The two-horned one”

The epithet Dhū’l-Qarnayn means “the possessor of the two horns” or “the two-horned one.” Its sense was debated from the start: al-Ṭabarī explained the horns as the two extremities — east and west, the setting and the rising of the sun — that the hero reached. Modern scholars more often trace the horns to the iconography of Alexander, who on Hellenistic coinage wears the ram-horns of Zeus-Ammon; the Syriac Legend itself has God set horns on Alexander’s head.

The Syriac Legend, told allusively

Every beat of the passage answers to the Syriac Legend, and in the same order: the God-granted dominion “upon the earth” (v. 84); the journey to “the setting of the sun” and its spring of dark mud (v. 86 — the Legend’s fetid sea); the journey to “the rising of the sun” over an unsheltered people (v. 90); the people at the pass “between two mountains” who beg protection (vv. 93–94); the wall of iron sheets and poured molten copper (v. 96 — the Legend’s gate of iron and bronze); and the end-times note that the barrier holds only “until the promise of my Lord comes,” when it will be levelled and the nations “surge over each other” before the blowing of the Horn (vv. 98–99 — the Legend’s sealed prophecy).

Notes

  • What is kept and what drops away. As with the Companions of the Cave earlier in the same sura, the Qur’an keeps the narrative core and strips the Christian furniture: no “Roman empire,” no Messiah, no explicitly Christian frame — the tale is abstracted into a sign of God’s power and of the coming Hour.
