The Treasury of Merit

The treasury of merit (thesaurus meritorum), also called the treasury of the Church, is in Catholic teaching the inexhaustible value of Christ's merits, joined by the prayers and good works of the Virgin Mary and the saints, held in common in the communion of saints, from which the Catholic Church applies the remission of the temporal punishment due to forgiven sin in granting indulgences, for the living or the dead.1 Two further questions are bound up with it in the scholastic account and in the dispute over it: whether the saints could perform works of supererogation, good works beyond what God commands and beyond what their own salvation required, and whether such merit or satisfaction can be applied to others by ecclesiastical authority. The discussion also touches the distinction between precepts and the “counsels of perfection” (poverty, chastity, obedience), the merits of Mary and the saints in the communion of saints, and the medieval penitential system of satisfaction. This timeline records the extant witnesses in date order, earliest first.

Who holds what (grouped by tradition). William Kent (Catholic; “Indulgences,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that the satisfactory works of the Virgin Mary and the sufferings of the saints “are added to the treasury of the Church as a secondary deposit, not independent of, but rather acquired through, the merits of Christ.”2 Paul VI's apostolic constitution Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1 January 1967, §5) calls the treasury “the infinite and inexhaustible value the expiation and the merits of Christ Our Lord have before God” and says that “It is Christ the Redeemer himself in whom the satisfactions and merits of his redemption exist and find their force.”3 The Catholic Church's Catechism (1992; §§1476–1477) describes the treasury as “the infinite value, which can never be exhausted, which Christ's merits have before God,” and says that it “includes as well the prayers and good works of the Blessed Virgin Mary” and of “all the saints, all those who have followed in the footsteps of Christ the Lord and by his grace have made their lives holy.”4

Martin Luther, styled in the preamble to his Ninety-Five Theses (1517) “Master of Arts and of Sacred Theology, and Lecturer in Ordinary on the same” at Wittenberg, holds there that the treasures out of which the pope grants indulgences are not the merits of Christ and the saints, “for even without the pope, these always work grace for the inner man, and the cross, death, and hell for the outward man” (thesis 58), and that “The true treasure of the Church is the Most Holy Gospel of the glory and the grace of God” (thesis 62).5 John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes III.5.2; definitive edition 1559) calls indulgences a profanation of Christ's blood, asking “how could the blood of Christ be more shamefully profaned than by denying its sufficiency for the remission of sins, for reconciliation and satisfaction, unless its defects, as if it were dried up and exhausted, are supplemented from some other quarter?”6 The Church of England's Article XIV (1571) rejects works of supererogation (see the 1571 entry below). Charles Hodge (Reformed; professor at Princeton Theological Seminary; Systematic Theology, vol. III, 1872), after setting out the doctrine he rejects, argues that supererogation “supposes an impossibility. It supposes that a rational creature can be better than he ought to be; i.e., than he is bound to be,” and that “the renunciation of all dependence upon our own merit, of all purpose, desire, or effort to make satisfaction for ourselves, and trusting exclusively to the satisfaction rendered by Jesus Christ, is of the very essence of Christian experience”.7

The Orthodox Confession (1638–1642) of Peter Mogila, Metropolitan of Kiev, answering “What, then, are we to think of the invocation of saints?”, says that the saints' “assistance is very necessary for us; not, indeed, that of their own power they can help us, but because by their prayers in our behalf they may obtain the grace and favour of God for us.”8 Alexei Khomiakov (1804–1860; Russian Orthodox theologian), in his essay The Church Is One, writes of the Last Judgment that “there is still need of justification by the sufferings and blood of Christ. Who, then, can continue to speak of the merits of his own works, or of a treasury of merits and prayers? Only those who are still living under a law of bondage.”9 Protopresbyter Michael Pomazansky (1888–1988; instructor at Holy Trinity Orthodox Seminary, Jordanville, from 1949), in his Orthodox Dogmatic Theology (first published in Russian, 1963), describes the Roman Catholic view of penances as one that “proceeds from legal concepts,” under which the pope, in giving indulgences, “applies to persons who are subject to penance the ‘merits of the saints,’ taking them from the so-called ‘treasury’ of supererogatory works”; a note to the passage in the English text states: “All these ideas were developed in 13th-century scholasticism and are totally foreign to Orthodox thinking.” He writes that where “certain Western teachers of the ancient Church” called penances “satisfactions,” “they were called this only in the moral sense, as a means for deepening the awareness of sinfulness in the sinner, this being ‘satisfactory’ for the aim of edification, but not as a legal justification.”10 John Breck (Orthodox; former professor at St Vladimir's Seminary), in a 2010 OCA column on why ascetic practice matters if salvation is by grace, writes of personal merit at the Judgment: “Unlike medieval Catholicism, Orthodoxy does not hold that a person can build up a ‘treasury of merits’ that will count in our favor at the Judgment Seat of Christ. What will matter then is our having surrendered our sin to God through confession, and our gestures of love (Mt. 25), together with the unshakable conviction that ‘Jesus Christ is Lord,’ and the unique Way to eternal life.” In his next paragraph he writes that “Orthodoxy does recognize, however, the importance of our ‘cooperation’ with God, what we term ‘synergy.’”11 The Orthodox Church in America's published answer on indulgences states that “There is no similar concept of indulgences within Orthodox Christianity.”12

Timeline

Thomas Aquinas on the treasury (c. 1252–56)

In his commentary on Book IV of Peter Lombard's Sentences (1252–56), the material printed as the Supplement to the Summa Theologiae (q. 25), Aquinas holds that indulgences avail because of “the oneness of the mystical body in which many have performed works of satisfaction exceeding the requirements of their debts”: “So great is the quantity of such merits that it exceeds the entire debt of punishment due to those who are living at this moment: and this is especially due to the merits of Christ.” Since “one man can satisfy for another,” these merits “are the common property of the whole Church,” and “those things which are the common property of a number are distributed to the various individuals according to the judgment of him who rules them all.” He places the treasury within his account of satisfaction and merit.13,14

Clement VI, Unigenitus Dei Filius (1343)

The jubilee bull of Clement VI (25 January 1343) is the earliest papal statement of the treasury recorded on this timeline. Of Christ's blood, poured out “copiously as a kind of flowing stream,” it asks “how great a treasure did the good Father acquire from this for the Church militant”; the treasure is committed “through blessed Peter, the keeper of the keys of heaven and his successors, his vicars on earth” to be dispensed for the remission of temporal punishment; there “should be no fear at any time” of its consumption or diminution; and to it “the merits of the Blessed Mother of God and of all the elect… are known to give their help.”15

The Thirty-Nine Articles reject works of supererogation (1571)

Article XIV of the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles states: “Voluntary Works besides, over and above, God's Commandments, which they call Works of Supererogation, can not be taught without arrogancy and impiety”; by them men declare “that they do more for his sake, than of bounden duty is required: whereas Christ saith plainly, When ye have done all that are commanded to you, say, We are unprofitable servants.”16
