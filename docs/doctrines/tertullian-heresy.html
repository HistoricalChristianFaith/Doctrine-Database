Was Tertullian a Heretic?

Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155–c. 220) wrote some of the earliest extant Christian theology in Latin. His works contain the Latin theological terms trinitas, persona and substantia, along with sacramentum and satisfactio, and his Prescription Against Heretics sets out an argument from the rule of faith against the Gnostic teachers of his day. He is cited on many points by later Latin writers; he has not been venerated as a saint; the book-list called the Decretum Gelasianum places his works among those “not received”; and Vincent of Lérins paired him with Origen as teachers whose later errors detracted from their earlier writings. His later works embrace the New Prophecy — the ecstatic movement out of Phrygia that its opponents called Montanism — and Augustine reports that he ended at the head of a group of his own, the Tertullianistae.

This timeline records how Tertullian came to be reckoned among the heretics: the judgements and reports passed on him, earliest first. Origen, with whom Vincent of Lérins paired him, was condemned by name in synodal, papal and imperial acts, traced on the condemnation-of-Origen timeline; no synod is recorded as having tried Tertullian, and the judgements on him below come from a heresy-catalogue entry, a book-list, a biographical notice, and later citation.

The timeline’s witnesses are the datable judgements and reports, earliest first: Tertullian’s own later works, in which he embraces the Paraclete and names the Christians who reject the New Prophecy “Psychics”; the generation after him, above all Cyprian of Carthage, who on Jerome’s report read him every day and who does not name him in his surviving works; Eusebius, who knew him as one versed in Roman law; the anonymous heresy-list transmitted under his own name; Jerome’s biographical notice; Augustine, whose heresy-catalogue gives him a numbered entry and contains the only independent notice of the Tertullianistae; Vincent of Lérins; the book-list called the Decretum Gelasianum; the medieval and Reformation reception; and modern scholarship. The disputed readings that follow the timeline take up whether he ever left the church of Carthage, what he was faulted for, and how his theology and biography have been read. Tertullian also appears as a witness on many other timelines of this site, from the origin of the soul and infant baptism to the Eucharist and the perpetual virginity of Mary.

Timeline

Tertullian’s rule of faith and the Latin “Trinity” (c. 200–210)

The Prescription Against Heretics, written about 203, borrows a term from Roman law — the praescriptio, a plea that stops a lawsuit before its merits are heard — and turns it against the Gnostics: on his argument, the Scriptures belong to the Church that has held the rule of faith from the apostles, and heretics, being no part of that succession, have no standing to argue from them. He states that rule in a single long sentence — there is “one only God, and… He is none other than the Creator of the world, who produced all things out of nothing through His own Word, first of all sent forth” — and sets philosophy against it: “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?”1

In the same book, answering the objection that trusted Christians sometimes defect, Tertullian asks how it is “that this woman or that man, who were the most faithful, the most prudent, and the most approved in the church, have gone over to the other side” — and answers that a defector was never truly approved, since “no one is a Christian but he who perseveres even to the end.” “But what if a bishop, if a deacon, if a widow, if a virgin, if a doctor, if even a martyr, have fallen from the rule of faith,” he presses, “will heresies on that account appear to possess the truth? Do we prove the faith by the persons, or the persons by the faith?”2 Jerome later applied the same distinction between a writer’s teaching and his person to Cyprian’s reading of Tertullian (below).

In Against Praxeas, written about 210 against a Roman teacher whom he charges with collapsing Father, Son and Spirit into one person, Tertullian writes that the one God is guarded by “the mystery of the dispensation… which distributes the Unity into a Trinity, placing in their order the three Persons — the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power.” The word trinitas in this treatise is among its earliest datable occurrences in Latin, and J. Chapman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) holds that Tertullian “must have invented a theological idiom and have coined new expressions.”3 The same treatise is written from within the New Prophecy. Its author writes as a man “better instructed by the Paraclete, who leads men… into all truth”; he reports that he and his “subsequently withdrew from the carnally-minded… on our acknowledgment and maintenance of the Paraclete”; and he opens the book by blaming Praxeas for an act of church politics at Rome. The bishop there, Tertullian reports, had “acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, and… had bestowed his peace on the churches of Asia and Phrygia,” and was on the point of confirming them when Praxeas, “by importunately urging false accusations against the prophets themselves and their churches, and insisting on the authority of the bishop’s predecessors in the see, compelled him to recall the pacific letter.” So Praxeas, in Tertullian’s phrase, “put to flight the Paraclete” even as, by his modalism, he “crucified the Father.” On Tertullian’s account, then, a Roman bishop first recognised the prophets and then recalled that recognition. The passage does not name the bishop: the ANF elucidation suggests Victor, the Catholic Encyclopedia’s “Montanists” article Eleutherus, and its “Praxeas” article Victor “or possibly Zephyrinus”; D. F. Wright (1976) holds that the Roman action “apparently did not amount to formal censure or excommunication.”4

Tertullian’s own testimony: the turn to the New Prophecy (c. 205–212)

Tertullian’s own later works are the earliest extant evidence of his adherence to the New Prophecy. In them the Paraclete promised in John’s Gospel is a living, still-speaking authority, whose office, he argues, is to carry Christian discipline beyond what the apostles left: in On the Veiling of Virgins he writes that “the reason why the Lord sent the Paraclete was, that, since human mediocrity was unable to take in all things at once, discipline should, little by little, be directed, and ordained, and carried on to perfection, by that Vicar of the Lord, the Holy Spirit,” righteousness having been “first in a rudimentary state” and only “now, through the Paraclete… settling into maturity.”5 He quotes the Phrygian prophets as Gospel preaching: in On Exhortation to Chastity “through the holy prophetess Prisca the Gospel is thus preached: that ‘the holy minister knows how to minister sanctity.’”6 The absolute dates of the works are disputed, and T. D. Barnes (1971) holds that they cannot be sorted into tidy “Catholic” and “Montanist” periods; the sequence followed here is Barnes’s relative chronology, which places the openly New-Prophecy treatises late, roughly 207–212.7

On this authority he presses a disciplinary programme — no second marriage, longer and stricter fasts, no flight in persecution, the veiling of unmarried girls — and divides Christians into two groups by their reception of the Spirit. In On Monogamy he writes from his own side: “Among us… whom the recognition of spiritual gifts entitles to be deservedly called Spiritual, continence is as religious as licence is modest… We admit one marriage, just as we do one God. But to the Psychics, since they receive not the Spirit, the things which are the Spirit’s are not pleasing.”8 The psychici — the “soulish,” merely natural Christians — are, in his usage, those who do not accept the Paraclete’s stricter rule; the spiritales are those who do.

In On Modesty he answers a bishop who had relaxed the penance for adultery: “I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops — issues an edict: ‘I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication.’” He denies that the power of the keys is the bishops’ to use so: the Church “will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops.”9 Whether this passage marks a separation from the church of Carthage is among the disputed readings below.

Other passages of the late works bear on the same question. In On Fasting he states that the New Prophecies are rejected “not that Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God, nor that they disjoin Jesus Christ (from God), nor that they overturn any particular rule of faith or hope, but that they plainly teach more frequent fasting than marrying.”10 And in On the Soul he describes a prophetess in the congregation: “We have now amongst us a sister whose lot it has been to be favoured with sundry gifts of revelation, which she experiences in the Spirit by ecstatic vision amidst the sacred rites of the Lord’s day in the church… After the people are dismissed at the conclusion of the sacred services, she is in the regular habit of reporting to us whatever things she may have seen in vision.”11 Douglas Powell (1975) and William Tabbernee (1997) read this evidence, with the withdrawal “from the carnally-minded” quoted above, as a rigorist circle within the Carthaginian church — in Powell’s phrase an ecclesiola in ecclesia — rather than a departure from it. The earliest extant notice of a separate sect, the Tertullianistae, is Augustine’s, further down this timeline; Tertullian’s own works do not use the name.12

The generation after: Cyprian, Hippolytus, and the anonymous heresy-list (c. 220–260)

No surviving text of the generation after Tertullian names him as a heretic, and the third-century catalogues of the Phrygians set out below do not mention him. In the same period his works were drawn on without his name.

The chief case is Cyprian, bishop of Carthage from 248, who died a martyr, and whose treatises draw on Tertullian’s without naming him. The prefatory notes of the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation and Ernest Evans (1953) trace his treatise on the Lord’s Prayer to Tertullian’s On Prayer, clause by clause; his book on patience reworks Tertullian’s On Patience, and his tract on the dress of virgins draws on On the Apparel of Women — each recast in Cyprian’s own Latin, and in each the source goes unnamed.13 That Cyprian read him daily is reported by Jerome, writing about 393: he had met in Italy an old man named Paul of Concordia who as a youth had been secretary to the aged Cyprian, and who said that Cyprian “was accustomed never to pass a day without reading Tertullian, and that he frequently said to him, ‘Give me the master’ [da magistrum], meaning by this, Tertullian.”14 Jerome is the only extant source for the anecdote, which he had at second hand, a century and a half after the events. Cyprian himself gives no reason for leaving his source unnamed. Evans connects the matter to Tertullian’s turn to the New Prophecy, which on his account “caused later generations to regard all his works with suspicion,” so that “it was through the disciple that his thoughts on the Lord’s Prayer became the common property of Christendom.”15

The third-century catalogues of Montanism do not name him. Hippolytus of Rome, writing his Refutation of All Heresies about 222–235, twice handles the sect — “The Phrygians… derive the principles of their heresy from a certain Montanus, and Priscilla, and Maximilla, and regard these wretched women as prophetesses, and Montanus as a prophet” — and names its founders and its errors without mentioning Tertullian.16 So too the earliest Latin heresy-list to cover the group, the anonymous Against All Heresies, which has come down appended to Tertullian’s own works and long circulated under his name. It divides the “Cataphrygians” into the followers of Proclus and of Aeschines and sets out their common blasphemy — “that the Paraclete has spoken in Montanus more things than Christ brought forward into the Gospel” — but has no “Tertullianist” branch and does not mention Tertullian.17

Novatian of Rome built his own On the Trinity on Tertullian’s — on Jerome’s description, “a sort of epitome of the work of Tertullian” — a work which, Jerome adds, many ascribed to Cyprian.18

Jerome’s biographical notice (c. 392)

The earliest extant connected account of Tertullian’s life is chapter 53 of Jerome’s De viris illustribus, a catalogue of Christian writers on the model of Suetonius, written about 392, more than a century and a half after Tertullian died. The notice states that he was “the presbyter,” chief of the Latin writers after Victor and Apollonius; that he was a Carthaginian, “the son of a proconsul or Centurion”; that he was “presbyter of the church until middle life”; that, “driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus”; that he wrote “directly against the church” the treatises On Modesty, On Fasts, On Monogamy and “six books On ecstasy” (now lost); and that “he is said to have lived to a decrepit old age.”19 Jerome’s verb for the turn is lapsus est, “he lapsed.”

In Against Jovinianus (393), which discourages remarriage without condemning it, Jerome cites Tertullian on marriage, and in Letter 84 (c. 400) he adduces Cyprian’s reading of “the master” as the precedent for reading a writer’s learning without following him into Montanism.20 T. D. Barnes (1971) holds that several details of the notice lack independent support: on his reading Tertullian’s own works do not call him a presbyter — he appears rather to number himself among the laity — do not mention his father, and do not mention a grievance against the Roman clergy, so that the presbyterate and the “envy and abuse” motive read as Jerome’s inference or embellishment.21

Whether Tertullian was ordained a presbyter (with the identification of him as a Roman jurist and his conversion “in middle life”), and whether resentment of the Roman clergy was his motive for the turn, are taken up as disputed readings below. The full notice is set out on Jerome’s page.22

Augustine’s heresy-catalogue: De haeresibus 86 (c. 428)

Near the end of his life, compiling a handbook of the heresies for the Carthaginian deacon Quodvultdeus, Augustine gave Tertullian his own numbered notice, chapter 86 of the De haeresibus — the earliest extant heresy-catalogue entry under his name, and the earliest extant notice of a distinct Tertullianist group. Augustine keeps it separate from the Montanists, whom he catalogues apart as the Cataphrygians (chapter 26). And he reports its end as an eyewitness: the Tertullianistae had dwindled “little by little down to our own time” to a last remnant in Carthage, and when he was there some years before, “the very few who remained went over into the catholic church and handed their basilica… over to the catholic church.”23

Augustine states a doctrinal charge — that Tertullian held the soul, and even God, to be a body — and adds that it is not the ground of the heresy: “yet he is not on this account said to have been made a heretic.” The grounds he gives are these: “It is not for this that Tertullian was made a heretic,” he writes, “but because, going over to the Cataphrygians — whom he had earlier destroyed — he began also to condemn second marriages, against apostolic teaching, as though they were fornication; and afterwards, divided even from them, he propagated his own little assemblies.” The turn to the New Prophecy, the ban on remarriage, and a final separation from the Montanists themselves are the three counts; the last clause, sua conventicula propagavit, is the earliest extant statement that Tertullian founded a group of his own.24

The later ancient report of the group, in the anonymous Praedestinatus (c. 435), follows Augustine’s. In the notice, sicut scripta eius indicant (“as his writings show”) introduces the teaching on the soul; Augustine names no source for the founding of the group. Whether it was one Tertullian founded, as Augustine states, or a Carthaginian rigorist circle that stayed in communion, as Powell (1975) and Tabbernee (1997) read the African evidence, is among the disputed readings below. Elsewhere Augustine argues against Tertullian’s corporealism on the origin of the soul, calling it “the ravings of Tertullian,” while leaving open the traducianism it serves; and against the Manichees he dates their sect “not only after Tertullian, but after Cyprian.” The full notice, with the Latin, is on Augustine’s page.25

Vincent of Lérins and the Decretum Gelasianum (434 – early sixth century)

In the Commonitorium of 434 Vincent of Lérins devotes a chapter to Tertullian, following the chapter he had given Origen, and sets the two side by side: “For as Origen holds by far the first place among the Greeks, so does Tertullian among the Latins,” he begins. He praises Tertullian as the most learned of his people — “every word almost was a sentence; every sentence a victory” — whose “many and ponderous volumes” the Marcionites, Apelleians, Praxeans, Hermogenians, Jews, heathens and Gnostics had felt. Then he turns: “Yet this man also… this Tertullian, I say, too little tenacious of Catholic doctrine, that is, of the universal and ancient faith, more eloquent by far than faithful, changed his belief,” so that “by his subsequent error he detracted from the authority of his approved writings.” In Vincent’s argument Tertullian is the Latin instance, as Origen is the Greek, of the learned teacher whose gifts became a trial to the Church.26 Vincent’s chapter cites no synod or anathema against Tertullian.

The other document of the period that names him is a book-list, the so-called Decretum Gelasianum, a register of works “to be received and not received” that travelled under the name of Pope Gelasius I and was for centuries read as a papal decree. Ernst von Dobschütz’s edition (1912), as F. C. Burkitt summarized it (1913), holds that it is an anonymous private compilation of the first half of the sixth century, most probably Italian: the section ascribed to Damasus quotes Augustine, so the ascription “is of no historical value,” and had it been an official decree of Gelasius “it would have been known and used by Dionysius Exiguus and Cassiodorus.”27 In its fifth part, a “list of apocryphal books” — “the remaining writings which have been compiled or been recognised by heretics or schismatics the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive” — the entry reads opuscula Tertulliani apocrypha, “the works of Tertullian… apocryphal.” The same list places among the works not received those of Arnobius, Lactantius, Cassian, Clement of Alexandria, Africanus, Commodian, Victorinus of Pettau, and Thascius Cyprianus — Cyprian, whom Jerome’s report has reading Tertullian every day.28

Apart from the Decretum, whose papal status von Dobschütz and Burkitt reject, no surviving act of a council or a pope names Tertullian; Origen’s condemnation by name is traced on its own timeline.29

Medieval transmission and the 1521 edition (c. 850–1539)

Tertullian was among the least-copied of the major Latin fathers (⚠ unverified). Ernest Evans (1953) writes that the survival of his works through the Middle Ages “must be regarded as almost a miracle,” all copies having seemed “to have disappeared from the libraries of Italy” once their reading was forbidden; Pierre de Labriolle (1924) writes that later ages “took advantage of his unsound reputation to copy from his writings abundantly — without giving his name.”30 The surviving text rests on few witnesses. The oldest is the ninth-century Codex Agobardinus (Paris, lat. 1622), given to the cathedral of Lyons by its archbishop Agobard; it is “the only witness to much of what it contains” — the sole surviving copy of the two books To the Nations and of On the Veiling of Virgins, among others — and its rear portion is lost, breaking off in the middle of On the Flesh of Christ, so that the further works its table of contents once listed are lost with it. P. Petitmengin and J. P. Carley (2003) set beside it two further independent sources: the twelfth-century Codex Trecensis at Troyes, and the lost Cluny–Hirsau corpus that became the source of the first printed edition.31 Among the lost works are the six books On Ecstasy, with a seventh added against Apollonius — the treatise Jerome named — which Chapman (1912) calls “the most important” of them, “the defence of the Montanist manner of prophesying.”32

On the first of July 1521, at Basel, the humanist Beatus Rhenanus brought out through the press of Johann Froben — reportedly on Erasmus’s advice — the editio princeps of Tertullian’s works, its title-page announcing them “drawn out of the shadows and rescued from neglect” (è tenebris eruta); Rhenanus revised and enlarged the edition in 1528 and 1539.33 Catholic and Protestant controversialists both drew on him. Catholic writers applied the argument of his Prescription Against Heretics to the Reformers — that those who broke with the Church’s tradition had no right to argue from her Scriptures; Protestants such as Martin Chemnitz answered that his “rule of faith” drew its content from Scripture, and pressed his attack in On Modesty on the bishop’s penitential edict against the papacy’s penitential claims (⚠ unverified). Rhenanus’s own annotations were placed on the Index of Paul IV in 1559. Tertullian’s arguments, in Mark L. Movsesian’s 2019 summary of R. L. Wilken’s Liberty in the Things of God, “appeared, refashioned and restated in varying ways, during the Reformation, in the writings of Catholics resisting Protestant rulers and of Protestants resisting Catholics.”34

Modern scholarship (1971–2007)

T. D. Barnes’s Tertullian: A Historical and Literary Study (1971) questioned much of Jerome’s biography. Douglas Powell’s 1975 article “Tertullianists and Cataphrygians,” followed by David Rankin (1995), Geoffrey Dunn (2004) and David Wilhite (2007), argues that Tertullian probably never left the catholic Church. On their reading his own writings record no synod, excommunication or departure; he writes as a man inside the Church; and the North African New Prophecy was a rigorist current within the Carthaginian congregation — the ecclesiola in ecclesia of Powell’s phrase — so that the “Montanist schism” and the separate “Tertullianist sect” are largely constructions of later heresiology, working back from Jerome and Augustine. Wilhite presses the case furthest: Tertullian a layman who never left the Carthaginian church.35

Tertullian’s theological terms remain in use. Trinitas appears in Against Praxeas among its earliest datable occurrences in Latin, and persona, substantia and satisfactio, words of the theatre, philosophy and the law-courts, appear in his works in theological senses; Harnack describes his transfer of “the technical terms of the jurists into the ecclesiastical language of the West.” Chapman’s Catholic Encyclopedia article (1912) calls him “the first of Latin theological writers,” who “must have invented a theological idiom and have coined new expressions.”36

Tertullian has not been canonized or given a feast day. The New World Encyclopedia states that he “was never recognized as a saint by the Eastern or Western Catholic churches,” his later turn to Montanism leading both “to refrain from considering him a Church father, despite his significant contributions as an ecclesiastical writer”; the Orthodox writer D. C. Ford holds that “the ‘Founder of Latin theology’ actually dies outside the Church”; and Anne Stokes, writing for the Catholic site Catholic365 (2026), pairs him with Origen as the great “non-saints” of the Church.37

Disputed readings

Questions about how Tertullian’s texts and the later reports about him have been read, cutting across the timeline above.

His standing in the church of Carthage and the counts against him

How his relation to the Carthaginian church, and the counts later laid against him, have been read.

Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage?

Powell (1975), Rankin (1995), Dunn (2004) and Wilhite (2007) read the African New Prophecy as a circle within the congregation; Augustine reports a Tertullianist body with its own basilica, and On Modesty 21 sets “the Church of the Spirit” against “the Church which consists of a number of bishops.”38

Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline?

Tertullian (On Fasting 1), Trevett (1996), Tabbernee and Wright (1976) place the offence in prophecy and discipline; the anonymous Against All Heresies names as the sect’s blasphemy that “the Paraclete has spoken in Montanus more things than Christ brought forward into the Gospel.”39

Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it?

Harnack held that the primitive church reserved the capital sins; d’Alès (1914), Poschmann and Rahner held that it always claimed the power to reconcile them.40

Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

Tertullian frames it as discipline perfected by the Paraclete; Augustine names it, “against apostolic teaching,” as a ground of the heresy.41

His theology

How three features of his teaching — on the Son, on the soul, and on the fault inherited from Adam — have been read.

Was Tertullian’s subordination of the Son one of order and origin, or of nature?

Turns on Against Praxeas 9 and Against Hermogenes 3; Braun, Kelly and Osborn read an order of derivation within one substance.42

How is Tertullian’s teaching that the soul is a body to be read?

Waszink sets it within Stoic physics, as a claim about the soul’s reality; Augustine names it an error while declining it as the ground of the heresy.43

How is the fault Tertullian says descends from Adam with the soul related to the doctrine of original sin?

Turns on On the Soul 40–41 and on Augustine’s use of the same transmission of the fault.44

Jerome’s biographical notice

How the details of the received life, which descend from Jerome’s chapter of c. 392, have been read.

Was Tertullian a presbyter, a jurist, and a convert in middle life?

Jerome calls him a presbyter; Barnes (1971) finds no independent support for it, and Wilhite (2007) reads him as a layman; Rankin (1995) holds that he understood himself in strongly clerical terms.45

Did resentment of the Roman clergy lead Tertullian to the New Prophecy?

Jerome gives the motive; Mohrmann (1951) reads in it “the resentment of saint Jerome himself with regard to the clergy of Rome,” and Barnes (1971) reads it as embellishment.46

Other disputed readings

  • Does “certum est, quia impossibile” (On the Flesh of Christ 5) express fideism? Moffatt (1916), Sider (1980) and Osborn (1997) read an Aristotelian argument from improbability; Harrison (2017) traces the slogan credo quia absurdum to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.47
  • Was Tertullian’s counsel to delay the baptism of little children (On Baptism 18) a Montanist position? Baptist-successionist genealogies (J. M. Carroll) enlist the Montanists as an early believers’-baptism dissent; Koperski dates On Baptism to Tertullian’s earlier period.48
  • How has the reception of Tertullian’s theology been held together with the withholding of sainthood? Turns on the distinction Tertullian himself draws between the faith and the persons, applied by Jerome (Letter 84) and Vincent.49
