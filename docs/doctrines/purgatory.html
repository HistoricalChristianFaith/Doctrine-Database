Purgatory and prayer for the dead

This timeline follows two related things through the record: the practice of prayer, alms and the Eucharistic offering for the dead, and teaching about a purification of the faithful after death, which Latin theology calls purgatory and which it has often described as a cleansing fire. Witnesses are listed by the date of their earliest extant attestation, from 2 Maccabees (c. 124 BC) to C. S. Lewis (1964). (Latin theology also spoke of a limbus infantum for infants who die unbaptized, a question traced under infant baptism.)

Who holds what. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) teaches that "all who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification," to which "the Church gives the name Purgatory"; it states that the Church "formulated her doctrine of faith on Purgatory especially at the Councils of Florence and Trent," that the tradition "speaks of a cleansing fire," and that "this teaching is also based on the practice of prayer for the dead."1 Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article "Purgatory" (1911), reads the early record (Tertullian, Cyprian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine) as witness to the doctrine, writing that "the Fathers speak, in the same breath, of oblations for the dead and a place of purgation."2 At the Council of Ferrara–Florence (1438–1439), Mark, Metropolitan of Ephesus, replying in writing to the Latin report on purgatory, affirmed that "those reposed in faith are without doubt helped by the Liturgies and prayers and almsgiving performed for them," but said that deliverance "thanks to a certain purgatorial suffering and temporal fire … we do not find either in the Scriptures or in the prayers and hymns for the dead, or in the words of the teachers."3 The Orthodox bishop Kallistos (Timothy) Ware, in The Orthodox Church, dates purgatory's emergence "as a point of dispute between east and west" to the thirteenth century, and writes that the Confession of Dositheus (1672), "in defending prayers for the dead," "came very close to the Roman doctrine of Purgatory, without actually using the word Purgatory itself."4 The Orthodox Church in America's published answer on prayer for the dead calls purgatory, a teaching "found among the Roman Catholics," "completely alien" to Orthodox Christianity, and states that "Orthodox Christians pray for the dead so that the Lord will have mercy on their souls."5 The Lutheran Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531) states that "the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit," while disapproving the application of the Lord's Supper ex opere operato for the dead;6 the Reformed Westminster Confession (1646) holds that at death souls go to heaven or hell and that "besides these two places for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none."7 The Church of England's Article XXII (Thirty-Nine Articles) states that "the Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory, Pardons, Worshipping, and Adoration, as well of Images as of Reliques, and also invocation of Saints, is a fond thing vainly invented, and grounded upon no warranty of Scripture, but rather repugnant to the Word of God."8 Anglican and Methodist witnesses (John Wesley, C. S. Lewis) appear in the timeline below.

Related doctrines: The living and the dead (the communion of the saints) — see also the intermediate state · indulgences · the treasury of merit · prayer to the saints · dream-apparitions of the dead · universal restoration (apokatastasis) · conditional immortality (annihilationism) · the resurrection of the body · eternal conscious torment (prayer for the dead sits beside prayer to the saints; indulgences and the treasury of merit concern the remission of temporal punishment, applied also to the dead; and the restoration and annihilationist pages trace other teachings about fire and judgment after death).

Timeline

2 Maccabees (c. 124 BC)

2 Maccabees is the earliest text on this timeline to describe an offering and prayer for the dead: Judas Maccabeus, finding idolatrous tokens on his fallen soldiers, sends a sin offering to Jerusalem for them "that they might be delivered from sin," reasoning that, but for the resurrection, "it had been superfluous and vain to pray for the dead."9

The Acts of Paul and Thecla (c. 160)

The apocryphal Acts of Paul and Thecla is the earliest Christian narrative on this timeline of prayer for the dead: Queen Tryphaena's dead daughter Falconilla appears in a dream asking that Thecla pray for her "that I may be transferred to the place of the just," and Thecla prays aloud that the departed "may live for ever."10

The Epitaph of Abercius (c. 190)

Abercius, bishop of Hieropolis in Phrygia, composed his own tomb-inscription while living and had carved on it a request that every believer who reads it pray for him: "He that discerneth these things, every fellow-believer, let him pray for Abercius." It is the earliest epigraphic request for prayer for the dead on this timeline; earlier in the same inscription he speaks of a "fish" eaten with "wine … mingled with bread," which the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) reads as Eucharistic language.11

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement of Alexandria teaches that God's punishments are not retributive but reformative — "God does not punish, for punishment is retaliation for evil. He chastises, however, for good to those who are chastised" — and that this discipline reaches the dead, whose souls, "when released from their bodies, are able to perceive more clearly" and so can still be brought to repentance. He describes the discipline as medicinal and educative.12

Perpetua (c. 203)

The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity is the earliest detailed Christian narrative on this timeline of a believer praying for a dead person: imprisoned at Carthage, Perpetua prays for her dead brother Dinocrates and sees him first in "a gloomy place," parched and unable to reach water, then — after she prays for him "every day" — "with a clean body well clad," refreshed and drinking freely, concluding "that he was translated from the place of punishment."13

Tertullian (c. 211)

Tertullian is the earliest Latin witness on this timeline to the practice: the Church offers oblations for the dead on the anniversary of their death — "as often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead" — and the widow "prays for his soul, and requests refreshment for him meanwhile." He lists the anniversary offering among the Church's customary observances.14

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

Origen, Clement's successor at Alexandria, reads Paul's "wood, hay, stubble" (1 Cor 3:12–15) of the sinner's own fire — "every sinner kindles for himself the flame of his own fire … [its] fuel and food are our sins" — and describes the fire as medicinal, "God our Physician" applying it to burn out the soul's defects so that "a restoration be effected." His fire is universal and healing, part of his teaching on restoration (apokatastasis), and set at the last judgment.15

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 253)

Cyprian, defending the restoration of penitents to communion, distinguishes the martyr who is "crowned" at once from the believer who must be "cleansed and long purged by fire" — "it is one thing, tortured by long suffering for sins, to be cleansed and long purged by fire; another to have purged all sins by suffering." Whether the passage describes purification after death or the penitential discipline of the lapsed in this life is disputed; see Cyprian's page.16

The Liturgy of Addai and Mari (c. 300)

The East-Syriac Liturgy of Addai and Mari (the Anaphora of the Blessed Apostles), a Eucharistic prayer still in use in the Church of the East, offers the oblation "for all the dead that have gone from amongst us" and prays God to "spare the offences and sins of the dead, through Thy grace and mercies for ever." It is the earliest liturgical text from the Syriac East on this timeline in which the offering is made for the departed.17

Lactantius (c. 310)

Lactantius, writing during the Great Persecution, describes a discriminating fire of judgment: one and the same divine fire scorches those "whose sins shall exceed either in weight or in number," yet is not so much as felt by "they whom full justice and maturity of virtue has imbued," who carry "something of God in themselves which repels … the violence of the flame." The fire tries each in proportion to sin and is set at the Last Judgment.18

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril of Jerusalem describes the Eucharistic supplication for "those who have fallen asleep" as "a very great benefit to the souls for whom the supplication is put up, while that holy and most awful sacrifice is set forth."19 The Mystagogic Catecheses stand here under their conventional ascription to Cyril; some manuscripts, and James Ussher (1624), name his successor John, and the authorship is disputed (the parties are named on his page).19

The Liturgy of Saint Mark (c. 350)

The Alexandrian Liturgy of Saint Mark sets the commemoration of the dead within the offering: the deacon "reads the record of the dead" (the diptychs) and the priest prays God to "give peace to the souls of our fathers and brethren who have fallen asleep in Jesus" and to "deem them worthy of the kingdom of heaven."20

Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355)

Aerius, a presbyter of Sebaste in Pontus, is the one ancient figure on this timeline recorded as rejecting prayer and offering for the dead. He held the naming of the departed in the liturgy to be useless and even harmful: "if the prayer of the people here has benefited the people there, no one should practice piety or perform good works" — the objection that vicarious post-mortem aid makes personal holiness dispensable. Epiphanius lists the rejection as the seventy-fifth heresy of his Panarion, appeals to "a world-wide agreement before Aerius and his Aerians," and answers it; Augustine's De Haeresibus also records it. Half a century later, on the neighbouring practice of prayer to the saints, Vigilantius of Calagurris (c. 406) objected and Jerome replied.21

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

Epiphanius defends prayer and offering for the dead against Aerius, who rejected it as pointless: the naming of and prayer for the departed is, he says, the Church's established and "useful" practice — "useful too is the prayer fashioned on their behalf, even if it does not force back the whole of guilty charges laid to them." It is the earliest extant written defence of the practice against a named objector.22

Ambrose of Milan (c. 378)

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, offers the Eucharist for the departed in his funeral orations. Over his dead brother he turns the eulogy into an offering: "to Thee I offer my sacrifice; accept favourably and mercifully the gift of a brother, the offering of a priest." Over the young emperor Valentinian II he asks: "give the holy mysteries to the dead; with devout affection let us beseech rest for him … let us attend [the young emperor's] soul with our offerings." Pietro Piacenza, in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), quotes the Latin of this line and writes: "So originated the Introit for the Mass for the Dead."23

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

Ambrosiaster, the anonymous Latin commentator, reads 1 Corinthians 3:15 of purification: the believer whose works are burnt "will be saved only by being purified through fire."24

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 382)

Gregory of Nyssa teaches a post-mortem purifying fire: the soul still "welded" to evil at death must pass through "the purgatorial fire" that refines it as a furnace refines gold from dross — "while evil is being consumed in the purgatorial fire, the soul that is welded to this evil must inevitably be in the fire too." What is not cleansed "here" by baptism and repentance must be "purified by fire" hereafter. Gregory describes the fire as medicinal and sets it within his teaching on restoration (apokatastasis), the restoring of every soul "after long succeeding ages."25

John Chrysostom (c. 392)

Chrysostom commends prayer and offering for the dead ("not in vain … is mention made of the departed in the course of the divine mysteries … that some refreshment may thereby ensue to them"), and reads the fire of 1 Corinthians 3 as the penalty of judgment rather than a purgation: "in saying, 'he shall be saved,' he hath but darkly hinted at the intensity of the penalty … himself shall remain forever in punishment."26

The Liturgy of Saint James (c. 400)

In the Jerusalem/Antiochene Liturgy of Saint James the Eucharist is offered "for the rest of the souls that have fallen asleep aforetime," and the great intercession asks God to "give rest" to all the faithful departed "from righteous Abel unto this day … in the bosom of Abraham."27

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine leaves a purifying fire after death as an open question — "it is not impossible that something of the same kind may take place even after this life … a matter that may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful" — while holding that the dead are aided by "the piety of their living friends," their prayers, alms, and the Eucharistic sacrifice.28

Caesarius of Arles (c. 520)

Caesarius of Arles teaches that the fire purges only slight sins, not grave ones — "in that fire it is slight sins which are purged, not serious ones" — capital sins barring heaven outright.29

Gregory the Great (c. 594)

Gregory the Great teaches: "there must be a cleansing fire before judgment, because of some minor faults that may remain to be purged away" (Dialogues IV.39) — restricted to "little and very light sins" and to those who in life "deserved by virtuous works to find such favour."30

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas teaches a purgatorial fire by which "the elect will be cleansed," assigned its own "abode" among the receptacles of souls, discharging the temporal debt of sin after guilt is forgiven — the limbus patrum having been emptied when "Christ descended into hell."31

Martin Luther (c. 1544)

Luther rejects purgatory and the endowed Masses for the dead (in the Smalcald Articles purgatory, "and every solemnity, rite, and commerce connected with it, is to be regarded as nothing but a specter of the devil"), and does not forbid simple prayer for the departed: since God has neither commanded nor forbidden it and the state of the dead is hidden, a Christian may pray "in uncertainty" — "Dear God, if the departed souls be in a state that they may yet be helped, then I pray that thou wouldst be gracious" — once or twice, then commend them to God. The confession he endorsed, the Apology of the Augsburg Confession (XXIV), draws the same line: "the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit," while rejecting the ex opere operato Mass for the dead.32

John Calvin (c. 1559)

Calvin rejects purgatory as contrary to the sufficiency of Christ's work: "purgatory is a deadly device of Satan; … it makes void the cross of Christ; … it undermines and overthrows our faith"; the limbus patrum likewise "is nothing but a fable." He declines to answer the 2 Maccabees 12 proof-text "lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books," and holds that "the whole Law and Gospel do not contain one syllable which countenances the right of praying for the dead."33

The Westminster Confession (1646)

The Westminster Confession (ch. XXXII) teaches that the souls of the righteous "are received into the highest heavens" and the wicked "cast into hell" immediately at death, and that "besides these two places for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none."34

John Wesley (c. 1750)

John Wesley, founder of Methodism and a Church-of-England priest, holds and practises prayer for the dead without the doctrine of purgatory: "I believe it to be a duty to observe to pray for the Faithful Departed," a general intercession he conceives himself "clearly justified" in "both by the earliest Antiquity, by the Church of England, and by the Lord's Prayer" — modelled on the Prayer Book's own burial collect, that the faithful departed with the living "may have their perfect consummation and bliss."35

C. S. Lewis (c. 1964)

C. S. Lewis, the Anglican lay writer, affirms both prayer for the dead and a purgatory of cleansing: "Of course I pray for the dead … I believe in Purgatory" — while granting that "the Reformers had good reasons for throwing doubt on 'the Romish doctrine concerning Purgatory' as that Romish doctrine had then become." He asks, "Our souls demand Purgatory, don't they?" — the redeemed soul itself asking to be washed before the presence of God.36

Disputed readings

How the origins of indulgences and the ancient record of prayer for the dead have been read.

  • Did the crusade indulgence give rise to the indulgence practice Luther protested in 1517? — on the line Thomas Madden traces from Urban II's remission for the First Crusade (1095) through Innocent III's redeemable crusade vow (1213–1215) to the St. Peter's indulgence, and on the indulgence's earlier roots.37
  • Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church? — on what Aerius's objection (c. 355), and Epiphanius's and Augustine's answers to it, show about the extent of the practice.38
