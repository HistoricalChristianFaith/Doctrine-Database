The New Testament on Matthew

Dates: c. 30–90 (the events, and the writing of the Gospels and Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: Matthew is named in all four lists of the Twelve and given one narrative: his call from the toll-booth. Matthew’s own Gospel alone tags him “the publican” in the list. A parallel call-scene in Mark and Luke names the man Levi, and the question whether Matthew and Levi are one man arises from these passages. The New Testament records nothing of his life after the call.

Claims & evidence

Called from the receipt of custom — a tax collector

Matthew’s Gospel tells his call in a single verse: a man at his toll-booth, summoned with two words, who rises and follows. In the ancient world a publican (Greek telōnēs) — a collector of tolls and customs for the Roman system — was reckoned among the worst of sinners, a class grouped with harlots; the Pharisees’ complaint in the scene concerns Jesus’ eating with tax-collectors.

And as Jesus passed forth from thence, he saw a man, named Matthew, sitting at the receipt of custom: and he saith unto him, Follow me. And he arose, and followed him.

— Matthew 9:9. The feast that follows draws the Pharisees’ complaint, “Why eateth your Master with publicans and sinners?” and the answer, “They that be whole need not a physician, but they that are sick” (Matthew 9:11–13).

“Matthew the publican” in the list of the Twelve

Matthew appears in every list of the apostles. Only in Matthew’s own Gospel is he marked with his old trade — “Matthew the publican”; the other lists do not give him the title. The Golden Legend, citing “the gloss,” reads the self-designation as a mark of Matthew’s humility.

Philip, and Bartholomew; Thomas, and Matthew the publican; James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus;

— Matthew 10:3. In Mark he is simply “Matthew” (Mark 3:18), in Luke “Matthew” (Luke 6:15), and in Acts, among the Eleven in the upper room, “Bartholomew, and Matthew” (Acts 1:13).

The parallel call-scene: Levi in Mark and Luke

Mark and Luke tell a parallel call-scene — the toll-booth, the “Follow me,” the rising and following, the feast with publicans — in which the man is named Levi. Mark names his father:

And as he passed by, he saw Levi the son of Alphaeus sitting at the receipt of custom, and said unto him, Follow me. And he arose and followed him.

— Mark 2:14. Luke tells it the same way and adds the feast: “And after these things he went forth, and saw a publican, named Levi, sitting at the receipt of custom: and he said unto him, Follow me. And he left all, rose up, and followed him” (Luke 5:27–28); “And Levi made him a great feast in his own house” (Luke 5:29). The three scenes run closely parallel; Jerome identifies Matthew with Levi (Lives of Illustrious Men 3).

Notes

  • “Son of Alphaeus.” Mark calls Levi “the son of Alphaeus” — the same patronymic borne by another of the Twelve, James the son of Alphaeus. The New Testament does not say whether Matthew/Levi was a brother of James, and never links the two apostles.
  • Whether Matthew is Levi. The Synoptics call the man at the toll-booth “Matthew” in one Gospel and “Levi” in two, in parallel scenes; but no text states the equation, and “Levi” is never listed among the Twelve. Jerome writes “Matthew, also called Levi”; Heracleon’s list, quoted by Clement of Alexandria, names the two separately.
  • After the call. Beyond the call-scene and his place in the lists, the New Testament records no word or deed of Matthew. His authorship of the first Gospel, his mission, and his death are reported in later sources, traced on the summary timeline.
