Was Matthew’s Gospel first written in Hebrew?

Disputed reading · on Matthew · witness: Papias, and the patristic chain

Related readings: Eusebius’s report that Pantaenus found a Hebrew Matthew in “India”, left there by Bartholomew, also figures in Where did Bartholomew preach: in “India”, in Armenia, or in both? · Did Thomas evangelize India?

The passage

So then Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able.

— Papias of Hierapolis (c. 125), in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.16.1

The question

Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome state that Matthew first wrote his Gospel in Hebrew (Aramaic, on J. E. Jacquier’s reading of the word) for Jewish believers. The question is whether the Greek Gospel of Matthew is a translation of such an original or a work composed in Greek, and what Papias meant by Matthew’s “oracles” (logia). It bears on the origin of the first Gospel and on how the patristic testimony relates to the Greek text.

A Semitic Gospel of Matthew stands behind the Greek

This reading holds that Matthew wrote his Gospel in Hebrew or Aramaic and that the Greek Gospel is a translation of it.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180), Origen (commentary on Matthew, c. 230), Jerome (Lives of Illustrious Men, 393), and J. E. Jacquier (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “Gospel of St. Matthew”, 1911).2

The testimony begins with Papias, who gathered the traditions of “the elders”. Irenaeus states that Matthew “issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect” while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome. Origen writes that he has “learned by tradition” that the first Gospel was written by Matthew “for the converts from Judaism, and published in the Hebrew language.” Eusebius gives the occasion: Matthew, preaching first to the Hebrews and about to go to other peoples, “committed his Gospel to writing in his native tongue.” Jerome adds that “the Hebrew itself has been preserved until the present day in the library at Cæsarea,” that the Nazarenes of Beroea used it, and that wherever the Evangelist quotes the Old Testament “he does not follow the authority of the translators of the Septuagint but the Hebrew.” Eusebius also reports that Pantaenus, about 190, found the Hebrew Matthew in “India”. A Gospel written for a Jewish-Christian readership in Palestine would suit their language.

Jacquier argues that “Hebrew” here means Aramaic, the spoken language, since Eusebius says the Gospel reproduced Matthew’s preaching: “we believe the second hypothesis to be the more probable, viz., that Matthew wrote his Gospel in Aramaic.” He holds that the Greek features of the Gospel “prove merely that the writer was thoroughly conversant with his language, and that he translated his text rather freely,” points to the Hebraisms in the text, and concludes that “no certain conclusion can be drawn against the existence of a Hebrew Gospel of which our First Gospel would be a translation.” He disputes that most of the Old Testament quotations follow the Septuagint, and accounts for the agreement with Mark through the translator: “To us it seems probable that Matthew’s Greek translator used Mark’s Greek Gospel, especially for Christ’s discourses.” On this reading the Fathers’ ascription and the Greek text belong together: Matthew’s Aramaic Gospel, rendered freely into Greek by a translator who drew on Mark.3

The Greek Matthew is a Greek composition

This reading holds that the Gospel of Matthew was composed in Greek.

Held by: Erasmus (humanist, 16th century), W. D. Davies and D. C. Allison (Matthew, International Critical Commentary, 1988), and R. T. France (The Gospel of Matthew, 2007).4

Erasmus, as Jacquier quotes him, wrote: “It does not seem probable to me that Matthew wrote in Hebrew, since no one testifies that he has seen any trace of such a volume.” The reading rests chiefly on the Greek text. Matthew reproduces large stretches of the Gospel of Mark, itself a Greek work, often word for word, as a Greek author using a Greek source does; and where it quotes the Old Testament in narrative it frequently follows the Greek Septuagint. On this reading those are the marks of a book written in Greek.

On this reading Papias’s word logia (“oracles,” “sayings”) may name something other than the finished Gospel: a collection of Jesus’ sayings, or a book of Old Testament testimonia (messianic proof-texts) that Matthew assembled ⚠ unverified (no holder of these identifications is named in the sources cited here). Papias’s remark that “every one interpreted them as he was able” fits a collection that needed translating. If the later Fathers are repeating Papias, their agreement goes back to his single sentence. The text Jerome saw among the Nazarenes is identified, on this reading, with the Jewish-Christian Gospel according to the Hebrews, a distinct work, which Jerome says in the same catalogue he had translated into Greek and Latin. The Gospel of Matthew is then a Greek work built on Greek Mark, and the tradition of a Hebrew Matthew concerns an earlier Semitic collection associated with the apostle.5
