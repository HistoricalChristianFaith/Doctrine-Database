The Golden Legend on Matthew

Dates: c. 1260 (Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda Aurea) · Wikipedia
Summary: A medieval account of Matthew: the story of his mission to Ethiopia, his contest with two sorcerers, his conversion of a king and the consecration of the king’s daughter Iphigenia to virginity, and his martyrdom at the altar when the next king wished to marry her. It was compiled about twelve centuries after the events it narrates, and this page reports what it tells about Matthew.

The legend

Matthew, the Golden Legend relates, came to preach in Ethiopia, in the city of Nadaber, where he lodged with the eunuch of Queen Candace whom Philip had baptized. There he confronted two enchanters, Zaroes and Arphaxat, who deceived the people with their arts and had themselves honoured as gods; Matthew undid their spells, healing where they had harmed, and mastered the two fire-breathing dragons they loosed. When he raised the dead son of King Egippus to life, the sorcerers fled to Persia (where, the legend adds, Simon and Jude would later defeat them), and the king with his wife and his daughter Iphigenia and all Ethiopia were baptized. Matthew, it says, taught there thirty-three years, built churches, and consecrated Iphigenia as head of more than two hundred virgins vowed to God.

After Egippus died, Hirtacus came to the throne and coveted Iphigenia, offering Matthew half his realm if he would win her consent to marriage. Matthew, preaching in the church on the goodness of lawful marriage but the greater good of a vow kept to God, refused to release her — and Iphigenia, vowed to Christ, would not consent. Enraged, Hirtacus sent a swordsman who struck the apostle down as he stood at the altar, having just celebrated the mysteries, “and so was consecrate a martyr”; a traditional date for his death about the year 68 is ⚠ unverified. When the king then tried to burn Iphigenia and her virgins, the fire turned and consumed his own palace instead; his son was seized by a demon, and the king himself, struck with leprosy, died by his own hand.

Notes

  • The emblems. Matthew’s traditional attributes in Western art are the angel or winged man, his Gospel-symbol, taken from Revelation’s four living creatures ⚠ unverified, and the sword or halberd of his martyrdom, which is associated with this legend ⚠ unverified.
  • Sources and date. The tale is described as descending from apocryphal Acts of Matthew and the mission-lists that placed him in Ethiopia ⚠ unverified; it was compiled about 1260. On such hagiography, see the apostles hub.
  • Relation to Clement’s notice. Heracleon, quoted by Clement of Alexandria (c. 200), names Matthew among examples in a discussion of confession; whether this bears on his death is disputed. Both readings are set out on Was Matthew martyred in Ethiopia?
