Do the Sudarium of Oviedo and the Shroud come from the same body?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the claim that the two relics wrapped one crucified head fails on its best case: the cloths' own radiocarbon dates lie centuries apart (Shroud 13th–14th c., Sudarium 7th c.), so “the same body” needs both laboratories to be wrong; AB blood-typing on aged, degraded stains is unreliable; and the geometric stain-coincidences are subjective and, on scrutiny, inflated. With the correspondence goes the Sudarium's chief bid for first-century antiquity.

The claim

The Sudarium of Oviedo is an image-less bloodstained face-cloth (c. 84 × 53 cm) kept at Oviedo, associated with the “napkin” that was “about his head” in John 20:7. Because its own documented history and radiocarbon date are late, its case for first-century origin rests almost entirely on a claimed forensic match with the Shroud of Turin: that both cloths covered the head of the same scourged, crucified man, and therefore date to the same first-century burial. The match is argued from a shared AB blood grouping and from dozens of geometric coincidences between the two patterns of bloodstains. This page weighs that same-body interpretation adversarially.

Who advances it

The correspondence is advanced by the Centro Español de Sindonología (its EDICES investigation team), the historian Mark Guscin, and the American researchers Alan and Mary Whanger, whose “Polarized Image Overlay Technique” generated the claimed count of coincident points; the AB blood claim traces to Pierluigi Baima Bollone (Sudarium) and Heller and Adler (Shroud). The chief critical voice is Hugh Farey (former editor of the British Society for the Turin Shroud Newsletter), reviewing César Barta's proponent work.

The evidence for — steelmanned

On the proponents' best case the parallels are striking. Both cloths are reported to carry human blood of group AB — a grouping uncommon in medieval Europeans but relatively common in the Near East. Guscin writes that “the first fact that confirms the relationship between the two cloths is that the blood on each belongs to the same group, AB.”1 The Whangers' overlay is said to yield some seventy points of coincidence on the frontal stains and about fifty on the rear, together with a matching nose-length (~8 cm), beard shape, and a pattern consistent with wounds from a crown of thorns; proponents add claims of shared Palestinian pollen. If sound, the convergence would argue that one head bled onto both cloths — and since a first-century face-cloth of Christ is exactly what the Oviedo tradition asserts, the Shroud's antiquity and the Sudarium's would stand together.2

Adversarial assessment

The decisive defeater: the dates cannot be reconciled. The two cloths have each been radiocarbon- dated, independently, and the results lie centuries apart — the Shroud to AD 1260–1390, the Sudarium to roughly the sixth-to-ninth centuries (most probably the eighth). If the two wrapped one body at one historical burial, both of those independent laboratory results must be wrong — the Sudarium's off by six centuries, the Shroud's by thirteen. The same-body thesis does not merely coexist with an awkward date; it requires the simultaneous failure of two separate dating programmes in two separate decades, in opposite directions from the target. That is a very large bill, and the proponents pay it the only way available — by attributing both results to contamination — which is special pleading applied twice over.3

The blood match is weaker than it sounds. AB typing on ancient, degraded bloodstains is notoriously unreliable: aged blood and microbial or fungal contamination can produce spurious A- and B-antigen reactivity, and a 2025 study found AB-type reactivity even on unstained areas of such cloths, implying the “AB” readings may be artefacts rather than genuine blood groups. Both cloths' AB claims rest on the same questionable method, so a shared result is as easily a shared artefact as a shared donor; nothing about it individuates one person.4

The geometric coincidences are subjective and inflated. The overlay method chooses which stains to compare, how to scale one cloth to the other, and how to treat transfer through folds; critics show that the counts depend on those choices, that irrelevant or weak similarities are folded into the total, and that headline figures of strong agreement are, in Farey's words, “wildly over optimistic.” A cloth pressed to a bleeding head will of course pick up stains in the regions a head bleeds; generic agreement of that kind does not establish a common origin, still less a common individual. Notably, even some proponents have lately demoted the geometric overlays to their “lowest tier” of evidence and sought firmer forensic footing.5

No a-priori dismissal — but the case does not clear the bar. The verdict is not that two cloths could not share an origin, nor a refusal to grant the tradition a hearing; it is that the specific evidence offered — a method-dependent blood group and a subjective stain-overlay — is too weak to carry the claim, and that the one hard, independent datum on each object (its radiocarbon date) points the other way and cannot be reconciled without discarding both. Where the evidence is granted its best reading, the thesis still fails.

Assessment

Weak. The interpretation that the Sudarium of Oviedo and the Shroud of Turin covered the same crucified head — and so share a first-century origin — does not hold on its best case. It founders on one decisive point and two supporting ones. Decisively, the cloths' own independent radiocarbon dates lie centuries apart (Shroud 1260–1390, Sudarium ~7th c.), so “the same body” requires both laboratory results to be wrong, in opposite directions — a double appeal to contamination that is special pleading. Supporting that, the shared AB blood grouping rests on a typing method unreliable on degraded blood (recent work finds AB reactivity even on unstained fabric), and the geometric stain-coincidences are subjective, scaling-dependent, and inflated, as even some proponents now concede. The correspondence is therefore not established, and the Sudarium's antiquity, which leans on it, falls with it. (The claim is weighed here on the forensics and the dating, not dismissed for requiring a relic to be old.)
