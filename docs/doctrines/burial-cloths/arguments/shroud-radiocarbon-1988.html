Does the 1988 radiocarbon test reliably date the Shroud to 1260–1390?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the medieval result is the consensus of radiocarbon science: three laboratories agreed, and the date converges with the cloth's first documented appearance. The reweave/contamination and statistical- heterogeneity objections are legitimate criticisms of a single, non-random corner sample and are not dismissed here; but the conservators found no mend, and no reanalysis yields an ancient date. The honest caveat is that the sampling was imperfect and a fresh, multi-site test would be worth doing — not that the medieval date has been overturned.

The claim (and its rivals)

The narrow interpretation weighed here is that the 1988 accelerator-mass-spectrometry dating reliably places the Shroud's linen in the fourteenth century (calibrated AD 1260–1390 at 95% confidence). Ranged against it is a genuinely-advocated counter-thesis — that the test is unreliable because the sampled corner was a later repair or was contaminated, so the true cloth may be far older — associated above all with Raymond Rogers's 2005 “invisible reweave” chemistry and with peer-reviewed reanalyses of the raw data. This page steelmans that counter-thesis as a defeater to the date and then asks whether it actually holds, under the wiki's dating-method discipline (name the method, state what would have to be true, test it, rate on the evidence).

Who advances it

The reliability of the medieval date is the position of the three dating laboratories (Arizona, Oxford, Zurich), the British Museum coordinators, and the mainstream of radiocarbon and textile science. The counter-case is advanced by sindonologists: Sue Benford and Joseph Marino proposed a sixteenth-century “invisible” repair of the sampled corner (2000–02); Raymond Rogers argued the chemistry of the sample bore this out (2005); and statisticians Marco Riani and Anthony Atkinson (2013) and Tristan Casabianca and colleagues (2019) argued from the released raw data that the sub-samples were not statistically homogeneous.

The evidence — the date and the objections steelmanned

The method and result. The dating method is AMS radiocarbon on cellulose — a well-understood technique whose principal failure modes here are (a) an unrepresentative or repaired sample and (b) undetected carbon-bearing contamination. In April 1988 a single strip was cut from one bottom corner (the Raes/radiocarbon corner), divided, and dated blind at three laboratories; the weighted result was AD 1260–1390, reported in Nature as “conclusive evidence that the linen of the Shroud of Turin is mediaeval.”1 Its great external corroboration is that this date brackets the cloth's first securely documented appearance, at Lirey in the 1350s — two independent lines (physics and archives) meeting on the same century.

The reweave / contamination defeater. The strongest form of the objection concedes the lab work and attacks the sample: the corner used was, on this view, structurally different from the main cloth — handled for centuries, possibly mended. Rogers reported that his corner threads contained cotton and a “yellow- brown plant gum” with dye, and that vanillin-loss kinetics implied a much older cloth, concluding the sample “was not part of the original cloth.”2 Benford and Marino posited a skilled sixteenth-century “invisible reweaving” that could splice younger thread into the sampled edge without a visible seam. This is a real precedent-type in textile conservation and cannot be waved away by authority.

The heterogeneity defeater. Independently, statisticians who obtained the laboratories' raw measurements argued that the sub-sample dates are not consistent with a single homogeneous cloth: Riani, Atkinson and colleagues found a spatial trend across the samples (2013), and Casabianca et al. (2019), using the raw data released after a 2017 request, concluded that “homogeneity is lacking in the data” and that the dating procedure “should be reconsidered.”3 These are peer-reviewed and are a legitimate criticism of the experimental design — one non-random corner sample is a thin basis for a verdict on a whole cloth.

Adversarial assessment

Do the defeaters actually hold at this artifact? Test them, do not merely grant them. The reweave requires an undetected splice of new thread into the corner — but the conservators who physically examined the Shroud, above all Mechthild Flury-Lemberg during the 2002 restoration (who saw the reverse of the cloth for the first time in centuries), reported no mend, patch, or added textile at the sampled region; and a 2010 re-examination of reserved 1988 threads (Freer-Waters and Jull) found no significant repair or gross contamination.4 Rogers's vanillin argument turns on assumed reaction rates and has not been reproduced. So the reweave, while a real kind of thing, is poorly evidenced at this cloth. The heterogeneity finding is stronger and should be conceded as far as it goes — the data do show scatter, and the sampling was methodologically weak — but it proves less than the counter-thesis needs: heterogeneity widens the uncertainty on a medieval date; it does not produce a first-century one. No reanalysis, including the critics', yields an ancient age; they yield a call to re-test.

Symmetry of skepticism. The discipline cuts both ways. Against complacency: “three labs agreed” does not license ignoring that they dated one corner, chosen for convenience, never sampled elsewhere — the design was thin, and the raw-data heterogeneity is a fair hit. Against special pleading: a live methodological criticism of the sampling is not a demonstration that the cloth is old, and stacking “maybe reweave, maybe contamination, maybe bad statistics” does not add up to an ancient date when each piece independently fails to deliver one and the documentary record independently agrees with the labs. The honest result is differentiated: the date is secure enough to be the consensus; the sampling is genuinely open to criticism; and the remedy the critics rightly ask for — a fresh, multi-site, published protocol — would settle it either way.

Why the supernatural does not enter here. Weigh dating on dating evidence. Even granting that the image may have formed by some unknown or miraculous process, that possibility does not relocate a radiocarbon signal: a miracle at the image is not a mechanism for making fourteenth-century flax read as first-century. The reliability of the date stands or falls on sampling and chemistry, and there the medieval result holds.

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation that the 1988 radiocarbon test reliably dates the Shroud's linen to AD 1260–1390 is the consensus of radiocarbon science and is corroborated by an independent line of evidence — the cloth's first documented appearance in the same century. The counter-thesis (reweave, contamination, statistical heterogeneity) is taken seriously, not dismissed: it correctly notes that a single non-random corner sample is a weak design, and the peer-reviewed heterogeneity of the sub-samples is a fair criticism worth a fresh, multi-site re-test. But tested at this artifact the defeaters do not deliver: conservators found no mend, later re-examination found no significant repair or contamination, and — decisively — no reanalysis, including the critics' own, yields anything but a widened medieval range; none produces a first-century date. The medieval date therefore rates sound, with the honest caveat that the original sampling was imperfect and that a new test would be worthwhile. (What the date does and does not prove about authenticity is weighed on the flagship page: a secure medieval date sinks authenticity, but the image's formation remains a separate, unresolved question.)
