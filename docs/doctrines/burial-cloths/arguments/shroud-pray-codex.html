Does the entombment miniature in the Hungarian Pray Codex (c. 1192–1195) depict the Shroud of Turin?

Disputed reading · on the burial cloths of Christ (the Shroud of Turin & the Sudarium of Oviedo) · witness: the Pray Codex miniature

The miniature

The evidence under dispute is a picture rather than a text. The Pray Codex, a Hungarian liturgical manuscript in the National Széchényi Library, Budapest, contains the Funeral Sermon and Prayer, dated 1192–1195. One of its illustrations shows, in an upper register, the body of Christ prepared for burial and, in a lower register, the angel showing the empty tomb to the three women.1 Four features of it figure in the debate: Christ is drawn wholly nude with the hands crossed over the pelvis; only four fingers show on each hand; an object in the scene carries a stepped, zig-zag pattern; and four small circles on that object appear to form the letter “L”. The Shroud of Turin carries, besides the damage of the 1532 Chambéry fire, small burn holes in L-shaped groups (the “poker holes”), which a copy painted at Lier in 1516 is reported to show ⚠ unverified.2

The question

Was the miniature drawn by an artist who had seen the cloth now in Turin, or does it follow the period’s ordinary conventions for an entombment scene? The answer bears on the Shroud’s history before its first securely documented appearance at Lirey c. 1354–1357, and on its relation to the 1988 radiocarbon date of AD 1260–1390.

A record of the Shroud itself

On this reading the artist had seen the Turin cloth, and the miniature preserves its distinctive marks a century and a half before Lirey.

Held by: Ian Wilson (The Mysterious Shroud and The Evidence of the Shroud, 1986) ⚠ unverified; Daniel C. Scavone (book review of The Turin Shroud: In Whose Image?) ⚠ unverified.3

Wilson set the miniature inside his wider thesis that the Shroud is the Byzantine Image of Edessa, the Mandylion, kept folded to show only the face and moved to Constantinople in 944; a Hungarian artist working under Byzantine influence in the 1190s could, on that account, have known the cloth or a faithful report of it.4 The proponents’ case rests on the convergence of the four features rather than on any one. Its anchor is the L of circles, which in Scavone’s words “perfectly reproduce four apparent ‘poker holes’ on the Turin Shroud”.5 The poker holes are an idiosyncratic blemish, and since they are older than the 1532 fire, a picture of them in a manuscript of the 1190s would place the cloth before 1200. Around that anchor the other features cluster. The man of the Shroud lies nude with his hands crossed low over the pelvis and no thumbs visible, and the Pray Christ is drawn the same way, with four fingers and no thumb on each hand, a combination the proponents regard as unusual for the period. The stepped pattern in the scene they read as the Shroud’s herringbone twill, a weave they distinguish from the plain weave of an ordinary winding-sheet. Taken together, on this reading, the chance that an artist arrived independently at all four features, and set the circles in the same L that the Shroud bears, is small enough that the simplest account is that the artist was working from the cloth itself.

An entombment scene in twelfth-century convention

On this reading the miniature follows the Byzantine-influenced Romanesque conventions of its time and has no connection with the Turin cloth.

Held by: Gian Marco Rinaldi (“Il Codice Pray”) ⚠ unverified; Colin Berry (2012); Andrea Nicolotti (The Shroud of Turin, Baylor University Press, 2019).6

Nicolotti holds that the codex “has no relation to the Shroud of Turin” and that the resemblances come from reading medieval iconographic conventions as a portrait of a relic. The nude, cross-armed Christ is, for him, a type found in contemporary works such as the Ingeborg Psalter and the Sacramentary of Robert of Jumièges. The object bearing the zig-zag he and Rinaldi identify as the tomb itself, a rectangular sarcophagus slab of a kind seen in other sacred images; the zig-zag is a recognized way of rendering stone, not a herringbone weave. The burial cloth, on this reading, is drawn separately, and carries no image of a man: in Nicolotti’s words, “it can be clearly seen wadded up atop the lid of the sarcophagus, which is itself placed sideways facing the beholder.” He adds that “the ways of depicting and decorating a slab of a sarcophagus are virtually endless: circles, rhombuses, squares, reticulation, dots, crosses, flowers, lozenges, colored veins, combinations of various colors and figures, with oculi or openings”.7 The small circles are, on this account, decoration: Rinaldi points to the same elements on the angel’s wing and on items of clothing, and Berry notes that “all four of the illustrations in the Pray Codex have those little circles, open or filled”.8 Nicolotti calls the L-shape a common design element in medieval manuscript illumination, and Berry argues that of all letter-shapes, an L is the one four points placed at random most often make.9 Read in its own artistic tradition, on this account, the miniature is a standard Romanesque entombment and Easter-morning scene.
