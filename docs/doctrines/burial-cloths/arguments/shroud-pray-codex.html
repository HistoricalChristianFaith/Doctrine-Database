Does the Hungarian Pray Codex depict the Shroud before 1200?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reading that the Pray Codex’s entombment miniature (c. 1192–1195) portrays the Turin Shroud, and so proves the cloth existed a century and a half before the radiocarbon window, does not hold on its best case. Each of its four features has an independent, well-attested twelfth-century artistic explanation, and the one genuinely arresting detail — the L-shaped set of circles read as the Shroud’s “poker holes” — is undercut by the fact that identical circles recur as ornament across the same manuscript. It is not unsupported: the convergence of nudity, crossed hands, apparent thumblessness, and that one L-pattern is a real coincidence a fair reader should feel. But a coincidence of conventional motifs is not a portrait, and this bridge cannot carry the Shroud across the 1260–1390 date.

The claim

The 1988 radiocarbon test dated the Shroud’s linen to AD 1260–1390, and the cloth’s first securely documented appearance is at Lirey c. 1350. The Pray Codex argument is the flagship attempt to break that wall: a Hungarian liturgical manuscript, firmly dated to c. 1192–1195 and held in the National Széchényi Library in Budapest, contains a full-page miniature of the burial and resurrection of Christ that (Ian Wilson and later sindonologists argue) can only have been drawn by someone who had seen the Turin Shroud itself.1 Four features are adduced as a convergence too precise for coincidence: Christ is shown wholly nude with hands crossed at the pelvis, right over left, exactly as on the Shroud; the thumbs are not shown (only four fingers per hand), matching the Shroud image, where the thumbs are folded from view; the cloth on the upper register bears a herringbone / zig-zag weave like the Shroud’s three-to-one herringbone twill; and a set of four small circles arranged in an “L” matches the distinctive L-shaped clusters of burn holes — the “poker holes” — visible on the Shroud. Because those poker holes are older than the 1532 Chambéry fire (a 1516 copy at Lier already shows them), the argument runs, a picture of them in a 1190s manuscript can only mean the Shroud predates 1200.2 If sound, the argument would refute the radiocarbon date at a stroke.

Who advances it

The identification was popularized by Ian Wilson, who set it inside his wider thesis that the Shroud is the Byzantine Image of Edessa / Mandylion folded to show the face, and has been pressed since by sindonologists including Ian Dickinson and, in peer-reviewed dress, by Giulio Fanti and Pierandrea Malfi, who argue the four-feature correspondence is statistically improbable by chance.3 Against it stands the mainstream art-historical judgement, marshalled most fully by the historian Andrea Nicolotti (whose The Shroud of Turin, 2019, is the standard critical reference), that the miniature has “no relation to the Shroud” and reflects ordinary Byzantine-Romanesque convention.

The evidence for — steelmanned

The strong form of the argument is not any one feature but their convergence, and its centre of gravity is the poker holes. The Shroud carries, apart from the well-known 1532 fire damage, four sets of small burn holes arranged so that three of them form the corners of an “L” — a genuinely idiosyncratic blemish, demonstrably older than the 1532 fire because it appears in a Shroud copy painted at Lier in 1516. In the lower register of the Pray miniature, on the lid Christ’s body rests on, a group of small circles sits in just such an L. If those circles record the actual poker holes, they cannot be explained as later coincidence, because the manuscript is a century older than the Lirey Shroud and three centuries older than the fire. Around that anchor the other three features cluster: the Shroud man’s hands are crossed low over the pelvis with no thumbs visible (anatomically, the Shroud image lacks thumbs, plausibly from median-nerve retraction after nailing through the wrists) — and the Pray Christ is drawn nude, hands crossed low, with four fingers and no thumb on each hand, an unusual combination for the period; and the textile in the upper scene carries a stepped, herringbone-like pattern that a sindonologist reads as the Shroud’s rare herringbone twill rather than a plain-weave winding sheet. Taken together, the proponents argue, the odds of an artist independently inventing all four — and placing the circles in the very L the Shroud bears — are vanishingly small.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The convergence is not nothing, and a fair page should not pretend otherwise: nude, low-crossed, thumbless hands plus an L of circles is a suggestive combination, and the poker-hole detail in particular is the one feature that is hard to derive from generic iconography. That residue is why the argument is weak and not unsupported, and why the flat dismissal “obviously unrelated” overreaches as badly as the sindonologists’ certainty.

Where it fails — each feature dissolves into convention. But tested one at a time, the four props are far weaker than their sum is made to sound. The nude, cross-armed Christ is a widespread Byzantine-Romanesque type: the near-contemporary Ingeborg Psalter (c. 1195) and other twelfth-century entombment and Threnos scenes show the same posture, which derives from burial iconography, not from any one cloth. The missing thumbs likewise recur in medieval and Byzantine hand-conventions and in the schematic four-finger drawing typical of the style; it need mean no more than a draughtsman’s shorthand. The “herringbone weave” is the most strained: the zig-zag it rests on is the standard medieval way of rendering the stepped stone of a sarcophagus lid, not a cloth at all — and a discarded, wadded-up grave-cloth is drawn separately on the same slab, so the object bearing the “weave” is most naturally the tomb, not the shroud.4

The poker holes — the anchor gives way. That leaves the L of circles carrying the whole argument, and it cannot. Small red circles are a recurring decorative motif throughout the Pray miniatures — the same discs appear on the angel’s wing and elsewhere in the illumination — so reading four of them as burn-holes is a selection made because the answer is known in advance. And an “L” is a weak target: with four points placed on a surface, some legible letter-shape is very likely, and the plain “L” is among the most probable, so the pattern-match cannot bear the evidentiary weight put on it. The circles are also positioned on the lid/steps, consistent with ornament on stone, not with holes burned through a linen sheet. Once the poker-hole reading is set aside as motif rather than record, the remaining three features are each individually conventional, and their “improbable” convergence is the ordinary look of a period entombment scene — which is exactly Nicolotti’s conclusion.

Even granting the resemblance, it cannot cross the date. Finally, the argument proves less than it needs even at its strongest. Suppose the artist had seen a Shroud-like cloth: that would establish that a herringbone image-bearing linen existed by 1195, not that it was this cloth, whose linen the laboratories independently dated to 1260–1390. Wilson’s own bridge concedes the point by routing the Shroud through the Edessa Mandylion — but the Mandylion is described in the sources as a face-cloth bearing a face, never as a full-length double body-image, so the identification is itself inferential and contested. The Pray Codex, at its very best, is a resemblance; a resemblance is not a chain of custody, and it does not touch the physical date of the fibres.

Assessment

Weak. The interpretation — that the Pray Codex miniature depicts the Turin Shroud and so proves it existed before 1200 — does not hold on its best available case. Its four props each have a well-attested twelfth-century explanation with no need of the Shroud: the nude low-crossed posture is a common entombment type, the thumbless hands a drawing-convention, the “herringbone” the standard rendering of a stone sarcophagus lid (beside which a separate grave-cloth is drawn), and the L of “poker holes” a selection from red circles that ornament the whole illumination, matched to a letter-shape that four random points readily make. It is rated weak rather than unsupported because the convergence, and the poker-hole detail above all, is a genuine coincidence that a candid reader feels — the argument is honestly advanced, not manufactured. But a convergence of individually conventional motifs is not a portrait; and even a real resemblance would show only that some image-cloth existed by 1195, not that this linen, radiocarbon-dated to 1260–1390, is older than its own fibres. The Pray Codex remains the most interesting of the pre-Lirey bridges and the least able to carry the weight the antiquity case needs.
