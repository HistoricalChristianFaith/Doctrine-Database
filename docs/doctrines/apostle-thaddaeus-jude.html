Thaddaeus / Jude

This apostle stands tenth in every list of the Twelve, in the third group of four, under more than one name: Matthew and Mark call him “Thaddaeus” (some manuscripts “Lebbaeus”), Luke and Acts, in the same place, “Judas of James,” and John records a question asked at the Last Supper by a “Judas, not Iscariot.” Jerome (398) took the list-names as three names of one apostle; other writers, among them Ephrem the Syrian and Bede, identified the apostle with Jude the brother of the Lord or with the author of the Epistle of Jude, who names himself “brother of James.” Two narratives became attached to the name: the tale of King Abgar of Edessa, healed and converted by a Thaddaeus, and the medieval account of a martyrdom with Simon the Zealot in Persia, linked with the two apostles’ joint October feast. This page records what the sources report about him, earliest extant source first: the names in the New Testament, Eusebius and the letters of Abgar, Jerome’s reading of the names, the apostle-lists and the Golden Legend, and modern scholarship. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on him; the disputed questions (whether he wrote the epistle, and whether he was the missionary to Edessa) are set out on their own pages below. Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament gives this apostle a fixed place in the lists and more than one name. In the third group of four he stands tenth: where Matthew and Mark write “Thaddaeus” (the Received Text of Matthew “Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus”), Luke and Acts write “Judas of James.” John, without listing the Twelve, names a “Judas, not Iscariot” among the disciples at the supper, and gives him one question. The New Testament records no mission or death for him. It also names a Jude, author of the short epistle, who calls himself “brother of James,” and a Judas among the “brethren of the Lord” (James, Joses, Simon, and Judas), whom John reports as not believing in Jesus and Acts lists beside the apostles after the Ascension. Whether the list-names denote one apostle, and whether that apostle is the epistle’s author, are questions later writers answer differently.1

Eusebius and the letters of Abgar (c. 325)

The earliest extant connected story attached to a “Thaddaeus” is the legend of King Abgar of Edessa, which Eusebius gives in full, saying he translated the correspondence of Abgar and Jesus from the royal archives. Eusebius three times places his Thaddaeus among “the seventy,” sent to Edessa by the apostle Thomas: “Thomas, one of the twelve apostles… sent Thaddeus, who was also numbered among the seventy disciples of Christ, to Edessa.” The Syriac telling, the Doctrine of Addai, names the missionary “Addai” and likewise reckons him one of the seventy-two. The sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum lists the letters among the apocrypha. Whether the Edessene missionary is the apostle of the Twelve is a disputed reading.2

Jerome on the three names (c. 398)

Commenting on the apostle-list of Matthew, Jerome took Thaddaeus, Lebbaeus, and Luke’s “Judas of James” to be one man — “he is called Judas son of James by the evangelist Luke, and elsewhere is named Lebbaeus… one must believe he had three names” — and in the same passage reports that “the history of the Church” sends “the apostle Thaddaeus” to Abgar at Edessa. In his Lives of Illustrious Men he lists “Jude the brother of James” as author of the epistle without calling him an apostle. Jerome’s explanation of the Lord’s brethren as cousins is recorded on the perpetual-virginity timeline; the identification of the apostle Judas with the epistle-writer is reported of Bede and, for the apostle’s kinship with Jesus, of Ephrem the Syrian.3

The apostle-lists and the Golden Legend (uncertain date; c. 1260)

The late catalogues supply a mission and a death and pair Jude with Simon the Zealot. The apostle-lists place his labours variously in Mesopotamia, Persia, and Armenia; the Edessene sources tell of a Thaddaeus, or Addai, of the seventy, and whether he is the apostle is a disputed reading. Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260) sends Simon and Jude together into Persia to confront the sorcerers Zaroes and Arphaxat and to die in a pagan temple, where the priests “ran upon the apostles and hewed them to death.” The Western calendar keeps their feast together on 28 October, and Western art gives Jude a club and Simon a saw as emblems of their deaths.4

Modern scholarship

Modern scholars divide on both identity questions. On the list-names, J. Jeremias (1971) and Richard Bauckham (2006) take Thaddaeus and “Judas of James” as alternate names for one man; J. A. Fitzmyer (1981) suggests the names of all twelve may not have been recalled with complete accuracy, and J. P. Meier (2001) that two different individuals, or a replacement of one by the other, may lie behind them. On the epistle, Bauckham (1983, 1990) reads the apostle as “Judas son of James” and assigns the letter to Jude the brother of the Lord; Catrin H. Williams reports the father-and-son reading of Luke’s phrase as widely held. H. J. W. Drijvers (1991) and Bauckham (2006) propose that Eusebius may have changed the Syriac “Addai” to “Thaddaeus”; Walter Bauer (1934) argued that the succession the Abgar legend builds was constructed to give Edessa’s catholic community an apostolic pedigree. The Armenian Apostolic Church venerates the apostle Thaddaeus, with Bartholomew, as the bearer of the Gospel to Armenia.5

Disputed readings

How the sources on this apostle’s identity and mission have been read.

  • Is the author of the Epistle of Jude the apostle “Judas of James”? — whether Luke’s “Judas of James” and the epistle’s “Jude… brother of James” are one man.6
  • Was the Thaddaeus of the Abgar legend, the missionary to Edessa, the apostle of the Twelve? — Eusebius’s “one of the seventy,” Jerome’s “apostle Thaddaeus,” and the Armenian tradition.7
