The Mark of the Beast and the Number 666 (Revelation 13:16–18)

Related doctrines: The book of Revelation — see also the date of Revelation

Revelation 13 closes with a riddle the text sets the reader: the beast forces "all, both small and great, rich and poor, free and bond, to receive a mark in their right hand, or in their foreheads," so that "no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark," and then — "Here is wisdom" — "let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man; and his number is Six hundred threescore and six."1 Two things have been argued over ever since: what the mark is (a literal brand, the imperial stamp on coin and contract, the coerced pinch of incense — or, spiritually, the stigma of apostasy, a parody of God's own seal on the foreheads of the redeemed), and what the number means — whether 666 (or the ancient variant 616) ciphers a name by gematria, the practice of summing the numerical values of letters, and if so, whose.2

This page gathers the interpreters earliest first. The earliest extant readers expected a future Antichrist and offered candidate names by gematria — Teitan, Lateinos, and others; none of the ancient and medieval interpreters surveyed here names Nero. The "Lateinos" reading (the number as "the Latin one," i.e. Rome) runs from Irenaeus through the Middle Ages and was applied to the papacy by sixteenth-century Protestant commentators; the identification with Nero Caesar is first attested in the 1830s; and the literal, futurist "mark" — barcode, microchip, and the rest — is later still. The question of when Revelation was written, and whether 666 bears on its date, is traced on the companion page; here the interest is the number's meaning and the mark's, traced across the interpreters who tried to read it.

Timeline

Irenaeus (c. 180)

The earliest extant discussion of the number. In Against Heresies 5.30, Irenaeus reports that 666 stands "in all the most approved and ancient copies," vouched by "those men who saw John face to face," and rejects the reading 616 as a copyist's error. He offers three Greek names that sum to 666 — Ευανθας (Euanthas), Λατεινος (Lateinos), "very probable… this being the name of the last kingdom, for the Latins are they who at present bear rule," and Τειταν (Teitan), his own lean — but declines to settle the matter, holding it "more certain… to await the fulfilment" of a still-future Antichrist. He names no emperor, and does not read Nero.3

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 200)

Hippolytus lists Titan and Evanthas ("and many others") and, of the names Irenaeus had given, settles on Λατεινος / Latinus, the Latin or Roman kingdom, as "a conjectural account," not "as if this were certainly his name." On the mark itself he reads the beast's imposition literally, against the type of Antiochus Epiphanes: none may buy or sell "without first sacrificing," so that the mark "received upon the right hand" is a compelled act of apostate worship. The future Antichrist, again, is unnamed.4

Victorinus of Pettau (c. 300)

In the earliest surviving verse-by-verse commentary on the Apocalypse, Victorinus prefers Τειταν ("which the Gentiles call Sol and Phœbus") and converts it by antiphrasis into the Latin cipher DICLUX — D+I+C+L+V+X = 666, "say light," since Antichrist "transforms himself into an angel of light." The received text, Jerome's recension of the commentary, also names Genseric, who sacked Rome in 455, long after Victorinus died; the ANF editor takes the name as a later interpolation.5

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

With Augustine the number drops out and the mark is read wholly spiritually. Following the Donatist Tyconius, whose lost Exposition turned the Apocalypse into a drama of the two cities, Augustine takes the beast in City of God 20.9 to be "the godless city as opposed to the city of God"; its image, the hypocrites "called Christians, not because of full faith but of false face"; and the refusal of its mark on "foreheads" and "hands" to mean that the faithful "refuse the stigma of false faith and bad morals." He calculates no name for the number.6

Oecumenius (c. 550)

The earliest surviving Greek commentary on Revelation, and, according to Max Nelson, the source of the list of descriptive names taken up by later Greek commentators. Oecumenius reckons 666 by ordinary Greek isopsephy and, defending the spelling Τειταν, piles up phrases that each total 666 — Λαμπετις (Lampetis), Βενεδικτος (Benedictus), "the victor," "wicked guide," "ancient slanderer," "an unjust lamb." Having found "many names," he concludes that "the authority to attach the more fitting one rests with the one who wills it"; he fixes no single name, and Nero is not among the candidates.7

Primasius of Hadrumetum (c. 560)

In the Latin line, Primasius reckons the number "according to the Greek" and gives two names, both = 666: ΑΝΤΕΜΟΣ (Antemos), "contrary to honor," and ΑΡΝΟΥΜΕ (Arnoume), "I deny" — the latter apt for Antichrist "since the name of belief is proper to Christ." He knew the interpolated Genseric but did not carry it forward; Max Nelson explains the omission by the Vandal king's not having proved to be the Antichrist.8

Andreas of Caesarea (c. 614)

Andreas inherits Oecumenius's list of names, prunes it, and adds a principled refusal to choose: "time and experience will reveal the actual significance of the number… were it necessary… that such a name be clearly known, the seer would have revealed it. But the divine grace did not consent that the name of the destroyer be noted in the divine book." Andreas states the reticence as a principle: the name is providentially concealed, and the interpreter should not presume to supply it.9

Bede (c. 730)

Bede gathers the Greek and Latin candidates into one place — Titan ("that is, giant"), and Primasius's Antemos and Arnoume — and sets beside them a symbolic reading. Six, the number "by which the world was made," signifies "the perfection of the work"; the annual tribute of gold that came to Solomon was 666 talents; so "what is rightly owed… to the true king, that same tribute the deceiving tyrant will presume to demand." For Bede the number figures a counterfeit as well as a cipher.10

Beatus of Liébana (c. 786)

The Spanish compiler whose illustrated commentary shaped the medieval imagination gathers the whole inheritance into a set-piece: the Antichrist bears seven names, one per head of the beast, each summing to 666 — Beatus lists EVANTAS, DAMNATVS, ANTEMVS, GENSERICVS, ANTICHRISTVS, TEITAN, and DICLVX (with an eighth, ACXYME, for the mark). Max Nelson identifies DAMNATVS as Beatus's own addition and ACXYME as his misreading of the Roman numeral DCXVI (616).11

Arethas of Caesarea (c. 900)

Arethas, reworking Andreas, carries the Greek tradition into the tenth century — pruning the inherited list of descriptive names (dropping Benedictus, keeping Teitan, Lateinos, and "the victor") but adding no new identification and endorsing his predecessors' reticence: a list of candidate names, none asserted, the true name reserved.12

Bonaventure (1274)

Bonaventure calculates no name. In the eighth of his Collations on the Seven Gifts (delivered at Paris in 1268), Bonaventure reads 666 as "a cyclical number" signifying not a person but three intellectual errors then troubling the schools, which he calls "heretical" — the eternity of the world, fatal necessity (astral determinism), and the unity of the intellect. For him the number signifies philosophical error.13

Protestant historicist readings — Bullinger and the Geneva Bible (c. 1557)

Sixteenth-century Protestant commentators applied Irenaeus's "Lateinos = the Latin kingdom" to papal Rome. Heinrich Bullinger's hundred sermons on the Apocalypse (1557) read the second beast as the papacy; the Geneva Bible's note on 13:18 states that 666 "signifieth Lateinus, or Latin, which noteth the Pope or Antichrist who useth in all things the Latin tongue"; John Napier (1593) and Joseph Mede (1627) built historicist systems on the same identification.14

Andreas Helwig and "VICARIVS FILII DEI" (1600)

The Lutheran Andreas Helwig, in Antichristus Romanus (1600), summed the Roman-numeral letters of VICARIVS FILII DEI — a phrase first used in the forged Donation of Constantine — to 666. Whether the phrase was ever a papal title is disputed: the Catholic apologist Patrick Madrid holds that it has never been an official papal title; the Adventist Samuele Bacchiocchi states that it was considered one. The Seventh-day Adventist writer Uriah Smith (1866) proposed the reading to his church and wrote that the pope wears the title "upon his pontifical crown in jeweled letters"; the Adventist historian Le Roy Froom wrote in Ministry (1948) that examination of the tiaras had "failed to disclose one engraved with the inscription Vicarius Filii Dei."15

The Nero-Caesar solution — Fritzsche, Benary, Farrar, Stuart (1831–1882)

R. H. Charles (1920) credits the identification to four scholars, Holtzmann, Benary, Hitzig and Reuss, who, P. J. Williams (2007) reports, proposed it independently; Ferdinand Benary's derivation dates from 1836, and Hitzig's and Reuss's from 1837; C. F. A. Fritzsche (1831) is also named as a proposer (⚠ unverified). The Hebrew letters of Neron Qesar (נרון קסר) sum to 666, and dropping the final nun — the Latin spelling Nero — yields exactly the 616 of the variant tradition. Popularized in English by F. W. Farrar (1882) and pressed by the American preterist Moses Stuart (1845), the "double solution" is presented by its proponents as explaining both numbers as one name in two languages; how the 616 variant should be read is itself disputed.16

Modern scholarship — Bauckham, Aune, Koester (1993–2014)

Richard Bauckham's Climax of Prophecy (1993), David Aune's Word commentary (1998), and Craig Koester's Anchor Yale (2014) read 666 as Neron Qesar, citing its fit with the 616 variant as well; P. J. Williams (2007) calls it "one of the consistently popular proposals of the modern period." G. K. Beale (1999) reads the number as symbolic rather than a cipher: 666 as a triple falling-short of the divine seven — "the completeness of imperfection" — with the multitude of rival solutions taken as evidence against any letter-count.17

Dispensational futurism — Darby to Left Behind (1830s–present)

A parallel modern stream reads the mark literally and future. Rising with J. N. Darby's dispensationalism (1830s), systematized in the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), and popularized by Hal Lindsey (1970) and the Left Behind novels (1990s), it expects a coming Antichrist to impose a real, universal mark required to "buy or sell" — identified in turn, as technologies emerged, with barcodes, RFID and microchip implants, and (in 2020–21) COVID-19 vaccines. Hank Hanegraaff of the Christian Research Institute reads the mark instead as "the intentional denial—in thought, word, and deed—of the lordship of Jesus Christ," not a technology; he finds "no biblical basis for believing that the mark of the Beast is a silicon microchip."18
