Cyril of Alexandria and the fall of Nestorius

Dates: patriarch of Alexandria 412–444 (b. c. 376, d. 444) · Wikipedia
Summary: Patriarch of Alexandria and theologian of the hypostatic union, venerated as a saint in the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and in Anglican and Lutheran calendars (feast 27 June in the Coptic, Catholic and Lutheran calendars; 9 June and 18 January in the Eastern Orthodox). This page tells the dispute with Nestorius from his side: he took up the case against Nestorius in 429, received Rome's mandate, issued the Twelve Anathemas, presided over the Council of Ephesus (431), which opened before the Antiochene bishops arrived and deposed Nestorius in a day, was himself deposed by the Antiochene council, sent gifts to officials at the imperial court, and in 433 agreed the Formula of Reunion with John of Antioch, which accepted Nestorius's deposition.

The see and the inheritance (412)

Cyril succeeded his uncle Theophilus as patriarch of Alexandria in 412. His early years saw the conflict with the prefect Orestes, an expulsion of the Jews from the city which Socrates narrates (he writes that Cyril, “accompanied by an immense crowd of people,” “drove the Jews out of the city”), and the murder of the philosopher Hypatia by a Christian mob in 415. Socrates, reporting the murder, wrote that it “brought not the least opprobrium, not only upon Cyril, but also upon the whole Alexandrian church” (his Greek, ou mikron mōmon, means “no small blame”). The NPNF editor notes that “the responsibility of Cyril in this affair has been variously estimated by different historians”: “Walch, Gibbon, and Milman incline to hold him guilty,” J. C. Robertson ascribes him “indirect responsibility,” and W. Bright wrote that “Cyril was no party to this hideous deed, but it was the work of men whose passions he had originally called out.”1

Taking up the case against Nestorius (429–430)

When reports reached Alexandria that the new bishop of Constantinople was preaching against the title Theotokos, Cyril moved first through his annual paschal letter and a private warning, then in the Second Letter to Nestorius. Against what he read as a division of Christ in Nestorius's preaching, he set out the hypostatic union:

Rather do we claim that the Word in an unspeakable, inconceivable manner united to himself hypostatically flesh enlivened by a rational soul, and so became man and was called son of man, not by God's will alone or good pleasure, nor by the assumption of a person alone.

— Second Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 4), source. The theological substance of this letter, and of his whole doctrine of the person of Christ, is set out on his christology page. At Ephesus in 431 the council majority read the letter and approved it as consonant with Nicaea.2

The Roman mandate and the Twelve Anathemas (430)

Cyril sent Pope Celestine a Latin dossier of Nestorius's own sermons, and when the Roman synod of August 430 condemned Nestorius, it committed the execution of the sentence to Cyril. Nestorius later wrote of the arrangement, “Who was judge? Cyril. And who was the accuser? Cyril”; John Chapman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) reads the commission as Celestine charging Cyril “to assume his authority and give notice in his name to Nestorius,” and the council at Ephesus as having “executed upon him the papal sentence”; in the Coptic Orthodox account of Fr. Moses Samaan (2009), Cyril at Ephesus “considered himself as representing Alexandria and Rome.” An Alexandrian synod then issued his Third Letter, closing with twelve anathemas. The first concerned the title; the twelfth confessed that the Word suffered in the flesh:

If any one confess not that the Word of God suffered in the Flesh and hath been crucified in the Flesh and tasted death in the Flesh… be he anathema.

— Third Letter to Nestorius, the Twelve Anathemas, source. The Antiochene bishops objected to the anathemas; Friedrich Loofs (1914) writes that after Ephesus “the Antiochians continued to hold Nestorius in esteem and to treat as heretical the anathematisms of Cyril.” Cyril's statement that the natures remain “unconfused” within the one Christ is set out on his christology page.3

Ephesus: the council convened and the deposition (June 431)

At Ephesus, Cyril presided. John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops were still on the road when he opened the council on 22 June, over the protest of the imperial commissioner Candidian and sixty-eight bishops. The council majority told Celestine that it had waited “sixteen whole days” amid illness and deaths, and that two of John's metropolitans brought word from him “not to put off the Synod, but to do what was right”; Nestorius wrote that the absent bishops had “besought you to wait.” Cyril's words at the opening survive in Nestorius's quotation of the acts:

This great and holy Council has been patient enough, waiting for the coming of the godly bishops, who are expected to come.

— Cyril at the opening, quoted in Nestorius, Bazaar of Heracleides, source. Nestorius, summoned three times and three times refusing to appear, was deposed in a single day's session by the council majority, and, according to John Chapman's Catholic Encyclopedia article, “the people of Ephesus, full of rejoicing, escorted the fathers to their houses with torches and incense.”4

Counter-deposed; the return to Alexandria (June–autumn 431)

Five days later John of Antioch reached Ephesus, and under the protection of the count Candidian held a counter-council that deposed Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus in turn; the emperor Theodosius at first confirmed both depositions, and Cyril was placed under guard. He left Ephesus and reached Alexandria before the council was dissolved. Nestorius, in exile, described his departure:

Cyril… who was being guarded with all caution hid himself from them that were guarding him in Ephesus… and set out from Ephesus and gained his [own] city without being hindered by any one.

— Nestorius, Bazaar of Heracleides, source. Evagrius records that the council majority, after summoning John three times, released Cyril and Memnon from his sentence.5

Presents to the imperial court during the reunion negotiations (432–433)

The Antiochenes had charged as early as 431 that Cyril turned the court by bribery: Acacius of Beroea reported hearsay that he had bribed the eunuch Scholasticus, a report Hefele (1883) doubted as coming “only from hearsay.” A catalogue sent from Cyril's side itemizes gifts for the officials and eunuchs around the emperor — carpets, ivory chairs and stools, ostriches, and, to a single official, “fifty pounds of gold… in order that he would help us in the cause.” Loofs (1914) dates the list to the same time as a letter of Cyril's archdeacon Epiphanius to Maximian of Constantinople, written to bring John of Antioch to accept the council's judgment on Nestorius, and Hefele narrates the same letter and presents within the union negotiations. By one reckoning the disbursements ran past a thousand pounds of gold ⚠ unverified. The catalogue reads:

…four ivory chairs, two ivory stools… two ostriches; and in order that he would help us in the cause about those matters which were written to him: fifty pounds of gold.

— Cyril, Letter 96, the catalogue of gifts (first item: to Paul the Prefect), from ACO 1.4, in the English translation reproduced by R. Pearse, source. The catalogue calls some of the gifts “customary suppliant gifts” (eulogiae). Hefele, who would defend the presents “as little as did Tillemont,” held that they “must be judged of not by our customs and circumstances, but by those of the East,” where “very many are simply customary recommendations of a cause which, in itself, is thoroughly just”; Philip Schaff wrote that such presents, “according to the Oriental custom of testifying submission to princes by presents, were not necessarily regarded as bribes”; Loofs (1914) held that the documents “show clearly that he did not even hold himself back from bribery”; Volker Menze (2023) writes that Cyril “paid the largest bribe known from antiquity”; and Maurits Sterk de Leeuw (2019) studies how Cyril's terminology of “blessings” “legitimized and enhanced his gifts as a resource to exercise influence on the imperial court.”6

The Formula of Reunion and the last years (433–444)

Two years after the council Cyril made peace with Antioch. Through the imperial envoy Aristolaus he and John of Antioch agreed the Formula of Reunion — a confession that affirmed Theotokos and spoke of “a union of two natures,” and that accepted Nestorius's deposition. Hefele (1883) reads it as John's acceptance of the council's sentence on a creed Cyril could own, “quite orthodox in meaning”; he records that Cyril defended the union in letters to Valerian of Iconium and Acacius of Melitene ⚠ unverified, showing, in Hefele's summary, “that there was a great difference between their faith and that of Nestorius.” Loofs (1914) reads it as Cyril's acceptance of “the Antiochian confession of faith.” He announced it in a letter whose opening Evagrius quotes: “Let the heavens rejoice and the earth be glad, for the middle wall of partition is broken down.” Georges Florovsky (Eastern Orthodox; lectures first published in Russian in 1931–1933) traces the schism after Ephesus to “a theological misunderstanding between the ‘Egyptians’ and the ‘Easterners’”; he writes that the Easterners' demand that Cyril renounce his “Chapters” “would mean a renunciation of the Ecumenical Council of Ephesus,” and reads the letter to John as revealing “St. Cyril’s serious concern over a vital issue of faith.” Cyril held Alexandria until his death on 27 June 444 ⚠ unverified. Pope Leo XIII later named him a Doctor of the Church.7

Reception and cross-references

  • Cyril's uncle Theophilus presided over the Synod of the Oak (403), which deposed John Chrysostom.
  • Cyril also used the formula “one incarnate nature of the Word” (mia physis), which he cited as Athanasius's; the formula's attribution, and its reception after Chalcedon, are set out on his christology page and on the christology timeline.
