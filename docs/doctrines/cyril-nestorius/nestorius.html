Nestorius and the road to Ephesus

Dates: c. 386–c. 451 (patriarch of Constantinople 428–431) · Wikipedia
Summary: Antiochene monk and preacher, bishop of Constantinople 428–431, whose objection to the title Theotokos began the dispute that ended in his deposition at Ephesus. He is honoured as a saint in the Assyrian Church of the East. This page tells the dispute from his side: the first sermon before the emperor; the defence of “Christ-bearer” over “God-bearer”; the letters exchanged with Cyril and the appeals to Rome; his refusal to attend the council Cyril opened before John of Antioch's bishops had arrived; his deposition, retirement, and exile to the Egyptian desert; and the defence he wrote there, the Bazaar of Heracleides, whose Syriac text became known in the West around 1900 and was published in 1910.

The Antiochene brought to the capital (428)

Nestorius was a presbyter and monk of Antioch, formed in the Antiochene theological tradition, which stressed the distinction of Christ's two natures, and known as a preacher. Brought to Constantinople and consecrated on 10 April 428, he opened his episcopate with a sermon before the emperor:

Give me, my prince, the earth purged of heretics, and I will give you heaven as a recompense. Assist me in destroying heretics, and I will assist you in vanquishing the Persians.

— quoted in Socrates, Church History VII.29, source. Socrates judged that those who could read character found in the words “his levity of mind, and violent and vainglorious temperament.”1

“Mother of Christ” preferred to “Mother of God” (428–430)

The dispute began when his chaplain Anastasius preached against calling the Virgin Theotokos, and Nestorius defended him. In his letters he wrote that “God-bearer” risked either making the eternal Godhead take its origin from a creature or fusing the natures in the manner of Apollinaris; he attributed the birth and the suffering to the humanity, and preferred a title that, in his view, named the united Christ:

Holy scripture, wherever it recalls the Lord's economy, speaks of the birth and suffering not of the godhead but of the humanity of Christ, so that the holy virgin is more accurately termed mother of Christ than mother of God.

— Second Letter to Cyril, source. Evagrius records a further statement of his — “I could never be induced to call that God which admitted of being two months old or three months old” — and calls him “the real teacher of the blasphemy”; Socrates, who had read his writings, would not “concede that he was either a follower of Paul of Samosata or of Photinus, or that he denied the Divinity of Christ.” A fragment of one of his homilies, preserved by later writers and collected by Friedrich Loofs, reads:

Christ in that [he is] Christ is not divided; for we have not two Christs or two sons, for there is not with us a first and a second Christ… but the son is double not by authority but by nature.

— Homily fragment (Loofs, Nestoriana, fragm. 292), source. His fuller position is on his christology page and the Theotokos timeline.2

The appeal to Rome (430)

Nestorius wrote to Pope Celestine, raising the case of Pelagian refugees he was sheltering and charging that Cyril was “hunting for subterfuges to avoid a holy synod.” Cyril had sent Rome a Latin dossier of Nestorius's own sermons. The Roman synod of August 430 condemned Nestorius and deputed Cyril to execute the sentence. Nestorius later wrote of the arrangement, “Who was judge? Cyril. And who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril.”3

Ephesus: the council he would not attend (June 431)

At Ephesus, Nestorius was summoned before the council Cyril opened before John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops had arrived. The council majority told Celestine that John had sent word “not to put off the Synod”; Nestorius wrote that the absent bishops had “besought you to wait.” He refused to appear. Juvenal of Jerusalem said that “with no good conscience did he decline to come unto the holy Council”; Nestorius said the guard at his lodging was for his protection:

Thou accusest me of posting soldiers around my house: [it was] not that they might do any wrong unto you but that they might hinder you from doing wrong unto me.

— Bazaar of Heracleides, Book 2, part 1, source. Summoned three times and three times refusing, he was deposed in a single day. To the synod's messengers, Socrates reports, he said: “I cannot term him God who was two and three months old… I am therefore clear of your blood, and shall in future come no more among you.”4

The counter-council, the bribery charge, and the gifts to the court (June 431–433)

Five days after his deposition John of Antioch arrived and, under the protection of the count Candidian, held a counter-council that deposed Cyril and Memnon; the emperor at first confirmed both sentences. Cyril left Ephesus (by Nestorius's account, escaping his guard) and reached Alexandria. The Antiochene side charged that Cyril had turned the court with money; Nestorius wrote that Cyril gave or promised much money to Count John, the imperial treasurer sent to Ephesus, and wrote of the count and of Cyril:

both he who sold the truth, that is John, and he who [did] more than he, that is Cyril, when the gold of iniquity was exacted…

— Bazaar of Heracleides, Book 2, part 1, source. Friedrich Loofs (1914) wrote that Nestorius here “is repeating party-gossip,” in which “there may be a foundation of truth.” A list of Cyril's gifts to the court survives in a catalogue sent from his side, which calls some of them “customary suppliant gifts”; Loofs dates it to the same time as a letter of Cyril's archdeacon Epiphanius aimed at bringing John of Antioch to accept the council's sentence, and Hefele (1883) places the same letter and presents in the reunion negotiations of 432–433. Hefele held that such presents “must be judged of not by our customs and circumstances, but by those of the East,” and Philip Schaff wrote that they “were not necessarily regarded as bribes”; Loofs held that the documents “show clearly that he did not even hold himself back from bribery.”5

Retirement, exile, and the desert (431–451)

Nestorius asked to return to his old monastery near Antioch, and did; the Formula of Reunion that reconciled Cyril and John in 433 accepted his deposition. Socrates reports that at Ephesus he had said, “Let Mary be called Theotocos, if you will, and let all disputing cease,” but that “no notice was taken of it.” A first edict confined him to the monastery; a second, in 435, banished him far into Upper Egypt, to the Great Oasis of the western desert. Evagrius gives the reason as his continued teaching, reported by John of Antioch. Socrates, writing while he still lived, closes: “he was banished to the Oasis, where he still remains.” He was carried off in a desert raid and released, moved under guard from place to place, and died in the Thebaid about 451.6

The Bazaar of Heracleides, and its modern reception (c. 450 — twentieth century)

In his last years in the desert Nestorius wrote a sustained defence of his whole career and doctrine, the Bazaar of Heracleides, in which he maintained, answering Cyril, that he had never taught two sons:

Of what dost thou accuse me, who say that two are united in one Son, whereby I wished to show the inconfusion of the natures in the union, in making use of the qualities of the natures? I seek not to make as it were two sons nor again the dissolution of the union, but I make use of one prosôpon of union as [formed] of the two ousias, as also Divine Scripture signifies.

— Bazaar of Heracleides, Book 2, part 1, source. A single damaged Syriac manuscript of the work survived in the library of the patriarch of the Church of the East at Kotchanes; it was copied from 1889, used by J. F. Bethune-Baker in 1908, and published by P. Bedjan in 1910. Bethune-Baker asked, “Did Nestorius mean what the Council thought he meant?”; Martin Jugie (Catholic, 1912), Friedrich Loofs (1914) and Georges Florovsky (Eastern Orthodox, 1931–1933) answered in different ways. The readings are set out at “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?”7

Reception and cross-references

  • A generation earlier, Cyril's uncle Theophilus had presided over the synod that deposed John Chrysostom; see Theophilus of Alexandria and John Chrysostom.
  • The Church of the East never accepted his condemnation and honours him, with Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia, among the three “Greek Teachers”; the Assyrian Church of the East reveres him as a saint.8
  • The readings of his “conjunction” language are set out at “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?”
