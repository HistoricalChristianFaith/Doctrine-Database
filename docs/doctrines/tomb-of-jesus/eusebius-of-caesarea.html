Eusebius of Caesarea on the tomb of Jesus

Dates: c. 260–339 · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Caesarea and Constantine's biographer, whose Life of Constantine III (c. 337) is the fullest ancient account of the tomb's recovery: the clearing of Hadrian's pagan fill, the cave coming to light “contrary to all expectation,” and the raising of the basilica over it.

Claims & evidence

Hadrian's suppression: earth heaped over the cave, a shrine of Venus above it

certain impious and godless persons had thought to remove [the sacred cave] entirely from the eyes of men… they brought a quantity of earth from a distance… and covered the entire spot… then … they prepare on this foundation a truly dreadful sepulchre of souls, by building a gloomy shrine of lifeless idols to the impure spirit whom they call Venus.

— Life of Constantine III.26, source.

The tomb discovered “contrary to all expectation”

But as soon as the original surface of the ground, beneath the covering of earth, appeared, immediately, and contrary to all expectation, the venerable and hallowed monument of our Saviour's resurrection was discovered. Then indeed did this most holy cave present a faithful similitude of his return to life.

— Life of Constantine III.28, source. In III.33 the emperor “adorned the sacred cave itself, as the chief part of the whole work.”

Constantine marvels that the spot lay hidden so long

that the monument of his most holy Passion, so long ago buried beneath the ground, should have remained unknown for so long a series of years, until its reappearance to his servants… surpasses all admiration.

— Constantine's letter to Bishop Macarius, quoted at Life of Constantine III.30, source.

An honest limit

Eusebius is the earliest and contemporary witness, and where his account differs from Jerome's later one — he names only the Venus/Aphrodite shrine over the cave, where Jerome adds a Jupiter over the tomb — Eusebius has priority. But two things are read into Eusebius rather than stated by him. He does not say the local church or Bishop Macarius pointed out where to dig; Macarius appears only as the recipient of Constantine's building-letter, and the finding is framed as a providential surprise. That the diggers knew the right spot — hence a surviving local memory of the site under the temple — is a reasonable modern inference, not Eusebius's claim. And Eusebius narrates no discovery of the Cross here; the True Cross / Helena tradition is a later fourth-century accretion (present already in Egeria).
