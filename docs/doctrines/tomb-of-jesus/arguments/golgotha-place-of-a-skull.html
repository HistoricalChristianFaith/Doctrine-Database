Golgotha, “the place of a skull”: is it named for Adam’s buried skull?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as an account of why the execution ground bears the name and where the first man lies, the Adam’s-skull tradition does not hold on its best case: the plain sense of the name is a place of execution (Jerome), Jerome’s own reductio (the two thieves were crucified on the same spot) tells against a uniquely-chosen grave, and Scripture’s own datum puts Adam’s burial at Hebron, not Jerusalem. Yet it is not false: it is a coherent and richly-received devotional typology — the second Adam dying and rising over the grave of the first — a pious overlay on the location, not a datum of it. Advanced by Origen of Alexandria, and rejected already by Jerome.

The claim

For there has come to me a certain tradition of this kind, that the body of Adam, the first man, was buried there where Christ was crucified, so that “as in Adam all die, so in Christ all shall be made alive”; that in that place, which is called the place of Calvary, that is, the place of the head, the head of the human race might find resurrection together with the whole people through the resurrection of the Lord the Savior, who there suffered and rose again.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew (Commentariorum Series 126), link.1 The assertion is that the execution ground is called “a place of a skull” (Golgotha / Calvary) because Adam’s skull was buried on that very spot — so that the second Adam died directly over the grave of the first, his redeeming blood falling on the head of the man in whom all had died.

Who advances it

Origen is the earliest witness, and he is careful to report rather than assert: it is “a certain tradition” that “has come to me,” not his own inference. From him it spread widely. In the Latin West Chromatius of Aquileia (c. 400) repeats it as settled — “it is reported that the body of Adam is buried in that very ground… Christ was crucified there where Adam was buried, that life might arise where death once entered.” The same tradition surfaces independently elsewhere in the fourth century, in both halves of the church: Ambrose of Milan places the cross “above Adam, as the Hebrews argue, for burial” (c. 389), and Athanasius of Alexandria that Christ “is crucified in the place of Calvary; which place the Jewish doctors say was the burial-place of Adam” — a breadth of Latin and Greek reception (and a candid pointer, in both, to its Jewish provenance) that is part of what keeps the reading live. In the Byzantine East it acquired a full legendary narrative: Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1100) tells how Adam’s first dwelling after Eden was Judea, how the people who buried him “marveling at the dead skull… buried it here, and from it gave this place its name,” and how “after the flood, Noah passed down the account of this to all.” By the Middle Ages the reading was universal devotional furniture — the skull-and-bones set at the foot of the crucifix in Christian art is Adam’s, the whole “Adam beneath the cross” iconography a visual sermon on the typology Origen first recorded.2

The evidence for

Stated at its strongest. The reading has three real supports. First, its pedigree and reach: it is attested from the third century (Origen) through the Latin fathers (Chromatius) to the Byzantine tradition (Theophylact) and the whole of medieval piety — a rare breadth of reception for any topographical tradition. Second, its typological fitness: it rests on a genuine Pauline scaffolding — “as in Adam all die, so in Christ all shall be made alive” (1 Cor 15:22), and “Awake thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light” (Eph 5:14), the very verse Origen’s heirs press — so that the cross planted over Adam’s grave dramatizes the redemption of the first man by the second in a single tableau. Third, the name itself invites it: “a place of a skull” (Origen glosses it “the place of the head”) is exactly the label a buried skull would leave behind, and no Gospel explains whose skull or why the name — a silence the tradition fills. The wiki does not dismiss the reading merely because it is pious, nor rule out a priori that a real relic could underlie a place-name (possibility is not excluded on principle).3

Adversarial assessment

The plain reading is an execution ground, not a grave. The sober and far better-attested explanation is Jerome’s: “Without the city outside the gate are the places where criminals are executed, and these have got the name of Calvary, that is, of the beheaded.” The name marks a public place of execution where the heads of the condemned fell — the ordinary sense of a “skull-place” — and Jesus was crucified there “that where the plot of criminals had been, there might be set up the flag of martyrdom.” Bonaventure and Aquinas both follow this reading, Bonaventure glossing Golgotha bluntly as “the place of beheading,” where “the heads of the condemned were stripped bare.”4

Jerome’s reductio. Jerome disposes of the Adam reading with a single question: if the Lord was crucified there so that his blood might drop upon the tomb of Adam, “let us ask him why, then, were the other thieves crucified in the same place? From which it is clear that Calvary does not signify the tomb of the first man, but the place of the beheaded.” The two criminals executed on the same ground show it was a standing execution field, not a spot uniquely chosen to lie above a relic.

Scripture’s own datum puts Adam elsewhere. Against the legend Jerome sets a rival, canonical location: “we read in the volume of Jesus the son of Nave [Joshua] that Adam was buried near Hebron and Arbee” (Josh 14:15, where the “greatest man among the Anakim” was read in Jewish tradition as Adam). Whatever the strength of that identification, it is at least a scriptural counter-tradition placing Adam’s grave at Hebron, twenty miles south — not under Golgotha.

The legend grows in the telling. Origen has only a bare “tradition that came to me”; eight centuries later Theophylact has a fully-furnished narrative — Adam’s Judean exile, the marveled-at skull, the transmission through Noah across the Flood. That accretion of circumstantial detail over time is the signature of a devotional legend elaborating itself, not of a topographical fact being handed down intact.5

The fathers themselves read the name freely. Cyril of Jerusalem, catechizing on the very spot, gives yet a third explanation — a purely spiritual one: “the place of a skull” is prophetic of Christ “the head of the body, the church… The head suffered in the place of the skull.” That a careful on-site witness could allegorize the name away from any buried skull altogether shows the etymology served the fathers as a peg for typology, not as a report of a grave — which is precisely how the Adam reading is best understood.6

A later, third reading. A different explanation of the name — that Golgotha was a skull-shaped outcrop or hill — is later still, and drove the 1883 identification of Gordon’s Calvary by its resemblance to a skull-faced escarpment. It has its own problems (the Gospels never call the place a hill at all — the “little hill,” monticulus, first appears with the Bordeaux Pilgrim in 333) and is weighed with the rival-site question on the Holy Sepulchre vs. Garden Tomb page; it is noted here only to complete the three readings of the name.7

Assessment

Weak. As an account of why the ground is called a skull-place and where the first man was buried, the Adam’s-skull tradition does not hold on its best available case. The plain sense of the name is a place of execution; Jerome’s reductio (the thieves crucified on the same spot) tells directly against a uniquely-chosen grave; a scriptural counter-tradition puts Adam at Hebron; and the legend visibly accretes detail from Origen’s bare report to Theophylact’s full narrative. But the rating is a judgment of probability, not of falsity: this is a less-probable reading of a location, not a demonstrable error, and its typological content — the second Adam redeeming the first over his grave — is coherent, scripturally scaffolded (1 Cor 15:22; Eph 5:14), and one of the most fruitful images in Christian art and devotion. The honest verdict is the one the fathers who rejected it already reached: a beautiful devotional typology laid over the site, not a datum of the site’s name or of Adam’s tomb. The sober execution-ground reading (Jerome, Bonaventure, Aquinas) is the one that carries the topography.
