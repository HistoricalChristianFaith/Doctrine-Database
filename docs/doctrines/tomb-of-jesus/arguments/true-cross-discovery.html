Did Helena discover the True Cross? (the inventio crucis)

Disputed reading · on The tomb of Jesus (Golgotha & the Holy Sepulchre) · witnesses: Eusebius of Caesarea, Cyril of Jerusalem, Egeria

The passage

The Emperor's Mother Helena having come to Jerusalem, searches for and finds the Cross of Christ, and builds a Church… [she] found three crosses in the sepulchre: one of these was that blessed cross on which Christ had hung, the other two were those on which the two thieves that were crucified with him had died.

— Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History I.17, source. Socrates gives the narrative in this form: Helena excavates Golgotha, uncovers three crosses, distinguishes the Lord's by a miracle, and recovers the nails. It is commemorated in the later Feast of the Finding of the Cross and expanded, by the Middle Ages, in the Golden Legend.1

The question

From Ambrose of Milan (395) onward, writers narrate that Constantine's mother Helena, at Jerusalem c. 326, found the cross of Christ at Golgotha; of the earlier sources, Eusebius (c. 337) describes the Constantinian excavation and Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) venerated cross-wood said to have been “found” under Constantine; neither names Helena as finding it.2 What is disputed is whether the Helena narrative records an event of the 320s, a real find later elaborated, or a story attached to Helena at the end of the fourth century. Friedhelm Winkelmann's source-critical reconstruction derives Rufinus, Socrates and Sozomen from one lost source, the Ecclesiastical History of Gelasius of Caesarea (c. 390), so that their accounts descend from a single late-fourth-century Palestinian narrative.3 The question concerns the discovery narrative, not the identification of the tomb, which has its own history on the timeline.

Helena found the cross at Golgotha

On this reading the narrative records what happened: Helena, visiting Jerusalem, had the ground at Golgotha opened and the cross recovered and identified.

Held by: Ambrose of Milan (De obitu Theodosii, 395); Rufinus of Aquileia (Ecclesiastical History X.7–8, c. 402); Paulinus of Nola (Letter 31, c. 403); Socrates Scholasticus (Ecclesiastical History I.17).4

The reading starts from what the earliest sources do record. Helena's pilgrimage to the holy places is historical: Eusebius describes it and the churches she dedicated at Bethlehem and on the Mount of Olives. Within a generation the find itself is attested: Cyril, writing to Constantius in 351, says the saving wood of the cross “was found” at Jerusalem in Constantine's day, and by the 380s Egeria records that the Jerusalem church kept the dedication of its basilicas on the same day as the finding, “because the Cross of the Lord was found on this same day.”5 Ambrose gives the first extant account naming Helena: she opens the ground, finds the cross, tells the true one by Pilate's titulus, and has one of the nails worked into a bridle-bit and another into a diadem for the emperor, which he reads as the fulfilment of Zechariah 14:20. Rufinus, Paulinus, Socrates and Sozomen tell the same event, with the true cross identified by a miracle: in Rufinus and Sozomen the healing of a dying woman in the presence of Bishop Macarius, in Paulinus the raising of a dead man. Sozomen considered a report that the place had been disclosed by “a Hebrew who dwelt in the East” and judged that “it seems more accordant with truth to suppose that God revealed the fact by means of signs and dreams.” On this reading the writers of the 390s and after set down in full an event that the Jerusalem church already commemorated in its liturgy by the 380s.6

A real find under Constantine, later elaborated into the Helena legend

On this reading cross-wood was found at the Constantinian excavation in the 320s, and the narrative told about it grew in stages afterwards.

Held by: Stephan Borgehammar (How the Holy Cross Was Found: From Event to Medieval Legend, 1991).7

The reading separates the event from the story told about it. The event is anchored in sources close to the dig. Eusebius describes in detail a genuine excavation of the site, the clearing of Hadrian's fill down to the rock-cut tomb, the kind of dig at which buried timber could come to light. Cyril, catechizing on the spot c. 350, speaks of “the holy wood of the Cross… seen among us to this day,” says that “the whole world has since been filled with pieces of the wood of the Cross,” and that it “was afterwards distributed piecemeal from hence to all the world”; his letter of 351 uses the verb heurethē, “was found,” and places the finding in Constantine's reign. A relic venerated and distributed from Jerusalem within about twenty-five years of the excavation, and described by the local bishop as found in Constantine's day, points to a recovery at that excavation.8 Borgehammar dates the find soon after Constantine's victory over Licinius (c. 325) and holds that a stable discovery narrative, from a Jerusalem source, was already circulating in the 330s. Helena's documented presence at the holy places in those years gives her association with the site a historical footing. The fuller story, with its Jewish informant and adversus Iudaeos themes, he places in a later Jerusalem composition of c. 415–450, layered on the earlier narrative of the find.9

The Helena discovery is a legend formed in the 390s

On this reading no discovery of the cross by Helena took place; the story attached itself to her at the end of the fourth century and grew from there.

Held by: Jan Willem Drijvers (Helena Augusta: The Mother of Constantine the Great and the Legend of Her Finding of the True Cross, 1992).10

The reading starts from the earliest witnesses. Eusebius, Constantine's biographer and a Palestinian bishop, narrates the clearing of the site in Life of Constantine III and devotes chapters 42–43 to Helena's pilgrimage and her churches “at the grotto… of the Saviour's birth” and “on the mount of his ascension,” with no mention of a cross; the cross appears in his work as Constantine's heavenly sign and standard, not as a recovered relic.11 Cyril, who refers to the cross-wood repeatedly, names no Helena, no three crosses, no titulus and no healing; his verb is the bare passive “was found.”12 The Helena narrative is first extant in Ambrose (395), and each later telling adds an element. Ambrose identifies the cross by the titulus; Rufinus (402) says the titulus did not show “clearly enough” and supplies the healing of a dying noblewoman and Bishop Macarius; Paulinus (the same years) has the cross raise a dead man and omits Macarius; Sozomen (440s) reports a Jewish informant; the Syriac tradition develops the Judas Cyriacus legend, in which a Jew named Judas reveals the place, converts, is renamed Kyriakos and is martyred, and the Protonike legend, which sets a first finding in the apostolic age.13 On Drijvers's account the legend attached to Helena in the 390s, and its three branches (Helena, Protonike, Judas Cyriacus) took shape and converged by the mid-fifth century.14
