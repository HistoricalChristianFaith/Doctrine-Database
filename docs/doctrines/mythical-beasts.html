Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical Beasts

Scripture is populated with great and strange creatures — the sea-tannin and the dragon (Gen 1:21; Ps 74:13; Isa 27:1; Rev 12), leviathan and behemoth (Job 40–41; Ps 104:26), the re'em the King James Version rendered "unicorn" (Num 23:22; Job 39:9; Ps 22:21), the "fiery flying serpent" (Isa 14:29; 30:6), and the satyrs and night-creatures of the prophets' oracles (Isa 13:21; 34:14). Christian interpreters read these along several axes: as literal animals (natural, if rare or now-extinct kinds), as allegory — the dragon for the devil or for sin, leviathan for death — or as pieces of pagan legend to be sifted from fact. Around them grew a large literature of story and report: dragon-slaying saints, sea-serpent reports, the medieval bestiary. This page traces how the great beasts were understood across time, earliest extant witness first.

Timeline

The chronological witnesses are being added, earliest first.

Herodotus (c. 440 BC)

Herodotus, writing four centuries before Christ, is the earliest witness on this page and its only pre-Christian pagan one: he reports the winged serpents of Arabia not as a figure to be interpreted but as plain natural history. He claims to have gone to see their remains himself — "almost exactly opposite the city of Buto" he saw "the back-bones and ribs of serpents in such numbers as it is impossible to describe" — and relays that each spring "the winged snakes come flying from Arabia towards Egypt, but are met in this gorge by the birds called ibises, who forbid their entrance and destroy them all," the service for which the Egyptians revere the ibis. The creature is described "shaped like the water-snake," its "wings… not feathered, but resembl[ing] very closely those of the bat."1 In Arabia the same serpents, "small in size, and of varied colours," swarm and guard the frankincense trees, driven off only by the smoke of styrax; and Herodotus folds them into a providential order of nature that keeps the deadly kinds few.2 He interprets no Scripture; his reports belong to the classical natural-history tradition of the "flying serpent," which Ammianus Marcellinus restates in the fourth century AD.

Tertullian (c. 207)

Tertullian, the earliest Christian witness on this page, reads Scripture's "unicorn" along neither of the doctrine's main axes: not as a natural creature to be identified nor as a figure of the devil, but as a christological type of the Cross. Twice — in Against Marcion III.18 and, almost word for word, in An Answer to the Jews 10 — he takes the blessing "his horns are the horns of a unicorn" (Deut 33:17) and maps the beast onto the cross: "a bullock in respect of both His characteristics: to some as severe as a Judge, to others gentle as a Saviour, whose horns were the extremities of His cross. For of the antenna, which is a part of a cross, the ends are called horns; while the midway stake of the whole frame is the unicorn."3 He sets aside zoological readings — "he was not… designated as a mere unicorn with its one horn, or a minotaur with two" — and joins the same figure to the "horns of the unicorns" of Psalm 22:21, read as "the extremities of the cross," a passion "neither David himself… nor any of the kings of the Jews" suffered.4 He does not treat as a difficulty that Deuteronomy gives the beast plural horns while calling it a "unicorn" — he turns the seam to use, the two horns the cross-beam, the "unicorn" the upright. The Physiologus, Ambrose and Aquinas, below, also read the unicorn christologically.

The Physiologus (c. 2nd–4th c.)

The Physiologus, the anonymous Greek book of animal lore compiled at Alexandria in late antiquity and a source of the medieval bestiaries, gives Scripture's "unicorn" a christological allegory. Its notice is brief and unzoological: the monoceros "is a small animal like the kid… and has one horn in the middle of his head," so strong that "the hunter cannot approach him"; he is taken only by a trick — "Hunters place a chaste virgin before him. He bounds forth into her lap," and she leads him "into the palace of kings."5 The whole scene is read as Christ: the fierce beast no power can subdue, tamed only in a virgin's lap, is the Son made flesh of the Virgin — "the Lord Christ has raised up for us a horn of salvation… by the intercession of the Mother of God" (Luke 1:69; Ps 92:10) — and his single horn signifies "I and the Father are one."6 Where Tertullian read the versional "unicorn" as a figure of the Cross, the Physiologus reads it as the Incarnation; the virgin-capture appears again in Ephrem, Gregory the Great, Isidore and the bestiaries.

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril of Jerusalem, instructing candidates for baptism, reads Scripture's dragon as allegory pressed into a baptismal typology. A great "dragon" lay in the waters — "the dragon that draweth up Jordan into his mouth" (Job 40:23 LXX) — and because "it was necessary to break the heads of the dragon in pieces" (Ps 74:14), Christ "went down and bound the strong one in the waters" at His own baptism, "that we might receive power to tread upon serpents and scorpions" (Luke 10:19). Cyril takes Job's picture of the beast at face value — "great and terrible," its tail too heavy for a fishing-boat — but bends the whole image toward salvation-history: "The Life encountered him, that the mouth of Death might henceforth be stopped," so that "the sting of death is drawn by Baptism."7 The typology completes itself in the candidate: as Christ went down to bind the dragon, so the baptized "goest down into the water, bearing thy sins," and the sealed soul is not "swallowed up by the terrible dragon."8 Augustine, a generation and a half later, also reads the same verse's dragon-heads as broken "upon the water" of Baptism.

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 360)

Athanasius, in the widely-read Life of Antony, reads the great beasts of Job as wholly allegory for the devil. Putting the discernment of spirits into the hermit's mouth, he has the demons appear "as the Lord revealed the devil to Job" — clothed in leviathan's fire and smoke, "From his mouth proceed burning lamps… his breath is coals and from his mouth issues flame" — the enemy wearing the mask of Job's monster to strike terror.9 The boast then collapses into Christ's victory: "like a dragon he was drawn with a hook by the Saviour, and… his nostrils were bound with a ring, and his lips bored with an armlet," leashed "as a sparrow, that we should mock him" — God's unanswerable questions to Job ("Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook?… Canst thou put an hook into his nose?", Job 41:1–2) answered in the Saviour, who has hooked and tamed the devil.10 He reads the dragon as the devil throughout, and does not take up whether leviathan is a real animal.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 368)

Basil the Great, preaching on Psalm 32 (LXX; = Ps 33) — "praise is comely for the upright" — reads Scripture's dragon as allegory, and turns it toward the moral life. The "crooked serpent" of Isaiah 27:1 is "the author of sin," against whom "the sword of God is drawn": the serpent is crooked, "which makes many twists and turns in its progress," so that "one who follows the serpent shows that his life is crooked, uneven and filled with contrarieties; but one who follows after the Lord makes his paths straight."11 Like Athanasius, Basil has no interest here in whether the dragon is a real animal; where Athanasius fixes on the enemy vanquished, Basil fixes on the enemy as tempter, the beast made a mirror of the twisting path the sinner walks.

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373)

Ephrem the Syrian reads the two great beasts of Job 40–41 along both of the doctrine's axes, and is the earliest witness on this page to hold them together. On "Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee" (Job 40:15) he gives a flat, one-line zoological identification: "The Behemoth is a dragon, that is, a land animal, just as the Leviathan is an aquatic sea animal" — the pair told apart simply by habitat, as real creatures of land and sea.12 Yet on Leviathan's boast in Job 41 the same monsters turn to allegory: their "prodigious nature" describes "the devil… destined to be conquered by the power of Christ when the fullness of time will come."13 He reads the beasts literally where the verse describes a creature and figuratively where it describes the enemy, in two terse glosses; Augustine, a generation later, likewise reads "dragons" literally on one psalm and figuratively on another.

Ammianus Marcellinus (c. 390)

Ammianus Marcellinus, a pagan historian writing in Latin amid the Christian generation of Ephrem and Augustine, carries Herodotus's flying serpent down into the fourth century AD. In the Egyptian excursus of his Res Gestae he records, as ordinary natural history, that the ibis is honoured for keeping the snakes down — it "sometimes encounter[s] flocks of winged snakes, which come laden with poison from the marshes of Arabia," which it "overcome[s] in the air, and then devour[s]."14 It is Herodotus's report — the Arabia-to-Egypt migration, the destroying ibis — restated some eight centuries on, and, like Herodotus, offered as fact by a writer with no stake in how Job's beasts should be read. He reports the creature as natural history, a pagan writer contemporary with the exegetes on either side of him.

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose of Milan, bishop of Milan from 374, reads Scripture's "unicorn" as a christological figure like Tertullian and the Physiologus before him — but along the strand of the horn's singleness, not the virgin-capture: the one horn signifies the one, only-begotten Word of God. Returning three separate times to Joseph's blessing (Deut 33:17), he asks outright, "who is the unicorn, if not the only begotten Son of God, and the only Word of God, which was in the beginning with the Father?" — the Word whose horn gores the nations "to the ends of the earth" not to destroy but to convert them ("I will strike and I will heal," Deut 32:39).15 He makes the move explicit on the very seam Tertullian had worked — Deuteronomy's plural "horns" on a beast called a "unicorn": granting the animal "is not found among the generations of wild beasts," he reasons that because it is one-horned it figures the one Word, "one, not many words."16 He gives no virgin-capture. Aquinas, in the thirteenth century, also reads the single horn of the one God.

Jerome (c. 408–410)

Jerome is the translator who fixed the Latin names for nearly every beast on this page — unicornis and rhinoceros for the re'em, draco volans for the "flying serpent," and lamia (the Hebrew Lilith), pilosi (satyrs), and onocentauri for the night-creatures of the oracles — and he reads them along all three of the doctrine's registers at once. As a Hebraist he holds to the plain lexical sense: on Isaiah 30:6 the saraph is a real venomous serpent, "which we can call the burner," and on Isaiah 14:29 the winged one is a draco volans — "so that you may understand a flying dragon."17 As a critic of pagan story he sifts legend from fact: translating the monsters of fallen Babylon and Edom — the sirens, the onocentaurs "imitating the fables of the Gentiles," and above all the lamia "which is called Lilith in Hebrew" — he tells the reader they belong to "the fables of the Gentiles and the creations of poets," and refuses to think Scripture "truly speaks… contrary to the nature of things."18 And as an allegorist he reads the residue "in the likeness of angels or demons," the flying serpents becoming evil rulers who "invent various heresies," while keeping "the Hebrew and historical explanation" expressly distinct from "those who follow the allegory."19 He names the beasts, sets aside the fables, and allegorizes what is left, and he is the only voice on this timeline to treat the satyrs and the Lilith/lamia of the prophets' oracles.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 415)

Augustine reads Scripture's "dragons" along both of the doctrine's central axes, and does so without treating them as rivals. Expounding "Praise ye the Lord from the earth. Dragons and all abysses" (Ps 148:7), he takes the dragon as a literal animal — it lives about the water, comes out of caverns, flies through the air, and is "a huge kind of living creatures, greater there are not upon the earth."20 When the psalm bids such beasts "praise" God, he refuses to allegorize the creature away: dragons do not "form choirs," but move the beholder to praise their Maker — "then the dragons praise God by your voices."21 Yet on "Thou hast broken the heads of the dragons in the waters" (Ps 74:13–14) the same word is allegory: the "dragons' heads" are "demons' pride" broken in the water of Baptism, and the one "great dragon… whose head was broken" is "the devil himself," his broken head read through Genesis 3:15 as "the beginning of sin," which "is pride."22 He reads the same word as a creature where the psalm lists creatures and as the devil where it celebrates God's victory.

Julian of Eclanum (c. 425)

Julian of Eclanum, in his Exposition on the Book of Job written during his exile, reads the great beasts of Job 40–41 along both axes but weights them toward the literal creature more decidedly than Ephrem or Augustine. On "Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee" (Job 40:15) he takes the beast as a real, terrifying animal and turns its very dreadfulness into a compact theodicy: "the power of the Creator did not only make those beasts that would have served human beings but also fashioned those who frighten them"; the "goodness of Providence" is seen because "it removed those beasts that would have been deadly from the midst [of humans] and placed them in the wilderness"; and there men "learn how severe he is against vices."23 He states his order of readings outright on "he is a king over all the children of pride" (Job 41:34): the literal is "the true description of the beast according to the faithful understanding of the story," its great size to be believed "because of the power of the Creator," and only then, without displacing the creature, does he grant a further sense "to a plane of higher intellect," so that "the strength of spiritual iniquity may be perceived more easily in the image of the bodies," the beast charged with "the sin of pride."24 He reads both senses, with the literal creature affirmed first and the allegory added atop it. Gregory the Great, a century and a half later, reads the same beasts as allegory of the devil.

Sozomen (c. 450)

Sozomen, the church historian, breaks from the exegetes on either side of him: he reads the dragon not as a biblical figure to be interpreted at all, but as a literal beast encountered in recent local history. Cataloguing holy bishops of the recent past in his Ecclesiastical History, he relays as local history that Donatus, bishop of Euroea in Epirus (fl. early fourth century), destroyed "a dragon of enormous size" that had stationed itself on the highway at Chamaegephyrae and "devoured sheep, goats, oxen, horses, and men." The bishop killed it unarmed — making the sign of the cross in the air and spitting into its mouth — and the carcass, "not inferior in size to the noted serpents of India," took "eight pair of oxen" to haul off for burning "that it might not… corrupt the air and generate disease."25 Sozomen gives no allegory: the beast is measured against the serpents of India and disposed of like a large dead animal, and the account is vouched for by local witnesses. Like Augustine, Julian, and later John of Damascus, he treats the dragon as an animal, but as a beast met in recent local history rather than in a verse. His is the earliest saint-versus-dragon account on this page; the motif is told centuries later of St George (see Disputed readings).

Gregory the Great (c. 595)

Gregory the Great, in the closing books of his vast Morals on the Book of Job, gives a sustained allegorical reading of Job's two great beasts: Behemoth (Job 40:15) and Leviathan (Job 41) are one figure — the devil, "the ancient enemy." On "Behold Behemoth, which I made with thee" he asks, "Whom does He suggest, under the name 'Behemoth,' except the ancient enemy? which being interpreted from the Hebrew word, means 'Animal' in the Latin tongue" — the beast's name a placeholder that "his malice… added below" fills in with a person, and, against the Manichees, a creature God "rightly fashioned by nature" who fell into being "the author… of wickedness."26 On God's question "Wilt thou be able to draw out Leviathan with a hook?" (Job 41:1) he gives the hook motif that Athanasius and Cyril also use, with its mechanism: "this Leviathan was caught with a hook, because… he seized… the bait of His Body, the sharp sting of His Godhead pierced him through" — Christ's flesh the bait, His concealed Godhead the barb that kills the devourer.27 On Job 40:15 he reads as allegory the verse Ephrem glossed as zoology — "Behemoth is a dragon, that is, a land animal."

Isidore of Seville (c. 636)

Isidore of Seville, archbishop of Seville and encyclopedist, files Scripture's and legend's great beasts as natural history: in the animal book of his Etymologies the dragon and the unicorn are entries in a zoology, not figures to be interpreted. His draco (Etym. XII.4) is the standard medieval dragon — "the largest of all the snakes, or of all the animals on earth," which "has its strength not in its teeth but in its tail" and kills without venom, "because it kills whatever it wraps itself around," lurking for the elephant to "wrap around their legs in coils and kill them by suffocating them."28 His one-horned monoceron (Etym. XII.2) he pins to the real rhinoceros — the name means "horn on the nose" — "the unicorn, because it has a single four-foot horn in the middle of its forehead," an elephant-fighting beast caught only when, "as those who have written about the natures of animals claim," "a virgin girl is set before" it and it "lay[s] its head" in her lap "with all ferocity put aside."29 He passes on the Physiologus's virgin-capture, but stripped of its allegory — the scene Gregory the Great had told as a figure of Christ made flesh in the Virgin, here reported as how the beast is trapped. Isidore reads both creatures as natural history; John of Damascus, a century later, likewise describes the dragon as a serpent without venom. Isidore also reports the versional "one horn" as zoology rather than fable.

Bede (c. 705)

The Venerable Bede, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, in his early Commentary on Revelation, reads Scripture's dragon as wholly allegory — not a creature at all, but one of the devil's names. On "he laid hold on the dragon, that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan" (Rev 20:2) he glosses the four titles the verse heaps on the enemy as a set of etymologies, each naming a vice: "He is called a dragon because of the malice of harming; a serpent because of the cunning of deceiving; the devil because of his fall from his status; and Satan because of his obstinacy in opposing the Lord."30 The "dragon" of Revelation is thus read straight through to the person of the devil, glossed alongside his other names, without taking up whether a dragon is a real animal. Bede reads the dragon as the devil, as Gregory the Great had read Behemoth and Leviathan, in a single glossarial sentence; he is a near contemporary of John of Damascus, who in the East describes the dragon as a real animal.

John of Damascus (c. 730)

John of Damascus, in a short treatise On Dragons and Ghosts, draws a firm line between real creatures and folkloric fiction: dragons exist, he grants, but they are ordinary animals — large serpents "borne of other serpents," growing "more than thirty cubits" and "as thick as a big log," a beast among the beasts Adam named.31 What he denies is the pagan and folk belief that dragons are intelligent beings who take human form, turn into serpents at will, and carry off women: God made only two rational natures, angels and men, so a scheming shapeshifting dragon "has not been true, and never will be" — and of the tale that thunder kills dragons, "When I heard this I laughed!"32 He describes a bearded, horned, golden-eyed kind (an agathodemon) and notes that dragons, alone among serpents, "do not emit poison," and he relays from Cassius Dio the giant serpent slain by the consul Regulus at the Bagradas, whose 120-foot hide was measured "by order of the senate" — cited as natural history, not legend.33 He devotes a whole treatise to the dragon as a real animal rather than an apparition.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican theologian, reads the beasts in three senses — literal, allegorical and christological — by the scholastic method of stacking the senses in order: the literal creature first, the figure it serves set atop it. On the literal plane he works from the zoology of his day (Aristotle, Pliny, Isidore): Job's re'em is "the rhinoceros or unicorn among wild animals, a very strong and fierce four-footed animal with one horn in the middle of his forehead" (Job 39:9), Job's two set-piece beasts are the two largest real animals — "Among all land animals, the elephant excels in size and strength, and among aquatic animals the whale" — so Behemoth is the elephant and Leviathan the whale, and Isaiah's "flying" saraph flies "not because it has wings, but because it leaps on the face of men" (Isa 30:6).34 Then the figure rides on the fact: the whole Job set-piece is at its deeper sense "the metaphor of an elephant and a whale" for the devil — "the properties of these animals are described as a metaphor of something else," shown where the text drops the animal for the truth ("He is the first of the ways of God"; "king over all the sons of pride") — and the unicorn of the Psalms is a figure of pride, the "horns of the unicorns" (Ps 22:21) the proud chief priests and rulers "who uniquely gloried in their knowledge of God."35 And on the very christological seam Ambrose had worked, Aquinas carries on the one-horn / one-God reading: on "the offspring of unicorns" (Ps 29:6) "the Jews are called unicorns inasmuch as they glory in the worship of one God," and Christ himself "is called a unicorn because according to his eternal generation he is without a mother, and according to his temporal generation he was a son without a father."36 He reads literally where the verse describes a creature, figuratively where it describes the enemy, and christologically on the single horn, layering the senses as Julian had layered two of them on Job.

Marco Polo (c. 1298)

Marco Polo, the Venetian merchant, is the timeline's one high-medieval traveler: in the account of his eastern journeys dictated c. 1298 he reports, as plain natural history, that the province of Carajan (Yunnan) breeds enormous clawed "serpents." Some "are ten paces in length… the bigger ones are about ten palms in girth. They have two forelegs near the head, but for foot nothing but a claw… The mouth is large enough to swallow a man whole, and is garnished with great [pointed] teeth."37 He adds the practical detail of a real quarry — the beasts lair in caverns by day and feed by night, and hunters kill them by burying iron-spiked stakes in the sand-tracks they take to the water, then prize the gall as medicine.38 Polo offers no allegory and reads no Scripture — he catalogues a beast; and his editor Henry Yule identifies it: "It cannot be doubted that Marco's serpents here are crocodiles, in spite of his strange mistakes about their having only two feet and one claw on each."39 Like Sozomen's, his is a this-worldly report given as fact.

Olaus Magnus (1555)

Olaus Magnus, the exiled Swedish archbishop and cartographer, carries the literal / natural-history strand off the traveler's road and onto the sea — the doctrine's one sea-serpent witness. In his Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus (Rome, 1555), the compendium of the northern peoples behind his Carta Marina, he reports a monstrous Norwegian sea-serpent as regional natural history, on the agreement of the coast's own mariners: "They who in Works of Navigation, on the Coasts of Norway, employ themselves in fishing or Merchandise, do all agree in this strange story, that there is a Serpent there which is of a vast magnitude, namely 200 foot long, and moreover 20 foot thick," lairing "in Rocks and Caves toward the Sea-coast about Berge" and coming out on clear summer nights to take "Calves, Lambs, and Hogs" or to feed at sea — "black," sharp-scaled, with "hair hanging from his neck a Cubit long" and "flaming shining eyes."40 The report then turns from natural history to portent: the serpent "puts up his head on high like a pillar, and catcheth away men, and he devours them; and this hapneth not, but it signifies some wonderful change of the Kingdom near at hand" — princes to "die, or be banished," or "Tumultuous Wars" to follow.41 He reports the serpent as natural history on the mariners' testimony, as Marco Polo had reported the serpents of Yunnan, and joins to it an omen-reading of the kind found in legend.

Athanasius Kircher (1665)

Athanasius Kircher, the Jesuit polymath, is the latest witness on this page and, after Olaus Magnus, its second early-modern voice: in the "De Draconibus" chapter of his vast survey of the underground world Mundus Subterraneus (Amsterdam, 1665) he treats dragons as real, cataloguable creatures of natural history, writing in the period of the scientific revolution. He holds "the existence of dragons, or rather serpents, of enormous size… entirely certain, for they are found everywhere, and most especially in Ethiopia, India, and in other places in the tropical zones."42 He also defends the winged dragon against the doubters: "there is no agreement among the Authors, who for the most part deemed them to be imaginary. But they are contradicted both histories from all eras and eyewitness accounts as well. Winged dragons — small, large, and enormous — have occurred in every era in all parts of the globe" — his witness the naturalist Pierre Belon, who reported finding in Egypt the complete bodies of winged dragons with bat-like wings and a serpent's tail, and a stuffed dragon "still… seen at Rome in the Museum of Cardinal Francis Barbarini."43 He reads the dragon as natural history, as Herodotus had (whose bat-winged Arabian serpent Belon's report closely resembles), and as John of Damascus and Marco Polo had, and he supports it in the 1660s with specimens and eyewitness reports.

The dragon in legend

Alongside the natural-history reports and the exegetes stand dragons of heroic poetry and popular print, the kind of story Jerome called "the fables of the Gentiles." They are set here, apart from the belief-timeline, because they come from epic and from a newspaper item rather than from exegesis or a natural-history report.

Beowulf's fire-dragon (Old English epic; MS c. 1000, narrative set c. 6th c.)

The Old English epic Beowulf closes with the aged hero's death fighting a fire-breathing, hoard-guarding dragon, the Northern/Germanic fire-drake. The worm, a "hoard-guard" brooding three hundred winters over the buried gold of a vanished people, is roused when a fugitive slave, fleeing a beating, steals a single gilded cup from its barrow — "the wretched fugitive… took the cup from that treasure-hoard."44 Finding its count of gold short, the dragon rages through the night sky wrapped in flame: "Then the baleful fiend its fire belched out, and bright homes burned. The blaze stood high all landsfolk frighting. No living thing would that loathly one leave as aloft it flew" — the destruction reaching the king's own hall, which "in brand-waves melted, that gift-throne of Geats."45 Aided by the young kinsman Wiglaf — who alone stands with him and strikes the beast low so its fire slackens — Beowulf drives home a war-knife and the two together kill it, "kinsmen both, athelings twain"; but the worm's venom works in the wound, and the hero dies of the bite.46 Unlike the classical flying serpent of Herodotus and Ammianus (offered as natural history) or the dragon-slaying reported by Sozomen (offered as local history), the fire-drake — winged, nocturnal, venomous, coiled on cursed gold — belongs to heroic poetry. The Norse Fáfnir is a later dragon of the same hoard-guarding kind.

The Tombstone winged monster (Tombstone Epitaph, 1890)

The 1890 Tombstone "winged monster" is the page's most recent dragon and its one modern-American specimen, a frontier newspaper item treated by later writers on the case as a period tall tale.47 On April 26, 1890 the Tombstone Epitaph of Tombstone, Arizona Territory, ran under "Found on the Desert" the tale of two ranchers who, on horseback and "armed with Winchester rifles," chased and shot a winged, alligator-like beast "found on the desert between the Whetstone and Huachuca mountains" — a creature "greatly exhausted by a long flight" that, wounded, "partly rolled over and remained motionless."48 The paper then gave its measurements — some ninety-two feet long, a head "about eight feet long" with "strong, sharp teeth," eyes "as large as a dinner plate," and a wingspan "from tip to tip about 160 feet" of "thick and nearly transparent membrane" — and promised proof (a wing-tip cut off, the hide to be "sent east for examination by the eminent scientists of the day") that never came: no follow-up, no specimen, no second witness, and — contrary to a durable later legend — no photograph.49 Unlike the reports of Sozomen and Marco Polo, it rests on a single anonymous newspaper item; the case is discussed in modern "thunderbird" and "living-pterosaur" literature.

Disputed readings

Questions about how parts of the record above have been read, each set out with its readings on its own page.

  • Did the early Christian writers read Scripture's dragons, leviathan and behemoth as real animals? — how the Fathers' literal and figurative comments are to be characterized, and whether their dragons can be identified with dinosaurs.50
  • Is St George's dragon-slaying a historical event? — whether the dragon-fight of the Golden Legend records an event in the martyr's life or a later legend joined to his cult.51
