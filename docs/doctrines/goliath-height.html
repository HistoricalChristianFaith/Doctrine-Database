How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits)

Everyone remembers Goliath as a nine-foot giant. The number comes from one verse — 1 Samuel 17:4 — and in the Masoretic Hebrew it reads "six cubits and a span." A cubit runs about eighteen inches and a span about nine, so the Masoretic Goliath stands roughly nine feet nine inches (~2.97 m). But the oldest witnesses to the verse do not agree. The Dead Sea Samuel scroll (4QSama), the Septuagint, and Josephus all give his height as "four cubits and a span" — about six feet nine (~2.06 m). The ESV prints "six" and footnotes the gap: "Hebrew; Septuagint, Dead Sea Scroll and Josephus four."1 The difference lies between manuscripts of one verse, not between two biblical books (as with who killed Goliath): one number in the Hebrew tradition of the Masoretic codices, another in the Qumran scroll and the Greek. It is one instance of the wider divergence between the Septuagint and the Masoretic Text set out on the Septuagint-vs.-Masoretic and Septuagint-origin pages.2

The figure bears on how the story is read. J. Daniel Hays holds that even a Goliath of about 6′9″ towered over Israelite men whose height he puts at about five feet to five feet three (the first-century skeleton from Giv‘at ha-Mivtar stood just under 5′6″), and that since 1 Samuel 9:2 makes Saul "higher than any of the people," the narrative sets Saul, not David, as the man who should have answered the challenge.3 Michael Heiser states that no skeleton of a nine- or ten-foot human has been recovered in the region.4 William Barrick and Clyde Billington hold that Goliath's armor and the fear he caused fit a man of eight feet or more.5 This page gathers the witnesses to the two readings, earliest first: the "four" of the Greek, the Qumran scroll and Josephus; the "six" of the Latin, rabbinic and Masoretic tradition; and the modern scholars who have argued the question. Which reading is original is set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The Septuagint (Old Greek, 2nd c. BC)

The earliest extant translation of the verse reads "four." The Septuagint's 1 Kingdoms (= 1 Samuel) 17:4 has the champion come out of Gath, "his height was four cubits and a span."6 The reading is carried by the uncials Vaticanus and Alexandrinus and by the Lucianic recension, from which Hays concludes that "four" was very likely the Old Greek reading; Symmachus and Origen's fifth column read "six," and the eighth-century Codex Venetus "five."7

4QSama — the Dead Sea Samuel scroll (mid-1st c. BC)

4QSama, the Samuel scroll from Qumran and by about a thousand years the oldest extant Hebrew copy of this verse, reads "four cubits and a span," with the Greek and against the medieval Masoretic codices.8 Hays writes that the Dead Sea Scrolls changed the standing of the Septuagint readings, which for much of the modern era had been viewed as secondary to the Masoretic Text; Peter Goeman, following Alexander Rofé, holds that the scroll's mixed and unique readings make it no simple Hebrew witness.9

Josephus (c. 93)

Josephus, retelling the duel in the Antiquities, describes the champion as "a man of vast bulk, for he was of four cubits and a span in tallness."10 Eugene Ulrich concluded that Josephus's bible for Samuel was Greek, "a slightly revised form of OG [Old Greek]," "intimately affiliated with the 4QSama tradition."11

Jerome and the Vulgate (c. 405)

Jerome, translating from the Hebrew of his day, renders 1 Samuel 17:4 with "six cubits and a span" (Vulgate sex cubitorum et palmo), the number of the Hebrew text; the Vulgate's "six" became the reading of the Latin West. 12

The Babylonian Talmud (c. 500)

The rabbinic tradition works from the Hebrew text that reads "six cubits and a span." The Talmud's Goliath haggadah (Sotah 42b) expounds the wording of 1 Samuel 17 — his descent from Orpah, his forty days of taunting, his fall — and mentions no shorter reading and no height figure.13 The Talmud's mountain-scale giant story belongs to Og of Bashan (Berakhot 54b), not to Goliath.14

Bede (c. 716)

Bede, in his Commentary on Samuel, takes the giant "six cubits and a palm high" as a figure of the devil, who "boasts to be similar to the Most High" and so counterfeits the perfection God measures in six days of work — tying the six cubits to the six days of creation and to Ezekiel's measuring-reed "of six cubits and a palm."15 He mentions no other reading of the number.

The Masoretic codices — Aleppo and Leningrad (c. 930–1010)

The earliest complete Hebrew Bibles — the Aleppo and Leningrad codices, the oldest complete Masoretic manuscripts, the Leningrad Codex being the base text of the modern critical editions (Biblia Hebraica, from 1937) — both read "six cubits and a span" (shesh ammot wa-zaret).16 Hays reports "six" in all the manuscripts of the Masoretic Text; the codices are about a thousand years later than 4QSama; Hays holds that Symmachus's "six" (c. AD 200) shows the reading already in the proto-Masoretic text by that date.7

J. Daniel Hays and the modern debate (2005)

J. Daniel Hays, gathering the witnesses (4QSama, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus, Josephus, the Lucianic Greek), argues that "four cubits and a span" is the original reading and the Masoretic "six" a copyist's slip; he cites P. Kyle McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary (1980) as treating "six" as probably a copyist's mistake.17 William Barrick (2007) holds that the Qumran "four" is a deliberate alteration; Clyde Billington (2007) holds that the Masoretic "six" is original and the Greek "four" a conversion into Egyptian royal cubits, putting Goliath at about eight feet; Hays replied to Billington the same year; and Peter Gurry and John Meade (2022) judge "six" "probably more likely original."5
