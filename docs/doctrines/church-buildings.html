The Place of Christian Worship — house churches to church buildings

"Why aren't we breaking bread in homes anymore — why is it only in church?" The earliest Christians had no buildings set apart for worship: they met in private houses, and the New Testament greets "the church that is in their house."1 Dedicated meeting-places are attested from roughly the close of the second century, and after the Empire ended the persecutions in the fourth century these grew into the spacious basilicas that are the ancestors of the modern church building. This page traces that development, earliest extant attestation first, and the question it raises: was the early absence of church buildings a principle of the faith (a conviction that worship belongs in homes, or that God is not to be housed in sacred buildings at all), or a necessity of the Christians' position as an unrecognized and intermittently persecuted body?

Related doctrines: The church and its practice — see also infant baptism · Baptist successionism · lex orandi, lex credendi

The Scriptural and historical backdrop

Three strands of evidence frame the question:

  • The house churches of the New Testament — Christians assembling in private homes: "the church that is in their house" (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2), and the believers "breaking bread from house to house" (Acts 2:46).1
  • The de-sacralization of place — the theme that God is not confined to buildings: "the Most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands" (Acts 7:48; 17:24), and the hour coming when true worshippers will worship "neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem … but … in spirit and in truth" (John 4:21–24); the apologists turned this against the pagan taunt that Christians had "no temples, no altars."2
  • The material record — no securely identified building set apart for Christian worship before the early-to-mid third century (the converted house-church at Dura-Europos, c. 233, is what Graydon Snyder calls the earliest identified Christian house church), followed by the rapid raising of "spacious" purpose-built churches once the persecutions relented, and the great basilicas after Constantine.3

Whether these strands add up to a positive principle or to the circumstances of an unrecognized body is disputed; see the Disputed readings below.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first. They run from the mid-second-century Roman church meeting in a private lodging (Justin), through the apologists who answered the pagan "no temples, no altars" taunt with a theological account of why Christians raised no temples (Origen, Minucius Felix) and the earliest attested buildings and imperial restitutions (Edessa, Dura-Europos, Gallienus, Milan), to the post-Constantinian building of "lofty and spacious" basilicas (Eusebius).

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Tried at Rome under the prefect Rusticus and asked point-blank where the Christians gathered, Justin first denied the premise of a single cult-site — "the God of the Christians is not circumscribed by place" — and then, pressed for a location, could name only the private lodging he stayed in: "I live above one Martinus, at the Timiotinian Bath … I am unaware of any other meeting than his" (Acts of Justin 2).4 The scene records a Roman congregation meeting in a private lodging a generation before the earliest physically attested church building; how it bears on the "necessity, not principle" question is disputed (see Disputed readings).

The church at Edessa (c. 202)

The earliest datable literary notice of a building set apart for Christian worship that J. B. Lightfoot found. Cataloguing the edifices swept away when the river Daysan inundated Edessa in the reign of Abgar the Great, the Chronicle of Edessa names among them "the temple of the church of the Christians" — a building of some standing, itemized alongside the royal palace.5 Edessa lay beyond the Roman frontier under kings who, J. B. Lightfoot notes, had early embraced Christianity, where "no such restraints would be imposed upon the Christians"; he adduced the notice in his "necessity, not principle" thesis.

The Dura-Europos house-church (c. 233)

What the Yale University Art Gallery calls the world's earliest known house-church: an ordinary courtyard house in the Roman garrison town of Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates, that a Christian community remodeled into a dedicated meeting-house — knocking two rooms into a single assembly hall and converting another into a frescoed baptistery — while the faith was still illegal. It was buried, and so preserved, when the city wall was packed with an earthen rampart during the Sasanian siege of 256.6 L. Michael White treats it as the type-specimen of the domus ecclesiae, the house adapted for worship, in the sequence he maps from house church to basilica.

Origen (c. 248)

Answering the pagan Celsus, Origen concedes that Christians "not only avoid temples, altars, and images" but says why: not from indifference, but because they will not "degrade the worship of God, and reduce it to the worship of material things wrought by the hands of men" — indeed they "are ready to suffer death … rather than debase … the conception which they have of the Most High God." Christians do have an altar and a temple, he argues, but spiritual ones: "the spirit of every good man" is the altar from which the incense of prayer ascends, and "our bodies are the temple of God"; what Christians refuse is to "build lifeless temples to the Giver of all life."7 Origen's terms are temples, altars and images; how far his answer bears on buildings for assembly is part of the disputed question (see Disputed readings).

Minucius Felix (c. 250)

In the Latin dialogue Octavius the pagan Caecilius taunts the Christians — "why have they no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images?" — and the Christian Octavius answers not by denying the fact but by owning it: "what temple shall I build to Him, when this whole world fashioned by His work cannot receive Him? And when I, a man, dwell far and wide, shall I shut up the might of so great majesty within one little building? Were it not better that He should be dedicated in our mind, consecrated in our inmost heart?"8 The work's date is disputed (c. 200–250, turning on its relation to Tertullian's Apology), and it falls within the pre-Constantinian period. Like Origen, Octavius presents the lack of temples as fitting a God whom no building can contain.

The rescript of Gallienus (c. 260)

Ending his father Valerian's persecution, Gallienus addressed a rescript to the bishops — preserved by Eusebius under the heading "The Peace Under Gallienus" — with a clause on their "places of religious worship" that is read either as releasing Christians from their retreats or, with Closs, McGiffert and Lightfoot, as restoring those places to them; a further decree returned the Christians' cemeteries.9 On the restoring reading, the order presupposes that Christian communities already held meeting-places and burial grounds, confiscable in persecution; by this rescript, Lightfoot writes, "all obstacles to the multiplication of churches were at length removed."

The Edict of Milan (313)

The settlement of 313 between Constantine and Licinius that ended the Diocletianic persecution in their territories. Beyond granting universal religious liberty, the edict — preserved in Latin by Lactantius and in Greek by Eusebius — twice orders the restitution, without charge, of confiscated Christian property: first the places "appropriated for their religious assemblies," then everything belonging "to their society in general, that is, to their churches." Licinius then "exhorted the Christians to rebuild their religious edifices."10 The clauses restore property the churches had held and lost under Diocletian (303).

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 316)

An eyewitness of the peace, Eusebius describes "every place which shortly before had been desolated by the impieties of the tyrants … and temples again rising from their foundations to an immense height, and receiving a splendor far greater than that of the old ones which had been destroyed," with "feasts of dedication in the cities." He preserves the panegyric he himself delivered at the dedication of the great rebuilt church at Tyre, hailing its builder-bishop Paulinus as "a new Beseleel … or Solomon, king of a new and much better Jerusalem," and glorying in the "lofty and spacious" house of God.11 Eusebius records the raising and dedication of purpose-built churches within a few years of the end of the persecution.

Disputed readings

How the early absence of buildings set apart for worship, and the New Testament's house churches, have been read.

  • Was the early Christians' lack of buildings set apart for worship a matter of principle or of circumstance? — read as a conviction about God (Minucius Felix, Origen), as a necessity of legal position (J. B. Lightfoot), as New Testament precedent without obligation (GotQuestions), and as a New Testament pattern for worship.12
