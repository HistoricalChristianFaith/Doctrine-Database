Bartholomew

Bartholomew is one of the Twelve. His name is a patronymic — bar-Tolmai, “son of Tolmai” — and the New Testament names him only in the three Synoptic lists of the apostles and in Acts, in the Synoptics always next to Philip. Later sources report that he carried the faith to “India” and left a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew there, that he died in Armenia, and that he was crucified, flayed, or beheaded; and many writers identify him with the Nathanael whom Philip brings to Jesus in John’s Gospel. This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: its entries are the datable sources that report on Bartholomew, set out earliest extant source first — the Gospel notices, the Alexandrian report of an eastern mission, the apostle-lists that name Armenia and a martyr’s death, the Golden Legend’s account of the flaying, and modern scholarship. Where he preached, and how he died, are questions whose readings are set out on their own pages under Disputed readings. For the sources common to all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names Bartholomew four times and does not describe him. He appears only in the lists of the Twelve — in Matthew, Mark, and Luke he is paired directly with Philip, and in Acts he stands after Thomas — and it records no saying, act, journey, or death of his. His name is a patronymic, “son of Tolmai.” John’s Gospel, which never mentions a Bartholomew, instead tells of Nathanael of Cana, whom Philip fetches to Jesus with “We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write,” and who is later named among the disciples who go fishing with Peter. Whether Bartholomew and Nathanael are one man is disputed: J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reports that “many scholars” identify them, while Augustine (Tractates on John 7) held that Nathanael was not one of the Twelve.1

Pantaenus’s report, preserved by Eusebius (c. 180, reported c. 325)

The earliest extant tradition of where Bartholomew went comes from Pantaenus, the head of the catechetical school of Alexandria, who (Eusebius reports) travelled as a missionary “as far as India” about the year 180 and found there a Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew that the Christians said “Bartholomew, one of the apostles, had preached to them, and left with them… which they had preserved till that time.” Jerome repeats the story in the late fourth century. It is the earliest extant notice of a mission by Bartholomew. Where Pantaenus’s “India” lay is read differently: as the Indian subcontinent, or as Arabia Felix or Ethiopia, lands ancient writers also called India; see the disputed reading.2

The apostle-lists (uncertain date)

The apostle-list literature — Pseudo-Hippolytus On the Twelve Apostles, Pseudo-Dorotheus, the Byzantine and Latin catalogues — is where Bartholomew’s death and burial place first appear; the lists keep Pantaenus’s India as his field. Pseudo-Hippolytus keeps the India of Pantaenus but adds the end: “Bartholomew, again, preached to the Indians, to whom he also gave the Gospel according to Matthew, and was crucified with his head downward, and was buried in Allanum, a town of the great Armenia.” It names Armenia as the place of his burial and gives his death as crucifixion. The lists are of uncertain date, related to one another (⚠ unverified), and differ on how he died.3

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend gives a full narrative: in “India” Bartholomew casts a demon out of the idol Astaroth, heals the mad daughter of King Polemius, and converts the king, and the king’s brother Astyages has him beaten and “flayed quick.” For the place of his death the Legend follows Dorotheus, “Alban, a city of great Armenia.” It records that the sources disagree on the manner — “the blessed Dorotheus saith that he was crucified… S. Theoderus saith that he was flayed, and it is read in many books that he was beheaded only” — and reconciles them by having him crucified, taken down, flayed, and beheaded. J. F. Fenlon (1907) attributes to the flaying legend the Bartholomew of later art, “flayed and holding in his hand his own skin,” as in Michelangelo’s Last Judgment.4

Modern scholarship (1905–2015)

J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) writes that no mention of Bartholomew occurs in ecclesiastical literature before Eusebius and that the manner of his death is “equally uncertain”; Sean McDowell (2015), assessing each apostle’s martyrdom account on a scale of probability, places Bartholomew’s among the least secure ⚠ unverified. On the mission, A. E. Medlycott (India and the Apostle Thomas, 1905) discusses the ancient use of “India” for Arabia and Ethiopia, while A. C. Perumalil and G. M. Moraes place Bartholomew’s field on the Konkan coast of India; the Armenian Apostolic Church honours him, with Thaddaeus, as its patron saint.5

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on where Bartholomew preached and how he died.

  • Where did Bartholomew preach: in “India”, in Armenia, or in both? — Pantaenus’s “India” beside the apostle-lists and the Armenian tradition.
  • How did Bartholomew die: crucified, flayed, or beheaded? — the apostle-lists, the Golden Legend, and modern scholarship.
