Bar Kokhba and Rabbi Akiva: “Son of the Star”

Dates: the Second Jewish Revolt, AD 132–135 (Simon bar Kosiba, d. 135; Rabbi Akiva, d. c. 135) · Wikipedia
Summary: Simon, the leader of the second revolt against Rome, is the one Jewish claimant openly proclaimed as the fulfilment of Balaam’s Star Prophecy. His own letters name him Shim‘on bar Kosiba; but the greatest sage of the age, Rabbi Akiva, reading “a Star (kokhav) steps forth out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17), recast his name as bar Kokhba, “Son of the Star,” and hailed him “the King Messiah.” A dissenting rabbi answered that the Messiah had not come; the revolt was crushed, Akiva martyred, Betar fallen — and the same tradition that had crowned him renamed him bar Koziba, “Son of the Lie.” This is the Star Prophecy’s clearest, and most catastrophic, Jewish application, and a textbook case of a messianic claim tested and found false.

Where it sits

Sixty years after the destruction of the Temple, a second revolt broke out in Judea (AD 132), provoked in part by Hadrian’s plans to refound Jerusalem as a pagan colony. Its leader, Simon, established an independent administration for some three years — his own coins and letters survive — before Rome ground the revolt down, storming the last fortress at Betar in 135, traditionally on the ninth of Av. The war’s messianic temper is fixed by the acclamation of Rabbi Akiva ben Joseph, the foremost rabbinic authority of the generation, who proclaimed Simon the Messiah on the strength of the Star Prophecy — and by the retort of a colleague who refused the identification. The episode is preserved in the rabbinic sources and, from the other side, in the Christian writers Justin and Eusebius, who knew Simon by his star-name.1

Claims & evidence

Rabbi Akiva: “This is the King Messiah”

Aqiba my teacher used to preach, there appeared a star out of Jacob — there appeared Koziba out of Jacob. When Rebbi Aqiba saw Bar Koziba he said, this is King Messias. Rebbi Joḥanan ben Torta said to him, Aqiba! Grass will grow from your jaws and still David’s son (still has to) come.

— Jerusalem Talmud, Ta‘anit 4:5, trans. Heinrich Guggenheimer. This is the pivot. Akiva reads the Hebrew of Numbers 24:17, dārakh kokhāv (“a star has stepped forth”), as dārakh Koziba (“Koziba has stepped forth”) — making Simon the star of the prophecy in person — and declares him “the King Messiah.” The dissent is recorded in the same breath: Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta retorts that grass will sprout from Akiva’s decayed cheeks before the true son of David comes. The parallel in Lamentations Rabbah sharpens the wordplay into a post-mortem verdict: “do not read it as star [kokhav] but as fraudulent [kozav].”2

The name: Kosiba → Kokhba → Koziba

The three forms of the name track the arc of the claim. His actual name, attested on administrative letters he dictated and sealed — recovered from the Cave of Letters in the Judean desert — was Shim‘on bar Kosiba. Akiva’s circle upgraded Kosiba to bar Kokhba (Aramaic “Son of the Star,” from kokhav), keyed to the Star Prophecy — the messianic reading. After the defeat the rabbis punned it the other way, to bar Koziba (“Son of the Lie/Disappointment,” from the root k-z-b, “to lie”), the form the Talmud uses throughout. The star-name itself, strikingly, survives only in Christian sources; the rabbis remember “Koziba,” and the coins bear only “Shim‘on.”3

The Christian witnesses: a false star that persecuted the church

The leader of the Jews at this time was a man by the name of Barcocheba (which signifies a star), who possessed the character of a robber and a murderer, but nevertheless, relying upon his name, boasted to them, as if they were slaves, that he possessed wonderful powers; and he pretended that he was a star that had come down to them out of heaven to bring them light in the midst of their misfortunes.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 4.6.2, trans. McGiffert. Eusebius preserves the Greek form of the star-name and its meaning (“which signifies a star”), and reads the whole claim as a fraud — Simon “pretended that he was a star that had come down … out of heaven.” A generation earlier, Justin Martyr — writing within living memory of the revolt — had already named him as a persecutor of Christians: “Barchochebas, the leader of the revolt of the Jews, gave orders that Christians alone should be led to cruel punishments, unless they would deny Jesus Christ and utter blasphemy.” The Christian angle is pointed: to the church, Simon was a false claimant to the very star-prophecy the church read of Christ, and the Christians’ refusal to fight for a rival messiah earned them his persecution.4

The coins: a star over the Temple? — contested

Many of the revolt’s large silver coins show the façade of the Jerusalem Temple with a star (or rosette-like emblem) above the gable, struck by overstriking Roman coins and legended “Jerusalem” and “Shim‘on.” It is tempting to read the star as a deliberate Numbers 24:17 boast — the “Son of the Star” stamping the prophecy on his currency. But the reading is genuinely contested: the coins never use the name bar Kokhba, and Jonathan Bourgel has argued the emblem belongs to a Temple-inauguration / Sukkot program (celestial fire consecrating the sanctuary), not a personal messianic sign, while others read the element as an ornamental rosette or vine rather than a star at all. The honest verdict is that the star-coinage is suggestive but not decisive evidence of a self-conscious Star-Prophecy claim — the messianic reading rests firmly on Akiva and the name, not on the numismatics.5

Notes

  • The discernment test failed. Bar Kokhba is the Star Prophecy’s clearest case of a messianic claim that came to nothing — and the tradition itself records the correction (Yohanan ben Torta’s retort; the renaming to “Son of the Lie”). It is a live instance of the wider problem of discerning true prophecy from false: a claim can be backed by the greatest authority of the age (Akiva) and the right proof-text and still be wrong. Compare the discernment of dreams, where a sign’s coming true is likewise no proof of its source.
  • Akiva’s stature makes it sharper. The claim is not fringe: Rabbi Akiva is among the most revered figures in rabbinic Judaism, which makes his misfired messianic identification a striking datum — and his martyrdom under Hadrian folds him into the tragedy. The rabbinic sources preserve both his acclamation and its refutation without erasing either.
  • The whole afterlife of one verse. That the same oracle underwrote the Qumran Messiahs, the star of Bethlehem, Josephus’s Vespasian, and now Bar Kokhba is the point of the Star Prophecy timeline: mutually exclusive claimants, one proof-text.
