Bar Kokhba and Rabbi Akiva: “Son of the Star”

Dates: the Second Jewish Revolt, AD 132–135 (Simon bar Kosiba, d. 135; Rabbi Akiva, d. c. 135) · Wikipedia
Summary: Simon, the leader of the second revolt against Rome, is the Jewish claimant recorded as proclaimed the fulfilment of Balaam’s Star Prophecy. His own letters name him Shim‘on bar Kosiba; Rabbi Akiva, reading “a Star (kokhav) steps forth out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17), hailed him “the King Messiah”; the star-name bar Kokhba, “Son of the Star,” is usually explained from that reading. A dissenting rabbi answered that the Messiah had not come; the revolt was crushed, Akiva died c. 135, Betar fell, and the rabbinic sources name him bar Koziba, read as “Son of the Lie.” Justin and Eusebius record the star-name from the Christian side.

Historical setting

Sixty years after the destruction of the Temple, a second revolt broke out in Judea (AD 132), provoked in part by Hadrian’s plans to refound Jerusalem as a pagan colony. Its leader, Simon, established an independent administration for some three years — his own coins and letters survive — before Rome ground the revolt down, storming the last fortress at Betar in 135, traditionally on the ninth of Av. The rabbinic sources record the acclamation of Rabbi Akiva ben Joseph, who proclaimed Simon the Messiah on the Star Prophecy, and the retort of a colleague who refused the identification. The episode is preserved in the rabbinic sources and, from the other side, in the Christian writers Justin and Eusebius, who knew Simon by his star-name.1

Claims & evidence

Rabbi Akiva: “This is the King Messiah”

Aqiba my teacher used to preach, there appeared a star out of Jacob — there appeared Koziba out of Jacob. When Rebbi Aqiba saw Bar Koziba he said, this is King Messias. Rebbi Joḥanan ben Torta said to him, Aqiba! Grass will grow from your jaws and still David’s son (still has to) come.

— Jerusalem Talmud, Ta‘anit 4:5, trans. Heinrich Guggenheimer. Akiva reads the Hebrew of Numbers 24:17, dārakh kokhāv (“a star has stepped forth”), as dārakh Koziba (“Koziba has stepped forth”) — making Simon the star of the prophecy in person — and declares him “the King Messiah.” The dissent is recorded in the same breath: Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta retorts that grass will sprout from Akiva’s decayed cheeks before the true son of David comes. The parallel in Lamentations Rabbah turns the wordplay the other way: “do not read it as star [kokhav] but as fraudulent [kozav].”2

The name: Kosiba → Kokhba → Koziba

The three forms of the name track the arc of the claim. His actual name, attested on administrative letters he dictated and sealed — recovered from the Cave of Letters in the Judean desert — was Shim‘on bar Kosiba. On the reconstruction usually given, Akiva’s reading recast Kosiba as bar Kokhba (Aramaic “Son of the Star,” from kokhav), keyed to the Star Prophecy, and after the defeat the rabbis punned it the other way, to bar Koziba (“Son of the Lie/Disappointment,” from the root k-z-b, “to lie”). Koziba is the form the Talmud uses throughout, including in its own text of Akiva’s derash (“there appeared Koziba out of Jacob”). The star-name itself survives in Christian sources; the rabbis remember “Koziba,” and the coins bear only “Shim‘on.”3

The Christian witnesses: Justin and Eusebius

The leader of the Jews at this time was a man by the name of Barcocheba (which signifies a star), who possessed the character of a robber and a murderer, but nevertheless, relying upon his name, boasted to them, as if they were slaves, that he possessed wonderful powers; and he pretended that he was a star that had come down to them out of heaven to bring them light in the midst of their misfortunes.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 4.6.2, trans. McGiffert. Eusebius preserves the Greek form of the star-name and its meaning (“which signifies a star”), and writes that Simon “pretended that he was a star that had come down … out of heaven.” Justin Martyr, writing within living memory of the revolt, records his treatment of Christians: “Barchochebas, the leader of the revolt of the Jews, gave orders that Christians alone should be led to cruel punishments, unless they would deny Jesus Christ and utter blasphemy.”4

The coins: a star over the Temple?

Many of the revolt’s large silver coins show the façade of the Jerusalem Temple with a star (or rosette-like emblem) above the gable, struck by overstriking Roman coins and legended “Jerusalem” and “Shim‘on.” The coins do not carry the name bar Kokhba. The emblem has been read as an allusion to Numbers 24:17 and the star-name, as a plain rosette, and, by Jonathan Bourgel (2024), as a sign of the restored Temple’s inauguration; the readings are set out on “What is the star-like emblem above the Temple on Bar Kokhba’s coins?”5

Reception and debate

  • Acclamation and retort. The rabbinic sources preserve both Akiva’s acclamation and Yohanan ben Torta’s retort, and later name Simon “Son of the Lie.” Akiva’s death under Hadrian falls in the same years. On how a true revelation is told from a false one, see the discernment of dreams.
  • One verse, several claimants. The same oracle is read of the Qumran Messiahs, of the star of Bethlehem, of Vespasian (on Martin Hengel’s identification of Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” with this verse), and of Bar Kokhba; see the Star Prophecy timeline.
