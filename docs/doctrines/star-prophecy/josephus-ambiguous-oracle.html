Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” and Vespasian

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (the Jewish War published c. AD 75) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Jewish War Josephus reports that what most drove the Jews to their revolt was “an ambiguous oracle … found in their sacred writings” that “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth” — and he writes that it was fulfilled not in a Jewish deliverer but in Vespasian, the Roman general proclaimed emperor on Jewish soil. Tacitus and Suetonius record a Judean world-ruler oracle and its application to the Flavians. Josephus does not name the text: Martin Hengel identifies it with the “Star Prophecy” of Numbers 24:17, and Per Bilde and N. T. Wright with Daniel.

Historical setting

Flavius Josephus, a priestly Jerusalemite and rebel commander who surrendered to the Romans in AD 67, won his life by prophesying to his captor Vespasian that he would become emperor — which, in AD 69, he did. Adopted as a client of the Flavian house, Josephus wrote his Jewish War about AD 75, a few years after Titus destroyed the Temple. The “ambiguous oracle” passage falls in his account of the portents that preceded the fall of Jerusalem (War 6.288–315), a list of signs that, he writes, the Jews misread.1

Claims & evidence

The oracle that drove the war — fulfilled, he says, in Vespasian

But now, what did the most elevate them in undertaking this war, was an ambiguous oracle that was also found in their sacred writings, how, “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth.” The Jews took this prediction to belong to themselves in particular, and many of the wise men were thereby deceived in their determination. Now this oracle certainly denoted the government of Vespasian, who was appointed emperor in Judea.

— Jewish War 6.312–313 (Book VI, ch. 5, §4), trans. William Whiston. Josephus reports that a written oracle of a world-ruler from Judea moved the Jews to war, and he applies it to Vespasian: the oracle was true, he says, but the Jews mistook its object; it “certainly denoted” Vespasian, the general who led the Roman war in Judea.2

The Roman historians: Tacitus and Suetonius

… in most there was a firm persuasion, that in the ancient records of their priests was contained a prediction of how at this very time the East was to grow powerful, and rulers, coming from Judaea, were to acquire universal empire. These mysterious prophecies had pointed to Vespasian and Titus, but the common people, with the usual blindness of ambition, had interpreted these mighty destinies of themselves…

— Tacitus, Histories 5.13, trans. Church & Brodribb. Suetonius reports the same: “an old and established belief, that it was fated at that time for men coming from Judaea to rule the world. This prediction, referring to the emperor of Rome, as afterwards appeared from the event, the people of Judaea took to themselves” (Vespasian 4). The two Roman historians report both a Jewish oracle of a world-ruler out of Judea and its application to Vespasian and Titus.3

Which oracle?

Josephus calls the text “ambiguous” and does not name it, and its identification is disputed. Martin Hengel identifies it with Balaam’s Star Prophecy, the oracle of a ruler rising from Israel that the Dead Sea Scrolls read messianically and on which Rabbi Akiva later hailed Bar Kokhba. N. T. Wright and Per Bilde identify it with Daniel, pointing to Josephus’s stress that the oracle fixed the time (“about that time”) and to Daniel’s week-prophecies. A composite reading (⚠ unverified — no named proponent located) takes it as a cluster of messianic texts (Genesis 49:10, Numbers 24:17, Daniel). The readings are set out on “Which scripture did Josephus mean by the ‘ambiguous oracle’?”4

Reception and debate

  • A Roman application. On Hengel’s identification, Vespasian is the one figure on the Star Prophecy timeline outside Judaism and Christianity to whom the oracle is applied; the others are the Qumran Messiahs, Jesus, and Bar Kokhba.
  • Josephus on Balaam. In his later Antiquities Josephus retells the Balaam episode at length but does not include the “star out of Jacob” from the fourth oracle. See the Balaam timeline’s Josephus on Balaam page.
  • Text and translation. The English is William Whiston’s (1737), keyed here to the Niese section numbers (War 6.312–313 = Book VI, ch. 5, §4). The Greek phrase rendered “ambiguous oracle” is chrēsmos amphibolos.
