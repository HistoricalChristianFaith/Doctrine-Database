Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” and Vespasian

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (the Jewish War published c. AD 75) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Jewish War Josephus reports that what most drove the Jews to their fatal revolt was “an ambiguous oracle … found in their sacred writings” that “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth” — and he insists it was truly fulfilled not in a Jewish deliverer but in Vespasian, the Roman general proclaimed emperor on Jewish soil. Tacitus and Suetonius independently record the same Judean world-ruler oracle and the same Roman turn upon it. Josephus never names the text; but because the “Star Prophecy” of Numbers 24:17 was the oracle of a rising Israelite ruler among the very revolutionaries he describes, many scholars identify his “ambiguous oracle” with it — making this the episode in which the Star Prophecy was seized and inverted into imperial propaganda.

Where it sits

Flavius Josephus, a priestly Jerusalemite and rebel commander who surrendered to the Romans in AD 67, won his life by prophesying to his captor Vespasian that he would become emperor — which, in AD 69, he did. Adopted as a client of the Flavian house, Josephus wrote his Jewish War about AD 75, a few years after Titus destroyed the Temple, partly to explain the catastrophe to a Graeco-Roman readership and to his own conscience. The “ambiguous oracle” passage falls in his account of the portents that preceded the fall of Jerusalem (War 6.288–315): a list of signs the Jews misread. It is a charged text, because Josephus is at once reporting a genuine current of messianic expectation and defending his own defection — his reading of the oracle onto Vespasian is also the justification of his life.1

Claims & evidence

The oracle that drove the war — fulfilled, he says, in Vespasian

But now, what did the most elevate them in undertaking this war, was an ambiguous oracle that was also found in their sacred writings, how, “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth.” The Jews took this prediction to belong to themselves in particular, and many of the wise men were thereby deceived in their determination. Now this oracle certainly denoted the government of Vespasian, who was appointed emperor in Judea.

— Jewish War 6.312–313 (Book VI, ch. 5, §4), trans. William Whiston. The passage is doubly remarkable. First, it is direct testimony that a specific written oracle of a world-ruler-from-Judea was inflaming the revolt — that first-century Jews were going to war on a proof-text. Second, Josephus turns that hope inside out: the oracle was true, he says, but the Jews mistook its object; it “certainly denoted” Vespasian. The man who fulfils the prophecy of the deliverer-from-Judea is the general who crushed Judea — the most audacious counter-claim in the whole history of the verse.2

The Roman historians confirm the oracle — and the appropriation

… in most there was a firm persuasion, that in the ancient records of their priests was contained a prediction of how at this very time the East was to grow powerful, and rulers, coming from Judaea, were to acquire universal empire. These mysterious prophecies had pointed to Vespasian and Titus, but the common people, with the usual blindness of ambition, had interpreted these mighty destinies of themselves…

— Tacitus, Histories 5.13, trans. Church & Brodribb. Suetonius reports the same: “an old and established belief, that it was fated at that time for men coming from Judaea to rule the world. This prediction, referring to the emperor of Rome, as afterwards appeared from the event, the people of Judaea took to themselves” (Vespasian 4). Two pagan historians, writing independently of Josephus, attest both halves of the story: a genuine Jewish oracle of a world-ruler out of Judea, and its Flavian appropriation to Vespasian and Titus. That three sources converge is strong evidence the oracle was real and widely known — and that its inversion onto Rome was official Flavian messaging, not merely Josephus’s private apologetic.3

Which oracle? — the Star Prophecy the leading candidate

Josephus deliberately leaves the text “ambiguous” and unnamed, and scholars divide over which Scripture he meant. The leading candidate is Balaam’s Star Prophecy: it is the oracle of a rising Israelite ruler par excellence, it was demonstrably central to the revolutionaries’ own circles (the Dead Sea Scrolls; soon Bar Kokhba), and Martin Hengel and others identify it outright. Against it, N. T. Wright and Per Bilde urge Daniel — because Josephus stresses the oracle fixed the time (“about that time”), and Daniel’s week-prophecies give a chronology that Numbers does not; a third view holds Josephus invokes a whole cluster of messianic texts (Genesis 49:10, Numbers 24:17, Daniel) rather than one verse. The question is genuinely open, and is weighed on its own argument page (contested). What is not in doubt is that the deliverer-from-Judea hope Josephus describes is the same hope the Star Prophecy fed elsewhere — which is why the identification is the natural one.4

Notes

  • The inversion. This is the one stratum on the Star Prophecy timeline where the claimed “fulfilment” is the enemy. Every other claimant — the Qumran Messiahs, Jesus, Bar Kokhba — is a Jewish (or Christian) deliverer; Vespasian is the Roman who ends the war the oracle started. Josephus’s move both defends his defection and drains the prophecy of its revolutionary charge — a political reading as much as an exegetical one.
  • Josephus and the withheld star. Tellingly, in his later Antiquities Josephus retells the Balaam episode at length but omits the “star out of Jacob” from the fourth oracle altogether — a reticence about a messianic world-ruler that fits a historian under Flavian patronage. See the Balaam timeline’s Josephus on Balaam page. That he elsewhere buries the verse makes his use of an unnamed “ambiguous oracle” here all the more pointed.
  • Text and translation. The English is William Whiston’s (1737), keyed here to the Niese section numbers (War 6.312–313 = Book VI, ch. 5, §4). The Greek phrase rendered “ambiguous oracle” is chrēsmos amphibolos.
