Does “until Shiloh come” (Genesis 49:10) foretell a personal coming ruler, or the course of Judah’s royal line?

Disputed reading · on The Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17) and Its Claimed Fulfilments

Related readings: Which scripture did Josephus mean by the “ambiguous oracle”?

The passage

The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be.

— Genesis 49:10 (KJV), source.1

The question

Jacob’s deathbed blessing of Judah shares the royal sceptre (Hebrew šēbeṭ) with Balaam’s oracle of Numbers 24:17, and its key word, šîlōh, is obscure: it has been construed as shello, “to whom it belongs,” as shai lo, “tribute to him,” and as the place-name Shiloh. What is disputed is whether the verse foretells a personal ruler who is to come, or speaks of the lasting rule of Judah’s line, with the personal sense supplied by its ancient translations and commentators.2

A coming ruler to whom the sceptre belongs

In brief: the verse foretells one person, the awaited king, for whom Judah’s sceptre is kept and whom the nations will obey.

Held by: the Septuagint; the Qumran Genesis pesher (4Q252); Targum Onkelos; Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 120).3

The Greek reads a coming figure: “until there come the things stored up for him; and he is the expectation of nations” — construing the consonants as “that which is his” and pointing the verse at one whom the nations await. The Qumran pesher 4Q252 (col. 5) glosses it directly: “the staff is the covenant of royalty, [the thou]sands of Israel are the feet, until the Messiah of Righteousness comes, the Branch of David; for to him and to his descendants has been given the covenant of royalty over his people for all everlasting generations.” Targum Onkelos names him: “until the Messiah come, whose is the kingdom”; Neofiti and Pseudo-Jonathan likewise replace “Shiloh” with the King Messiah, as all three do with the star of Numbers 24:17.4

Christian readers hold that the one for whom the sceptre was kept is Christ. Justin reads the ruler not failing from Judah “until he come for whom it is laid up”; Ambrose expounds “until he comes for whom it has been reserved … he is the expectation of the nations”; Rufinus reads the “until” as a datable sign — when Herod, a foreigner, took the throne, “the one to whom the kingdom had been reserved immediately came”; and Cosmas Indicopleustes holds that “the whole of this prophecy had its fulfilment in the Lord Christ.” The New Testament calls Jesus “the Lion of the tribe of Judah” (Revelation 5:5) and notes that “our Lord sprang out of Judah” (Hebrews 7:14), the tribe of the sceptre. On this reading the Jewish versions, Qumran and the Fathers agree in hearing a person in “Shiloh,” and the verse’s built-in “until” marks the time of his coming.5

Judah’s dynastic rule, read messianically in its reception

In brief: in Jacob’s own horizon the verse speaks of Judah’s tribal supremacy and the Davidic throne; the personal, messianic sense came with the ancient readings of its obscure word.

Held by: John J. Collins (The Scepter and the Star, 2nd ed. 2010).6

The case reads the verse first as one of Jacob’s tribal blessings. In that setting the sceptre and the lawgiver’s staff are emblems of Judah’s leadership among the tribes, and the blessing’s horizon is the royal house that came from Judah, the throne of David. The obscure šîlōh does not by itself name a person: the Masoretic consonants admit several constructions, and the personal force of the Septuagint and the Targums comes from how they read those consonants (as shello, “to whom it belongs,” or shai lo, “tribute to him”). The ancient versions are therefore early witnesses to how the verse was read, and their interpretive renderings are the record of that reading. On this account Genesis 49:10 and Numbers 24:17 share a history: each is a royal oracle about Israel’s kingship, and each was read, in the Second Temple period, of a coming royal deliverer, and both carry the royal sceptre (šēbeṭ). Collins takes the title of his study of Qumran messianism, The Scepter and the Star, from Numbers 24:17, whose oracle pairs the star with the sceptre.7
