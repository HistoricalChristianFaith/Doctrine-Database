Which scripture did Josephus mean by the “ambiguous oracle” of Jewish War 6.312–313?

Disputed reading · on The Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17) and Its Claimed Fulfilments · witness: Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” and Vespasian

Related readings: Does “until Shiloh come” (Genesis 49:10) foretell a personal coming ruler, or the course of Judah’s royal line?

The passage

But now, what did the most elevate them in undertaking this war, was an ambiguous oracle that was also found in their sacred writings, how, “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth.” … Now this oracle certainly denoted the government of Vespasian, who was appointed emperor in Judea.

— Jewish War 6.312–313 (Whiston), source.1

The question

Josephus does not name the scripture he calls an “ambiguous oracle,” and Tacitus (Histories 5.13) and Suetonius (Vespasian 4) report a Judean prediction of rulers “coming from Judaea” without naming it either. What is disputed is which text, or which group of texts, Josephus had in view; the answer bears on whether Balaam’s oracle of Numbers 24:17 is the scripture Josephus reports as driving the revolt of 66–70.2

The oracle is Daniel’s timetable of kingdoms

In brief: Josephus means the prophecies of Daniel, which set the coming kingdom in a sequence that could be dated.

Held by: Per Bilde (Flavius Josephus between Jerusalem and Rome, 1988); N. T. Wright (The New Testament and the People of God, 1992).3

The case starts from Josephus’s own wording. The oracle, he says, foretold that “about that time” one from their country would rule the habitable earth: it was read as a chronological prediction, telling its readers that the deliverer’s hour had arrived. Daniel is the scripture whose prophecies are built as timetables — the seventy weeks and the four kingdoms — and so it is the text from which first-century readers could compute that the time was now. A people going to war because they believed the appointed moment had come is, on this reading, a people reckoning by Daniel. Josephus’s “about that time” is the mark of that reckoning, and the oracle’s ambiguity lies in whose kingdom the timetable announced: the rebels read it of themselves, and Josephus of Vespasian.4

The oracle is Balaam’s Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17)

In brief: Josephus means the “Star out of Jacob,” the oracle of a ruler rising from Israel that the revolutionary movement read of its deliverer.

Held by: Martin Hengel (The Zealots, Eng. trans. 1989).5

Hengel reads Balaam’s oracle as the revolutionary proof-text of the Jewish freedom movement and identifies it as the oracle behind Josephus’s prophecy to Vespasian. The case rests on three points. First, content: Numbers 24:17–19 foretells one who comes “out of Jacob” and “shall have dominion,” which matches Josephus’s “one from their country” who will govern the habitable earth, and the rulers “coming from Judaea” whom Tacitus and Suetonius report. Second, currency: the verse is recorded as a messianic text in the very milieu Josephus describes — the Dead Sea Scrolls read it of the coming Prince (CD 7:18–21), set it among their eschatological proof-texts (4Q175) and pray it as a war-oracle (1QM 11:6–7), and within a lifetime of the War Rabbi Akiva hailed Simon bar Kosiba on it as “King Messias.” Third, the label: the star-and-sceptre imagery leaves its referent open — one figure or two, a ruler of Israel or a ruler who arises in Judea — which is what allows Josephus to call the oracle “ambiguous” and to apply it to a Roman general proclaimed emperor on Judean soil. On this reading the oracle Josephus describes is the one verse whose use as a call to arms is recorded on both sides of AD 70.6

The oracle is a cluster of messianic texts read together

In brief: Josephus has no single verse in view but the braided messianic prophecy of Genesis 49:10, Numbers 24:17 and Daniel.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case starts from Josephus’s choice of words. He calls the oracle “ambiguous” and leaves it unnamed, and the expectation he describes has several parts: a ruler, from their own country, whose rule reaches the whole habitable earth, at a fixed time. Those parts are found across a set of texts that were read together. Genesis 49:10 shares the royal sceptre with Numbers 24:17, and the Qumran community read both of a coming royal Messiah (4Q252 on Genesis 49:10; CD 7:18–21 on Numbers 24:17). Genesis 49:10 also carries its own clock — “the sceptre shall not depart from Judah … until Shiloh come” — which Justin (Dialogue 52) and Rufinus (Blessings of the Patriarchs 1.7) read as a datable pointer to the moment the sceptre passed to Herod. Daniel supplies the succession of kingdoms. On this reading Josephus is summarizing a tradition of messianic prophecy, and “about that time” and “governor of the habitable earth” are the points on which its several texts agreed.7
