Do Zechariah’s “two anointed ones” and the Branch “a priest upon his throne” describe two figures or one?

Disputed reading · on The Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17) and Its Claimed Fulfilments · witness: the Dead Sea Scrolls

The passage

Then said he, These are the two anointed ones, that stand by the LORD of the whole earth.

— Zechariah 4:14 (KJV).

Behold the man whose name is The BRANCH; and he shall grow up out of his place, and he shall build the temple of the LORD: Even he shall build the temple of the LORD; and he shall bear the glory, and shall sit and rule upon his throne; and he shall be a priest upon his throne: and the counsel of peace shall be between them both.

— Zechariah 6:12–13 (KJV), spoken over “Joshua … the high priest” (6:11). Read beside the Qumran pesher on Numbers 24:17:

The star is the Interpreter of the Law who shall come to Damascus … The sceptre is the Prince of the whole congregation, and when he comes he shall smite all the children of Seth.

— Damascus Document (CD) 7:18–21, trans. Geza Vermes.1

The question

The Damascus Document reads Balaam’s star and sceptre as two coming figures, a priestly “Interpreter of the Law” and a royal “Prince of the whole congregation,” and the Qumran texts look for “the Prophet and the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel” (1QS 9:11). Zechariah names “two anointed ones” and crowns the high priest Joshua with the word that the Branch “shall be a priest upon his throne.” Jerome, canvassing the Hebrew “two sons of oil,” records among the readings current in his day that the two branches of olives “represent the priesthood and the Law.” What is disputed is whether Zechariah’s texts describe two anointed heads, a priest and a prince, or one figure who is both king and priest, and so how they relate to Qumran’s two Messiahs.2

One priest-king: the Branch is Christ

In brief: Zechariah’s Branch, who rules and is a priest on his throne, is one person, the king who is also “a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek.”

Held by: Origen; Basil of Caesarea; Gregory of Nyssa; Cyprian of Carthage.3

The case reads Zechariah 6:12–13 as one figure. The man who sits and rules on the throne is the one who is priest upon it. The Septuagint renders “Branch” as Anatolē, “the East” or “the Dayspring,” and the Fathers read it of Christ: Basil, “Behold a man, the Orient is his name … the sun of righteousness”; Gregory of Nyssa, the “sun of righteousness … designated rising by the prophecy”; and Origen before them.4

The reading joins Zechariah to Psalm 110, which addresses one figure as enthroned king, “Sit thou at my right hand” (v. 1), and as eternal priest, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (v. 4). Hebrews 5–7 builds on that union a Christology of the king from Judah who is also the Melchizedekian priest (Hebrews 7). Cyprian reads Melchizedek, king of Salem and “a priest of the Most High God,” as one who “bore a type of Christ,” and Cosmas Indicopleustes applies the Psalm’s “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec” to Christ. On this reading the priestly and royal offices that Zechariah sets side by side, and that Qumran assigned to the star and the sceptre, belong to one anointed person.5

Two anointed heads: the post-exilic priest and prince

In brief: Zechariah’s “two anointed ones” are the high priest Joshua and the Davidic governor Zerubbabel, and that paired leadership is the pattern of Qumran’s Messiahs of Aaron and Israel.

Held by: Carol L. Meyers & Eric M. Meyers (Haggai, Zechariah 1–8, 1987); James C. VanderKam (“Messianism in the Scrolls,” 1994); John J. Collins (The Scepter and the Star, 2nd ed. 2010).6

The case reads Zechariah in the restored community after the exile. The two olive trees beside the lampstand are “the two sons of oil” (bənê hayyiṣhār), the priest and the prince who together head the returned community. In 6:11–13 the Masoretic text crowns Joshua the priest alone, yet ends “the counsel of peace shall be between them both”; Meyers & Meyers discuss the reconstruction of an original double crowning of Joshua and Zerubbabel, which gives the plural its two referents.7

VanderKam and Collins trace the Qumran two-Messiah expectation to that diarchy. The Rule looks for “the Prophet and the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel” (1QS 9:11); the Damascus Document repeats the pair (CD 12:23–13:1; 14:19; 19:10–11); and at the eschatological banquet of the Rule of the Congregation the Priest precedes and blesses before “the Messiah of Israel” (1QSa 2:11–22). CD 7:18–21 then assigns the star to the priestly figure and the sceptre to the royal one. Collins sets the diarchy beside priestly-eschatological writings (Jubilees, the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, Aramaic Levi) as the sources of the scheme, and treats the New Testament’s single priest-king and Qumran’s pair as two uses of shared Old Testament texts, without evidence of dependence between them. On this reading Zechariah’s two anointed ones are the scriptural form of the priest-and-prince leadership that Qumran projected onto the last days.8
