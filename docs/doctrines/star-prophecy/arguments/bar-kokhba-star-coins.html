What is the star-like emblem above the Temple on Bar Kokhba’s coins, and does it allude to Numbers 24:17?

Disputed reading · on The Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17) and Its Claimed Fulfilments · witness: Bar Kokhba and Rabbi Akiva

The passage

a façade of the Second Temple shows a glimpse of the ark of the covenant between two sets of pillars with a star above

— Museum of the Bible, catalogue description of a Bar Kokhba Year 2 sela (minted c. AD 133), source.1

Aqiba my teacher used to preach, there appeared a star out of Jacob — there appeared Koziba out of Jacob. When Rebbi Aqiba saw Bar Koziba he said, this is King Messias.

— Jerusalem Talmud, Ta‘anit 4:5, trans. Heinrich Guggenheimer, source.2

The question

The revolt’s large silver coins (tetradrachms, the Jewish sela‘im, struck over Roman coins) show on the obverse the columned façade of the Jerusalem Temple with the Ark between the pillars, legended “Jerusalem”; the reverse shows the lulav and etrog (the “four species”) and the name “Shim‘on.” From the second year of the revolt a small emblem appears above the façade.3 Rabbi Akiva’s reading of Numbers 24:17 and the star-name bar Kokhba, “Son of the Star,” are recorded in the rabbinic sources and in Justin and Eusebius. What is disputed is what the emblem on the coins represents: a star alluding to the prophecy and the star-name, a decorative rosette, or a sign belonging to the dedication of the restored Temple.4

A star alluding to the prophecy and the star-name

In brief: the emblem is a star, set over the Temple as a sign of the leader hailed as the Star of Numbers 24:17.

Held by: Baruch Kanael (1971) ⚠ unverified; Mary E. Smallwood (The Jews under Roman Rule, 1976) ⚠ unverified; Hillel I. Newman (“Stars of the Messiah,” 2015) ⚠ unverified.5

The case starts from the leader’s title. Akiva read dārakh kokhāv, “a star has stepped forth,” of Simon and proclaimed him “King Messias,” and the name that came from that reading, “Son of the Star,” is the verse itself. Eusebius preserves the star-name and its meaning, “Barcocheba, which signifies a star.” A star raised over the sanctuary the movement meant to rebuild is the emblem such a claim would choose.6

The coinage as a whole is a program of national restoration: the Temple façade, the Ark, the four species of the Feast of Tabernacles, the slogans “For the freedom of Jerusalem” and “Shim‘on, Prince of Israel” (Nasi’), all struck over the images of Rome. On a currency this deliberately charged with meaning, a star over the Temple reads as one more sign of the same program. Bourgel reports this as the popular understanding, “a stellar design alluding to the Aramaic nickname Bar Kokhba,” and William Horbury (2014), on Bourgel’s report, endorses it cautiously.7

A common rosette without symbolic value

In brief: the mark is an ordinary decorative rosette, and the revolt’s coins and documents carry no messianic self-description by its leader.

Held by: Gedalyahu Alon ⚠ unverified; Leo Mildenberg (The Coinage of the Bar Kokhba War, 1984) ⚠ unverified.8

The case starts from the engraving and from what the leader issued in his own name. The element above the façade is small and schematic, and it has been described both as a star and as a rosette; on this reading it is “a common rosette devoid of symbolic value.”9 Its holders read the coinage and the documents from the Judean Desert together: the coins name the leader only as Shim‘on, with “Eleazar the Priest” on some first-year issues, and his administrative letters from the Cave of Letters call him Shim‘on bar Kosiba. The star-name is preserved by the rabbis and the Christian writers, while the media the leader controlled — coins and letters — give him his own name and title, “Prince of Israel.” On this reading the coins’ program is the Temple, Jerusalem’s freedom and the festival of the four species, and the rosette above the gable is part of the ornament of that façade.10

Fire or glory from heaven at the Temple’s inauguration

In brief: the obverse and reverse together depict the future dedication of the restored Temple, and the emblem is the holy fire or the glory of God descending on it.

Held by: Jonathan Bourgel (“A Representation of the Inauguration Ceremony of the Restored Temple?”, 2024), offered as a tentative reinterpretation.11

Bourgel reads the two faces of the tetradrachm as one image: the façade of the Temple on the obverse and the four species on the reverse are “complementary symbols,” joined “to represent the future inauguration ceremony of the restored temple,” staged on the pattern of a Tabernacles festival. In the biblical accounts a consecrated house is marked by fire from heaven consuming the sacrifices and by the glory of the Lord filling it (2 Chronicles 7:1–3; 1 Kings 8:10–11). The element above the gable is, on this reading, that sign: “the holy fire descending from heaven to consume the sacrifices,” or the return of the glory of God (kavod YHWH) to the sanctuary. Bourgel holds that “Son of the Star” was neither a self-designation nor part of the regime’s official propaganda, so the emblem is read within the coin’s own Temple program. He also proposes that the tetradrachm imagery may answer the coins issued for the founding of the colony of Aelia Capitolina on the site of Jerusalem: a restored and rededicated Temple set against the Roman foundation.12
