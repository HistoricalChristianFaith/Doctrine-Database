The Dead Sea Scrolls on the Star Prophecy

Dates: c. 2nd century BC – AD 68 (the Qumran library; the relevant scrolls copied c. 100–1 BC) · Wikipedia
Summary: The community of the Dead Sea Scrolls is the earliest datable body of readers to take Balaam’s “Star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) as a prophecy of the coming Messiah — three centuries before any Christian use. The Damascus Document splits the oracle’s star and sceptre into two eschatological figures, a priestly “Interpreter of the Law” and a royal “Prince of the whole congregation,” matching the sect’s twofold expectation of the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel; the Testimonia collect the verse among a catena of messianic proof-texts; and the War Scroll prays it as an oracle of the final battle. These are the load-bearing witnesses that the “Star Prophecy” was a live and central messianic text in Second Temple Judaism, wholly independent of the Gospel.

Where it sits

The scrolls found from 1947 in the caves above the Dead Sea are the library of a Jewish sect (widely, though not universally, identified with the Essenes) that flourished from the second century BC until the Roman destruction of its settlement in AD 68. Its members read the Scriptures as a code to their own age and awaited an imminent end — and among the texts they copied and composed, Balaam’s fourth oracle recurs as a keystone of that hope. Because the manuscripts are securely dated by palaeography and radiocarbon to the last century BC and the first century AD, they fix the messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 as demonstrably pre-Christian: whatever Matthew or the rabbis later made of the star, Jews were already reading it of the Messiah before Jesus was born.1

Claims & evidence

The star and the sceptre split into two Messiahs (Damascus Document)

The star is the Interpreter of the Law who shall come to Damascus; as it is written, A star shall come forth out of Jacob and a sceptre shall rise out of Israel (Num. xxiv, 17). The sceptre is the Prince of the whole congregation, and when he comes he shall smite all the children of Seth (Num. xxiv, 17).

— Damascus Document (CD) 7:18–21, trans. Geza Vermes. This is the most explicit ancient reading of the oracle. Where the Hebrew has a single image-pair, the sect’s interpreter (a pesher, “this is that”) resolves it into two coming figures: the star is the “Interpreter of the Law” (the priestly, teaching Messiah), and the sceptre is the “Prince of the whole congregation” (the royal, Davidic Messiah) who executes the oracle’s violence — “he shall smite all the children of Seth.” The reading maps the verse onto the community’s well-attested expectation of a pair of anointed ones and, remarkably, quotes the same “children of Seth” clause that a Christian Father would later apply to Christ alone.2

The oracle among the messianic proof-texts (Testimonia, 4Q175)

I see him but not now. I behold him but not near. A star shall come out of Jacob and a sceptre shall rise out of Israel; he shall crush the temples of Moab and destroy all the children of Sheth.

— Testimonia (4Q175), lines 9–13, trans. Geza Vermes. This single sheet gathers a short anthology of texts the sect read of the last days: the “prophet like Moses” (a conflated Deuteronomy 5 and 18), then Balaam’s star oracle (Numbers 24:15–17), then the blessing of Levi (Deuteronomy 33:8–11), then a curse on the rebuilder of Jericho. The sequence is itself an argument: a coming prophet, a coming king (Numbers), and a coming priest (Levi) — the star oracle placed deliberately in the royal-messianic slot of the catena, confirming that its collectors read it of the awaited ruler.3

The oracle prayed as a war-oracle (War Scroll, 1QM)

There shall come forth a star out of Jacob, a scepter shall rise out of Israel, and shall crush the forehead of Moab and tear down all sons of Sheth.

— War Scroll (1QM) 11:6–7, trans. Wise, Abegg & Cook. Here the same verse is not interpreted but invoked: in a battle-liturgy recalling how God delivered Goliath into David’s hand, the oracle is prayed as a promise of the eschatological victory over the nations. The War Scroll shows the third mode in which the community used the text — alongside the two-Messiah pesher of CD and the proof-text catena of the Testimonia — as a battle-cry of the final war, the Star of Jacob leading Israel against the sons of darkness.4

Notes

  • Why it matters. The scrolls are the decisive evidence against treating the messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 as a Christian retrojection. Before the Gospel, a Jewish community had already made the “Star out of Jacob” the language of its messianic hope — splitting it (CD), cataloguing it (4Q175), and praying it (1QM). This is the deep background of both the star of Bethlehem and the “Son of the Star” acclamation of Bar Kokhba.
  • One verse, two Messiahs. The CD pesher is the clearest ancient case of the star and sceptre being read as two figures rather than one — a possibility latent in the Hebrew that Christian reading (one Messiah, Christ) and the Bar Kokhba acclamation (one king) both collapse. The sect’s dual expectation of “the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel” is the frame; John J. Collins named his standard study of it The Scepter and the Star after this very verse.
  • Translations. The wording of the scrolls is translation-sensitive. The CD and 4Q175 quotations here follow Geza Vermes, The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English; the 1QM quotation follows Michael Wise, Martin Abegg & Edward Cook, The Dead Sea Scrolls: A New Translation — other editions render the same lines differently (e.g. “sons of Sheth” / “children of Seth” / “sons of pride”), so each block is pinned to its translator.
