Does the Bethlehem cave tradition rest on local memory, or on inference from cave-stables and Isaiah 33:16?

Disputed reading · on Where Was Christ Born? Cave, Stable, or House · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Origen, Eusebius of Caesarea

Related readings: Does Jerome’s report of an Adonis grove over the Bethlehem cave attest early Christian veneration of the site? · Was the Christian cave-birth borrowed from the cult of Mithras?

The passage

But when the Child was born in Bethlehem, since Joseph could not find a lodging in that village, he took up his quarters in a certain cave near the village; and while they were there Mary brought forth the Christ and placed Him in a manger, and here the Magi who came from Arabia found Him.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 78 (c. 160), source.1

And to this day the inhabitants of the place, who have received the tradition from their fathers, confirm the truth of the story by shewing to those who visit Bethlehem because of its history the cave in which the Virgin bare and laid her infant, as the prophecy says…

— Eusebius of Caesarea, Demonstratio Evangelica 7.2 (Ferrar trans.; written before Constantine’s basilica), source.2

The question

The Protoevangelium of James (c. 150) and Justin (c. 160) place the birth in a cave near Bethlehem, which neither Luke nor Matthew mentions; Origen (c. 248) and Eusebius (c. 320) report that the cave is shown to visitors by the people of the place. The question is whether this tradition preserves a location remembered at Bethlehem from near the events, or arose by inference — from Luke’s manger and the cave-stables of the Judaean hills, or from the Septuagint of Isaiah 33:16, which Justin cites for the cave. On the answer turns how far behind the second century the history of the site can be traced.

A birthplace remembered and shown at Bethlehem

The cave was a real place whose location the Bethlehem community kept and pointed out, from near the events into the fourth century.

Held by: Origen (Against Celsus, c. 248); Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica, c. 320); Fr. Lawrence Farley (Orthodox, No Other Foundation, 2015); Ken Dark (archaeologist, The Conversation, 2020; grants the cave’s pre-fourth-century importance, does not address memory from the events).3

Origen offers the cave to Celsus as evidence beyond Micah and the Gospels: “there is shown at Bethlehem the cave where He was born,” and “this sight is greatly talked of in surrounding places, even among the enemies of the faith.”4 Eusebius, before any church stood there, presents the showing as an inheritance received “from their fathers.” Farley reconstructs that inheritance in generations: “It does not take a very robust faith to believe that a family receiving news from local shepherds that they had heard angels declare that the Messiah had been born in a local cave would preserve that information as part of their family lore for several generations.” He reads Justin’s use of Isaiah as resting on a cave already known — “Justin’s far-fetched application of the cave in Is. 33:16 LXX is only likely given that he already knew that the birth was in a cave and thus read Isaiah in that light” — and notes that the Protoevangelium, “written about the same time, reflects the same tradition.”5 Justin states the cave as fact before he cites Isaiah; the Protoevangelium tells the cave story with no prophecy attached; and the later Palestinian writers who name the cave or manger, from Origen to Cyril and Jerome, ground Bethlehem on Micah 5:2 and do not repeat Justin’s Isaiah text.6 Dark argues from the building: the Church of the Nativity “was specifically designed to display the cave as the primary physical focus of the church,” and “the best interpretation of this layout is that the church and its altar were positioned because of the pre-existing religious importance of the cave.” With Justin and the Protoevangelium, he holds, the sources “attest that Bethlehem had at least one Christian holy place within a generation or two of the composition of the Gospel of John,” and the fourth-century builders “inherited – rather than created – a network of holy places.”7

A cave inferred from Luke’s manger, local stables, and prophecy

The cave entered the story by inference — Palestinian Christians picturing Luke’s manger in the kind of cave-stable they knew, with a Greek proof-text to hand — and the shown site records later belief rather than a remembered location.

Held by: Joan E. Taylor (Christians and the Holy Places: The Myth of Jewish-Christian Origins, 1993).8

Taylor’s book argues, in Farley’s summary, “that none of the holy sites on which Constantine built churches in the Holy Land were authentic.” For Justin she proposes, as Farley reports her, that a writer from Palestine, where caves served as stables, read a cave into Luke’s account of the manger without any received tradition behind it. Of Origen’s report she writes that “all his words really tell us is that the pagan people of Bethlehem believed that Jesus was born there. The probability is that the pagans arrived at this notion by an identification of Jesus with Adonis, not from any ancient tradition,” and she suggests a confusion of “Adonis” with the Syriac adawni, “my lord”: “It would perhaps have been natural for a Christian visitor from Jerusalem to ask the locals about a cave where ‘my lord’, adawni was born.”9 The one prophecy cited for the cave has one only in Greek: the Septuagint of Isaiah 33:16 has the righteous man dwell “in a high cave of a strong rock,” where the Hebrew reads “his place of defence shall be the munitions of rocks,” and Justin cites the Greek as “the sign which foreshadowed the cave.” ⚠ unverified: Taylor counts this proof-text among the things that could have supplied or reinforced the detail.10 The reading points to a precedent in later Nativity scenery: the ox and the ass, absent from the Gospels, were drawn from Isaiah 1:3 and the Septuagint of Habakkuk 3:2, which the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew cites by name.11 ⚠ unverified: Taylor sets the shown sites within a holy-places culture that took shape in the fourth century, with the Bar Kokhba war of 132–135 and the Constantinian re-founding of the sites disturbing any continuous local witness.12 On her account, the cave of the second-century texts records how Palestinian Christians pictured Luke’s manger, and the cave shown in the third and fourth centuries records what Bethlehem’s people and visitors had come to believe about it.
