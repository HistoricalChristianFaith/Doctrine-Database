Was the Christian cave-birth borrowed from the cult of Mithras?

Disputed reading · on Where Was Christ Born? Cave, Stable, or House · witness: Justin Martyr

Related readings: Does the Bethlehem cave tradition rest on local memory, or on inference from cave-stables and Isaiah 33:16? · Does Jerome’s report of an Adonis grove over the Bethlehem cave attest early Christian veneration of the site?

The passage

“When those who record the mysteries of Mithras say that he was begotten of a rock, and call the place where those who believe in him are initiated a cave… those who presided over the mysteries of Mithras were stirred up by the devil to say that in a place, called among them a cave, they were initiated by him.”

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 70, 78 (c. 160), source.1

The question

In the same discourse in which he reports the birth in a cave near Bethlehem, Justin names the cave in which worshippers of Mithras were initiated. The question is how that resemblance is to be explained: as a Mithraic imitation of Christian prophecy, as a borrowing of the cave into the Nativity story from Mithraism, or as two unrelated images in cults with different myths. No scholarly proponent of the borrowing thesis for the cave is named in the sources consulted for this page.

A Mithraic imitation of the prophets

The prophecies came first; the Mithraists, prompted by demons, copied them, so the Mithraic cave is a counterfeit of the cave the prophets foretold.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 70, 78, c. 160; First Apology 66).2

When the Mithraists say that their god “was begotten of a rock” and call their place of initiation a cave, Justin asks, “do I not perceive here that the utterance of Daniel, that a stone without hands was cut out of a great mountain, has been imitated by them, and that they have attempted likewise to imitate the whole of Isaiah’s words?” He then quotes Isaiah 33:16 in the Septuagint, “he shall dwell in the lofty cave of the strong rock,” and in chapter 78, after telling of the birth in the cave near Bethlehem, he returns to it as “the sign which foreshadowed the cave.” The Mithraic rites, on his account, were contrived by those who “were stirred up by the devil” to make a cave their place of initiation. He brings the same charge against the bread and cup of the Mithraic mysteries in the First Apology. For Justin the order is Daniel and Isaiah first, then the Mithraic copy, and the cave of the Nativity is the place where Isaiah’s sign was fulfilled.3

A cave taken over from Mithraism

The cave-birth, which the Gospels do not mention, came into the Christian story from the cult of Mithras, whose god was born from a rock and whose sanctuaries were caves.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

Rock and cave are at the centre of Mithraic imagery: the god is the petra genetrix, born from the rock, a scene carved in sanctuaries across the empire, and the sanctuaries themselves were built or fitted out as artificial caves, the spelaeum.4 The two cults spread through the second- and third-century Roman world side by side, among the same populations, and Justin, writing about 160 and raised in Palestine, sets the Mithraic cave directly beside the cave of the Nativity. The thesis draws on the model of Franz Cumont (1896–99), who held Roman Mithraism to be a lightly Romanised continuation of an ancient Iranian cult of Mithra, old enough on that account to have shaped early Christianity.5 On this reading, a mystery religion that worshipped its god in caves supplied the cave setting that Luke and Matthew lack.

Separate images in a Roman-era cult

Roman Mithraism arose in the same era as Christianity, and its rock-birth and initiation-cave are different images from an infant’s birth in a cave.

Held by: Manfred Clauss (The Roman Cult of Mithras, 2000; on the cult’s date and myth, not the Nativity cave); Roger Beck (The Religion of the Mithras Cult in the Roman Empire, 2006; likewise); Tim O’Neill (History for Atheists, 2016).6

Clauss and Beck treat the Roman mysteries as a creation of the Roman imperial period, first attested in the later first century AD and flourishing in the second and third, and set aside Cumont’s model of continuity with the Iranian Mithra.7 The Mithraic birth, as O’Neill describes it, is that of a god “born, fully formed and entirely adult, from a rock,” with no mother and no infancy, and “there is nothing in the Mithraic birth scenes to suggest this happened in ‘a cave.’” The cave Justin names is the spelaeum, where initiates were received, not a birthplace. O’Neill quotes Clauss on the figures who flank the rock-birth: “There are … no grounds for calling these two figures ‘shepherds’ in the wake of the Christian nativity story,” and he reports Beck calling the association of Mithras’ birth with December 25 “the hoariest of ‘facts’.”8 On this reading the two caves do different work in two cults of the same age — a hall for initiates on one side, a shelter for a newborn on the other — and the Nativity cave belongs to the Christian sources that report it, from the Protoevangelium and Justin onward.
