Does Jerome’s report of an Adonis grove over the Bethlehem cave attest early Christian veneration of the site?

Disputed reading · on Where Was Christ Born? Cave, Stable, or House · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Does the Bethlehem cave tradition rest on local memory, or on inference from cave-stables and Isaiah 33:16? · Was the Christian cave-birth borrowed from the cult of Mithras?

The passage

“From the time of Hadrian to the reign of Constantine — a period of about one hundred and eighty years — the spot which had witnessed the resurrection was occupied by a figure of Jupiter; while on the rock where the cross had stood, a marble statue of Venus was set up by the heathen… Even my own Bethlehem, as it now is, that most venerable spot in the whole world of which the psalmist sings: ‘the truth hath sprung out of the earth,’ was overshadowed by a grove of Tammuz, that is of Adonis; and in the very cave where the infant Christ had uttered His earliest cry lamentation was made for the paramour of Venus.”

— Jerome, Letter 58 to Paulinus 58.3 (Fremantle trans., c. 395), source.1

The question

Jerome, who lived at Bethlehem from 386, is the only surviving ancient writer to report a grove of Tammuz–Adonis at the cave. Justin, Origen, and Eusebius describe the cave without mentioning one, and Eusebius describes a pagan shrine built over the tomb of Christ at Jerusalem; Cyril (c. 350) recalls that at Bethlehem “a few years ago the place was woody.”2 The question is whether the grove was a pagan defilement of a place Christians already honoured, and so attests veneration of the cave before Constantine, or whether a pagan cult of Adonis at the spot came first and lies behind the identification of the cave as Christ’s birthplace.

A pagan defilement of a Christian holy place

The grove was planted over a place Christians already venerated, as part of a pagan effort to pollute their holy sites, and so it fixes the cave as a Christian site before Constantine.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 58, c. 395).3

Jerome sets the Bethlehem grove in a series with the figure of Jupiter on the place of the resurrection and the marble Venus on the rock of the cross, and he gives the motive in the sentence between them: “The original persecutors, indeed, supposed that by polluting our holy places they would deprive us of our faith in the passion and in the resurrection.” He supplies the Tammuz–Adonis equation himself, in his Commentary on Ezekiel at 8:14, where the women “mourning for Adonis” mourn the one “in the Hebrew and Syriac languages… called Thamuz.”4 The pattern he describes is attested at Jerusalem by Eusebius, who reports that “impious and godless persons” buried the cave of the resurrection under a raised platform and built over it “a gloomy shrine of lifeless idols to the impure spirit whom they call Venus,” which Constantine cleared to recover the tomb.5 Ken Dark, writing on that report, holds that since Hadrian “built temples at, or near, both the Jewish temple at Jerusalem and the most important Samaritan shrine at Mount Gerizim,” Eusebius’s account “may be more than late Roman speculation.”6 Jerome knew Bethlehem as a resident, and he gives the grove as an aside in a letter arguing that holiness does not depend on living in the Holy Land, with Psalm 85:11 applied to the town. On this reading a cult planted to pollute a holy place marks where that place was: the cave was a Christian site before the grove was set over it, and the cult Constantine’s builders cleared lay over a spot the church already honoured.7

An Adonis cult behind the identification of the cave

A grove of Tammuz–Adonis stood at Bethlehem, and the local belief that a “lord” was born in the cave arose from that cult, which Christians then took as a report of Christ’s birth.

Held by: Joan E. Taylor (Christians and the Holy Places, 1993).8

Taylor, as Farley quotes and reports her, takes the Tammuz grove to have stood in Bethlehem, and she sets it against Justin’s cave, which she reads as lying “somewhere nearby the village, but nevertheless outside it”; on that ground she suggests that Justin did not know the cave later shown in the town. Of Origen’s report that the cave was talked of among non-Christians she writes: “all his words really tell us is that the pagan people of Bethlehem believed that Jesus was born there. The probability is that the pagans arrived at this notion by an identification of Jesus with Adonis, not from any ancient tradition.” She proposes a confusion of the name “Adonis” with the Syriac adawni, “my lord”: “It would perhaps have been natural for a Christian visitor from Jerusalem to ask the locals about a cave where ‘my lord’, adawni was born.”9 Jerome’s own word for the cult, “Tammuz,” comes from Ezekiel 8:14, the verse whose mourning women he glosses as mourners for Adonis; ⚠ unverified: Taylor reads the name in Letter 58 as taken from that text.10 On this reading the order runs the other way from Jerome’s: the cave was a place of lamentation for Adonis, the pagans of Bethlehem spoke of a lord born there, and Christian enquirers heard in that local talk the birthplace of Christ, which the fourth-century church then built over.
