Jerome on the birthplace of Christ

Dates: c. 395 (lived beside the Bethlehem grotto c. 386–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome settled at Bethlehem in 386 and lived out his last thirty-odd years beside the cave of the Nativity, which he treats throughout as the real birthplace. He is, however, a mixed witness: the site he keeps, the apocryphal embroidery he throws out. He reports that from Hadrian to Constantine a pagan grove sacred to Tammuz/Adonis overshadowed the very cave (Letter 58, c. 395); he shows Paula entering the grotto and beholding the manger (Letter 108, c. 404); he laments in a Nativity homily that the clay manger had been replaced with one of silver; yet in Against Helvidius (c. 383) he flatly rejects the apocryphal midwife/Salome legend and reads Luke’s manger-and-inn straight. He grounds Bethlehem, like Origen and Eusebius, on the prophecy of Micah.

Claims & evidence

The Adonis grove over the cave (Letter 58.3, c. 395)

Writing to Paulinus of Nola to argue that holiness does not depend on living in the Holy Land, Jerome recalls that for some 180 years, from the emperor Hadrian (c. 135) to Constantine, the great Christian sites lay buried under pagan cult — a statue of Jupiter on Golgotha, a marble Venus on the rock of the cross — and Bethlehem was no exception:

Even my own Bethlehem, as it now is, that most venerable spot in the whole world of which the psalmist sings: “the truth hath sprung out of the earth,” was overshadowed by a grove of Tammuz, that is of Adonis; and in the very cave where the infant Christ had uttered His earliest cry lamentation was made for the paramour of Venus.

— Letter 58 (to Paulinus) 58.3, source.1 Jerome takes the Tammuz cult to be the Greek Adonis — the identification he spells out in his Commentary on Ezekiel at 8:14, where the women “mourning for Adonis” are, he says, mourning for the one “in the Hebrew and Syriac languages… called Thamuz.”2 The datum is double-edged, and Jerome reads it in the direction that helps: the pagans, meaning to defile the Christian holy place, in fact marked it — the sacred grove sat exactly where the cave was, so the cult that tried to bury the memory instead preserved its location. This is the load-bearing text for the argument that Hadrian’s desecration inadvertently attests an already-known site. It is worth weighing carefully: Jerome is the only ancient source for a Bethlehem Adonis grove, and the wider theory that the Tammuz cult originated the holy-place tradition has been challenged by modern scholarship (notably Joan Taylor), so the inference is suggestive rather than decisive — adjudicated on its own argument page (assessment: plausible; single-sourced, and weaker than the cave-memory case it supports).

Paula at the cave and the manger (Letter 108.10, c. 404)

In the memorial letter for his patron Paula, Jerome describes her pilgrimage to Bethlehem — where she and he had both come to live — and her devotion at the grotto:

After this she came to Bethlehem and entered into the cave where the Saviour was born. Here, when she looked upon the inn made sacred by the virgin and the stall where the ox knew his owner and the ass his master’s crib… she protested in my hearing that she could behold with the eyes of faith the infant Lord wrapped in swaddling clothes and crying in the manger, the wise men worshipping Him, the star shining overhead, the virgin mother, the attentive foster-father, the shepherds coming by night…

— Letter 108 (the Life of Paula) 108.10, source.3 For Jerome the cave, the stall, and the manger are one venerated place, shown and prayed at — the same pointed-out grotto that Origen and Eusebius had already reported, now the site of the Constantinian basilica beside which Jerome and Paula founded their monasteries.

The clay manger and the silver crib (Nativity homily, c. 400)

Preaching on Luke 2 — most likely in Bethlehem itself — Jerome wishes he could see the original manger, and mourns that piety had swapped the humble clay crib for one of silver:

O, if only I were permitted to see that manger in which the Lord lay! Now, as an honor to Christ, we have taken away the manger of clay and have replaced it with crib of silver, but more precious to me is the one that has been removed.

— Homily on the Nativity of the Lord, trans. Marie Liguori Ewald, The Homilies of Saint Jerome (Fathers of the Church 57, 1966), pp. 221–28.4 The complaint is itself a witness: by Jerome’s day a physical manger was on display in the grotto and had been grand enough to be re-cased in silver — the shrine that Constantine had adorned “with silver and gold” (so Eusebius) had reached the crib. Jerome preferred the poverty of the clay.

The cave he keeps, the midwife he throws out (Against Helvidius, c. 383)

Jerome venerates the grotto, but he will not have the apocryphal accretions that had grown around it. Arguing against Helvidius that Mary remained a virgin, he reads Luke’s account plainly and dismisses the miraculous midwife/Salome episode of the Protoevangelium tradition as fable:

No midwife assisted at His birth; no women’s officiousness intervened. With her own hands she wrapped Him in the swaddling clothes, herself both mother and midwife, “and laid Him,” we are told, “in a manger, because there was no room for them in the inn”; a statement which, on the one hand, refutes the ravings of the apocryphal accounts, for Mary herself wrapped Him in the swaddling clothes, and on the other makes the voluptuous notion of Helvidius impossible, since there was no place suitable for married intercourse in the inn.

— The Perpetual Virginity of Blessed Mary (Against Helvidius) 10, source.5 This is what makes Jerome a discriminating witness rather than a credulous one. He keeps the cave and the manger — places he could visit — but discards the legendary embroidery (the attending midwives, Salome’s withered hand) that the Protoevangelium of James had wrapped around the same scene. The grotto he treats as memory; the apocryphal wonders he treats as “ravings.”

Notes

  • Jerome is a mixed witness: he affirms the cave/manger site and prays there, yet rejects the apocryphal midwife legend and reads Luke straight. The two attitudes are consistent — he distinguishes the locatable place from the legendary decoration attached to it.
  • The Letter 58 Adonis-grove notice is the key datum for the argument that Hadrian’s pagan desecration inadvertently attests the Bethlehem site. It is single-sourced (Jerome alone) and its wider “Tammuz-origin” form is disputed (Joan Taylor), so it is suggestive, not decisive — weighed on its own argument page (assessment: plausible).
  • Like Origen and Eusebius, Jerome anchors Bethlehem on Micah, and does not derive the cave from Isaiah’s “lofty cave” the way Justin had — a datum for the queued isaiah-cave-lxx argument.
  • Jerome appears on this wiki also as a witness on other timelines (he is a principal in the Origenist controversy and an infant-baptism witness, among others). He lived beside the Bethlehem grotto from 386 until his death, so he could speak of the site as a neighbour.
