The Bordeaux Pilgrim on the birthplace of Christ

Dates: c. 333 · Wikipedia
Summary: The anonymous pilgrim from Bordeaux, whose Itinerarium Burdigalense of 333 is the oldest surviving Christian travel diary of the Holy Land, records that at Bethlehem, “where the Lord Jesus Christ was born,” a basilica had been raised “by Constantine’s command.” He fixes the site and the new monument on the ground within a few years of its building — but his terse itinerary names the birthplace and the church, not a cave. His value is as the earliest datable pilgrim witness that Bethlehem was an established destination, distinct from the theologians who describe the grotto.

Claims & evidence

The basilica at Bethlehem, “by Constantine’s command”

From Jerusalem as you go to Bethlehem, four miles above the road on the right hand side is the tomb where Rachel, wife of Jacob, is buried. From there two miles on the left-hand side is Bethlehem, where the Lord Jesus Christ was born; there a basilica was made by Constantine’s command.

— Itinerarium Burdigalense (Bordeaux Pilgrim), trans. Andrew S. Jacobs, source.1 The notice is a bare road-log entry, ticked off between Rachel’s tomb and the burials of Ezekiel, Asaph, Job, Jesse, David and Solomon that follow it, but it is precisely dated: the pilgrim travelled in 333, only a few years after Constantine’s church over the grotto was begun (c. 327–333). It is the earliest firsthand, independently datable confirmation that the basilica Eusebius describes was in fact standing at Bethlehem, and that Christian travellers were already coming to see it.

Notes

  • What it does and does not attest. The pilgrim confirms the place (Bethlehem, “where the Lord Jesus Christ was born”) and the monument (a basilica built at Constantine’s command), locating both on the ground in 333. He does not mention the cave or manger — his itinerary is a clipped list of stations and distances, not a description of what was shown inside. So he corroborates the Constantinian building that Eusebius reports, and the standing of Bethlehem as a pilgrim destination, without independently attesting the grotto as such.
  • Pilgrim attestation reaches the fourth century, not the first. Like Egeria a half-century later, the Bordeaux Pilgrim shows a living, organised cult at Bethlehem by the 330s — a datum for the breadth of the tradition, not for eyewitness proof of the birthplace. It bears on the queued cave-authentic-memory question by showing how quickly and firmly the site was fixed after Constantine, but a 331-year gap still separates it from the Nativity.
