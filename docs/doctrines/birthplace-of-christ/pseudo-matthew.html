The Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew on the birthplace of Christ

Dates: c. 600–650 · Wikipedia
Summary: This Latin infancy gospel, though late, reworks much older material (the second-century Protoevangelium of James above all) and is the text that fuses the two older pictures into the scene Western imagination has kept ever since: Mary bears the child in a cave, then on the third day moves to a stable and lays him in the manger, where an ox and an ass adore him. The cave-and-stable hybrid and the two beasts — neither of them in the canonical Gospels — are its enduring legacy.

Claims & evidence

The birth in the dark cave, lit from within

And he commanded the blessed Mary to come down off the animal, and go into a recess under a cavern, in which there never was light, but always darkness, because the light of day could not reach it. And when the blessed Mary had gone into it, it began to shine with as much brightness as if it were the sixth hour of the day. … The light from God so shone in the cave, that neither by day nor night was light wanting as long as the blessed Mary was there.

— Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew 13, source. The birth itself happens in the cave, exactly as in the Protoevangelium, whose “great light” in the cave this passage develops. The stable has not yet entered the story.

On the third day, from cave to stable — and the ox and the ass

And on the third day after the birth of our Lord Jesus Christ, the most blessed Mary went forth out of the cave, and entering a stable, placed the child in the stall, and the ox and the ass adored Him. Then was fulfilled that which was said by Isaiah the prophet, saying: The ox knows his owner, and the ass his master’s crib. The very animals therefore, the ox and the ass, having Him in their midst, incessantly adored Him. Then was fulfilled that which was said by Abacuc the prophet, saying: Between two animals you are made manifest. In the same place Joseph remained with Mary three days.

— Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew 14, source. Here is the join that the earlier witnesses never make: the family keeps both the cave (from the apocryphal and Palestinian tradition) and the stable-with-manger (drawn from Luke), by having Mary move from the one to the other on the third day. The result is the familiar grotto-and-stable of later Nativity art. The ox and the ass, absent from every canonical account, are imported here straight from prophecy — Isaiah 1:3 (“the ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master’s crib”) and the Old Latin / Septuagint reading of Habakkuk 3:2 (“between two animals thou art made known”), where the Hebrew has “in the midst of the years.”

Notes

  • Date and character: the Latin compilation is usually dated to the early-to-mid seventh century, but it is a reworking of far older matter — chiefly the Protoevangelium of James (c. 150) and the Infancy Gospel of Thomas — so its traditions are much older than its composition. It is the West’s principal channel for the apocryphal infancy material.
  • The two beasts grow from proof-text, not from any narrative memory: Isaiah 1:3 and Habakkuk 3:2 (LXX / Old Latin) supply the ox and the ass, just as Justin had earlier read the cave itself out of Isaiah 33:16 (“the lofty cave of the strong rock”). Prophecy generating scenery is a recurring engine in this tradition — a point that bears on whether the underlying cave is remembered or constructed.
  • Where the canonical Gospels give a manger and a “house,” and the earliest localizing sources give a cave, this text is the first to hold cave, stable, and manger together in one sequence — the source of the “cave and stable” picture and of the Western crib scene later dramatized by Francis of Assisi.
