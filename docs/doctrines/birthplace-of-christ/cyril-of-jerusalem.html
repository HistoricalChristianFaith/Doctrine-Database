Cyril of Jerusalem on the birthplace of Christ

Dates: c. 350 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril, catechizing the baptismal candidates of Jerusalem around 350 — a few miles from Bethlehem and while Constantine’s basilica over the grotto still stood new — invokes “the place of the manger, which received the Lord” as a real, locally-known spot that would testify against the heretics. He appeals throughout to sites his hearers already know, grounding the birthplace on Micah, not on Isaiah’s “lofty cave.” His notice is an independent Palestinian voice for the Bethlehem memory, contemporary with the basilica.

Claims & evidence

“The place of the manger, which received the Lord” will testify

And if the heretics gainsay the truth, the Holy Ghost shall convict them… the place of the manger, which received the Lord, shall put them to shame. The shepherds, who then received the good tidings, shall bear witness; and the host of the Angels who sang praises and hymns… the Temple into which He was then carried up on the fortieth day… and Symeon who then took Him up in his arms, and Anna the prophetess who was present.

— Catechetical Lectures 12.32, source.1 Cyril lists “the place of the manger” among the witnesses that will rise against those who deny Christ’s true birth — alongside the shepherds, the angels, the Temple, Symeon, and Anna. The rhetoric is a courtroom of witnesses, but the manger’s place is set among concrete things, a spot with a location that could “put them to shame.” Like the Bordeaux Pilgrim a generation earlier, he names the manger and its place without, here, spelling out a cave; what he attests is the venerated Bethlehem site itself.

An appeal to places his Jerusalem hearers already know

But now hear the place of the promise, as Micah says, And thou, Bethlehem, house of Ephrathah… out of thee shall come forth unto Me a ruler, to be governor in Israel… But assuredly as to the places, thou being an inhabitant of Jerusalem, knowest also beforehand what is written in the hundred and thirty-first psalm. Lo! we heard of it at Ephrathah, we found it in the plains of the wood. For a few years ago the place was woody.

— Catechetical Lectures 12.20, source.2 This is the local-catechesis angle. Cyril is not arguing from a distance: he reminds his candidates that, “being an inhabitant of Jerusalem,” each of them already knows the Bethlehem sites for himself, even noting a detail of recent memory — “a few years ago the place was woody.” The Bethlehem tradition here is not something he must establish but something he can take for granted with an audience that lives beside it. That makes him an independent fourth-century Palestinian voice for the site, distinct from Origen and Eusebius at Caesarea — a point that bears on the queued cave-authentic-memory argument, whose strength turns partly on how many independent local witnesses attest the spot.

Bethlehem grounded on Micah, not on Isaiah’s cave

As the quotation above shows, Cyril anchors the birthplace on Micah 5:2 — “the place of the promise, as Micah says” — supported by Psalm 132 (131 LXX) and Habakkuk, not on the “lofty cave of the strong rock” of Isaiah 33:16 that Justin had read into the story.3 He thus joins Origen, Eusebius, and later Jerome in resting Bethlehem on Micah rather than on the Isaiah proof-text — a datum for the queued isaiah-cave-lxx argument, since it shows the Palestinian witnesses attesting the site without leaning on Justin’s reading of Isaiah to justify it.

Notes

  • The Catechetical Lectures were delivered to baptismal candidates at Jerusalem around 348–350, while Cyril was still a presbyter; he became bishop of Jerusalem soon after. The city lies only a few miles from Bethlehem, and Constantine’s Church of the Nativity over the grotto (dedicated 339) was standing new in his hearers’ lifetime — the same monument Eusebius describes and the Bordeaux Pilgrim saw being built.
  • Cyril attests the manger’s place and grounds Bethlehem on Micah, but in this lecture he does not use the word “cave” — so he witnesses to the venerated site and its liturgical memory, not (in so many words) to the grotto’s form. On that he is like the Bordeaux Pilgrim and unlike Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome, who name the cave outright.
