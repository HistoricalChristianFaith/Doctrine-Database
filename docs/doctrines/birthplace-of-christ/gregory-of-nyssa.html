Gregory of Nyssa on the birthplace of Christ

Dates: c. 386 (c. 335–395) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching on the feast of the Nativity, Gregory of Nyssa — the Cappadocian bishop and brother of Basil the Great — names both the cave and the manger and reads each as a figure: the cave is “the lightless and subterraneous life of mankind” that Christ enters, the manger the “abode of irrational animals” where the Bread of Life is set out so that the irrational may “become rational.” He is a Greek-Eastern witness confirming the cave-and-manger reading of the Nativity in the Cappadocian church of the late fourth century — not a shown-site witness (he preaches in Cappadocia, not at Bethlehem) but a preacher for whom the cave is the settled, unremarkable furniture of the story, taken as given and turned to allegory.

Claims & evidence

The cave: the darkened life of mankind that Christ enters

But the glad tidings of the Angels exhort us to bring our discourse back to Bethlehem and to behold the mysteries in the cave. What is this? A Child wrapped in swaddling clothes and lying in a manger; and she who remains a Virgin after giving birth, the incorrupt Mother, cares for her offspring… When you see the cave in which the Master is born, understand the lightless and subterraneous life of mankind, into which He enters, appearing to those sitting in darkness and the shadow of death.

— Homily on the Nativity of Christ, source.1 Gregory takes the cave for granted — it is simply where the Angels’ tidings send the preacher’s eye, “the mysteries in the cave” — and then allegorizes it: the dark, underground grotto stands for the “lightless and subterraneous life of mankind” that the incarnate Word descends into, “appearing to those sitting in darkness and the shadow of death” (Luke 1:79; Isaiah 9:2). The cave is not argued for or defended here as a fact; it is the assumed scene of the birth, so familiar that its whole interest for Gregory has become symbolic.

The manger: the beasts’ feeding-trough where the Bread of Life is set out

The manger is the abode of irrational animals, in which the Word is born, that the ox may know his Owner and the ass his Master’s crib; the ox stands for him who is subject to the Law, while the ass, that beast of burden, stands for him who is weighed down with the sin of idolatry… For this reason, therefore, the Bread of Life, which descended from Heaven, is set forth in the manger, which is the dwelling-place of irrational animals, so that those irrational animals might partake of rational food and become rational.

— Homily on the Nativity of Christ, source.2 The manger draws the same double reading. It is the beasts’ feeding-trough — “the abode of irrational animals” — and the ox and the ass of Isaiah 1:3 (“the ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master’s crib”) become the two peoples: the Jew “subject to the Law” and the Gentile “weighed down with the sin of idolatry.” Because the manger holds fodder for irrational beasts, Gregory reads the child laid in it as “the Bread of Life, which descended from Heaven,” set out so that the irrational “might partake of rational food and become rational.” He then makes the manger the meeting-place of the two: the Lord, lying “between the ox and the ass,” has “broken down the middle wall of partition” (Ephesians 2:14) to make of the two “one new man.”3

Notes

  • Like Ephraem the Syrian, Gregory is not a witness to a shown cave, as Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome are in Palestine. He preaches in Cappadocia and never appeals to a visited grotto; his interest is homiletic and allegorical. What his sermon attests is that by the 380s the cave-and-manger picture was the settled, unquestioned scene of the Nativity in the Greek East — taken as read and mined for meaning, not asserted against a rival.
  • Uniquely among the witnesses gathered here, Gregory holds the cave and the manger together and reads both allegorically — the cave as humanity’s dark condition, the manger as the beasts’ trough turned into the table of the Bread of Life. The ox and the ass, absent from the canonical Gospels, he supplies (as later Nativity art would) from Isaiah 1:3, the same proof-text the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew would draw them from; but where Pseudo-Matthew narrates the beasts as history, Gregory keeps them frankly figural.
  • The homily In diem natalem Christi (Greek text in Migne, Patrologia Graeca 46.1128–1149) is a genuine work of Gregory, delivered on the feast of Christ’s Nativity; the c. 386 date is a timeline anchor within his episcopate rather than a certain year. A critical text and study appears in A. Radde-Gallwitz, ed., Gregory of Nyssa’s Doctrinal Works (Oxford, 2018); the English quoted here follows the translation published at the Mystagogy Resource Center.
