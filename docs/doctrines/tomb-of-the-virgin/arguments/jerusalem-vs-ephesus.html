Did Mary end her earthly life at Jerusalem or at Ephesus?

Disputed reading · on The Tomb of the Virgin (Church of the Sepulchre of St. Mary)

The passage

Behold thy mother! And from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home

— John 19:27 (KJV), source.1

And in that valley is a basilica of Saint Mary, where they say her house was, in which also she was taken up from her body.

— The Piacenza Pilgrim, Itinerarium §17 (c. 570), on the valley of Gethsemane, source.2

The question

The New Testament records neither Mary’s death nor her burial. Two places have been venerated as the end of her earthly life: a rock-cut tomb in the Kidron valley at Gethsemane, Jerusalem, and a house on a hill above Ephesus; the question is which place the sources point to, and it is distinct from the question of the Assumption, which has its own timeline. Among the sources used for this page, the Ephesus reading’s holders are Tillemont, known here only through a report of his reading of a letter of the Council of Ephesus (431), a visionary, and the priests who located the house; a modern academic defence of it was not located.

Mary died and was buried in Jerusalem, at Gethsemane

Mary ended her life in Jerusalem and was laid in the Kidron-valley tomb now venerated as hers.

Held by: John of Damascus (Greek, homilies on the Dormition preached at Gethsemane, c. 730); the Euthymiac History (c. 550–750), which gives the account to Juvenal of Jerusalem at Chalcedon (451); the Catholic Encyclopedia (art. “Tomb of the Blessed Virgin Mary”); Lino Cignelli (Franciscan biblical scholar, 2005).3

The last notice of Mary in the New Testament places her among the disciples at prayer in Jerusalem (Acts 1:14). The earliest narratives of her end, the Liber Requiei and the Syriac Six Books, tell of her burial and place the grave in the Kidron valley at Gethsemane. The Catholic Encyclopedia holds that “The apocryphal works of the second to the fourth century are all favourable to the Jerusalem tradition,” and Cignelli traces them to “a Judaeo-Christian prototype, clearly written within the mother church of Jerusalem some time during the second century, and, in all probability, composed for liturgical use right at the Tomb of Our Lady.”4 A church over the tomb is attributed to Patriarch Juvenal in the fifth century; the Piacenza Pilgrim saw “a basilica of Saint Mary” there c. 570, and Arculf (c. 680) a round two-storey church holding “the empty stone sepulchre of the holy Mary, where she was once laid to rest.”5 In the Euthymiac History Juvenal tells the Emperor Marcian that Mary was buried in Gethsemane and that her tomb, when opened, held only her grave-clothes. The site has been venerated in the same place through its Byzantine, Crusader and later rebuildings; Bellarmino Bagatti excavated there in 1972, and his conclusion, as See the Holy Land summarizes it, was “that the place where Mary had been buried was clearly located in a cemetery used during the first century.”6 On this reading the tomb in the Kidron is where Mary was laid, and it is venerated empty because, in the tradition’s own account, she was taken up from it.

Mary lived out her life near Ephesus, in John’s care

John took Mary with him to Asia, and she ended her life in a house built for her near Ephesus.

Held by: Tillemont (on the Council of Ephesus’s letter of 431, as reported by Meistermann, 1912) ⚠ unverified; Anne Catherine Emmerich (Catholic Augustinian nun and visionary, d. 1824; visions published by Clemens Brentano, 1852); the Abbé Julien Gouyet (French Catholic priest, 1881); Fathers Poulin and Jung (Lazarist missionaries of Smyrna, 1891).7

The reading starts from the cross: Jesus entrusted Mary to the beloved disciple, who “took her unto his own home,” and that disciple, John, is the apostle associated with Ephesus; Mary, on this reading, went where he went. Barnabas Meistermann (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that “Modern writers hold, however, that Mary died and was buried at Ephesus,” and gives as their documentary ground the letter sent in 431 by the members of the Council of Ephesus to the clergy of Constantinople, which says that Nestorius “reached the city of Ephesus where John the Theologian and the Mother of God, the Holy Virgin, were separated from the assembly of the holy Fathers”. Tillemont, he reports, completed the elliptical phrase with “have their tombs”, and “He is followed by a few writers.”8 Emmerich described a house that the Apostle John had built for Mary at Ephesus, where, in her account, Mary lived to the end of her life, and gave details of its location and of the surrounding topography. On 18 October 1881 Gouyet, following those descriptions in Brentano’s book, found a small stone building on a mountain overlooking the Aegean and the ruins of Ephesus, and held it to be the house Emmerich described and the place where Mary had lived her final years; in 1891 Poulin and Jung, using the same guide, found the building again. That discovery, the Wikipedia article on the house reports, “revived and strengthened a Christian tradition dating from the 12th century, ‘the tradition of Ephesus’.”9 The Catholic Church has not pronounced on the house’s authenticity, but it has received pilgrims there with papal favour: Leo XIII blessed the first pilgrimage in 1896, Pius XII made the house a Holy Place in 1951, and Paul VI (1967), John Paul II (1979) and Benedict XVI (2006) visited it.9 On this reading the hillside house above Ephesus is where Mary, in John’s keeping, spent her last years.

The sources date the cult, not the burial

The venerated sites record where Christians came to honour Mary’s end, which is a later matter than where it happened.

Held by: Joan E. Taylor (Christians and the Holy Places, 1993).10

Taylor reads the whole apparatus of venerated Holy Land sites, the Tomb of the Virgin among them, as a Christian growth of the fourth century and later rather than a memory carried continuously from the first century. A venerated place, on this reading, is evidence of when and where a cult took hold. On this reading, the Kidron tomb attests the age and continuity of the Jerusalem cult of Mary’s Dormition rather than the place of her burial.
