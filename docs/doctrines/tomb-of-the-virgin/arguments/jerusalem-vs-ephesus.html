Jerusalem or Ephesus: where did Mary end her life?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the Kidron tomb-tradition is by far the older and better-attested of the two claims, and the only one that names a tomb at all; that the Kidron is the authentic site of Mary’s dormition and burial is the more probable reading, short of proven because it still rests on non-apostolic tradition.

Related arguments: the tomb’s authenticity — see also Does the excavated tomb’s first-century date show it is Mary’s?

The claim

Two places compete for the memory of Mary’s last days. Jerusalem holds that she died and was buried in the Kidron valley at Gethsemane, where her tomb — found empty — has been venerated for some fifteen centuries. A rival tradition holds that Mary went with the Apostle John to Ephesus (on the strength of John 19:26–27) and ended her life there, at the hillside house rediscovered in the nineteenth century as the “House of the Virgin” (Meryém Ana Evi). The question here is not whether Mary was assumed — that is weighed on the Assumption timeline — but which of the two sites has the better claim to be where her earthly life ended.

The evidence for the Kidron

It is early and continuous. The Gethsemane tomb is localized in the fourth-to-sixth-century Dormition narratives, built over by a church in the fifth century, and described by the Piacenza Pilgrim as “a basilica of Saint Mary” already in c. 570 — then by Arculf (c. 680) as a round two-storey church with Mary’s “empty stone sepulchre.”1 The site has been held in unbroken continuity of place ever since, through Byzantine, Crusader, and modern rebuildings, the rock-cut crypt surviving even Saladin’s demolition of the Crusader church above it in 1187.

It is materially a genuine ancient tomb. The 1972 excavation identified the venerated chamber as part of a real first-century Jewish necropolis — so the Kidron claim is anchored to an authentic Second-Temple-period burial ground, not a devotional fiction.2

Ephesus makes no tomb-claim. The decisive asymmetry: the Ephesus tradition concerns a house, a place of residence, and there is no ancient tradition of Mary’s tomb at Ephesus. Even that residence-tradition is modern — it rests on the visions of Anne Catherine Emmerich (d. 1824), published by Clemens Brentano in 1852, and the ruins were located only in 1881; during Emmerich’s beatification the Vatican found Brentano had added “creative liberties” inseparable from her own words.3 So on the specific question of a burial-place, Jerusalem has an ancient, continuous, materially-attested site and Ephesus has none.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Against Ephesus, the Kidron wins decisively: it is centuries older, attested by independent pilgrims, materially a first-century tomb, and the only site that claims a grave. The Ephesus case for Mary’s death there is thin — a single strand of nineteenth-century private revelation resting on a scriptural inference (John took Mary “unto his own home,” and John is traditionally tied to Ephesus) that even at its strongest concerns her residence, not her end.

What is overstated. Winning against Ephesus is not the same as being proven. The Kidron identification rests on the Dormition apocrypha — narratives the church itself branded spurious — and the earliest datable localization is already some four centuries after Mary’s lifetime; the tomb is venerated as empty, so there is nothing in it to test. The New Testament’s silence about where Mary died leaves open, in bare principle, that her life ended somewhere neither tradition names. The Kidron is the best-supported answer, not a demonstrated one.4

Assessment

Plausible. That the Kidron at Gethsemane is the authentic site of Mary’s dormition and burial is the more probable reading and clearly the stronger of the two traditions — ancient, continuous, materially anchored, and unrivalled by an Ephesus tradition that names no tomb and rests on modern private revelation. It falls short of sound because the identification is carried by non-apostolic tradition rooted in apocrypha, the attestation begins centuries after the event, and Scripture’s silence leaves the ending formally open. Prominence and antiquity of the cult are established; the identity of the occupant is not.
