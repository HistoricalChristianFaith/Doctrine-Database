Clement of Alexandria on Matthias

Dates: c. 150–215 (Clement of Alexandria) · Stromata
Summary: Clement, writing around A.D. 200, is the earliest extant author after the New Testament to mention Matthias — and he does so by quoting a book that bore the apostle’s name, the Traditions of Matthias, a collection of ascetic sayings he cites with respect but not as Scripture. In the same Stromateis Clement records a second notice, that some identified the tax-collector Zacchaeus with “Matthias,” a sentence whose name the English translation renders “Matthew.”

Claims & evidence

The Traditions of Matthias: “Wonder at what is before you”

Three times Clement quotes a work he calls the Traditions (Paradōseis) of Matthias. The first is an epistemological maxim he sets at the head of knowledge:

The beginning of knowledge is wondering at objects, as Plato says is in his Theaetetus; and Matthew exhorting in the Traditions, says, “Wonder at what is before you;” laying this down first as the foundation of further knowledge.

— Stromateis 2.9. (The Ante-Nicene Fathers translation prints “Matthew” here, but the work Clement names is the Traditions of Matthias — one instance of the variation between the two names noted below.)

The ascetic teaching: fight and abuse the flesh

The second citation gives the Traditions their characteristic ascetic note. Clement is contrasting what he presents as the self-mastery of Nicolaus with the licence the Nicolaitans claimed from him, and adduces Matthias to the same effect:

It is said that Matthias also taught that one should fight the flesh and abuse it, never allowing it to give way to licentious pleasure, so that the soul might grow by faith and knowledge.

— Stromateis 3.4. The saying sets the mastery of the flesh toward the end that “the soul might grow by faith and knowledge.” Clement cites Matthias approvingly; he also notes that the Basilidians, whom he counts among the heretics, claimed Matthias as a secret authority. Eusebius (c. 325) later lists a “Gospel of Matthias” among the works cited by the heretics (see the Eusebius page).

The Zacchaeus notice — and the “Matthias / Matthew” variant

In Book 4 Clement retells the story of the repentant chief tax-collector who promised fourfold restitution, and names him with an aside:

It is said, therefore, that Zaccheus, or, according to some, Matthew, the chief of the publicans, on hearing that the Lord had deigned to come to him, said, “Lord, and if I have taken anything by false accusation, I restore him fourfold;” on which the Saviour said, “The Son of man, on coming to-day, has found that which was lost.”

— Stromateis 4.6. The Ante-Nicene Fathers translation reads “Matthew,” but the Greek text runs Zakchaion toinun, hoi de Matthian phasi — “Zacchaeus, or, as some say, Matthias.” The sentence is the earliest extant notice of an identification of the apostle chosen by lot with the publican Zacchaeus of Jericho. How the identification has been read is set out on the identity page.

Reception and debate

  • The sayings and their attribution. Clement attests that by c. 200 a body of ascetic sayings circulated under Matthias’s name. W. Schneemelcher’s New Testament Apocrypha treats the Traditions among the second-century apocrypha (see the timeline; and the Eusebius page on the “Gospel of Matthias”).
  • The Matthias/Matthew variation. Twice in these passages the name varies — “Matthew” in the ANF at 2.9, “Matthew” for the Greek “Matthias” at 4.6. The two names are close in Greek, and the same variation appears in the manuscripts of the cannibal-city romance.
  • Cited, not canonised. Clement quotes many books he does not treat as Scripture. The Traditions of Matthias are one such: ascetic counsel he cites with approval, and a text the Basilidians had also claimed. Eusebius, about a century later, lists a “Gospel of Matthias” among works “to be cast aside.”
