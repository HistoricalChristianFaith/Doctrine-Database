Was Matthias the same man as Zacchaeus, or as Barnabas?

Disputed reading · on Matthias · witness: Clement of Alexandria

The passage

It is said, therefore, that Zaccheus, or, according to some, Matthew, the chief of the publicans…

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromateis 4.6, Ante-Nicene Fathers translation. The Greek runs Zakchaion… hoi de Matthian phasi, “Zacchaeus, or, as some say, Matthias,” where the English translation prints “Matthew.” In Acts the candidate set beside Matthias is “Joseph called Barsabas, who was surnamed Justus” (Acts 1:23), and Barnabas’s given name is also Joseph (Joses in the KJV, Acts 4:36).1

The question

Acts names Matthias once, at his election, and two later identifications fuse him with a better-known figure: with Zacchaeus, the chief publican of Luke 19, and with Barnabas. The question is whether either identification preserves a memory of Matthias under a second name, or whether Matthias is a person distinct from both. No modern scholarly proponent of either identification is named in the sources surveyed here.

Matthias was Zacchaeus the publican, under a second name

In brief: the repentant chief publican of Jericho and the apostle chosen by lot are one man.

Held by: those whom Clement of Alexandria reports in Stromateis 4.6 (c. 200) as saying the publican was “Matthias.”2

The identification has a second-century root. Clement, an early witness writing at Alexandria around 200, records that “some” gave the converted chief of the publicans the name Matthias, and the Greek of his sentence reads Matthian. The pairing answers to what each figure lacks in the Gospel record: Zacchaeus is a vivid convert whose later life Luke does not tell, Matthias an apostle whose election Acts narrates but who has no other story. Joined, the publican who promised fourfold restitution becomes the man numbered with the Eleven. Zacchaeus also held a place in later church memory as a leader: the Apostolic Constitutions (7.46) name “Zacchæus, who was once a publican” first bishop of Caesarea in Palestine. On this reading Clement’s “some” transmit an early tradition about who the twelfth apostle was before his election.3

Matthias, Barnabas and Zacchaeus were three different men

In brief: Matthias is the man Acts 1 describes, distinct from Zacchaeus, from Barnabas and from his fellow candidate Joseph Barsabbas.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

Eusebius of Caesarea (Ecclesiastical History 1.12, c. 325) reports Barnabas and Matthias separately among those said to be of the Seventy; he does not address either identification.4 Acts gives the twelfth apostle a defining qualification: he had to have “companied with us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John” (Acts 1:21–22). On this reading that qualification identifies Matthias as a disciple from the time of John’s baptism. Acts introduces Joseph Barsabbas (1:23) and Joseph Barnabas (4:36) as two men in two scenes. Eusebius’s report of those said to be among the Seventy (Ecclesiastical History 1.12.1–3) names Barnabas, Matthias and Matthias’s fellow candidate separately. On this reading Matthias is the eyewitness of Acts 1, chosen by lot and otherwise unrecorded, and the other names attached to him arose as Matthias was confused with figures who bore similar names.5

Matthias was Barnabas

In brief: the apostle chosen by lot is the Joseph surnamed Barnabas, Paul’s companion.

Held by: the Clementine Recognitions (I.60; c. 3rd–4th c.).6

The Recognitions make the identification in their account of the apostles answering the people in the Temple one after another: “After him Barnabas, who also is called Matthias, who was substituted as an apostle in the place of Judas, began to exhort the people that they should not regard Jesus with hatred, nor speak evil of Him.” The names around the election give the context: one candidate is “Joseph called Barsabas”, and Barnabas is “Joses, who by the apostles was surnamed Barnabas” (Acts 4:36), a Joseph given a second name by the apostles. On this reading the apostle of the lot and the apostle of the Gentile mission with Paul are one disciple known under more than one name.7
