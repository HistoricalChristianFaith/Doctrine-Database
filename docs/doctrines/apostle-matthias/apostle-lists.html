The apostle-lists and the Golden Legend on Matthias

Dates: c. 6th–13th century (apostle-lists; Golden Legend c. 1260) · Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Matthias”
Summary: A mission and a death for Matthias, which the New Testament does not report, are supplied by the later catalogues, which give differing accounts. The Synopsis ascribed to Dorotheus and to Nicephorus sends Matthias to “Ethiopia,” by which they mean Colchis on the eastern Black Sea, among barbarians and man-eaters, and bury him at Sebastopolis. On how he died they differ: stoned and beheaded at Jerusalem, crucified in Colchis, or dead of old age. The medieval West kept the stoned-and-beheaded story, gave him the axe as his emblem, and located his relics at Trier — the only claimed apostolic tomb north of the Alps.

Claims & evidence

The mission field: “Ethiopia,” that is, Colchis

The list-literature places Matthias in the Caucasus. In the tradition transmitted under the name of Dorotheus of Tyre and echoed by Nicephorus, he “preached the Gospel to barbarians and man-eaters in the interior of Ethiopia, at the harbour of the sea of Hyssus, at the mouth of the river Phasis. He died at Sebastopolis, and was buried there, near the Temple of the Sun.” The “Ethiopia” meant is not the African kingdom but the old idea of a Colchian “Ethiopia” east of the Black Sea — the region of modern Georgia, the Phasis being the Rioni, Sebastopolis modern Sukhumi. Man-eaters also appear in the cannibal-city romance.

The death: three or four accounts

The sources give several accounts of how Matthias died. The most widespread Western tradition holds that he was stoned by the Jews at Jerusalem and then beheaded with an axe — “stoned, and… afterwards beheaded” — which is why medieval and later art puts an axe or halberd in his hand as the instrument of his death. The Greek Synopsis, keeping him in Colchis, has him crucified there. Pseudo-Hippolytus’s On the Twelve Apostles, by contrast, gives him no martyrdom at all: “Matthias… fell asleep” — died of old age at Jerusalem and was buried there. The accounts are thus of a death by stoning and beheading, by crucifixion, or in old age.

The relics: Trier, Rome, and a fortress in Georgia

The relic-cult multiplied his graves. The great Western claim is Trier, where the Benedictine abbey of St. Matthias holds what it presents as the apostle’s tomb, the only claimed burial of an apostle north of the Alps. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports the Helena tradition as “It is said that St. Helena brought the relics of St. Matthias to Rome, and that a portion of them was at Trier.” The Wikipedia article “Matthias the Apostle” reports that the remains are claimed to have been brought to Italy through Helena, with part interred at the abbey of Santa Giustina in Padua and the rest at the abbey of St. Matthias in Trier. A portion was also claimed at Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome ⚠ unverified; another burial-tradition keeps his grave at the Gonio fortress on the Georgian Black-Sea coast, consistent with the Colchis mission. Bollandus, in the Acta Sanctorum, doubted whether the “Matthias” relics venerated at Rome belonged to the apostle rather than to a later Matthias, bishop of Jerusalem (c. 120), “whose history would seem to have been confounded with that of the Apostle” (Catholic Encyclopedia).

Reception and debate

  • The catalogues. These catalogues record how Matthias was remembered and commemorated in the churches that used them. The shared treatment of the apostle-lists is set out on the apostles hub.
  • The axe. Matthias’s iconographic emblem — an axe or halberd — depicts the stoned-and-beheaded tradition, as the saw does for Simon.
  • “Ethiopia.” The Synopsis locates its “Ethiopia” at the Phasis and the sea of Hyssus. The earliest extant attestation of the placement, among the sources surveyed here, is in the list-literature.
