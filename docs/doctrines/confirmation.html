Confirmation & Chrismation (the spiritual seal)

What follows the baptismal water? The earliest extant descriptions, from about 200, add an anointing with oil (the “chrism”) and the laying on of a hand, which their authors connect with the gift of the Holy Spirit. The passages its witnesses cite include the post-baptismal hand-laying by which the Samaritans and the Ephesians received the Spirit (Acts 8:14–17; 19:1–7), Paul's language of God having “sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit” (2 Corinthians 1:21–22; Ephesians 1:13), and Hebrews 6:2, which lists “baptisms” and “laying on of hands” among the foundations. This page records the witnesses earliest first: Tertullian, the Apostolic Tradition and Cyprian; Cyril of Jerusalem on the chrism and Ambrose on the “spiritual seal”; Innocent I's letter of 416 reserving the signing of the forehead to bishops; and the medieval Latin theologians and councils. Today the Latin Catholic Church confers confirmation, ordinarily by the bishop, on those baptized as infants years after their baptism, while the Eastern churches confer chrismation at baptism, the presbyter anointing with myron a bishop has consecrated.1 How that difference arose is itself disputed (see Disputed readings). The Council of Florence (1439) listed confirmation among seven sacraments, and the Council of Trent (1547) defined it as “a true and proper sacrament.”

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian gives the earliest Latin account of what follows the font: “when we have issued from the font, we are thoroughly anointed with a blessed unction” — the chrism from which Aaron, and Christ, are called “anointed” — and then “the hand is laid on us, invoking and inviting the Holy Spirit through benediction,” whereupon “willingly descends from the Father that Holiest Spirit.” He narrates the anointing and the hand-laying as one sequence with the font.2

The Apostolic Tradition (c. 215)

The church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome gives a detailed liturgy of the seal: after the font the neophyte is “anointed by the presbyter with the oil of thanksgiving,” then comes to the bishop, who lays on his hand praying that God “send into them thy grace” (the versions of this prayer differ on whether it asks for the Spirit; see detail), “pouring the oil of thanksgiving… and putting it on his forehead,” and “signing them on the forehead.” The anointings come in two stages, the presbyter’s first and then the bishop’s forehead-seal, within one ceremony that continues from the font to the eucharist. (Bradshaw, Johnson and Phillips read the work as composite; it is dated here by the traditional ascription.)3

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 255)

Cyprian, in the rebaptism controversy, describes the church's standing practice and its warrant in the Samaritans of Acts 8: the baptized “are brought to the prelates of the Church, and by our prayers and by the imposition of hands obtain the Holy Spirit, and are perfected with the Lord's seal.” Elsewhere he writes that one “is not born by the imposition of hands when he receives the Holy Ghost, but in baptism.”4

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril's catechesis On the Chrism expounds the seal: the Unction received after the font is “the anti-type of that wherewith Christ was anointed; and this is the Holy Ghost,” so that the baptized “are properly called Christs.” Like the eucharistic bread after the invocation, the holy ointment “is no more simple ointment” but “Christ's gift of grace” — applied to the forehead, ears, nostrils, and breast.5

Jerome (c. 380)

In Jerome’s Dialogue Against the Luciferians, the Luciferian speaker appeals to “the laying on of hands after baptism and then the invocation of the Holy Spirit” as a custom of the churches with proof in Acts, and Jerome’s speaker Orthodoxus grants that it is “the practice of the Churches” for the bishop to visit those baptized by presbyters and deacons far from the towns, “and by the laying on of hands to invoke the Holy Ghost upon them.” Orthodoxus gives as authority for its reservation to the bishop that “after our Lord's ascension the Holy Ghost descended upon the Apostles,” and describes the practice as kept “more by way of honouring the episcopate than from any compulsory law,” for it would be pitiable “if the Holy Ghost descends only at the bishop's prayer.” He grounds the reservation in church order, lest “we shall have as many schisms in the Churches as there are priests.”6

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose instructs the newly baptized on the “spiritual seal”: after the anointing “remember that you received the seal of the Spirit” — the sevenfold gift of wisdom, understanding, counsel, strength, knowledge, godliness, and holy fear — for “God the Father sealed you, Christ the Lord strengthened you, and gave the earnest of the Spirit in your heart.”7

Pope Innocent I (416)

Innocent I, answering Decentius of Gubbio, rules that presbyters may anoint the baptized with chrism, “but with chrism which has been consecrated by a bishop; they are not permitted, however, to sign the forehead with that same oil, which signing pertains to bishops only, when they confer the Spirit Paraclete.” He grounds the rule in “ecclesiastical custom” and in Peter and John giving the Spirit to the baptized Samaritans of Acts 8. It is the earliest extant papal text reserving the forehead-sealing to bishops.8

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500)

Pseudo-Dionysius, in The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, counts the “Initiation of Muron” — the consecration of the ointment (chrism) — among his sacred rites, a “perfecting Service of the same rank” as the eucharist; its “perfecting unction” gives “the abiding of the supremely Divine Spirit.” In his Eastern rite the hierarch who baptizes seals with the myron in one continuous initiation.9

The Venerable Bede (c. 710)

Bede, commenting on the apostles laying hands on the baptized Samaritans (Acts 8:17), restates Innocent I's rule almost word for word: because Philip “was one of the seven” deacons and not an apostle, he could not confer the Spirit, “for this is reserved to bishops alone.” Presbyters may “anoint the baptized with chrism, but only with chrism consecrated by the bishop; however, they are not to anoint the forehead with the same oil, which is reserved to bishops alone when they impart the Paraclete Spirit.”10

Peter Lombard (c. 1155)

Peter Lombard, in Book IV of the Sentences, gives confirmation its own distinction (dist. 7) after listing seven sacraments in dist. 2. Its power is “the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength (ad robur), who in baptism was given for remission (ad remissionem)”; the rite is reserved to “the highest priests” (bishops), presbyters being permitted to touch the baptized on the breast “but not to sign the forehead with chrism”; it is in one sense of “greater veneration” than baptism — given by worthier ministers, on a worthier part of the body, bestowing “a greater increase of virtues” — “although baptism avails more for remission”; and, like baptism and orders, it is not to be repeated.11

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Thomas Aquinas, in the twelve articles of Summa Theologiae III, q.72, treats confirmation article by article. Confirmation is a distinct sacrament, instituted by Christ “not by bestowing, but by promising it” in the promise of the Paraclete (a.1); its matter is chrism of oil and balsam consecrated by a bishop (a.2–3); it imprints a sacramental character, an indelible “spiritual power… in respect of… the spiritual combat with the enemies of the Faith” (a.5); its effect is an increase of sanctifying grace “for growth and stability in righteousness” (a.7); and its proper minister is the bishop, in whom the apostles' place is held (a.11). He reads the rite through the figure of the soldier of Christ: the seal is placed on the forehead so that “neither fear nor shame may hinder him from confessing the name of Christ.”12

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence, in Exsultate Deo, the decree of union with the Armenians, defines confirmation's matter, form, effect and minister: its matter is “the chrism prepared from the oil… and from the balsam… blessed by a bishop,” its form “I sign thee with the sign of the cross and confirm thee with the chrism of salvation,” its effect that “the Holy Spirit is given for strength… so that the Christian might boldly confess the name of Christ,” and its ordinary minister the bishop.13

The Council of Trent (1547)

The seventh session of the Council of Trent, in its canons on confirmation, defined confirmation as “a true and proper sacrament,” not “an idle ceremony,” and anathematized the claim that “of old it was nothing more than a kind of catechism”; it anathematized those who deny virtue to “the sacred chrism”; and anathematized anyone who says “that the ordinary minister of holy confirmation is not the bishop alone, but any simple priest soever.”14
