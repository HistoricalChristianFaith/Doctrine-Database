Confirmation & Chrismation (the spiritual seal)

What happens to the newly baptized after the water? From the earliest centuries the church added a second gesture — an anointing with fragrant oil (the “chrism”) and the laying on of a hand — understood to seal the Christian with the Holy Spirit. Its scriptural warrants are the post-baptismal hand-laying by which the Samaritans and the Ephesians received the Spirit (Acts 8:14–17; 19:1–7) and Paul's language of God having “sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit” (2 Corinthians 1:21–22; Ephesians 1:13), with Hebrews 6:2 pairing “baptisms” and “laying on of hands” among the foundations. This page traces the rite earliest witness first — from the undivided initiation of Tertullian and Cyprian, through the developed chrism-theology of Cyril of Jerusalem and the “spiritual seal” of Ambrose, to the parting of the ways: the Latin West, following Innocent I, separated the sealing into a later rite reserved to the bishop and named it confirmation; the Greek East kept it joined to baptism as chrismation, the presbyter anointing with myron the bishop consecrates. The rite is defined as one of the seven sacraments at Florence (1439) and dogmatized at Trent (1547).

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian gives the earliest Latin account of what follows the font: “when we have issued from the font, we are thoroughly anointed with a blessed unction” — the chrism from which Aaron, and Christ, are called “anointed” — and then “the hand is laid on us, invoking and inviting the Holy Spirit through benediction,” whereupon “willingly descends from the Father that Holiest Spirit.” He already distinguishes the two rites the West would later gather into confirmation, but narrates them as one seamless initiation with baptism.1

The Apostolic Tradition (c. 215)

The Roman church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus gives the earliest detailed liturgy of the seal: after the font the neophyte is “anointed by the presbyter with the oil of thanksgiving,” then comes to the bishop, who lays on his hand praying that God “send into them thy grace,” “pouring the oil of thanksgiving… and putting it on his forehead,” and “signing them on the forehead.” The two-stage anointing — presbyter first, then the bishop’s forehead-seal — is already the shape Innocent I would later reserve; the rite runs as one continuous initiation with baptism. (Authorship and date are disputed — many now read the work as composite; it is dated here by its early-third-century horizon.)2

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 255)

Cyprian, in the rebaptism controversy, describes the church's standing practice and its warrant in the Samaritans of Acts 8: the baptized “are brought to the prelates of the Church, and by our prayers and by the imposition of hands obtain the Holy Spirit, and are perfected with the Lord's seal.” Baptism gives the new birth; the hand-laying, the Spirit — two moments held together but distinguished, the second reserved to the bishop.3

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril's catechesis On the Chrism gives the fullest early theology of the seal: the Unction received after the font is “the anti-type of that wherewith Christ was anointed; and this is the Holy Ghost,” so that the baptized “are properly called Christs.” Like the eucharistic bread after the invocation, the holy ointment “is no more simple ointment” but “Christ's gift of grace” — applied to the forehead, ears, nostrils, and breast.4

Jerome (c. 380)

Jerome, defending the rite against the Luciferians, affirms “the laying on of hands after baptism and then the invocation of the Holy Spirit” as an apostolic custom of the churches — yet explains its reservation to the bishop with rare candour: it is kept “more by way of honouring the episcopate than from any compulsory law,” for it would be pitiable “if the Holy Ghost descends only at the bishop's prayer.” The reservation guards church order, lest “we shall have as many schisms in the Churches as there are priests.”5

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose gives the classic Latin statement of the “spiritual seal”: after the anointing “remember that you received the seal of the Spirit” — the sevenfold gift of wisdom, understanding, counsel, strength, knowledge, godliness, and holy fear — for “God the Father sealed you, Christ the Lord strengthened you, and gave the earnest of the Spirit in your heart.” His word “strengthened” (confirmavit) supplies the very keynote of the later Western rite.6

Pope Innocent I (416)

Innocent I, answering Decentius of Gubbio, draws the line that would define the Western rite: presbyters may anoint the baptized with chrism, “but with chrism which has been consecrated by a bishop; they are not permitted, however, to sign the forehead with that same oil, which signing pertains to bishops only, when they confer the Spirit Paraclete.” It is the earliest papal reservation of the forehead-sealing to the episcopate.7

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500)

Pseudo-Dionysius, in the first systematic treatise on the church's rites, counts the “Initiation of Muron” — the consecration of the ointment (chrism) — among his sacred mysteries, on a par with baptism and the eucharist; its “perfecting unction” gives “the abiding of the supremely Divine Spirit.” In his Eastern rite the hierarch who baptizes seals with the myron in one continuous initiation.8

The Venerable Bede (c. 710)

Bede, commenting on the apostles laying hands on the baptized Samaritans (Acts 8:17), carries the Western reservation into the early-medieval Latin church, restating Innocent I almost word for word: because Philip “was one of the seven” deacons and not an apostle, he could not confer the Spirit, “for this is reserved to bishops alone.” Presbyters may “anoint the baptized with chrism, but only with chrism consecrated by the bishop; however, they are not to anoint the forehead with the same oil, which is reserved to bishops alone when they impart the Paraclete Spirit.”9

Peter Lombard (c. 1155)

Peter Lombard, in Book IV of the Sentences — the theology textbook of the medieval West — gives confirmation its own distinction (dist. 7) in the sacramental list he had just fixed at seven, and settles the questions the schools would inherit. Its power is “the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength (ad robur), who in baptism was given for remission (ad remissionem)”; the rite is reserved to “the highest priests” (bishops), presbyters being permitted to touch the baptized on the breast “but not to sign the forehead with chrism”; it is in one sense of “greater veneration” than baptism — given by worthier ministers, on a worthier part of the body, bestowing “a greater increase of virtues” — “although baptism avails more for remission”; and, like baptism and orders, it is not to be repeated. Because the Sentences became the set text every theologian lectured on, this placement handed the rite, in textbook form, to Aquinas and the councils.10

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Thomas Aquinas, in the twelve articles of Summa Theologiae III, q.72, gives confirmation its mature scholastic theology — the account Florence and Trent would draw on directly. Confirmation is a distinct sacrament, instituted by Christ “not by bestowing, but by promising it” in the promise of the Paraclete (a.1); its matter is chrism of oil and balsam consecrated by a bishop (a.2–3); it imprints a sacramental character, an indelible “spiritual power… in respect of… the spiritual combat with the enemies of the Faith” (a.5); its effect is an increase of sanctifying grace “for growth and stability in righteousness” (a.7); and its proper minister is the bishop, in whom the apostles' place is held (a.11). Reading the whole rite through the soldier-of-Christ figure — the forehead-seal so that “neither fear nor shame may hinder him from confessing the name of Christ” — he supplies the strengthening-for-bold-confession keynote (robur) that the councils would echo.11

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence, in Exsultate Deo, gave confirmation its first full ecumenical definition: its matter is “the chrism prepared from the oil… and from the balsam… blessed by a bishop,” its form “I sign thee with the sign of the cross and confirm thee with the chrism of salvation,” its effect that “the Holy Spirit is given for strength… so that the Christian might boldly confess the name of Christ,” and its ordinary minister the bishop.12

The Council of Trent (1547)

Against the Reformers — who read the apostolic hand-laying as an extraordinary, unrepeatable event — the seventh session of the Council of Trent defined confirmation as “a true and proper sacrament,” not “an idle ceremony” or “a kind of catechism”; anathematized those who deny virtue to “the sacred chrism”; and declared that “the ordinary minister of holy confirmation is… the bishop alone.”13

Arguments

Related arguments — the minister

The rite's sharpest division is not over what it does but who may do it — the East–West split already visible in the timeline. The Latin West, following Innocent I, reserved the forehead-seal to the bishop and made “the bishop alone” its ordinary minister; the Greek East kept the seal joined to baptism, the presbyter anointing with chrism the bishop consecrates. Two genuinely advocated readings, each ancient and self-consistent, best read together and weighed adversarially on their own pages.

Must confirmation be reserved to the bishop? (the bishop-reserved reading) — contested

The Western discipline — Acts 8 (the apostles, not the deacon Philip, conferred the Spirit), Innocent I, Florence, Trent's “bishop alone.” Strong as a rule of church order rooted in a real apostolic pattern, and Jerome names its purpose (guarding against schism); but pressed into a claim that the bishop is the necessary minister it collides with the unbroken Eastern presbyteral practice (granted valid even by Rome), with Jerome's own “honour, not compulsory law,” and with the Latin church's dispensations of the rite to priests. Disputed between the traditions, no clear winner (contested).

May a presbyter be the proper minister of confirmation? (the presbyteral reading) — contested

The Eastern practice: the presbyter who baptizes chrismates at once, with myron the bishop or patriarch consecrates — the older, undivided shape (Cyril, Pseudo-Dionysius), and a valid seal even by the reckoning of the church that reserves the rite to bishops. Strong on validity; but whether the presbyter is the proper minister, or only an extraordinary one acting with the bishop's chrism, is disputed against the Western reservation and the Acts 8 pattern. Strong on validity, unresolved on ordinariness (contested).

Related arguments — the scriptural warrant

The rite claims two scriptural pillars, and each is tested on its own page. The first is the narrative two-step of Acts — the baptized Samaritans on whom the apostles later lay hands so they receive the Spirit (8:14–17), and the Ephesian disciples on whom Paul lays hands after baptism (19:1–7). The second is Paul's language of the “seal” and “earnest” of the Spirit (2 Corinthians 1:21–22; Ephesians 1:13), from which the rite took its very names (chrism, the signaculum spirituale, “the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit”). Whether either proves confirmation a rite distinct from baptism, conferring a further grade of grace, is weighed adversarially.

Do Acts 8 and 19 warrant confirmation as a distinct sacrament? — contested

The post-baptismal hand-laying is really in the text and soundly grounds an ancient practice of sealing; but the leap to a distinct standing sacrament is contested — Chrysostom reads the Acts gift as the extraordinary “Spirit of miracles,” Acts gives no uniform ordering of Spirit and baptism, and the sacrament's distinctness is later theology, not narrative (contested).

Does the “seal” and “earnest” of the Spirit warrant a distinct sealing-rite? — weak

The seal / anointing / earnest language is genuinely the rite's own vocabulary — it gave the chrism-sealing its names, and Ambrose reads the neophyte's sealing straight out of 2 Corinthians 1. But as proof of a rite distinct from baptism it does not hold: the texts name no separate sealing moment, predicate the seal of all believers as already accomplished, and are read by the weight of the fathers — the chrismating East included — as the gift of the Spirit in initiation itself, with the “earnest” an eschatological pledge rather than a liturgical act. A distinctly weaker peg than the Acts hand-laying (weak).

Is confirmation a distinct sacrament, or the completion of baptism / a human ceremony? (the Reformed denial) — contested

The Reformers' positive counter-thesis, distinct from the proof-text arguments above: confirmation is not a distinct sacrament instituted by Christ. Calvin (Institutes IV.19) argues the apostolic hand-laying conferred the extraordinary Spirit of the apostolic age and “ceased… only for a time,” so the medieval rite is a human addition — even “a noted insult to baptism” — best restored as a catechetical profession of the child's faith; the Thirty-Nine Articles rank it among the “commonly called Sacraments” that are not sacraments of the Gospel. Judged by the Reformed rule (a sign instituted by Christ in the Gospel) the denial is strong — there is no dominical institution, and even Aquinas grounds it only on a promise; but as a total account it overreaches, treating an ancient, unbroken sealing (the chrismating East above all) as a medieval fiction and reading “insult” into a rite the tradition never set against baptism. The dispute turns on the definition of “sacrament” itself, with no neutral arbiter (contested).
