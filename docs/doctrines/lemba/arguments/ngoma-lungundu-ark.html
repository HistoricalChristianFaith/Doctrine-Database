Is the Lemba ngoma lungundu the Ark of the Covenant?

Type: historical argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the functional parallels are real, the lineal-descent claim is not. That the Lemba sacred drum played an Ark-like role in the migration story is a genuine and long-noticed comparison; but the stronger thesis — that the biblical Ark was itself a drum-like object and that this drum is its physical descendant — rests on a radical minority reading of the Ark, on functional parallels that are generic to sacred war-palladia across Africa and the Near East, and on an identification the Lemba themselves do not make · advanced by Tudor Parfitt, The Lost Ark of the Covenant (2008), narrated on the Lemba timeline (the 2008 block).

This page weighs a claim about an object and its history, not about the Lemba community’s tradition or dignity. That the ngoma lungundu is a real and revered sacred drum, and that the Lemba are its hereditary carriers, is not in question here; what is weighed is only the narrower historical claim that it is the Ark of the Covenant or its lineal descendant. Nothing in the assessment bears on the depth or sincerity of the tradition.

The claim

the ngoma [the drum] is a descendant of the wooden ark of Deuteronomy made by Moses as mentioned in the Talmud and other commentaries.

— Tudor Parfitt, in a private communication reported by Magdel le Roux, “Ngoma Lungundu: An African Ark of the Covenant” (2009). Parfitt’s book presses the thesis in its fullest form: that the Ark was not the gold-plated chest of the Exodus description but an older, drum-like ritual object — “the dwelling place of God, carried… forbidden to touch the ground and connected with death, fire, smoke and noise” — and that the Lemba ngoma lungundu, which he rediscovered in a Harare museum storeroom, is “the last thing on earth in direct descent from the Ark of Moses.” The reading folds two distinct claims together: a radical reinterpretation of what the biblical Ark was, and a claim of physical descent from it to this particular drum.1

Who advances it

The comparison is older than Parfitt. The Lutheran missionary Harald von Sicard, who spent his career among the Lemba, was “possibly the first scholar to draw definite parallels between the Ark and the ngoma lungundu”; the very title of his 1952 study, Ngoma lungundu: Eine afrikanische Bundeslade (“an African Ark of the Covenant”), states the equation. He judged that the Lemba, an elite priestly group, had carried “the concept of the Ark of the Covenant and concomitant related Semitic customs” southwards into Africa, perhaps under the influence of the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast. Tudor Parfitt took the tradition further — back to the Arabian Peninsula and to a claim of literal descent — in The Lost Ark of the Covenant (2008), building on Philip R. Davies’s argument that ancient Israel knew more than one Ark and that the term overlapped with the ephod. The fullest scholarly treatment of the parallels is Magdel le Roux’s 2009 essay, which lays the two traditions side by side in detail — and then declines the descent claim.2

The evidence for

The steelman is stronger than a first hearing suggests, and honesty requires setting it out. First, the functional parallels are real and numerous. As le Roux documents, the ngoma and the Ark share a striking cluster of roles: both were carried and never allowed to touch the ground or be touched improperly; both were borne by a hereditary priestly caste (the Kohathite sons of Levi for the Ark; the priestly Buba clan for the ngoma); both held sacred objects inside; both went before the people to show the way and to signal where to camp; both were carried into battle as a war-weapon and guarantor of victory; both were associated with a divine voice, with fire, and with a pillar of flame; and for both, loss would mean calamity. Second, the etymological and textual case that the Ark itself may have been simpler and older than the Exodus chest. Deuteronomy 10 describes a plain wooden box of acacia, with no gold or cherubim; the priestly, gold-plated description in Exodus, on the view Parfitt adopts from modern scholarship, was written when the Temple dominated Israelite life and the older object had to be dignified to fit it. Parfitt presses further that the Hebrew aron could be linked to roots meaning “to make a ringing sound,” and that the Ark’s regular association with trumpets, noise, and processions leaves open a musical or percussive character. Third, the physical object. Parfitt reports that the ngoma he examined in Harare carries “unusual” interlaced handles and an interwoven side-pattern that he connects to Davies’s suggestion that the ephod’s casing decorated a drum. This wiki does not treat the survival of a sacred object as impossible merely because it would be remarkable; the question is what the evidence shows.3

Adversarial assessment

The parallel role holds as a comparison; the descent claim does not follow from it. That two sacred palladia are carried, kept from the ground, borne by priests, and taken to war is a real resemblance — but it is exactly the resemblance one expects between any two sacred war-objects, and it does not single out a line of descent. As the Zimbabwean historian Rob Burrett objects, “wooden drums — ceremonial drums and war drums with great powers similar to those attributed to the ark — are an integral part of African culture”; the ngoma’s traits are the common inheritance of the region, not a fingerprint of Sinai. And the form is wrong: the Ark is everywhere described as a box, coffer, or chest, while the ngoma is a single-headed drum. Le Roux, the parallels’ most careful advocate, concedes the point flatly: “There have never been any suggestions that the Ark was a drum.”4

The Lemba themselves do not make the identification — and the drum is not even primarily theirs. This is the decisive weakness. After extensive fieldwork le Roux reports that “the Lemba themselves never make the connection between the ngoma lungundu and the Ark of the Covenant,” and that she “could not find a single oral tradition among the Lemba or any written source which indicates a link between the ngoma and the Ark of the Covenant”; in her judgement “the link to the Ark of the Covenant was only suggested later by Jewish groups or missionaries.” The equation is thus an outsider’s reading, originating with the missionary Von Sicard, not a native tradition — the opposite of the Sena-migration story, which the Lemba do tell of themselves. Worse for the descent claim, the earliest references make the ngoma a Venda object that the Lemba merely carried: “the Venda people possessed it, the Lemba carried it, and it is still a Venda object today.” A drum owned by a Bantu people and only serviced by the Lemba is a poor candidate for a relic transported by a lost tribe out of Israel.5

The surviving object is late, and the reconciliation is a chain of unattested rescues. A splinter from the drum radiocarbon-dates to about AD 1300–1350 — probably the oldest surviving wooden object in southern Africa, but some two thousand years after Moses. Parfitt bridges the gap with Lemba lore that the original ngoma destroyed itself (traditions place a fire in 1770) and was rebuilt, so that the surviving drum is only “the son of the original.” This is not itself refuted by the wiki’s openness to the miraculous: the point is not that a relic could not survive, but that dating the actual object gives the claim no support, and the descent is carried entirely by an unverifiable oral chain of destructions-and-remakings that could be told of any old drum. Two further quiet difficulties: the rabbinic tradition treats a drum as susceptible to uncleanness (it is sat upon, and covered with animal skin), making it an unlikely vessel for the tablets; and Parfitt himself, pressed on rival explanations, conceded that theories counter to his own are “wholly plausible.”6

The radical premise carries too much weight. The whole edifice depends on first accepting that the biblical Ark was really a drum — a minority conjecture built from a possible etymology, the plain wooden box of Deuteronomy 10, and Davies’s multiple-Arks reading. Each step is defensible in isolation and none is mainstream; stacked into a claim that this Harare drum descends from Moses’s Ark, the conjunction becomes very improbable. The functional parallels that begin the case are the honest strength; everything Parfitt builds on top of them is conjecture beyond the evidence.7

Assessment

Weak. Taken as a comparison — that the ngoma lungundu functioned in Lemba and Venda memory much as the Ark did in Israel’s — the observation is real, old, and worth making, and it keeps the claim off the floor of unsupported. Taken as Parfitt takes it — that the biblical Ark was a drum-like object and that this particular drum is its lineal descendant — it does not hold on its best case. The parallels are the kind any two sacred war-palladia share and single out no line of transmission; the object is a Venda drum the Lemba only carried; the surviving artifact dates a millennium and more too late and is rescued only by an unverifiable “son of the original” tradition; and, most tellingly, the identification is an outsiders’ thesis that the Lemba themselves have never made. Even the parallels’ fullest scholarly champion, le Roux, treats the two as kindred myths rather than as an object and its descendant, and Parfitt himself grants that rival readings are “wholly plausible.” It is the least secure leg of the whole Lemba case, and its weakness neither confirms nor refutes the better-supported claim of a real Middle-Eastern paternal ancestry weighed elsewhere.
