Did the ancestral Lemba build Great Zimbabwe?

Type: historical argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the stonework is the work of ancestral Shona-speakers, dated and stratified into a continuous local sequence; the “Lemba built it” thesis is entangled with the long-discredited colonial “Semitic builders” trope and does not hold on its best case. What survives is much smaller and separable: a real Lemba presence in the trade world Great Zimbabwe belonged to, and a modest, genuinely-entertained possibility that Lemba craftsmen took part as masons in an already-African city — not that they raised it · advanced on the Lemba timeline.

The verdict here is about who built a monument, not about the Lemba’s dignity or the reality of their ancestry. Rating the building claim weak takes nothing from the well-attested Middle-Eastern male line the Lemba carry (weighed separately under Are the Lemba descended from an ancient Israelite/Jewish community?) or from their real, centuries-long role as traders and metalworkers across southern Africa. The point is narrower: the case that their ancestors were the builders of Great Zimbabwe rests on a racial premise the archaeology refutes.

The claim

They claim that one of their clans, the Tovakare, were the actual builders of Zimbabwe. They even call them Tovakare Muzimbabwe, which means ‘the ones that built Zimbabwe.’

— the Lemba tradition as reported by NOVA/PBS, “Ancient Origins?” The Lemba are, in Tudor Parfitt’s words, particularly insistent among the region’s peoples that their ancestors raised the great stone enclosure. Two very different claims travel under this banner and must be weighed apart: a maximal claim — that a Semitic people (the ancestral Lemba, or the lost northern race behind them) built Great Zimbabwe because the local Africans could not have — and a minimal claim — that Lemba craftsmen participated as masons and traders in a city that was otherwise an African achievement. The maximal claim is the historic thesis and the one this page is titled for; the minimal claim is the most that survives scrutiny.1

Who advances it

The claim has two quite separate lineages, and conflating them is the whole trouble. The Lemba’s own oral tradition is the older and the more modest: local traditions that the early Lemba built the stoneworks were recorded as far back as William Bolts (1777) and again by nineteenth-century travellers, and the Lemba say their ancestors were skilled masons contracted by tribal kings. This strand runs on through the Zimbabwean archaeologist Ken Mufuka and the historian Tudor Parfitt, both of whom allow that the Lemba (or the Venda) may have participated in the building — not that they were its sole authors.2

The colonial “Semitic builders” trope is the other lineage, and the poisoned one. From Karl Mauch (1871), who declared the ruins the work of the Queen of Sheba and a lintel-beam Lebanese cedar carried by Phoenicians, through J. Theodore Bent (1891–93), who settled on a “Semitic race … of Arabian origin,” European writers refused to credit Africans with the stonework and cast about for a northern, non-black builder. In the twentieth century Robert Gayre — founder of the race-science journal Mankind Quarterly — revived the case, arguing that the Shona had never built in stone and crediting instead the ancestors of the present-day Lemba as a partly-Semitic trader stock. It is this second lineage, grafting the Lemba onto a racial theory, that makes “the Lemba built Great Zimbabwe” sound stronger and more sinister than the Lemba’s own comparatively humble tradition.3

The evidence for

Stated at its strongest, the case has four legs. First, the Lemba tradition itself is specific and old: a named clan, the Tovakare, glossed “the ones that built Zimbabwe,” and a memory recorded independently since the eighteenth century that the ancestors were the masons of the great walls. Second, the Lemba were genuinely a trading and metal-working people — “for hundreds of years … the great traders of southern Africa,” and nineteenth-century observers reported them renowned for mining and metallurgy, exactly the skills a stone-and-gold city drew on. Great Zimbabwe was the hub of an Indian Ocean gold trade the Lemba demonstrably worked, so a Lemba presence there is not in doubt. Third, a burial parallel: unlike neighbouring Bantu peoples, who bury their dead in a crouched posture, the Lemba bury theirs extended, as the ancient Zimbabweans did. Fourth, and crucially for keeping the claim off the racist rocks, the Lemba are by any reckoning an African people: whatever northern male line they carry, by the medieval centuries when Great Zimbabwe rose they had “become decidedly African, having so thoroughly intermixed with Bantu Africans over many hundreds of years” — so a Lemba hand in the building would not vindicate the “outsiders did it” myth.4

Adversarial assessment

The stonework is ancestral-Shona work, and the evidence is not merely consensual but stratified. The colonial exotic-origin theories were dismantled by excavation, not by taste. David Randall-MacIver’s excavations (1905–06) found the material culture African throughout and dated the site to the medieval, not the biblical, past; Gertrude Caton-Thompson’s 1929 dig confirmed it, reporting that examination of all the evidence “can produce not one single item that is not in accordance with the claim of Bantu origin.” The city belongs to a continuous local sequence — the Gokomere culture from around AD 200, flowering into the Shona states — with major construction running from about the eleventh to the fifteenth century and radiocarbon anchoring it. That this finding was so robust that the Rhodesian government censored it — ordering archaeologists not to say publicly that black Africans built Great Zimbabwe, on pain of dismissal — is the reverse of the usual worry: here authority leaned against the African attribution, and the science held anyway. The mainstream reading is not propped up by consensus; it is what the ground yields.5

The maximal claim rides on a premise the archaeology refutes. “The Lemba (a Semitic people) built Great Zimbabwe” in Gayre’s sense depends on the assertion that the Shona could not or did not build in stone — the exact colonial premise Randall-MacIver and Caton-Thompson overturned. Once that premise falls, the maximal claim has nothing to stand on: there is no artifact, inscription, or stratum that requires a northern builder, and the whole apparatus of Phoenician masons and Sabaean architects is a nineteenth-century projection. This wiki rejects the trope on the evidence, not because it is offensive — the offensiveness is beside the point; the point is that it is false. And the symmetry cuts the other way too: the African attribution does not rest on bare authority but on excavated stratigraphy and dating, so it cannot be unseated by the mere possibility that skilled outsiders lent a hand.6

The minimal claim survives, but it is a different and smaller thing — and even its friends hedge it. That Lemba craftsmen worked at Great Zimbabwe as masons, metalworkers, or traders is entirely possible and mildly supported: the Lemba were real specialists in exactly these trades, the burial parallel is a genuine (if slight) datum, and serious scholars — Mufuka, Parfitt — keep the door open to Lemba or Venda participation. But participation in an African-built city is not authorship of it, and it is authorship the tradition and the trope both assert. Parfitt himself, who takes the Lemba seriously and disavows the racist myth, treats the Tovakare “we-built-Zimbabwe” claim as inflated — partly a later echo, he suspects, of missionary and outside retelling folded back into Lemba memory. The honest residue is the trade world, not the walls: the robust, uncontested Lemba link is to the Indian Ocean commerce Great Zimbabwe lived on, which is where the Lemba story genuinely touches the site.7

Assessment

Weak. The interpretation the argument serves — that the ancestral Lemba built Great Zimbabwe — does not hold on its best available case. The stonework belongs to ancestral Shona-speakers on stratigraphic and radiometric evidence that survived even a colonial government’s attempt to suppress it, and the maximal “Semitic builders” form of the claim rests on the discredited premise that Africans could not have raised it. It is rated weak rather than unsupported only because a genuine kernel remains: the Lemba were real traders and metalworkers in the world Great Zimbabwe belonged to, a specific old tradition (the Tovakare clan) claims a mason’s role, a burial parallel is real, and reputable scholars allow limited Lemba participation in an African city. That modest possibility is not the building thesis, and even it is hedged by its own advocates. Keep the two apart, as the core descent page does: a real Middle-Eastern ancestry and a real place in the gold trade are one thing; authorship of the monument is another, and on the evidence it belongs to the Shona.
