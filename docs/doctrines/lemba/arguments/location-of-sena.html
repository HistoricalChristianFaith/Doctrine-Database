Where is the Lemba’s “Sena”? The Hadrami ghost town, Sana’a, and the Zambezi Sena

Type: historical/geographic argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the identification of the Lemba’s lost origin-city Sena with the ruined town actually named Sena in the eastern Wadi Hadramaut (Tudor Parfitt’s find) is the strongest single candidate on the table, and a genuinely impressive one — a real, obscure town of that exact name, a failed irrigation dam matching the tradition, a wadi and a port that fit the migration route, and matching clan-names in the same district. But it is strong by the lights of geography and onomastics and weak by the lights of evidential security: it rests on a name-match, a dam-failure dated only by local legend, and non-diagnostic southern-Arabian genetics, while the Lemba’s own tradition gives “Sena” several incompatible glosses (Sana’a, Judea, Egypt, Ethiopia, and a second, rebuilt Sena on the Zambezi). Genuinely disputed, no decisive proof either way — and the question matters because the strength of the Hadramaut identification is what props up the migration-from-Arabia leg of the whole case.

This is a narrow geographic question and should be kept apart from the two broader ones weighed elsewhere on this timeline — whether the Lemba’s real Semitic paternal input is specifically Israelite (contested) or Arab-Muslim (plausible). Locating Sena in the Hadramaut does not by itself decide either: a Hadrami origin is equally the home of Yemeni Jews and Yemeni Muslims. What it settles, if it holds, is only whence the migration came, not who came.

The claim

The Sena of the Lemba migration formula — “We left Sena, we crossed Pusela, we came to Africa and there we rebuilt Sena” — is a real place: the ghost town of Sena at the end of the Wadi Masila in the eastern Hadramaut of Yemen. It was a considerable town sustained by a great stone-built irrigation dam; when the dam failed, around the tenth or eleventh century, the population dispersed — some of them, as Semitic traders, down the Indian Ocean coast into south-east Africa, where they became the Lemba.

— the Hadramaut identification in its strongest form, as Tudor Parfitt advanced it in Journey to the Vanished City (1992). It is a testable geographic hypothesis, and the reason the Lemba moved from folklore to a scientific question at all. Two things should be separated at the outset. The formula names two Senas: a northern city the people left, and an African city they rebuilt. This page is about the first — the lost origin. The rebuilt Sena is a distinct and much better-documented matter (see below).1

Who advances it

The identification is Tudor Parfitt’s, and it is the linchpin of his Yemeni-trader reading of Lemba origins. Commissioned by the Lemba leader M. E. R. Mathivha to “go and find Sena,” Parfitt first supposed the name might point to Sana’a, the Yemeni capital, or to the coastal town of Sayhut. Working among the Jews of Yemen, he was told by an imam in the holy town of Tarim of a place he had never heard of — a ruined settlement still called Sena to this day, at the end of the Wadi Masila in the remote eastern Hadramaut, roughly sixty kilometres east of Tarim and forty kilometres inland from the Arabian Sea. That the lead came from a local informant, not from Parfitt’s own list of guesses, is a point in the identification’s favour: it was not simply the name he had set out to find.2

The evidence for

First, a real town of the exact name, matched by a distinctive detail. The Lemba tradition holds that Sena was an unusually fertile place, watered by a great dam. Parfitt reports finding at the Hadrami Sena the remains of a “mighty, stone-built dam that permitted intensive irrigation,” which cracked “perhaps around about the tenth or eleventh century,” after which the area could no longer sustain a large population and the people left. A specific, non-obvious feature of the legend — prosperity resting on a single failed dam — is matched on the ground. This is more than a bare name-coincidence.3

Second, the route fits. A valley — the Wadi al-Masila — leads from Sena down to the port of Sayhut on the southern Arabian coast, from which the monsoon trade ran to Mombasa, Zanzibar, and as far south as Sofala in Mozambique. Parfitt identifies the Lemba’s unlocated crossing-point, Pusela (Phusela), with this Masila valley. The geography supplies exactly the exit corridor a migration from Sena into south-east Africa would need, and it lands at the same Indian Ocean gold ports that fed Great Zimbabwe and the Mutapa state.4

Third, matching clan-names and a genetic affinity. Parfitt notes that tribes of the eastern Hadramaut bear names strikingly like the Lemba clan-names — the Sadiki, the Hamisi, and others — and the Y-chromosome work he helped lead (Thomas et al., 2000) found the Lemba paternal lineages closest to those of the men of the Hadramaut among the populations sampled. The onomastic and genetic pointers run in the same direction as the geographic one: toward southern Arabia.5

Adversarial assessment

The name-match is intrinsically weak, and the tradition is not fixed. “Sena” is a short, recurrent place-element, and the Lemba themselves have never spoken with one voice about it. The earliest written record (Mamadi, 1940) and later fieldwork gather a spray of incompatible glosses — a town in Yemen, Sana’a, Judea, Egypt, Ethiopia, and the Swahili-era trade town of Sena on the Zambezi. Parfitt’s own first guesses were Sana’a and Sayhut. A tradition that can be read onto half a dozen places is a memory in search of a map, and any single identification is choosing one reading of an under-determined text. The Hadrami Sena is the best-fitting choice; it is still a choice.6

The dam date is legend, not stratigraphy, and the surviving population is late. The tenth-to-eleventh century failure date is what “the legends there have it” — other tellings put the dam’s collapse nearer c. 1200 — so the one datum that would tie the dispersal to a workable migration window is itself only loosely dated. Worse for a direct genealogical link, the present town of Sena is of relatively recent settlement, so its living inhabitants and the Lemba would not share recent common ancestors: the modern Sena cannot be sampled to confirm the descent, and the argument leans instead on the general southern-Arabian affinity, which is real but not specific to this one town.7

The route and clan-name matches invite confirmation bias. Parfitt set out expressly to “find Sena,” and identifications made in that mode — Pusela = Wadi Masila, the clan-names, the dam — are the kind a determined searcher tends to find. Parfitt himself later wrote a whole source-critical book, The Lost Tribes of Israel: The History of a Myth (2002), arguing that lost-tribe identifications are largely constructed; and the Parfitt–Egorova study of how the Lemba genetics were received warns against treating a romantic quest’s finds as “irreproachably objective.” The eastern-Hadramaut clan-name parallels are suggestive, but they are also exactly what the rival Arab-Muslim reading would predict, and they are not quantified against how common such names are across the peninsula.8

The rebuilt Sena is real — which cuts two ways. The second Sena of the formula is not a mystery at all. Vila de Sena on the Zambezi in Mozambique is a documented Swahili-Muslim trading outpost that carried Indian Ocean goods inland to Great Zimbabwe and Mutapa in exchange for gold; the Lemba even carry a group-name, Vha-Sena (“people of Sena at the Zambezi”), and le Roux records their tradition that in Africa they rebuilt Sena, the last time on the Zambezi. This is a genuine strength for the tradition’s internal coherence — the “rebuilt Sena” is a place that actually existed and sat on exactly the trade world in question. But it also opens the door to retrojection: a trading people living at, and named for, an African Sena could plausibly have projected a matching “Sena we left” back onto a northern origin. The two-Sena structure of the formula tells against the crudest form of that worry (the tradition does distinguish a departed city from the rebuilt one), but it does not dispel it — the well-attested African Sena is at least as good an anchor for the word as the ruined Arabian one, and it is the one the historians of the Zambezi actually document.9

Assessment

Contested. Of the candidates for the Lemba’s lost origin-Sena, Parfitt’s ruined town in the Wadi Masila is far and away the strongest: it is a real place bearing the exact name, it matches a specific and non-obvious feature of the legend (prosperity on a great dam that failed), it sits at the head of a plausible migration corridor down to the Indian Ocean gold ports, and it is backed by convergent onomastic and genetic pointers toward southern Arabia. Taken on its geographic and onomastic merits, it is a remarkable fit and deserves to be treated as the leading hypothesis. But it cannot be called sound or even confidently probable, for reasons that are not about the supernatural but about evidential security: the identification rests on a name that recurs across the region, a dam-failure dated only by local legend, and a general southern-Arabian genetic affinity that does not fasten specifically on this town (whose modern population is in any case too late to sample for the descent). Meanwhile the Lemba’s own tradition offers “Sena” in several incompatible glosses, and the rebuilt Sena on the Zambezi is a documented town that makes retrojection a live alternative. So the question is genuinely disputed, and honestly so: strong by geography and onomastics, weak by the security of the chain — the very profile this wiki records as contested. Its bearing on the wider case is worth stating plainly: because a Hadrami origin is equally consistent with a Jewish and a Muslim Yemen, even a firm answer here would locate the migration’s starting point without settling whose migration it was.
