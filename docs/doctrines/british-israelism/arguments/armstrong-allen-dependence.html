Did Herbert W. Armstrong's booklet derive from J. H. Allen's Judah's Sceptre (1902)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — this is the authorship question, kept sharply distinct from the truth question the rest of this doctrine weighs: not “is the Anglo-Israel identification correct?” (weighed on the descent page, and answered unsupported) but “where did the text of Herbert W. Armstrong's famous booklet come from?” Armstrong presented The United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy (first issued 1945; repeatedly revised) as a divinely revealed “master key” to prophecy that God had opened to him. The claim weighed here is that its content instead derives, in structure, examples, and in places nearly verbatim wording, from J. H. Allen's Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright (1902; repr. 1917) — a book Armstrong read decades earlier and never named in the booklet. The parallel-passage record is dense and specific, Armstrong himself conceded reading Allen, and the dependence was later acknowledged by the head of Armstrong's own church, who noted that the copied text carried Allen's historical errors over with it. The derivation therefore rates sound. (That verdict says nothing about whether the doctrine is true: a borrowed argument and a false argument are independent questions, and here the borrowed argument is also, on its own merits, unsupported.)

The claim

It is a well known fact that Mr. Armstrong did not originate this teaching. In fact, earlier editions of the [United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy] plagiarized vast portions of a book entitled Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright. It is not possible to say that this was revealed to Mr. Armstrong, when in fact, we can see where he copied it from — including the historical errors.

— Joseph Tkach Jr., who succeeded to the leadership of Armstrong's own Worldwide Church of God, stating the dependence as settled fact.1 J. H. Allen (1847–1930), a Holiness minister, had published Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright in 1902, splitting the biblical inheritance so that Judah keeps the royal “sceptre” (the Davidic line, traced to the British throne) while Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh receive the national “birthright” (the British Commonwealth and the United States). Herbert W. Armstrong (1892–1986) built his booklet on exactly that Ephraim-Britain / Manasseh-America scheme, and carried it — through The World Tomorrow broadcasts and The Plain Truth — to an audience Allen never reached. The question here is not whether Armstrong knew of the idea (everyone grants the doctrine long predates him) but whether the text of his booklet is substantially derived from Allen's book in particular.2

Who advances it

The charge is pressed most fully by Ralph Orr, a former minister of Armstrong's church writing for its post-Armstrong successor (now Grace Communion International), whose study Anglo-Israelism and “The United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy” lays the two texts side by side and concludes that the booklet “so tightly follows Allen that the plagiarism is obvious,” though Allen is never named in it. The unusual weight of the case is that it comes from inside the movement: the Worldwide Church of God, after Armstrong's death, publicly repudiated British Israelism, withdrew the booklet from circulation in 1990, and through its own leadership (Tkach) acknowledged the copying. A detailed side-by-side parallel record is maintained at the critical archive allen-armstrong.org, and comparable summaries appear in the church's own literature.3

The evidence for

The parallels are numerous, specific, and often near-verbatim. The record does not rest on the shared thesis (which is generic to the whole Anglo-Israel tradition) but on shared particulars that a common subject does not explain:

  • Where Allen lists Irish place-names he reads as traces of the tribe of Dan — “Dans-Lough, Dan-Sower, Dan-Monism, Dun-dalke, Dun-drum, Don-egal Bay and Don-e-gal City, with Dun-glow and Lon-don-derry” — Armstrong reproduces the same list, in the same order (“Dans-Laugh, Dan-Sower, Dun-dalk, Dun-drum, Don-egal Bay, Don-egal City, Dun-gloe…”). Ireland offered many other candidate names; that Armstrong's run tracks Allen's selection and sequence is hard to explain by independent research.
  • Allen illustrates that a whole nation can be Israelite though few individuals descend from a given tribe by the analogy “just as the great bulk of Americans are not Californians”; Armstrong writes “just as most Americans are not Californians” (the 1945 text used Oregonians). The chosen comparison, not just the point, carries over.
  • Allen's chapter title “Race Versus Grace” recurs as Armstrong's refrain “race, not grace”; both give the identical b'rith-ish etymology (“British” from Hebrew b'rith covenant + ish man), with the same handling of the unwritten Hebrew vowels; both describe the crown of the Irish sovereigns as having “twelve points”; and both narrate the legend of Jeremiah bringing a princess, a scribe (Simon Brach / Baruch), a harp, and the coronation stone to Ireland in the same order and detail.

It is the accumulation of such particulars — the same examples, chosen from many available, in the same sequence, sometimes in the same words — that carries the case, exactly as a run of shared idiosyncratic choices (not shared subject-matter) is what marks textual dependence anywhere.4

Armstrong conceded reading Allen, and his own church conceded the copying. Armstrong, who otherwise framed the doctrine as revealed to him, admitted in a 1980 Bible study that he had read “that book of J. H. Allen on the truth about the lost Ten Tribes.” And Tkach's admission goes further than mere reading: the copied text, he granted, brought Allen's historical errors along with it — a tell of transcription rather than of independent verification, since an independently reasoned case would not be expected to reproduce the source's specific mistakes.1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. On the narrow authorship question the evidence is unusually strong for a literary-dependence claim, and it is strong from the friendly side: the fullest documentation and the frankest admissions come from Armstrong's own church and its successor, not from hostile outsiders, so there is no motive to manufacture a tally. The parallels are not generic commonplaces of the Anglo-Israel genre but shared particular choices — a place-name list in identical order, a specific state-and-nation analogy, a chapter-title refrain, the same etymological handling, and the reproduction of Allen's own errors. Combined with Armstrong's admission that he read the book, that pattern is what textual dependence looks like.

The honest qualifications — and why they do not lower the rating. Three cautions are owed, and each narrows the claim rather than defeating it. First, “derived from” is not “wholly copied from”: Armstrong reorganized the material for a mass radio-and-magazine audience and added elements that are his own — above all the fusion of the Anglo-Israel scheme with Sabbatarian theology and the reading of the covenant curses of Leviticus and Ezekiel as prophecies against modern America and Britain, which is not in Allen. The claim is that the booklet's core structure and much of its content derive from Allen, not that Armstrong contributed nothing. Second, the word “plagiarism” carries a moral charge that a few commentators resist — preferring “utilisation” and faulting Armstrong only for not crediting Allen — and the Philadelphia Church of God, which now holds the booklet's copyright and still defends it, disputes the plagiarism label. But that is a quarrel over the ethics and the label, not over the fact of derivation: “utilised heavily without attribution” already concedes everything this assessment rates. Third, and most important to keep clear: this verdict is about provenance, not truth. That an argument was borrowed says nothing, either way, about whether it is correct — a borrowed argument can be true and an original one false. Here the borrowed argument happens also to be unsupported, but that is established on the evidence of philology, history, and genetics (the descent page), not by the fact of copying.

Assessment

Sound. That Armstrong's The United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy is substantially derived from J. H. Allen's Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright is about as well-established as a literary-dependence claim gets: the two texts share not merely a thesis but a run of specific particulars — a Dan-place-name list in the same order, the “not all Americans are Californians” analogy, the “race, not grace” refrain, the identical b'rith-ish etymology, the twelve-pointed crown, the Jeremiah-to-Ireland legend — sometimes nearly word for word; Armstrong admitted reading Allen; and his own church's leadership acknowledged the copying, noting that even Allen's historical errors were carried over, before withdrawing the booklet in 1990. The reservations are real but narrow the claim rather than defeat it: Armstrong added his own Sabbatarian and American-prophecy material, and defenders quarrel over the ethical label “plagiarism,” not over the derivation itself. The interpretation therefore rates sound. This is a verdict about where the booklet's text came from, kept deliberately separate from whether its identification is true — that question is weighed, and answered unsupported, on the descent page, and the tradition's four-century arc is told on the British Israelism timeline.
