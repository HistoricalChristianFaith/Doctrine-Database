Matthias

Matthias is the one apostle added to the Twelve after the Ascension — and the only one whose calling the New Testament describes in full: between the Ascension and Pentecost, Peter proposed that the empty place of the fallen Judas be filled by a man who had “companied with us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us,” from the baptism of John to the day of the Ascension; two were put forward, Joseph Barsabbas Justus and Matthias, the company prayed, cast lots, “and the lot fell upon Matthias.” After that verse the New Testament does not name him again. What later sources report of him — where he preached, how he died, and whether he was a better-known figure under another name — comes from later writings: the ascetic Traditions of Matthias quoted by Clement of Alexandria, and a “Gospel of Matthias” that Eusebius lists among works “cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles”; a notice that places him among the Seventy; an apocryphal romance that sends him to a city of cannibals, in whose manuscripts the hero is called now Matthias, now Matthew; and the apostle-lists that carry him to “Ethiopia” (that is, Colchis on the Black Sea) and give differing accounts of his death — stoned and beheaded at Jerusalem, crucified, or dead of old age. His relics are claimed at Trier, the only claimed apostolic tomb north of the Alps, and at a fortress in Georgia. This page sets out what the sources report about him, earliest extant source first: the Gospel account of his election, the second-century writings that bear his name, Eusebius’s two notices, the cannibal-city romance, the apostle-lists and the medieval relic-cult, and modern scholarship. This is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on him. Whether Matthias was Zacchaeus or Barnabas under another name is set out under Disputed readings below. For the shared survey of the sources on the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

Matthias enters the New Testament at a single scene and leaves it at the same one. In the days between the Ascension and Pentecost, Peter stood among some hundred and twenty believers and proposed that the “office” (episkopē) vacated by Judas be filled; the replacement had to be an eyewitness of the whole of Jesus’ public life — “of these men which have companied with us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John, unto that same day that he was taken up from us” — and a witness of the resurrection. Two men met the test: Joseph called Barsabbas Justus, and Matthias. The company prayed to the Lord “which knowest the hearts of all men” to show which he had chosen, “and they gave forth their lots; and the lot fell upon Matthias; and he was numbered with the eleven apostles.” He is not named again in the New Testament, nor are most of the Eleven listed in Acts 1:13. Paul hands on the creed that the risen Christ was seen “of the twelve” (1 Corinthians 15:5).1

Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215)

The earliest extant notice of Matthias after the New Testament concerns a book bearing his name. Clement of Alexandria three times quotes a Traditions of Matthias (Paradōseis Matthiou) — “Wonder at what is before you,” the saying runs, “laying this down first as the foundation of further knowledge” — and reports that “Matthias also taught that we ought to fight against and abuse the flesh, and not give way to it for the sake of pleasure.” Clement cites the work without treating it as Scripture, and he reports that the Basilidians, whom he counts among the heretics, claimed Matthias as an authority. In the same Stromateis Clement preserves a second notice: retelling the story of the repentant tax-collector, he writes “Zacchaeus — or, as some say, Matthias” — the earliest extant notice of an identification of the apostle chosen by lot with the publican of Jericho. The identification, and the variation between “Matthias” and “Matthew” in the texts, are set out under Disputed readings. By about 200, Clement attests, a body of ascetic sayings circulated under Matthias’s name.2

Eusebius (c. 325)

Eusebius gives Matthias two brief notices. In Book 1 of the Ecclesiastical History he passes on the tradition — its earliest extant record — that Matthias, “who was numbered with the apostles in the place of Judas,” and Barsabbas Justus with him, had first been among the Seventy whom Jesus sent out ahead of him. In Book 3, sorting the writings in circulation into accepted, disputed and rejected, he lists a “Gospel of Matthias” among those “cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles” — with the Gospels of Peter and Thomas — works “to be cast aside as absurd and impious.” In Eusebius, then, Matthias’s name appears both in the tradition of the Seventy and on the title of a gospel he rejects.3

The apocryphal Acts of Andrew and Matthias (2nd–4th c.)

The Acts of Andrew and Matthias — among the Anthropophagi, “the man-eaters” — opens with the apostles casting lots for their mission-fields; the lot sends Matthias to a city of cannibals who blind, drug, and fatten their captives to eat them on the thirtieth day. Matthias is imprisoned and his eyes put out; Christ sends Andrew across the sea to rescue him, and after a run of miracles the city is converted. Many manuscripts, and the Anglo-Saxon Andreas built on the story, call the hero “Matthew,” the name of the evangelist; the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that in the apocryphal writings “Matthew and Matthias have sometimes been confounded.”4

The apostle-lists and the Golden Legend (c. 6th–13th c.)

The late catalogues supply a mission and a death the earlier sources do not report, and give differing accounts of both. The apostle-lists — the Synopsis ascribed to Dorotheus, Nicephorus, and their kin — send Matthias to preach “in Ethiopia,” meaning not Africa but Colchis on the eastern Black Sea (the harbour of Hyssus, the mouth of the Phasis), among “barbarians and man-eaters,” and bury him at Sebastopolis by the Temple of the Sun. On his death the traditions differ: stoned by the Jews at Jerusalem and then beheaded with an axe (whence the axe in his images); crucified in Colchis; or, in Pseudo-Hippolytus, dead of old age at Jerusalem. The medieval West, following the Golden Legend, kept the stoned-and-beheaded story and the axe, and located his relics at Trier, where the abbey of St. Matthias still shows the only claimed apostolic tomb north of the Alps (the Catholic Encyclopedia reports that Helena “brought the relics of St. Matthias to Rome, and that a portion of them was at Trier”), while another tradition keeps his grave at the Gonio fortress in Georgia. Bollandus, in the Acta Sanctorum, doubted whether the relics at Rome were the apostle’s rather than those of a later Matthias, bishop of Jerusalem.5

Modern scholarship

W. Schneemelcher’s New Testament Apocrypha (rev. ed., 1991–92) treats the Traditions and the Gospel of Matthias among the second-century apocrypha bearing Matthias’s name. The earliest extant attestation of the mission to Colchis and of the accounts of his death, among the sources surveyed here, is in the list-literature. Sean McDowell’s survey of the apostles’ deaths, The Fate of the Apostles (2015), places the evidence for Matthias’s death among the weakest for any of the apostles. Whether “Matthias” was a second name for Zacchaeus or Barnabas is set out under Disputed readings below.6
