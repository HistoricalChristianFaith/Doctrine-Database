Predestination, grace & free will

Predestination, grace & free will is the doctrine of how God’s saving grace, His eternal election, and the human will fit together — the question that split the Latin West for a century after Augustine and has never fully closed. All sides confess that salvation is by grace; they divide on where grace begins and how far it reaches. Does grace merely assist a will that takes the first step on its own (Pelagius), or must God work even the will to believe — the initium fidei — before a man can turn to Him (Augustine)? Is election God’s free choice that some may believe, or His foreknowledge of who would believe? And if grace is decisive, is there a matching predestination of the lost to death, or only a passing-by that leaves them in the fault they chose? This timeline traces the answers — from the Pelagian crisis through the Augustinian settlement, its “semi-Pelagian” softening, the conciliar resolution at Orange, the ninth-century double-predestination flare, and the early-modern grace controversies — earliest first. It is the doctrinal root beneath the wiki’s justification timeline: whether a sinner contributes anything to his acceptance is, at bottom, a question about grace and the will.

Related doctrines: Grace, faith, and justification — see also justification (how a sinner is made right with God).

Timeline

Pelagius — the will’s native power (the thesis, c. 405–418)

Pelagius, a British ascetic teacher at Rome, is the provocation that forced the doctrine open. He taught that God has implanted in human nature a real possibilitas — a standing power to choose good or evil — so that “a man could be without sin… if he wished; for this capacity has been given to him by God.” Grace, on his scheme, secures the ability (in nature), plus the law and the example of Christ; the willing and the doing of the good are man’s own. Because this made the beginning of salvation a human act, the African church judged it a denial of grace.1 See the detail page: Pelagius on predestination, grace & free will.

Augustine against Pelagius — grace works even the will to believe (c. 412–429)

Augustine of Hippo is the hinge of the whole doctrine. Forced to define grace, he taught that “it is God who both works in man the willing to believe, and in all things prevents us with His mercy”; that grace does not destroy but establishes free will (“grace cures the will whereby righteousness is freely loved”); that predestination is “nothing else… [than] the foreknowledge and the preparation of God’s kindnesses” by which the elect are certainly saved, chosen “not because they were already [believers]… but that they may believe”; and that even final perseverance is God’s gift. He reached this against his own earlier view, which he openly retracted.2 See the detail page: Augustine of Hippo on predestination, grace & free will.

The Council of Carthage condemns Pelagianism (418)

Some two hundred African bishops, meeting at Carthage on 1 May 418 (their canons soon approved by Pope Zosimus), fixed the church’s answer to Pelagius in a series of anathemas. They condemned any who says that the grace of God “has power only for the remission of sins… and not also for help, that they be not committed”; any who says grace merely reveals the commandments “that we may know what we ought to strive after… but that… [the power] is not also given to us to love and to be able to do” them; and any who makes grace only what lets us fulfil the commands “more easily.” Grace, that is, is not information or assistance but the power to will and do the good — the death-blow to Pelagianism.3

John Cassian — the “both-and” (Conference 13, c. 426)

John Cassian, in the thirteenth Conference, voices the middle position a later age would call semi-Pelagian. Against Pelagius he insists nothing good is done without God’s help and that grace often draws the unwilling (Paul, Matthew); against Augustine’s hardest edge he holds that God sometimes fosters a good will “which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts” (Zacchaeus, the good thief), and that there are “seeds of goodness” in nature awaiting grace. Grace and free will, he says, “seem opposed… but really are in harmony”; to withdraw either is to break “the rule of the Church’s faith.” It is his allowance that the beginning of faith may sometimes be man’s that the next generation would reject.4 See the detail page: John Cassian on predestination, grace & free will.

Prosper of Aquitaine defends Augustine against the Massilians (c. 429–434)

Prosper of Aquitaine, Augustine’s foremost lay defender, answered the monks of Marseilles who granted that grace increases faith while making its beginning man’s own. Faith itself is God’s gift, he wrote, “without whose grace no one runs to grace”; from “the beginning right to the end both the inception and the perfection of the good work is attributed to the Lord.” His letter to Augustine (with Hilary’s) prompted the reply-treatises On the Predestination of the Saints and On the Gift of Perseverance; his Against the Collator attacked Cassian’s Conference 13 by name.5 See the detail page: Prosper of Aquitaine on predestination, grace & free will.

The Second Council of Orange — the settled Western answer (529)

Presided over by Caesarius of Arles and confirmed by Pope Boniface II, the Second Council of Orange gave the West its authoritative settlement. It received Augustine on the decisive point — that “not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith… [is] a gift of grace,” not of nature — and so excluded the semi-Pelagian middle. But it stopped short of Augustine’s hardest edge: it affirmed that after baptismal grace the will truly cooperates, and it anathematized with “utter abhorrence” the claim “that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God.” Grace-alone to begin, without double predestination.6 See the detail page: Caesarius of Arles and the Council of Orange.

Gottschalk of Orbais — double predestination, condemned (848–855)

Three centuries later the Saxon monk Gottschalk of Orbais (c. 808–868) pressed Augustine into a symmetrical twofold predestination: God “predestined the holy angels and elect human beings to eternal life, and… equally predestined… all reprobate human beings… to rightly eternal death” (on their foreseen evil merits). Condemned at Mainz (848) and flogged and imprisoned at Quiersy (849), his twin-predestination was rejected at the Second Synod of Quiersy (853), where Hincmar of Reims’s four chapters affirmed one predestination — of the elect to life — with the reprobate merely foreknown, “predestined [to] eternal punishment” but not to perish. A rival synod at Valence (855) leaned back toward Gottschalk, confessing “the predestination of the elect to life and the predestination of the impious to death” while still denying that God foreordains anyone to evil — the very line Orange had drawn.78

The De Auxiliis controversy — Molina’s middle knowledge vs. the Thomists (1588–1607)

The medieval and early-modern schools reopened the finer question Orange left — how prevenient and cooperating grace relate — without reviving Pelagius. The Jesuit Luis de Molina (1535–1600), in his Concordia (1588), proposed scientia media (“middle knowledge”): God knows what any free creature would freely do in any circumstance, and elects by choosing which circumstances to actualize — reconciling efficacious grace with an untouched freedom. The Dominican Domingo Báñez (1528–1604) answered with intrinsically efficacious grace and praemotio physica (physical premotion), the efficacy resting in the grace, not in foreseen consent. Clement VIII convened the Congregatio de Auxiliis (1598); in 1607 Paul V ended it without condemning either side, permitting Thomists and Molinists each to hold their own.9

Jansen’s Augustinus and the Five Propositions, condemned (1640–1653)

Cornelius Jansen (1585–1638), bishop of Ypres, revived in his posthumous Augustinus (1640) a rigorist Augustinianism: grace intrinsically efficacious and, in fallen man, unresisted; a “sufficient” grace that does not suffice; Christ dead only for the elect. Innocent X’s bull Cum occasione (1653) condemned five propositions drawn from it — among them that “some of God’s commandments are impossible to just men who wish and strive to keep them,” that “in the state of fallen nature no one ever resists interior grace,” and that it is “semi-Pelagian to say that Christ died… for all men.” The Catholic Church thus fixed its boundary against an over-Augustinian reading as firmly as Orange had against the semi-Pelagian one.10

Arminius and the Synod of Dort — the Reformed settlement (1610–1619)

Within the Reformed world the same fault line ran between Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609) and his heirs. The Remonstrants’ five articles (1610) taught conditional election — God saves those “who through the grace of the Holy Spirit shall believe… and shall persevere” — universal atonement, and resistible grace. The Synod of Dort (1618–19) answered with the “five points”: unconditional election (“not on the basis of foreseen faith… but… for the purpose of faith”), limited atonement, total depravity, irresistible grace, and the perseverance of the saints — Augustine’s election-before-merit carried through, on the Protestant side, to its systematic end.11

Arguments

These cut across the timeline — each weighs a single sub-claim adversarially and carries its own assessment.

Related arguments — the eternal decree

Two of the arguments concern the structure of God’s eternal decree itself — the ground of election (is it conditioned on the faith God foresees?) and the symmetry of reprobation (is there a positive decree of the lost, parallel to election?). Both turn on the same engine, Romans 9:11 — the children “not yet born, neither having done any good or evil” — and are best read together.

Election on foreseen faith — does God choose post praevisa merita? (assessment: contested)

The conditional-election argument — that God elects those He foresees will believe and persevere (the Remonstrant post praevisa merita), so foreseen faith is the ground of election, not its consequence. It has a genuine case: the plain sequence of Romans 8:29 (“foreknew… predestinated”), the available corporate reading of Romans 9, and the settled teaching of the Christian East (John of Damascus: predestination “based on foreknowledge”) and the Arminian tradition, all without lapsing into Pelagianism. But its sharp Remonstrant form is pressed hard: once the foreseen faith is granted to be God’s own gift, election-on-foreseen-faith either collapses into unconditional election or rests on a foreseen free non-resistance that Romans 9 (“not of works… nor of him that willeth”), 1 Corinthians 4:7, and Ephesians 1’s “good pleasure of his will” exclude — and Augustine, Aquinas, and Dort converge in denying foreseen merit as election’s ground. Strong by Eastern and Arminian lights, denied by the Latin convergence, with no consensus winner.

Double predestination — is there a positive decree of reprobation? (assessment: contested)

The double-predestination argument — that reprobation is a real act of the divine will (not bare foreknowledge of who will damn themselves) is broadly defensible; even Aquinas grants a will to permit sin and impose punishment. What genuinely divides the traditions is the symmetry: whether the decree of reprobation mirrors election (unconditioned, parallel to it) or is asymmetric (permissive, resting damnation on foreseen sin). The Reformed confessions and Romans 9 press for the parallel; Orange (529), Quiersy (853), and the Thomist distinction press against it — and even Gottschalk and Dort quietly break the symmetry, grounding the decree of death on foreseen demerit lest God be made the author of sin. Strong by one tradition’s lights, condemned by another’s, with no consensus winner.

The initium fidei — can the first movement toward faith be the will’s own? (assessment: weak)

The initium fidei argument — the “semi-Pelagian” both-and (grace completes and sustains, but the first assent can be the unaided will’s, which grace then meets) is coherent and genuinely not Pelagian, and it rests on a real observation — Scripture narrates conversions of two shapes (Paul drawn while set on evil; Zacchaeus and the thief seeming to reach first). But its decisive proof-texts run the other way (1 Cor 4:7 makes faith a thing received; Phil 2:13 puts God behind the willing), its counter-examples show only that grace need not be felt as prior, and the one council that ruled on it (Orange, 529) condemned it — a judgement the otherwise-divided Reformed and Catholic traditions both kept.
