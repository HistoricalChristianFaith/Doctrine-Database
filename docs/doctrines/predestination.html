Predestination, grace & free will

Predestination, grace & free will is the doctrine of how God’s saving grace, His eternal election, and the human will are related. The writers on this timeline differ on where grace begins and how far it reaches. Does grace assist a will that can take the first step on its own (Pelagius), or must God work even the will to believe — the initium fidei — before a man can turn to Him (Augustine), or do grace and the will work together from the first (John Cassian, as Orthodox writers read him: a synergy)? Is election God’s choice that some may believe, or His foreknowledge of who would believe? And is there a predestination of the lost to punishment, or only a passing-by that leaves them in their own fault? This timeline records the extant witnesses, earliest first: Pelagius and Augustine (c. 405–429); the Council of Carthage (418); John Cassian (c. 426) and Prosper of Aquitaine (c. 429–434); Fulgentius of Ruspe (c. 520); the Second Council of Orange (529); Gottschalk of Orbais and the synods of Quiersy and Valence (848–855); Erasmus (1524) and Luther (1525); the De Auxiliis controversy (1588–1607); the Remonstrance and the Synod of Dort (1610–1619); and Jansen’s Augustinus and its condemnation (1640–1653). The related question of how a sinner is made right with God is traced on the justification timeline.

The communions and their theologians describe predestination in their own terms. The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) teaches that “the predestination or eternal election of God… extends only over the godly, beloved children of God, being a cause of their salvation,” and rejects the teaching that “some, without regard to their sins, from the mere counsel, purpose, and will of God, are ordained to condemnation so that they cannot be saved.”1 The Reformed Westminster Confession (1646) teaches that “some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life; and others foreordained to everlasting death,” the elect being chosen “without any foresight of faith, or good works.”2 The Orthodox Confession of Dositheus (Synod of Jerusalem, 1672) teaches that God, who “would have all men to be saved,” predestined or condemned “since He foreknew the one would make a right use of their free-will, and the other a wrong.”3 The Methodist Articles of Religion (1784) teach that “we have no power to do good works… without the grace of God by Christ preventing us, that we may have a good will, and working with us, when we have that good will.”4 The Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (1974) writes that “there is no opposition between freedom and grace in the Byzantine tradition,” grace securing “that cooperation, or synergy, between the divine will and human choice.”5 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) teaches that when God “establishes his eternal plan of ‘predestination’, he includes in it each person’s free response to his grace,” and that “God predestines no one to go to hell.”6

Timeline

Pelagius — the will’s native power (c. 405–418)

Pelagius, a British ascetic teacher at Rome, taught that God has implanted in human nature a real possibilitas — a standing power to choose good or evil — so that “a man could be without sin… if he wished; for this capacity has been given to him by God.” Grace, on his scheme, secures the ability (in nature), together with the law and the example of Christ; the willing and the doing of the good are man’s own. Augustine and the African bishops held that this made the beginning of salvation a human act and denied grace; Pelagius’s teaching was condemned at Carthage and by Pope Zosimus in 418.7 See the detail page: Pelagius on predestination, grace & free will.

Augustine against Pelagius — grace works the will to believe (c. 412–429)

Augustine of Hippo, writing against Pelagius and his followers, taught that “it is God who both works in man the willing to believe, and in all things prevents us with His mercy”; that grace does not destroy but establishes free will (“grace cures the will whereby righteousness is freely loved”); that predestination is “nothing else… [than] the foreknowledge and the preparation of God’s kindnesses” by which the elect are certainly delivered, chosen “not because they were already [believers]… but that they may believe”; and that final perseverance is God’s gift. He describes this as a change from his own earlier view, which he retracted.8 See the detail page: Augustine of Hippo on predestination, grace & free will.

The Council of Carthage condemns Pelagianism (418)

Some two hundred African bishops, meeting at Carthage on 1 May 418 (their canons soon approved by Pope Zosimus), answered Pelagius in a series of anathemas. They condemned any who says that the grace of God “has power only for the remission of sins… and not also for help, that they be not committed”; any who says grace merely reveals the commandments “that we may know what we ought to strive after… but that… [the power] is not also given to us to love and to be able to do” them; and any who makes grace only what lets us fulfil the commands “more easily.” In these canons grace is the power to will and do the good, not only knowledge of the commands or an easier fulfilment of them.9

John Cassian — grace and free will “in harmony” (Conference 13, c. 426)

John Cassian, in the thirteenth Conference, holds that nothing good is done without God’s help and that grace often draws the unwilling (Paul, Matthew), and also that God sometimes fosters a good will “which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts” (Zacchaeus, the good thief), and that there are “seeds of goodness” in nature which God must quicken. Grace and free will, he says, “seem opposed… but really are in harmony”; to withdraw either is to break “the rule of the Church’s faith.” Prosper of Aquitaine attacked the Conference by name. Joseph Pohle (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) places Cassian among the Semipelagians, a term the same encyclopedia dates to the 1590s; Orthodox writers, among them Seraphim Rose (1983), read Conference 13 as the Orthodox teaching of synergy; and A. M. C. Casiday (2006) argues that Cassian was not a Pelagian of any sort (see the detail page).10 See the detail page: John Cassian on predestination, grace & free will.

Prosper of Aquitaine defends Augustine against the Massilians (c. 429–434)

Prosper of Aquitaine, a lay theologian of southern Gaul, wrote in Augustine’s defence against the monks of Marseilles who, on his report, granted that grace increases faith while making its beginning man’s own. Faith itself is God’s gift, he wrote, “without whose grace no one runs to grace”; from “the beginning right to the end both the inception and the perfection of the good work is attributed to the Lord.” His letter to Augustine (with Hilary’s) prompted the reply-treatises On the Predestination of the Saints and On the Gift of Perseverance; his Against the Collator attacked Cassian’s Conference 13 by name.11 See the detail page: Prosper of Aquitaine on predestination, grace & free will.

Fulgentius of Ruspe — predestination to punishment, not to sin (c. 520)

Fulgentius of Ruspe (c. 468–533), bishop of a small African see and twice exiled to Sardinia under the Arian Vandal kings, wrote on grace in the century between Augustine and Orange. He taught that predestination is God’s “gracious love” empowering the elect (not a response to foreseen merit), that “the number of the elect” is fixed — “no one of that full number may lose his eternal grace, nor may any outside that total attain” it — and that faith itself is “not from us but a gift from God,” so that grace is not given to all alike. Answering his correspondent Monimus, he taught that God predestines the reprobate to punishment (justly, for their own foreseen sins) but never to sin itself — “the one who deserts God is deserted by God.”12 See the detail page: Fulgentius of Ruspe on predestination, grace & free will.

The Second Council of Orange (529)

Presided over by Caesarius of Arles and confirmed by Pope Boniface II, the Second Council of Orange adopted canons drawn largely from Augustine and Prosper. It taught that “not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith… [is] a gift of grace,” not of nature; it affirmed that after baptismal grace the baptized are able, “with the aid and cooperation of Christ,” to do what pertains to salvation; and it anathematized with “utter abhorrence” the claim “that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God.”13 See the detail page: Caesarius of Arles and the Council of Orange.

Gottschalk of Orbais — twofold predestination, and the synods (848–855)

The Saxon monk Gottschalk of Orbais (c. 808–868), reading Augustine and Fulgentius, confessed a twofold predestination: God “predestined the holy angels and elect human beings to eternal life, and… equally predestined… all reprobate human beings… to rightly eternal death” (on account of their foreseen evil merits). Condemned at Mainz (848) and flogged and imprisoned at Quiersy (849), his twofold predestination was rejected at the Second Synod of Quiersy (853), where Hincmar of Reims’s four chapters affirmed one predestination — of the elect to life — with the reprobate foreknown, “predestined [to] eternal punishment” but not to perish. A synod at Valence (855) confessed “the predestination of the elect to life and the predestination of the impious to death” while denying that God foreordains anyone to evil, citing the Synod of Orange.14,15 See the detail page: Gottschalk of Orbais on predestination, grace & free will.

Erasmus’s Diatribe — a cooperating freedom (De libero arbitrio, 1524)

Desiderius Erasmus (c. 1466–1536) wrote against Luther on the freedom of the will in On the Freedom of the Will (September 1524). Free choice, he wrote, is “a power of the human will by which a man can apply himself to the things which lead to eternal salvation, or turn away from them”; the Fall left this power “damaged by sin… nevertheless not extinguished.” Of those who “attribute most of all to grace and practically nothing to free choice, yet do not entirely remove it,” he wrote that their view “seems probably enough”; he defended a cooperation in which grace leads and the will is a real second cause. His declared targets were the readings that make “free choice… a mere empty name” and hold that “all things that happen come about by sheer necessity.”16 See the detail page: Erasmus on predestination, grace & free will.

Luther’s The Bondage of the Will (De servo arbitrio, 1525)

Martin Luther (1483–1546) replied within the year. Against Erasmus’s preference for restraint he held that the question must be asserted (“take away assertions and you take away Christianity”), and that it is no trifle: “you and I have nothing in common, for I consider it vital.” The unregenerate will, he taught, has no power to choose God; it is “as it were a beast between the two” — if God rides it, it goes where God wills; if Satan, where Satan wills — and it cannot choose its rider. Ending his reply, Luther thanked Erasmus that “unlike all the rest you alone have attacked the real issue, the essence of the matter in dispute,” not the “trifles” of “the papacy, purgatory, indulgences.” In 1537 he wrote that he acknowledged none of his books as truly his own except this one and the Catechism (see the detail page; the related doctrine of justification by faith is traced on its own timeline).17 See the detail page: Martin Luther on predestination, grace & free will.

The De Auxiliis controversy — Molina’s middle knowledge and the Thomists (1588–1607)

The Jesuit Luis de Molina (1535–1600), in his Concordia (1588), proposed scientia media (“middle knowledge”): God knows what any free creature would freely do in any circumstance, and elects by choosing which circumstances to actualize — a reconciliation, on Molina’s account, of efficacious grace with the will’s freedom. The Dominican Domingo Báñez (1528–1604) taught intrinsically efficacious grace and praemotio physica (physical premotion), the efficacy resting in the grace, not in foreseen consent. Clement VIII convened the Congregatio de Auxiliis (1598); in 1607 Paul V ended it without condemning either side, permitting Thomists and Molinists each to hold their own.18 See the detail page: Luis de Molina, Domingo Báñez & the De Auxiliis controversy.

Arminius, the Remonstrants and the Synod of Dort (1610–1619)

In the Dutch Reformed church a dispute over election arose between Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609) and his followers and their opponents. The Remonstrants’ five articles (1610) taught election of those “who through the grace of the Holy Spirit shall believe… and shall persevere,” Christ’s death for all, and grace that is not irresistible. The Synod of Dort (1618–19) answered the articles in its Canons, teaching that election is “not on the basis of foreseen faith… but… for the purpose of faith,” and treating the extent of Christ’s redemption, the corruption of the will and the manner of conversion, and the perseverance of the saints. The later summary of these heads under the acronym “TULIP” is not Dort’s own wording (see the detail page).19 See the detail page: Jacobus Arminius, the Remonstrants & the Synod of Dort.

Jansen’s Augustinus and the Five Propositions (1640–1653)

Cornelius Jansen (1585–1638), bishop of Ypres, set out in his posthumous Augustinus (1640) a reading of Augustine on grace which the Catholic Encyclopedia summarizes as grace efficacious and, in fallen man, the will moved by the stronger “delectation,” with “no place for purely sufficient grace.” Innocent X’s bull Cum occasione (1653) condemned five propositions as drawn from it — among them that “some of God’s commandments are impossible to just men who wish and strive to keep them,” that “in the state of fallen nature no one ever resists interior grace,” and that it is “semi-Pelagian to say that Christ died… for all men.” Jansen’s defenders, led by Antoine Arnauld and Port-Royal, accepted the condemnation of the propositions but denied that they were taught in the book.20 See the detail page: Cornelius Jansen on predestination, grace & free will.
