The Veneration of the Cross

In several Christian rites the Cross is honoured with bodily gestures: the faithful bow to it and kiss it.1,2 On Good Friday the Roman rite holds an Adoration of the Cross, with the antiphon “We adore your Cross, O Lord” (Crucem tuam adoramus, Domine);3,4 the Byzantine rite sets the Cross in the middle of the church for veneration on the Third Sunday of Lent.5 The communions describe the act differently. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) calls the honour paid to the figure of the Cross proskynēsis, distinct from the latreia that belongs to the divine nature alone, and John of Damascus calls the Cross “the most marvellous of all” Christ's works and speaks of “conferring the adoration on Him.” John Calvin held that the dulia/latria distinction does not set such honour apart from worship (see his entry below).6 This timeline records how the practice was described, sung, defined and opposed, earliest extant attestation first.

Related doctrines: Sacred images and the veneration of the holy — see also the veneration of images and icons · the veneration of relics

Who holds what. In the Orthodox churches the Cross set out on the Third Sunday of Lent is, in the Orthodox Church in America's account, “to be venerated as that reality by which man must live to be saved”;5 Alexander Schmemann (Orthodox, Great Lent, 1974) writes that the hymns of that Sunday develop the theme of the Cross “in terms not of suffering but of victory and joy.”7 The Orthodox Church in America's The Orthodox Faith (vol. II, “Elevation of the Cross”) describes the faithful who “elevate the Cross and bow down before it in veneration and worship to God.”8

In the Catholic Church, Fernand Cabrol (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reports two accounts of the honour: Albert the Great, Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure and Thomas Aquinas taught that “the worship rendered to the Cross and the image of Christ is that of latria,” given to the image “with a relative worship”; Bellarmine, Bossuet and Petavius taught, with the Second Council of Nicaea and the Council of Trent, that the honour paid to images and relics is “a relative veneration” and not latria.9 Aquinas gives as his reasons that the Cross represents the figure of Christ and its “contact with the limbs of Christ,” and adds: “we speak to the cross and pray to it, as to the Crucified Himself.”10 The Congregation for Divine Worship's Paschalis Sollemnitatis (1988), on the celebration of the Easter feasts, states that on Good Friday the Church “adores the cross” and that “the personal adoration of the cross is a most important feature in this celebration.”11

Among the Protestant churches, Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reports of the crucifix that “In Lutheran churches there has always been much tolerance for the crucifix either upon or behind the altar.”12 Stefan Gramenz (Gottesblog, a Lutheran liturgy blog, 2026) reports that Matthaeus Ludecus, dean of Havelberg Cathedral, included a full mass for the Exaltation of the Holy Cross in his Missale (1589), and that Ludecus's Vesperale notes that the language of the hymns and chants for the feast “referring to the cross is metonymy, and is truly understood as referring to the crucified”; Gramenz adds that “The piety of the Christian is not directed toward the cross itself, but toward the Crucified.”13 The Episcopal Church's 1979 Book of Common Prayer provides that on Good Friday “a wooden cross may now be brought into the church and placed in the sight of the people,” with devotions whose first anthem begins “We glory in your cross, O Lord.”14 Modern academic statements of the Reformed and Lutheran positions on the veneration of the Cross have not yet been located for this timeline.

Timeline

Egeria and the Adoration of the Cross at Jerusalem (c. 384)

The Western pilgrim Egeria describes the Good Friday rite at Golgotha: the wood of the Cross is set on a linen-covered table, the bishop “holds the extremities of the sacred wood firmly in his hands, while the deacons… guard it,” and the whole people file past, “bowing down at the table,” to “kiss the sacred wood and pass through” — touching it with forehead and eyes. She gives as the reason for the guard that someone is said to have once “bitten off and stolen a portion” of it.1 Herbert Thurston (Catholic, 1908) traces the Western Good Friday veneration of the Cross back to this Jerusalem practice, as Amalarius had in the ninth century.15

Venantius Fortunatus and the hymns of the Cross (569)

For the reception of a relic of the True Cross at Poitiers, Venantius Fortunatus wrote Vexilla regis prodeunt, first sung in the procession that brought the relic to Radegund's monastery, and Pange lingua… proelium certaminis, which the Latin rite chants during the Adoration of the Cross on Good Friday. Its Crux fidelis stanza speaks to the wood in the second person: “Faithful Cross! above all other, one and only noble Tree… sweetest wood and sweetest iron! Sweetest Weight is hung on thee!” (dulce lignum, “sweet wood”).16

John of Damascus on worshipping the Cross (c. 743)

In the Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.11, John of Damascus holds that “the most marvellous of all” of Christ's works “is His precious Cross,” that “this same truly precious and august tree… is to be worshipped as sanctified by contact with His holy body and blood,” and that the cross-image is worshipped “not honouring the tree (God forbid) but the image as a symbol of Christ… conferring the adoration on Him.” His chapter on images follows at IV.16.17

The Second Council of Nicaea defines the honour of the Cross and the images (787)

The council that restored the images names the Cross in the same clause. Its Definition rules that “just as the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross, so also the venerable and holy images” are to be set forth and given “honourable reverence (proskynēsis), not indeed that true worship of faith (latreia) which pertains alone to the divine nature,” and that incense and lights may be offered to them “as to the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross and to the Book of the Gospels and to the other holy objects… according to ancient pious custom.”18

John Calvin's Reformed rejection (1543/1559)

John Calvin asks “Of what use, then, were the erection in churches of so many crosses of wood and stone, silver and gold, if this doctrine were faithfully and honestly preached…?”, the doctrine that “Christ died that he might bear our curse upon the tree… and, in short, reconcile us to God the Father,” from which “the people would learn more than from a thousand crosses of wood and stone”; holds that the dulia/latria distinction was “invented for the very purpose of permitting divine honours to be paid to angels and dead men with apparent impunity”; and writes that calling the honour paid to images dulia rather than latria “is just the same as if they were to confess that they worship their images without worshipping them.” In the Treatise on Relics he argues that the pieces of the true Cross exhibited across Europe, if collected, “would form a whole ship's cargo.”19
