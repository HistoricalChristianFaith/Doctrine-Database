Isidore of Seville on astrology

Dates: c. 620 (the Etymologies, Book III, chapters 24–27, on astronomy and astrology; the work was compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at Isidore's death); Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Etymologies, his encyclopedia, Isidore defines astronomy as "the law of the stars," the study of the heavens' courses; then, under the chapter title "The difference between astronomy and astrology," he cuts astrology in two — a "natural" part (the seasons, the reckoning of sun and moon) that is kept, and a "superstitious" part, "that which the mathematici follow, who prophesy by the stars… and endeavor to predict the nativities and characters of men," which he condemns. In his scheme the nativity-casting is refused and the study of the stars is kept.

Claims & evidence

Astronomy as "the law of the stars"

Isidore opens with astronomy proper, and treats it as a discipline of reason: the study of the heavenly bodies' motions and figures. He presents the observation of the courses of the sky as knowledge, not superstition.

Astronomy is the law of the stars, and it traces with inquiring reason the courses of the heavenly bodies, and their figures, and the regular movements of the stars with reference to one another and to the earth.

— Etymologies III.24 ("On the name of astronomy"), Brehaut 1912 translation. Earlier writers on this timeline also distinguish astral fatalism from the study of the heavens: compare Clement, Origen, Basil and Augustine.1

The difference between astronomy and astrology: astrology "in part natural, in part superstitious"

The chapter is titled "The difference between astronomy and astrology." Astronomy embraces the motions of the heavens; astrology Isidore splits in two.

There is some difference between astronomy and astrology. For astronomy embraces the revolution of the heavens, the rise, setting, and motion of the heavenly bodies, and the origin of their names. Astrology, on the other hand, is in part natural, in part superstitious.

— Etymologies III.27, Brehaut 1912. By dividing the word itself, rather than condemning "astrology" as a whole, Isidore separates genethlialogy from the reckoning of times and seasons.2

Natural astrology: the seasons and the courses of sun, moon, and stars

The "natural" part, as Isidore describes it, is the practical astronomy of the calendar: watching the luminaries to know the seasons. He does not condemn it.

It is natural astrology when it describes the courses of the sun and the moon and the stars, and the regular succession of the seasons.

— Etymologies III.27, Brehaut 1912. The description matches the use of the lights that Genesis 1:14 assigns them, "for signs, and for seasons," read as reckoning rather than as fate.3

Superstitious astrology: the mathematici who cast nativities

Isidore rejects the second part, and names it: the horoscope-casters who parcel the zodiac out over the body and read a man's birth and character from the stars. This, for him, is the superstition.

Superstitious astrology is that which the mathematici follow, who prophesy by the stars, and who distribute the twelve signs of the heavens among the individual parts of the soul or body, and endeavor to predict the nativities and characters of men from the course of the stars.

— Etymologies III.27, Brehaut 1912. The mathematici are the nativity-casters whom Augustine had consulted and renounced and whom Basil mocked; the distribution of the twelve signs among the parts of the body (melothesia) is the practice Leo the Great and the First Council of Braga condemned (see the accommodation question).4

Who discovered it: Egyptians, Chaldeans — and Abraham

Isidore's chapter on the origins repeats the ancient tradition of the art's inventors, and includes the Abraham motif that Clement had also used: the patriarch as an early astronomer.

The Egyptians were the first to discover astronomy. And the Chaldeans first taught astrology and the observance of nativity. Moreover, Josephus asserts that Abraham taught astrology to the Egyptians.

— Etymologies III.25 ("On its discoverers"), Brehaut 1912. Isidore assigns the birth-horoscope a human origin among the Chaldeans; Tertullian and Lactantius trace the art to the fallen angels. The Abraham note parallels Josephus (Antiquities I.8) and echoes Clement's use of the patriarch as the man who "ascended… to the knowledge of Him who created" the stars.5

Notes

  • Isidore's contribution here is a definition rather than a new argument against astrology: he divides the word into a "natural" and a "superstitious" part, condemning the casting of nativities and keeping the study of the heavens. The Etymologies circulated widely as a reference work in the early Middle Ages (⚠ unverified).
  • Chapter and section numbering varies by edition; Brehaut (1912) numbers the astronomy material III.24–27, and the standard modern translation (Barney, Lewis, Beach, and Berghof, Cambridge, 2006) numbers it within Book III as well. The passages here are quoted from Brehaut, An Encyclopedist of the Dark Ages.
