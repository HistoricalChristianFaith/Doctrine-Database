Origen on astrology

Dates: c. 231 (the discussion survives as Philocalia 23, excerpted from Book III of the Commentary on Genesis, on Genesis 1:14; anthologized c. 360s by Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus); Origen c. 184–253 · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen builds his position on the proof-text the astrologers cited — God's word that the lights of heaven should "be for signs" (Genesis 1:14). His argument rests on a single distinction: the stars signify events but do not cause them. They are a book whose characters the angelic "Powers" (and, exceptionally, inspired men) can read, while the ordinary astrologer, mistaking a sign for its author, was "deceived" into supposing the stars "were the original causes of the events which the Word says the signs indicate." By this distinction Origen holds both divine foreknowledge and human free will: that God (or an angel) can read the future off the sky, he argues, no more compels a man than a written prophecy does.

Astral necessity and free will (Philocalia 23.1–2)

Origen opens by naming what he takes to be at issue. It is not the innocent among the nations who "err in the matter of fate" that troubles him most, but Christians "distracted at the thought that human affairs may be governed by necessity, and cannot possibly be otherwise than is ruled by the stars." If that were true, he argues, the entire moral and evangelical order collapses:

And a consequence of this opinion is the complete destruction of Free Will; and a further result is that praise and blame are unmeaning, and the distinction between acceptable conduct and conduct deserving of blame is lost. And if this be so, there is an end of that Divine judgment which we preach; an end also of God's threats of punishment awaiting sinners; an end, too, of a blessed future for those who have devoted themselves to the higher life.

— Philocalia 23.1. Further, he argues, astral fatalism would make God the author of every sin: the men who "through the revolution of the whole heavens… abandon themselves to the most savage and inhuman practices, murders, and piratical outrages" would be guiltless, and the blame would fall on the Creator who "so ordered the universe." "The champions of these noble principles," Origen concludes, "may free their fellow-men from all guilt, but they make God the cause of all that is bad and blameworthy."1 Basil (c. 378) and Augustine (c. 400–417) also press a free-will and divine-justice objection; Origen sets his beside the claim that the heavens do foreshow events.

Signs, not causes: the Powers read the book (Philocalia 23.6)

Origen's reconciliation of the two follows. He grants that the stars are "for signs" — their motions are so ordered "that observers may take and know the signs from the grouping of all those stars." But he withdraws the reading from human hands and denies that a sign is a cause:

And when I say "observers," I do not mean men (for ability to really learn from the movements of the stars what will befall every individual soul… far transcends human capacity), but I refer to the Powers, which must for many reasons have this knowledge… But men being deceived by certain observations, or even by the teaching of Angels who have lost their proper rank… supposed that they from whom they thought they received the signs were the original causes of the events which the Word says the signs indicate.

— Philocalia 23.6. Origen names two errors here: on his account the astrologer exceeds human capacity (the readers are the angelic "Powers," and the fallen angels who taught men the art did so "to the injury of our race"), and he treats the indicator as the agent. Origen states the logic later: even where a horoscope's predictions come true, the practitioner "will say that they are right, not because the stars cause the events, but only because they indicate them."2

The heavens as one of God's books (Philocalia 23.16, 20)

The image Origen offers in place of astral causation is that of a text. A prophecy foretells without compelling; so does the sky. The future can be written in the stars exactly as it is written in the Prophets, and reading it there no more forces the outcome than reading it in a scroll:

…so neither do the signs, which God appointed to be indicative, interfere with our Free Will; but, like a volume of prophecy, the heavens as a whole, being as it were one of God's books, may contain the future.

— Philocalia 23.16. Answering finally why God should have made such signs at all, Origen says they are set for the blessed and the angelic "Powers who administer human affairs" to read — and describes the heavens as a book:

God created in the heavens beings who have been taught and shall be taught… to read the signs which God gives as if they were written and stamped on the face of the sky… for all of them have been from everlasting to everlasting engraved in the book of the heavens, a book worthy of God.

— Philocalia 23.20. On this account the Magi were not astrologers vindicated but rare men permitted to read a page of that book: a foreknowledge granted, not a fate imposed.3

Notes

  • Origen accepts the proof-text cited for astrology, "let them be for signs" (Genesis 1:14), and reads it strictly: signs, and signs are not causes. He grants that the heavens foreshow, and denies the inference that they therefore compel. See the summary timeline.
  • The distinction lets Origen hold divine foreknowledge and prediction from the heavens without astral necessity. He makes a similar reconciliation of foreknowledge with freedom against determinist readings of Pharaoh's hardening elsewhere in Philocalia 23 and 27 — God's knowing an act does not cause it.
  • Origen locates the human art's origin partly in "the teaching of Angels who have lost their proper rank," as do Tatian and Tertullian, who hold that the fallen angels taught the forbidden sky-craft — see the sons of God and the Nephilim. Origen's own emphasis falls on the art's logic: mistaking a sign for a cause.
  • One of the charges later levelled against Origen was that he held "astronomy is the cause of events," the astral fatalism this piece argues against. An anonymous defence of Origen summarized by Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 117) listed it among the charges it called slanders. The charge is discussed on its own page under the condemnation of Origen.
