Hippolytus of Rome on astrology

Dates: c. 225 (the Refutation of All Heresies, Book IV); Hippolytus c. 170–235 · Wikipedia
Summary: Hippolytus devotes the beginning of Book IV of his Refutation of All Heresies to a technical exposition of the Chaldeans' method, the fixing of the horoscope and the arithmetic of the zodiac, in order to argue against it. His stated aim is double: to show that the "futile art" fails on its own terms, and to show that the teachers he opposes had borrowed the astrologers' machinery for their own systems. He describes the assistant who strikes a metal rim to signal the instant of birth to the star-watcher on a height, then argues that the birth is too indefinite, and the signal too slow, for the horoscope to be fixed — and that once the horoscope is removed, "the entire system of the Chaldeans vanishes along with" it.

The horoscope as the foundation of the art (Refutation IV.3)

Astrology, Hippolytus explains, is built on a single load-bearing operation — catching the point of the zodiac rising on the eastern horizon at the moment of birth. Everything else is derived from it:

The originating principle, and, as it were, foundation, of the entire art, is fixing the horoscope.

— Refutation IV.3, newadvent.org. He lays out the machinery in order to argue from it: on his account the whole scheme depends on this one measurement, so if the measurement cannot be made, nothing built on it stands.1

The metallic rim and the instant of birth (Refutation IV.4)

One passage describes the actual apparatus of the birth-chart. An attendant sits by the woman in labour and, at the moment of delivery, strikes a metal rim — a gong — to signal the astrologer stationed on a height, who then marks the zodiacal sign rising at that instant:

For when they allege that the person sitting beside the woman in travail at the time of parturition gives, by striking a metallic rim, a sign to the Chaldean, who from an elevated place is contemplating the stars, and he, looking towards heaven, marks down the rising zodiacal sign; in the first place, we shall prove to them, that when parturition happens indefinitely, as we have shown a little before, neither is it easy to signify this (birth) by striking the metallic rim.

— Refutation IV.4. His objection is twofold. The birth itself, he argues, "happens indefinitely", so there is no single instant to signal. And if there were one, the sound takes time to travel: as with an axe-blow seen before it is heard, "a sufficiently long time after the descent of the axe, the sound of the stroke is heard," so the signal reaches the star-watcher only after an interval, and the sign he marks is no longer the sign that was rising. On this argument the horoscope is never "infallible." Basil (c. 378) presses a practical objection about the unobservable birth-instant in his own Hexaemeron; Hippolytus works it out through the travel of the signal.2

The horoscope and the rest of the chart (Refutation IV.3)

Hippolytus draws a structural conclusion. Because every other point of the chart — the meridian above, the setting-point at the western horizon, the anti-meridian below — is reckoned from the ascendant, he argues that without the ascendant none of them can be found:

Whence, if the horoscope be removed, it necessarily follows that neither any celestial object is recognisable in the meridian, or at the horizon, or in the point of the heavens opposite the meridian; but if these be not comprehended, the entire system of the Chaldeans vanishes along with (them).

— Refutation IV.3. Having argued that the horoscope cannot be fixed, he concludes that the whole art falls with it.3

Hippolytus's stated purpose in describing the technique (Refutation IV.2)

Hippolytus is explicit that this technical tour has a polemical purpose. Astrology is a "futile art" that trades on false hope:

…we shall not hesitate to furnish a brief refutation respecting these, establishing that the futile art is calculated both to deceive and blind the soul indulging in vain expectations…

— Refutation IV.2. The passage also serves the book's governing thesis, as Hippolytus states it: that the teachings he opposes are not fresh revelations but borrowed pagan learning. Having described the astrologers' apparatus, he later argues that the teachers he names "derived their system" from it, and that the cosmologies of the Gnostic schools rework the Chaldeans' machinery. The account of astrology thus serves two of his aims: arguing against the star-casters, and tracing the sources of the systems he opposes.4

Notes

  • Hippolytus's argument in these chapters is internal and mechanical: rather than pressing the moral consequences of fatalism (that it voids reward and punishment), as Basil and Augustine do at length, he examines the observational procedure of casting a horoscope and argues that the art fails at its first step. He says little here of the moral and free-will objections.
  • His target in Book IV is the divinatory use of star-lore, set out as a source for the systems he opposes, rather than the study of the heavens as such (see the summary timeline).
  • The same problem of the unobservable instant bears on the argument from twins (see Augustine): the interval supposed to distinguish two horoscopes is, on Hippolytus's argument, too small for the astrologer himself to observe or signal.
