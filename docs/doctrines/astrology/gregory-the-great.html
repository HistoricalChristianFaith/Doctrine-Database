Gregory the Great on astrology

Dates: c. 591 (the tenth of the Homilies on the Gospels, preached at Rome for the Epiphany on Matthew 2:1–12); Gregory c. 540–604, pope from 590 · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching on the Magi, Gregory names the error he is answering: the Priscillianists, whom he calls heretics and reports as holding "that every man is born under the influence of a star," and who cited the Bethlehem star — "His star," they said, the star ruling His fate — in support. Gregory answers from the Gospel's word order. The star "went before them until it came and stood over where the Child was"; so it was "not the Child who followed the star, but the star that followed the Child." Man, moreover, was made before and above the luminaries: the stars were made "for man," not man for the stars. And Jacob, born gripping Esau's heel yet unlike him in life, is his counter-example to a fate fixed by a shared birth-instant.

Claims & evidence

The Priscillianists and the Magi's star

Gregory is unusual on this timeline in naming a contemporary party he answers. The Priscillianists — a Spanish movement condemned by Leo the Great and by councils — are reported by Gregory as holding astral fatalism and as citing the Gospel of the Epiphany in its support: if the Lord Himself had a natal star, on their reasoning as he gives it, then every birth is starred. (Whether Priscillian himself taught astral fatalism is discussed on the accommodation page.)

It is well to know that one of the errors of the Priscillianist heretics consists in believing that every man is born under the influence of a star. In order to confirm this notion, they bring forward the instance of the star of Bethlehem that appeared when the Lord was born, and which they call His star, that is, the star ruling His fate and destiny.

— Homilies on the Gospels 10, thedivinelamp (older English translation). Gregory answers the objection not by denying the star but from the details of Matthew's narrative.1

Not the Child who followed the star, but the star that followed the Child

Gregory's answer turns on Matthew's sequence. On his reading the star does not lead the Child through the sky; it goes ahead of the Magi and halts over the Child — behaving as a servant that attends its Lord, not a power that governs Him.

It went before them until it came and stood over where the Child was. Whence we see that it was not the Child who followed the star, but the star that followed the Child, as if to show that the Child ruled over the star, instead of the star ruling over Him.

— Homilies on the Gospels 10, thedivinelamp. Ignatius, Tertullian, and Chrysostom likewise read the Magi's star as subordinating the heavens to Christ rather than submitting Him to them.2

The stars were made for man, not man for the stars

Gregory then reverses the order of dignity the astrologers assume. To let the stars rule a man's destiny, he argues, is to set above him the very things created to serve him — an inversion of the creation order, in which the luminaries are made for the sake of the creature who bears God's image.

Neither was man made for the stars, but the stars for man; and if we say that they rule over his destiny, we set them above him, for whose service they were created.

— Homilies on the Gospels 10, thedivinelamp. Ambrose makes an argument from the order of creation at greater length, noting that the luminaries were made only on the fourth day, after the earth had greened — "the beetle is older than the sun." For both, the life of the world cannot hang on lights kindled to serve it.3

Jacob and Esau: one birth, opposite lives

Gregory closes with the counter-example of twins — the twins Jacob and Esau, born, as he presents it, at a single moment yet leading different lives. If one horoscope fixed one fate, he argues, the two who share it most exactly should share their destinies.

When Jacob came out of his mother's womb, and his hand took hold of his brother Esau's heel, the first could not have been perfectly born without the second immediately following. Yet such was not in after-life the position of these two brothers, whom their mother brought forth at one birth.

— Homilies on the Gospels 10, thedivinelamp. Augustine uses Esau and Jacob the same way (On Christian Doctrine II.22; City of God V.4).4

Notes

  • Each of the homily's arguments has an earlier parallel on this timeline: the star-follows-the-Child reading (cf. Chrysostom), the order-of-creation argument (cf. Ambrose), and the Jacob-and-Esau counter-example (cf. Augustine). The homily attests that these arguments were preached at Rome c. 591, against the Priscillianists, whom the First Council of Braga had condemned in 561.
  • Gregory's report of the Priscillianists' appeal to the Magi's star records the same narrative being read to the opposite conclusion from his own.
  • The passages are quoted from the older English rendering of Homily 10 (on Matthew 2:1–12, for the Epiphany); the standard modern translation is David Hurst, Forty Gospel Homilies (Cistercian, 1990), where this is Homily 10.
