Did some early Christians accommodate astrology?

Disputed reading · on Astrology and the Stars · witness: Firmicus Maternus, with Zeno of Verona, the Valentinian Excerpta ex Theodoto, and the Priscillianists

The passage

Until baptism, they say, Fate is real, but after it the astrologists are no longer right.

— Excerpta ex Theodoto 78 (Valentinian teaching recorded by Clement of Alexandria, c. 200), trans. R. P. Casey, hypotyposeis.org.1

The question

Most of the witnesses on the summary timeline argue against astral fatalism. Alongside them stand Christian-era writers and groups who used, reinterpreted, or were condemned for astrological ideas: the astrologer Firmicus Maternus, Zeno of Verona's baptismal sermon on the zodiac, the Valentinian teaching recorded in the Excerpta ex Theodoto, and the Priscillianists of Spain. The question is how these cases are to be described — as an error condemned by bishops and councils, as a genuine accommodationist strand within early Christianity, or (for the Priscillianists) as a charge laid by opponents — and so how uniform the early Christian response to astrology was.

An error of Priscillian and the pagans, condemned by bishops and council

Astral determinism held by Christians in Spain was a named error, set beside the pagans' teaching and attached to the followers of Priscillian, and was condemned as such.

Held by: Leo the Great (bishop of Rome, Letter 15, 447); the First Council of Braga (561), canons 9–10.2

Writing to Turibius, bishop of Astorga, in 447, Leo warns against "that monstrous division of the whole human body among the twelve signs of the zodiac," and in a sermon he speaks against those "who falsely allege that the entire condition of human life depends on the influences of the stars." The bishops at Braga in 561 anathematize anyone who believes "that human souls and bodies are bound by fatal stars, as the pagans and Priscillian said," and anyone who holds the twelve signs to be distributed "per singula animi vel corporis membra… nominibus patriarcharum adscripta." For these witnesses the belief that the birth-stars bind soul and body has a name and a pedigree: it is what the pagans taught and what Priscillian and his followers were held to teach. Their letters and canons place it outside the faith they profess, and set out in legal form the boundary between that faith and the astrologers' doctrine of fate.

A diversity of attitudes: rejection, accommodation, appropriation

Alongside the polemic, some Christians folded astral fate under Providence, reinterpreted the zodiac devotionally, or practised the art, so that the early Christian response ranged from rejection through accommodation to appropriation.

Held by: Franz Skutsch (1910), as reported and endorsed by Clarence Forbes (ACW 37, 1970); Tim Hegedus (Early Christianity and Ancient Astrology, 2007).3

Skutsch, in Forbes's report, observed "a few cases in ancient and modern times where believing Christians were devotees of astrology, though of course they denied the divinity of the celestial bodies." Hegedus holds that "it is inaccurate to depict early Christian attitudes to astrology entirely in terms of polemical or apologetic refutation." Four bodies of evidence carry the case. Firmicus Maternus wrote the Mathesis (c. 334–337), which subordinates the stars to "the Highest God" and exempts the emperor from their decree, and by his next book was a campaigning Christian (Skutsch held that he was already a Christian, or intimately acquainted with Christianity, when he wrote it). Zeno, bishop of Verona (c. 362–380), in a sermon "on the twelve signs, to the newly-baptized," gives each sign a Christian sense — the Lamb for Aries, the two Fishes as Jew and Gentile made "one people of Christ" — and unfolds "the secrets of the sacred horoscope." The Valentinians whose teaching Clement records held that Fate is real until the font, and that "a strange and new star arose doing away with the old astral decree." And the canons and sermons from Leo to Braga to Gregory the Great, aimed at Christians who read their lives in the stars, attest on this reading a practice within the Church.4

Priscillian condemned for astral fatalism rather than its professor

Priscillian's own surviving works do not profess astral fatalism; the charge comes from his opponents and the later condemnations.

Held by: Henry Chadwick (Priscillian of Avila, 1976); Marco Conti (Priscillian of Avila: The Complete Works, 2010).5

This reading turns from the canons to the works that survive under Priscillian's own name. Read on their own terms, those works do not teach that the stars bind souls and bodies. The astral determinism set beside his name in Leo's letter and in the Braga canons is what his opponents and the later councils attributed to him and his followers, so that the Priscillianist evidence records an accusation made and a doctrine condemned. Priscillian is, on this reading, a figure condemned for the doctrine, and the evidence about what he himself taught is to be sought first in his own writings.
