John Chrysostom on astrology

Dates: c. 390 (the sixth of the Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, preached at Antioch, on Matthew 2:1–2, the coming of the Magi); Chrysostom c. 349–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom's sixth homily on Matthew takes up the objection that the Magi's star warrants astrology. He answers it on three fronts. First, from Christ's purpose: if Christ were born under astrology's law, how did He come to "put down astrology, and take away fate"? Second, and at greatest length, that the Bethlehem star was "not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all," but "some invisible power," as he argues "from its very course," for it moved north to south (not east to west), shone in mid-day while the sun blazed, and started, stopped, hid, and reappeared with the Magi, coming at last to stand over the very head of the Child: conduct "not… of a star, but of some power highly endued with reason." Third, that the Magi's procedure was not astrology: genethlialogy, he argues, reasons from a birth-hour to a fate, while the Magi reasoned from a star to seek a newborn king.

Claims & evidence

The argument from purpose: Christ came to put down astrology and take away fate

Chrysostom opens with an argument from purpose. Matthew's narrative has the Magi led by a heavenly sign, which the astrologer cites as a warrant. For Chrysostom the effect of Christ's coming was the overthrow of the art, not its endorsement, so the sign cannot have been an act of astrology submitting the Lord to the stars.

How then, if He had His birth according to that law, did He put down astrology, and take away fate, and stop the mouths of demons, and cast out error, and overthrow all such sorcery?

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.1, newadvent.org. Other writers draw their own claims from the Nativity star: Ignatius (magic destroyed at the star's appearing), Tertullian (the art permitted "until the Gospel") and Gregory the Great (the star followed the Child) (see the summary timeline and the Magi).1

Not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all

The longest part of the homily argues that the object the astrologer cites was not a star. Chrysostom argues it "from its very course": on his account the star's behaviour departs from the motions of real stars, and so shows a rational power wearing a star's appearance.

Thus, that this star was not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all, as it seems at least to me, but some invisible power transformed into this appearance, is in the first place evident from its very course.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.2, newadvent.org. He then lists the features of its motion on which he rests the point.2

It moved north to south, not east to west

For there is not, there is not any star that moves by this way, but whether it be the sun you mention, or the moon, or all the other stars, we see them going from east to west; but this was wafted from north to south; for so is Palestine situated with respect to Persia.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.2, newadvent.org.3

It shone in mid-day, while the sun was up

For it appears not in the night, but in mid-day, while the sun is shining; and this is not within the power of a star… But this by the excess of its own splendour overcame even the beams of the sun.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.2, newadvent.org.4

It hid and reappeared, and started and stopped with the travellers

On Chrysostom's reading the star keeps pace with a human errand, showing itself and vanishing, halting and resuming, as the Magi's journey required, and finally leaves the height of heaven to stand over one house.

…when they were to move, it moved; when to stand, it stood, dispensing all as need required.

…all which is not like the motion of a star, but of some power highly endued with reason.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.2, newadvent.org. To point out the Child, he argues, it could not act as a star acts, but had to descend: "unless it left that height and came down, and stood over the very head of the young child" — which is what the evangelist means: "Lo, the star went before them, till it came and stood over where the young Child was."5

The direction of the Magi's reasoning

Chrysostom's last argument is that the Magi did not practise astrology. Genethlialogy, he says, predicts a fate from the hour of a birth already known; the Magi did the reverse, seeing a sign and inferring that a king had been born, and going out to find and worship the newborn. That, for him, is not the astrologer's business.

For surely this is not the business of astrology, to know from the stars who are born, but from the hour when men are born to predict what shall befall them.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI.3, newadvent.org. On this argument, whatever the Magi read in the sky, they did not cast a nativity, the specific art Chrysostom condemns, and so the Gospel account gives astrology no warrant.6

Notes

  • Ignatius declares that at the star's appearing every kind of magic was destroyed, and Gregory the Great argues from Matthew's word order; Chrysostom argues from the star's motions that it was "not a star at all."
  • His argument from the direction of the Magi's reasoning grants that a star heralded the birth while denying that this is genethlialogy, which on his account reasons only from a birth-hour to a destiny.
  • Quotations follow the NPNF (first series, vol. 10) translation by George Prevost, revised by M. B. Riddle; the homily is numbered VI in that edition (on Matthew 2:1–2).
