Clement of Alexandria on astronomy and astrology

Dates: c. 198–203 (the Stromata / Stromateis); Clement fl. c. 150–215 · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement distinguishes the study of the heavens from the reading of destiny in them. For Clement, the study of the heavens is not a rival to faith but a rung on the ladder to God: the true "Gnostic" takes from every branch of learning — music, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy — whatever leads to truth, and by astronomy in particular the mind is "elevated along with heaven" and rises, as Abraham did, "to the knowledge of Him who created" the stars. What Clement praises is the observation of the heavenly courses, not the casting of nativities: astrology proper he lists among the human arts the Egyptians and Chaldeans "introduced," and the Chaldean claim to read destiny from the seven planets he reports as a Gentile doctrine, attributed to the Chaldeans, not as his own.

Astronomy "lifts the mind to God" (Stromata VI.11)

In the sixth book of the Stromata, describing how the mature Christian ("the Gnostic") avails himself of all human learning as a preparation for knowledge, Clement runs through the sciences of the quadrivium and assigns each its use. For Clement, astronomy draws the mind up from the earth toward the divine order of the heavens, and from that contemplation to the heavens' Maker — the very ascent he credits to Abraham the star-gazer:

And by astronomy, again, raised from the earth in his mind, he is elevated along with heaven, and will revolve with its revolution; studying ever divine things, and their harmony with each other; from which Abraham starting, ascended to the knowledge of Him who created them.

— Stromata VI.11, newadvent.org (the chapter is numbered X in the ANF/Ante-Nicene edition, "The Gnostic Avails Himself of the Help of All Human Knowledge"). The frame is explicit: "to him knowledge is the principal thing… he applies to the subjects that are a training for knowledge, taking from each branch of study its contribution to the truth." In Clement's scheme astronomy is one of those contributions, a study that lifts the mind toward its Author.1

Astrology among the arts men "introduced" (Stromata I.16)

Clement does not here trace astrology to fallen angels, as Tatian and Tertullian do. In his catalogue of the useful arts the "barbarians" invented and passed to the Greeks, he lists astrology among human discoveries — the work of the Egyptians and the Chaldeans, set beside geometry, augury, and the alphabet:

And barbarians were inventors not only of philosophy, but almost of every art. The Egyptians were the first to introduce astrology among men. Similarly also the Chaldeans.

— Stromata I.16, newadvent.org. Clement's subject here is the antiquity and inventiveness of the "barbarian" nations rather than the merits of the art. Where Tatian, Tertullian, and Lactantius make astrology a thing taught to men by the rebel angels, Clement in this passage treats it as a human institution devised by the peoples of the East.2

The Chaldeans' astral "sympathy" (Stromata VI.16)

When Clement does describe the astrologers' distinctive doctrine — that the courses of the seven planets govern human life — he sets it out as a Gentile teaching, in the mouths of "the Chaldeans," not as his own:

The mathematicians also say that the planets, which perform their course around the earth, are seven; by which the Chaldeans think that all which concerns mortal life is effected through sympathy, in consequence of which they also undertake to tell things respecting the future.

— Stromata VI.16, newadvent.org. The they think and they undertake attribute the claim to the Chaldeans: Clement records what the nativity-casters hold. His own use of the number seven in the same passage is symbolic and scriptural, not predictive.3

Notes

  • Astronomy and astrology. Clement praises the study of the heavenly courses and does not adopt the casting of nativities. A distinction of this kind also appears in Origen (the stars "signify but do not cause"), Basil and Augustine, and in a heading of Isidore of Seville's Etymologies ("the difference between astronomy and astrology").
  • Greek and barbarian learning. Clement takes what he judges true in Greek and barbarian learning as a stepping-stone to Christ, and treats star-science more favourably than the polemicists on this timeline. He does not write an extended attack on genethlialogy of the kind found in Basil's Hexaemeron.
  • Abraham the astronomer. Clement uses the motif of Abraham ascending from the stars more than once (he repeats it for arithmetic in the next chapter), a motif also found in Hellenistic-Jewish writing. In his use the patriarch looks up at the same heavens the Chaldeans read for destiny, and passes through them to the God who made them — contemplation, not divination.
