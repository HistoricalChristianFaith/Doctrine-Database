Eusebius of Caesarea on astrology

Dates: c. 315 (Praeparatio Evangelica Book VI); Eusebius c. 260–339 · Wikipedia
Summary: The whole sixth book of Eusebius's Preparation for the Gospel is a sustained refutation of astral fate (heimarmenē). Eusebius argues the case in his own voice — that a determining Fate destroys free will and, with it, philosophy, religion, law, praise, and blame — and then marshals a dossier of witnesses against the doctrine: the pagans' own oracles (quoted from Porphyry) convicted out of their own mouths, the philosophers Oenomaus, Diogenianus, and Alexander of Aphrodisias, the Syrian astrologer Bardaisan (Bardesanes), and finally Origen on Genesis 1:14. He belongs on this timeline on two counts: for his own anti-fate argument, and because Book VI is the sole source that preserves the Greek text of Bardaisan's diversity-of-customs argument — the very words the earlier Bardaisan witness on this page is quoted through.

Claims & evidence

The oracles' "fate" is mere star-gazing, and it destroys free will

Eusebius opens Book VI by turning the pagans' own oracles against them: their gods, he shows from Porphyry's collection, foretell nothing by any divine power but only guess from the stars "just as men do" — and in doing so they subvert human freedom by chaining not only outward events but the will itself to the heavens.

These things show that their divination is not from any divine power in them, but from observation of the stars according to mathematical principles; so that in this they differ nothing from other men, nor show any work of a higher or more divine nature. But see how they also destroy our free-will, by referring not only external events and things independent of us, but also our own purposes, to the course of the stars.

— Praeparatio Evangelica VI.1, trans. E. H. Gifford (1903), tertullian.org. The book's stated aim is to show the fate-doctrine false "both by the wickedness of their system" and "by the error and falsity of their opinions."1

Astral fate abolishes philosophy, religion, law, and moral responsibility

The heart of Eusebius's own refutation (Chapter VI, "Refutation of the argument in defence of Fate") is a moral reductio: if a man's purposes are extorted by the stars, then virtue earns no praise, vice no blame, and the whole framework of reward and punishment collapses.

For if we must refer not only external events, but also the desires founded upon reason, to the stars and fate, and if human judgements are extorted by some inexorable necessity, there will be an end of your philosophy, an end also of religion: nor is there, as we thought, any praise of virtue for the good, nor any friendship with God, nor any worthy fruit of self-denying toils, if universal causation has been usurped by necessity and fate.

He drives the same point through the law-courts: astral fate would make punishment and honour alike unjust.

Moreover this doctrine would overthrow laws, which are made for the sake of their usefulness to man… Nor will it be right to punish offenders, since for the same reason they have done no wrong, nor to award honours to the doers of the noblest deeds, though these customs of reward and punishment have severally been a chief cause of checking injustice and of readiness to do good.

— Praeparatio Evangelica VI.6, tertullian.org. This is the standard patristic case — free will, providence, and moral responsibility set against astral necessity — that Basil and Augustine press at length.2

Free will is directly felt — we are not "lifeless puppets"

Against the fatalist, Eusebius appeals not to a further argument but to immediate self-awareness: the freedom of the will is a datum of consciousness, as evident as pain or pleasure.

And would it not be most shameless and impudent to say that we are moved like lifeless puppets pulled by strings this way and that by some external power, to will of necessity to do this or that, and to choose other things against our will? For we plainly feel ourselves desiring this or that by our own impulse and motion…

…so we consciously feel ourselves moving of ourselves and of our own purpose, and choosing some things and rejecting others; thus the freedom and independence of the rational and intelligent nature in us is in any case justly to be acknowledged.

— Praeparatio Evangelica VI.6, tertullian.org.3

The carrier of Bardaisan — the diversity-of-customs argument preserved in Greek

Having argued the case himself and quoted the philosophers, Eusebius reaches for the strongest witness he can find from within the astrologers' own discipline: a Syrian who had "pursued his inquiries to the highest point of Chaldean science."

Now therefore it is time to examine also the arguments of the astrologers against the Chaldean sect… And my proofs on this subject I shall present to you from one who is by birth a Syrian, and has pursued his inquiries to the highest point of Chaldean science. The man's name is Bardesanes, and in his Dialogues with his companions it is recorded that he spake as follows…

— Praeparatio Evangelica VI.9–10, tertullian.org. What follows in Chapter X is the long extract in which Bardaisan argues that whole nations under one sky keep incompatible laws — the Seres who never murder, the Brahmans who touch no flesh, the Christians who keep one law "in every division of mankind because of man's free-will." That Greek excerpt is a fragment "preserved in Greek only by Eusebius"; the Bardaisan witness earlier on this timeline is quoted through this transmission (alongside the Syriac original and the parallel in the pseudo-Clementine Recognitions). Eusebius is thus both an anti-fate witness in his own right and the channel through which one of the debate's earliest arguments survives.4

Notes

  • Book VI is deliberately structured as a graded refutation: Eusebius's own argument (chs. I–VI) → the oracles convicted from Porphyry → the pagan philosophers (Oenomaus, Diogenianus, Alexander of Aphrodisias) → Bardaisan the insider astrologer (ch. X) → Origen on "let them be for signs" (Genesis 1:14, ch. XI). His case rests chiefly on the logical and moral grounds — the destruction of free will and responsibility — rather than the demonological aetiology that astrology was taught by the fallen angels (weighed on the fallen-angels argument).
  • Eusebius's puppet-and-strings image and his appeal to the immediate consciousness of choice anticipate the same moves in Gregory of Nyssa's Against Fate and Augustine; the whole book stands in the free-will tradition that runs from Tatian forward.
  • Eusebius appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on the Nephilim, the divine council, the first language, and the canon — here he is added to the astrology timeline, where his sixth book belongs.
