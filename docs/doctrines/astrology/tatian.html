Tatian on astrology

Dates: c. 165–175 (the Address to the Greeks / Oratio ad Graecos); Tatian fl. c. 120–180 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tatian, the Assyrian pupil of Justin Martyr, gives an account of where Fate came from. In chapters 8–9 of his Address to the Greeks he traces astral determinism to the fallen angels turned demons, who "introduced Fate" by teaching men to read the positions of the stars, and who set the animal-figures of the zodiac in the sky to make their own tyranny look like a natural order. Against that bondage he sets the Christian's freedom: "we are superior to Fate… and… we reject its lawgivers."

The demons "introduced Fate" (Address 8)

Tatian's demonology (chapters 7–8) makes the rebel spiritual powers the authors of astral determinism. Having fallen, the demons taught men to read destiny from the sky — a game of chance dressed up as a science:

For, having shown them a plan of the position of the stars, like dice-players, they introduced Fate, a flagrant injustice. For the judge and the judged are made so by Fate; the murderers and the murdered, the wealthy and the needy, are the offspring of the same Fate; and every nativity is regarded as a theatrical entertainment…

— Address to the Greeks 8, newadvent.org. For Tatian, if Fate makes "the judge and the judged" alike, then guilt and desert dissolve, and the astrologer's "nativity" is a puppet-show. He names the source: "Such are the demons; these are they who laid down the doctrine of Fate."1

Tatian on the zodiac (Address 9)

In chapter 9 Tatian turns to the zodiac itself. The constellations — the beasts and figures traced on the sky — are for him not a map of nature's order but the demons' own fabrication, set up so that they might be worshipped as gods and their rule over human life seem written into the heavens:

The delineation of the zodiacal circle is the work of gods.

— Address to the Greeks 9. (Tatian speaks of "gods" here as the pagans do — the beings he has just called fallen demons.) For Tatian the animal-figures in the sky are an artifact, not a cause.2

"We are superior to Fate" (Address 9)

Against that bondage Tatian sets the Christian's release from it. Where the pagan follows "wandering" stars and the demons who lawgive through them, the Christian knows the one unwandering Lord and is therefore free of Fate and its ordinances:

But we are superior to Fate, and instead of wandering demons, we have learned to know one Lord who wanders not; and, as we do not follow the guidance of Fate, we reject its lawgivers.

— Address to the Greeks 9. Tatian plays on the astrologers' own vocabulary (the planets are the "wanderers," planetai): the heavenly bodies they consult are themselves erratic, while the God the Christian follows is not. To "reject its lawgivers" is to renounce both the demons and the astral Fate they legislate — a freedom that is, for Tatian, the very content of conversion.3

Reception and debate

  • Fallen angels as the teachers of the art. Tertullian (c. 203) also names the fallen angels as the source of astrology ("those angels, the deserters from God… were… the discoverers of this curious art"), and in 1 Enoch 8 the Watchers teach astrology to men. The Watcher-instruction tradition ties this doctrine to the sons of God and the Nephilim.
  • Fate and moral responsibility. Alongside the demonology Tatian argues that astral Fate voids moral responsibility, since it makes "the judge and the judged" alike its offspring. Basil ("neither reward for justice, nor punishment for sin"), Gregory of Nyssa, and Augustine each make the same objection at length in their own works.
  • Astronomy and astrology. Tatian calls the "delineation of the zodiacal circle" the demons' work; the targets he names in chapters 8–9 are astral Fate and the worship of the heavenly bodies.
