Firmicus Maternus on astrology

Dates: fl. c. 334–350; the astrological Mathesis c. 334–337, the Christian De errore profanarum religionum c. 346–348 · Firmicus a Sicilian of the senatorial class (vir clarissimus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Firmicus Maternus wrote an eight-book Latin handbook of astrology and, a decade or so later, a Christian tract urging the emperors to stamp paganism out. His Matheseos libri VIII (the "Mathesis," c. 334–337) is a defence and manual of genethlialogy that meets the moral objections pressed against the art by folding astral fate under a supreme God, exempting the emperor from the stars, and demanding that the astrologer himself be a man of blameless piety. Whether he was already a Christian when he wrote it is disputed; by De errore (c. 346–348) he writes as one "formed by the instruction of the sacred readings."

The Mathesis (c. 334–337)

Where Tatian, Tertullian and Lactantius class astrology as a demonic fraud, Firmicus presents it as a sacred science — divina scientia — whose practitioner is a kind of priest. The work answers the stock objections (that fate destroys virtue, that the art is impious) by subordinating the stars to a single supreme divine power rather than abolishing them. For Firmicus the art is real and its decrees binding, and the astrologer's duty is to read them reverently.1

The emperor exempt from the stars (Mathesis II.30)

In one passage Firmicus exempts the reigning emperor. The astrologer, he warns, must never cast the emperor's nativity, because the emperor stands outside the fatal order, his destiny reserved to the highest God:

In fact no astrologer is able to find out anything true about the destiny of the Emperor. For the Emperor alone is not subject to the course of the stars and in his fate alone the stars have no power of decreeing. Since he is master of the whole universe, his destiny is governed by the judgment of the Highest God…

— Mathesis II.30, trans. Jean Rhys Bram. (Latin: "Solus enim imperator stellarum non subiacet cursibus et solus est, in cuius fato stellae decernendi non habeant potestatem.") Firmicus lets the stars decree over everyone but the emperor, whom the "Highest God" governs directly.2

The astrologer must be pure (Mathesis II.30)

Firmicus also sets out a moral charge to the astrologer. The genuine astrologer, addressed through the work's dedicatee Lollianus Mavortius, is to be a man of blameless conduct — sober, honest, no taker of oaths for money, no frequenter of "nocturnal sacrifices," open and available to all:

Esto pudicus integer sobrius, parvo victu, parvis opibus contentus, ne istius divinae scientiae gloriam ignobilis pecuniae cupiditas infamet.

— Mathesis II.30 ("Be modest, upright, sober, content with little food and small means, lest the love of base money defame the glory of this divine science"). He goes on: keep your friends' loyalty; "keep your honesty uncorrupted in all your activities; never stain your self-respect by becoming a false witness"; and "be pure and chaste" before approaching the work. The charge presents the art, rightly practised, as ennobling rather than corrupting its servant.3

De errore profanarum religionum (c. 346–348)

A decade or so later the same author writes as a Christian. De errore profanarum religionum ("On the error of the profane religions") is addressed to the emperors Constantius and Constans and urges them to abolish the pagan cults by force. It quotes the Shema, preaches the blood of the Lamb, and sets the sacraments against the mysteries:

Seek the bread of Christ, the cup of Christ, that the substance of man, scorning the frailty of earth, may be sated with immortal nourishment.

— De errore, trans. Clarence Forbes. Firmicus describes himself as now "formed by the instruction of the sacred readings" (sacrarum lectionum institutione formatus), a sentence in which, Forbes writes, "apparently he alluded to his conversion." De errore does not recant the Mathesis or single out astrology for attack; it is directed at the mystery-cults and the worship of the elements, not at the horoscope.4

Reception and debate

  • Was he already a Christian when he wrote the Mathesis? Clarence Forbes, in the introduction to his ACW translation (1970), reports that "the general and natural view has prevailed that Firmicus was converted to Christianity at some time in the years, approximately a decade, intervening between the composition of the Mathesis and that of the De errore." Franz Skutsch (1910) argued instead "that Firmicus was already a Christian or at least intimately acquainted with Christianity when he wrote the astrological work," from two doxologies in the prooemia of Books 5 and 7; Wendland, as Forbes reports, "called Firmicus' prayers Stoic rather than Christian in tone and origin," and Norden located their source in Neoplatonism. (Forbes credits Mommsen with the dating of the Mathesis to 334–337.)5
  • Forbes also reports, and endorses, an observation of Skutsch's: "Skutsch observed correctly that in spite of St. Augustine's polemic, there were a few cases in ancient and modern times where believing Christians were devotees of astrology, though of course they denied the divinity of the celestial bodies." Whether Firmicus belongs among Christian accommodators of astrology is taken up in the disputed reading on accommodation; see also the summary timeline.6
  • Casting an emperor's nativity (or asking when he would die) was a capital offence under Roman law (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced here); the exemption in Mathesis II.30 also keeps the astrologer clear of that offence.
