Gennadius of Massilia on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 470 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gennadius's handbook of church dogmas devotes a chapter to the soul's origin. It names Origen's pre-existence and the propagation of souls with the body as errors, in a single sentence, and states creationism as what “we say.” The soul is created and infused once the body is already formed. The book circulated for centuries under Augustine's name.

He stands tenth on this timeline, after named Latin writers who argued (Tertullian), asserted (Jerome), declined to decide (Rufinus, Augustine) or assumed (Cassian). Gennadius writes creationism into a handbook of dogmas, with the alternatives labelled as errors.1

The chapter

There is no published English translation of the De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus. The chapter is given here in Latin, with a rendering in the prose below that is our own from that text:

Animas hominum non esse ab initio inter caeteras intellectuales naturas, nec simul creatas, sicut Origenes fingit; neque cum corporibus per coitum seminatas, sicut Luciferiani, Cyrillus et aliqui Latinorum praesumptores affirmant, quasi naturae consequentiam servantes. Sed dicimus creationem animae solum Creatorem omnium nosse, et corpus tantum per conjugii copulam seminari, Dei vero judicio coagulari in vulva et compingi atque formari, ac formato jam corpore animam creari et infundi, ut vivat in utero homo ex anima constans et corpore, et egrediatur vivus ex utero plenus humana substantia.

— De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus, ch. xiv, Latin text. That the souls of men were not from the beginning among the other intellectual natures, nor created all together, as Origen imagines; nor sown with the bodies through intercourse, as the Luciferians, Cyril, and certain presumptuous Latins affirm, as though they were preserving the consistency of nature. But we say that the Creator of all alone knows the creation of the soul; that the body only is sown through the union of marriage, but by God's judgment is coagulated in the womb and compacted and formed; and that, the body being now formed, the soul is created and infused, so that in the womb a man consisting of soul and body lives, and comes forth from the womb alive, full of human substance.

The chapter does four things. The first is a condemnation of Origen by name. The second is a condemnation of traducianism, with a list of holders. The third is a statement that only the Creator knows how a soul is created: creationem animae solum Creatorem omnium nosse — only the Creator knows how a soul is created. The fourth is a chronology: body first, formed by God's judgment in the womb, and then the soul created and infused into it. Tertullian and Gregory of Nyssa had held that soul and body begin together; Gregory's objection to the body-first reading of Genesis was that a man must not be “antecedent and posterior to himself.”2

Four chapters later he uses the doctrine as a premise. Arguing that the soul does not die with the body, he gives as his reason that it was not sown with the body:

Anima humana non cum carne moritur, quia non cum carne, ut superius diximus, seminatur; sed formato in ventre matris corpore, Dei judicio creatur et infunditur, ut vivat homo intus in utero, et sic procedat nativitate in mundum.

— De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus, ch. xviii, Latin text: the human soul does not die with the flesh, because it is not sown with the flesh, as we said above; but, the body having been formed in the mother's womb, by God's judgment it is created and infused, so that the man may live within in the womb and so proceed by birth into the world. Jerome had argued that a propagated soul would share the condition of the beasts, which perish with the body; Gennadius gives non-propagation as the ground of the soul's survival. Tertullian had held that a soul can be handed down and still be immortal.3

The list of traducianists

“Luciferiani, Cyrillus et aliqui Latinorum praesumptores” names three parties. The Luciferians were the rigorist party descended from Lucifer of Cagliari, whose quarrel with the rest of the church was about the reception of former Arians and not about the soul at all. Which Cyril is meant is not stated. The third item is a phrase, not a list.

Rufinus had named “Tertullian or Lactantius among the Latins”; Lactantius's own text denies that souls come from the parents. Jerome had named “Tertullian, Apollinaris, and the majority of the Western divines,” a testimony C. Dubray (1912) judges “exaggerated, as no other writer of prominence is found to advocate Generationism as certain.” In 498 Anastasius II wrote to the bishops of Gaul against people there who held that parents transmit souls.4

Circulation and later citation

The treatise circulated widely as a work of Augustine, printed among Augustine's writings as well as under Gennadius's name; Adrian Fortescue (1909) writes that it is “now universally attributed to Gennadius.”

Aquinas cites it in the sed contra of the article in which he calls traducianism heretical, and supplies the one clause of the chapter that exists in published English:

On the contrary, It is written in De Eccl. Dogmat. xiv that “the rational soul is not engendered by coition.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.118 a.2, source. About a century earlier (c. 1150) Peter Lombard's Sentences heads the chapter on Eve's soul: “De anima mulieris, quae non ex anima viri, quia animae non sunt ex traduce” (on the woman's soul, which is not from the man's soul, because souls are not by propagation). Tertullian had argued the opposite of Eve's soul.5

What the chapter contains

Gennadius states creationism without a proof-text — not John 5:17, not Hebrews 12:9, not Ecclesiastes 12:7 — and without argument: the chapter gives an assertion, a list of errors, and a chronology of the womb. It does not mention the difficulty Augustine had put to Jerome, of how a soul created innocent and joined to flesh comes under condemnation.

Adrian Fortescue (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) describes the treatise as “full of Semipelagianism, either open or implied,” with “original sin carefully evaded.” Gennadius's own notice on Augustine, in his catalogue of Christian writers, praises the great books and adds a clause about the unborn that is variously transmitted: in the NPNF English version, on the resurrection of the dead Augustine “wrote with equal sincerity, and left it to the less able to raise doubts respecting abortions”; Fortescue renders it as the charge that Augustine “caused doubts about the question of unborn children to the simple.”6

The chapter is a handbook statement, not a conciliar definition. Dubray (1912) writes that “there are no strict definitions condemning Generationism as heretical,” while holding that generationism “could not be held without temerity.”7

Reception and debate

  • The chapter numbering used here is Migne's, which is what Aquinas and the standard citations follow. The critical edition — C. H. Turner, “The Liber Ecclesiasticorum Dogmatum,” Journal of Theological Studies 7 (1905–6), 78–99 — numbers the chapters differently, so a reference to “chapter xiv” in older and newer literature may not point to the same text.
  • Under Augustine's name the treatise circulated alongside Augustine's own statements that he did not know the soul's origin.
  • The date given here, c. 470, is the conventional placing of the treatise and is approximate. Gennadius was still writing in the early 490s (his catalogue of Christian writers mentions Pope Gelasius, who reigned 492–496) and died about 496; whether the De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus is early or late in his career is bound up with the unsettled question of which of his lost works it is a part of.
  • Gennadius supplies incidental testimony used elsewhere on this timeline: his catalogue identifies Orosius as the man “sent by Augustine to Hieronymus to teach the nature of the soul,” which is the 415 consultation seen from seventy years later, and it lists Cassian's conferences by subject, including the one that carries Cassian's creationism.
  • For the rest of the witnesses see the summary timeline.
