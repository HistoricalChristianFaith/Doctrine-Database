Origen of Alexandria on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 253 · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen gives the third answer — that souls existed before their bodies — and writes that the church's teaching has not decided the question. In De Principiis he notes that neither traducianism nor any rival is taught “with sufficient clearness” in the rule of faith, and into that gap he puts a theory: rational minds were made equal, freedom made them unequal, and a mind that cooled from the divine warmth became a soul and was assigned a body proportioned to what it had become. He twice marks the whole discussion as exploratory, and presents the theory as a defence of God's justice against the inequality of human lots. Justinian's edict of 543 anathematized the pre-existence of souls.

Two features of the text bear on everything below. First, the Greek of De Principiis is largely lost; what is quoted here is the Latin of Rufinus of Aquileia, who admitted to editing as he translated and whose fidelity Jerome attacked at length. Second, this is Origen's most speculative work, and he says so inside it. Both points are taken up in their own sections below. Origen stands second on this timeline, a generation after Tertullian, and the two answer the same question differently.1

The question as left open in the preface

The preface to De Principiis is a survey of what the apostolic preaching has fixed and what it has left open, and the origin of the soul is placed in the second category:

But with respect to the soul, whether it is derived from the seed by a process of traducianism, so that the reason or substance of it may be considered as placed in the seminal particles of the body themselves, or whether it has any other beginning; and this beginning, itself, whether it be by birth or not, or whether bestowed upon the body from without or no, is not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church.

— De Principiis, Preface §5, source. Two things are already fixed in the same paragraph: the soul has “a substance and life of its own” and will be judged after death, and “every rational soul is possessed of free-will and volition.” What is not fixed, on Origen's account, is where it came from. Whether the question was open when he wrote is the subject of a disputed-reading page on the condemnation-of-Origen timeline. Rufinus (400) and Augustine (in works down to his Retractations) also declined to decide among the options.2

Made equal, made unequal by their own freedom

Origen frames the theory around a difficulty about God. If God is good and just and creates without constraint, He had no reason to make some creatures better than others; yet, Origen observes, endowments and lots are unequal. His answer is that the inequality is not original but earned:

He created all whom He made equal and alike, because there was in Himself no reason for producing variety and diversity. But since those rational creatures themselves, as we have frequently shown, and will yet show in the proper place, were endowed with the power of free-will, this freedom of will incited each one either to progress by imitation of God, or reduced him to failure through negligence. And this, as we have already stated, is the cause of the diversity among rational creatures, deriving its origin not from the will or judgment of the Creator, but from the freedom of the individual will.

— De Principiis II.ix.6, source. The conclusion he draws is a theodicy: “the Creator will neither appear to be unjust in distributing… to every one according to his merits; nor will the happiness or unhappiness of each one's birth, or whatever be the condition that falls to his lot, be deemed accidental.” Origen presents pre-existence in the service of that conclusion. Gregory of Nyssa read the doctrine instead as sliding into the pagan transmigration tales (see his page).3

Jacob in the womb: the proof-text

Origen does not claim that Scripture teaches pre-existence; he holds that it presses the question. Paul's handling of Jacob and Esau — chosen before birth, “neither having done any good or evil” — raises, on Origen's reading, the charge of injustice without explaining how the injustice is avoided, and Origen offers an explanation:

As, therefore, when the Scriptures are carefully examined regarding Jacob and Esau, it is not found to be unrighteousness with God that it should be said, before they were born, or had done anything in this life, the elder shall serve the younger; and as it is found not to be unrighteousness that even in the womb Jacob supplanted his brother, if we feel that he was worthily beloved by God, according to the deserts of his previous life, so as to deserve to be preferred before his brother…

— De Principiis II.ix.7, source. The move is conditional (“if we feel that…”): the text says the choice preceded any deed done in this life, and Origen adds a prior life in which deeds could be done. In the same paragraph he quotes Paul's clause that the purpose of election stands “not of works, but of Him who calls.”4

The soul as a mind grown cold

The mechanism by which a pre-existent mind becomes an embodied soul is carried by an etymology. Origen assembles the scriptural language in which God is a fire, angels a flame, and the saints fervent, against the language in which sinners' love “waxes cold” — and then proposes that the very word soul records a fall in temperature:

… we have to inquire whether perhaps the name soul, which in Greek is termed ψυχή, be so termed from growing cold out of a better and more divine condition, and be thence derived, because it seems to have cooled from that natural and divine warmth, and therefore has been placed in its present position, and called by its present name.

From all which this appears to be made out, that the understanding, falling away from its status and dignity, was made or named soul; and that, if repaired and corrected, it returns to the condition of the understanding.

— De Principiis II.viii.3, source. The derivation of psychē from cooling was not Origen's own: the Stoics used it, as Tertullian reports a generation earlier, to argue that the soul enters the newborn with its first breath of cold air, and Tertullian answered that use by pointing to peoples born in the tropics. In Origen the cooling is moral rather than meteorological, and he puts it as a question (“whether perhaps”).5

Origen applies the theory to the inequality of minds at birth. Since the falling-away “is not the same in all,” some understandings “retain something even of their former vigour, and others again either nothing or a very small amount”: hence “some are found from the very commencement of their lives to be of more active intellect, others again of a slower habit of mind, and some are born wholly obtuse, and altogether incapable of instruction.”6

“Not… in a dogmatic manner”

Origen marks the status of all this twice, and both marks are in the same book. In the middle of the discussion he hands the conclusion back to the reader:

Our statement, however, that the understanding is converted into a soul, or whatever else seems to have such a meaning, the reader must carefully consider and settle for himself…

— De Principiis II.viii.4, source — adding that these views are put forward not in a dogmatic manner but “simply as opinions, treated in the style of investigation and discussion.” And he closes the book on the same note:

We have brought forward as we best could these points regarding the rational soul, as topics of discussion for our readers, rather than as dogmatic and well-defined propositions.

— De Principiis II.viii, closing, source. The anathemas of 543 are phrased against an assertion: “whoever says… let him be anathema.” Whether they reach Origen's own inquiry or a later Origenist teaching is the subject of disputed-reading pages on the condemnation-of-Origen timeline, among them whether his condemned opinions were offered as inquiry and whether the anathemas of 553 condemned sixth-century Evagrian Origenism.7

What we are reading: Rufinus's Latin

The Greek De Principiis survives only in fragments and in quotations made by later critics. The continuous text is the Latin version made by Rufinus of Aquileia around 398, and Rufinus openly described what he had done to it:

I admit that I put something of my own into the work; as I stated in my Preface, I used my own discretion in cutting out not a few passages; but only those as to which I had come to suspect that the thing had not been so stated by Origen himself…

— Rufinus, Apology in Defence of Himself (to Pope Anastasius I) §7, source. Jerome, who had once admired Origen, made this the centre of his attack: the doctrine about the Father, Son and Spirit, he charged, “had been changed by the translator so as to give a more orthodox meaning,” and the selectivity was itself the offence — “if you mean to make a veracious and faithful translation, why do you change some things and leave others untouched?”8

Rufinus's declared policy was to cut passages he suspected had not been “so stated by Origen himself.” The passages quoted on this page that teach pre-existence stand in his Latin; the disclaimers stand at the end of a discussion and at the transition to a new topic. Rufinus's own testimony to Anastasius describes Origen's position as an opinion among others he had found in books. The whole quarrel over the translation is followed on the Origenist controversy timeline.9

The condemnation

The anathemas state the doctrine as a set of assertions:

If anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema.

— The Anathemas Against Origen, i, transmitted with the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553), source. The earlier text is Justinian's edict of 543, whose first anathema describes the mechanism of De Principiis II.viii — souls that “had previously been spirits and holy powers, but that, satiated with the vision of God, they had turned to evil, and in this way the divine love in them had died out.”10

Reception and debate

  • Origen and Tertullian share some ground: both deny that the soul arrives at birth from outside. They divide on whether the soul's history begins at conception or before it, and they treat the same Greek etymology differently: Tertullian answers it, Origen adopts it.
  • Gregory of Nyssa (379) rejected pre-existence on the ground that it slides into the pagan transmigration tales; John of Damascus (c. 743) rejected it by name. How far either commits himself between traducianism and creationism is discussed on their pages.
  • Origen's theodicy and Augustine's account of original sin both address why a newborn is born into disadvantage. Augustine located it in a single fall of the race, and in Letter 166 (415) set out his difficulty in saying how the guilt of that fall reaches a soul God has just made (see his page).
  • Origen also appears as a witness on the intermediate state, the Nephilim, and the Old Testament canon. For the rest of the witnesses on this question see the summary timeline.
