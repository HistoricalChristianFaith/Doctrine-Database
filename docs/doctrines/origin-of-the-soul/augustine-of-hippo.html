Augustine of Hippo on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 415 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine left the question undecided. He enumerated the options in his thirties, declined to choose between them at fifty-eight, sent Jerome a whole treatise asking for the answer at sixty-one, was still saying “I do not know” at sixty-five, and repeated it in his last years when he went back over his own books. He tied the question to the Pelagian controversy over how Adam's guilt reaches a newborn.

He stands eighth on this timeline, one entry after Jerome, to whom he put the question. His position is not a fourth option beside traducianism, creationism, and pre-existence, but a set of conditions he sets for any answer: whatever the answer is, it must leave the soul immaterial, must leave God guiltless of the sinner's sin, and must leave intact the church's practice of baptising infants in haste. He did not find a theory he judged to meet all three, and said so.1

The four opinions, and the decision not to decide

The list is Augustine's own, and he drew it up early. Writing On Free Will in the 390s he set out four ways a soul might come to be in a body, and then deliberately framed his argument so that it would stand whichever of them turned out to be true. When critics complained about the passage some twenty years later, he defended his refusal to give an answer:

For whether all souls are derived by propagation from the first, or are in the case of each individual specially created, or being created apart from the body are sent into it, or introduce themselves into it of their own accord, without doubt this creature endowed with reason, namely, the human soul — appointed to occupy an inferior, that is, an earthly body — after the entrance of sin, does not govern its own body absolutely according to its free will.

— Letter 143 (to Marcellinus), §6, source. He explains that he wrote “after the entrance of sin,” not “after he sinned,” so that the clause would hold whether the sin in question was the man's own or the first parent's, and names his reason:

… the obscurity of this most mysterious subject, the origin of the soul, compels me to do as I have done; let them rather stretch out a friendly hand to me, confessing my ignorance, and desiring to know whatever is the truth on the subject…

— Letter 143, §7, source. In the same letter, expounding Ecclesiastes 12:7 — the dust returns to the earth, the spirit to God who gave it — he notes that a traducianist can read the verse as an exact parallel, the spirit going back to its source as the body goes back to its source, and that the parallel breaks at just one point:

… for there is no difference of opinion as to the original stock of the body, but there is as to the original stock of the soul.

— Letter 143, §8, source. On his reading, since all agree that the body descends, the verse's symmetry does not decide the question of the soul.2

A fifth possibility — that the soul is a piece of God's own substance — he left off the list on purpose, and explained why when Jerome's taxonomy included it. It is not a theory of how the soul gets into a body at all but a theory of what the soul is, and it was the position of the Manichees he was arguing against; he had answered it separately by showing that a fragment of an immutable God could not be corrupted. Setting it aside, the question he wanted settled was narrow and precise: “I desire to know which of the other four opinions we ought to choose.”3

The obstacle: an infant snatched away by death

Augustine connects this question with another. He held — and against the Pelagians insisted — that an infant dying without baptism is not saved, and that what condemns it is guilt derived from Adam. On traducianism the soul descends from Adam's and inherits Adam's condition along with the flesh. On creationism, he writes, he cannot see how the guilt is contracted; and that difficulty is what he gives for not adopting the view he says he is willing to hold:

I ask where can the soul, even of an infant snatched away by death, have contracted the guilt which, unless the grace of Christ has come to the rescue by that sacrament of baptism which is administered even to infants, involves it in condemnation?

— Letter 166 (to Jerome), §6, source. He puts the difficulty twice more in the same letter, each time tightening it. If the newly made soul does not sin, “how is it compatible with the justice of the Creator, that, because of their being united to mortal members derived from another, they are so brought under the bond of the sin of that other, that unless they be rescued by the Church, perdition overtakes them”? And if it does sin, when? He states the dilemma:

For if new souls are made for men, individually, at their birth, I do not see, on the one hand, that they could have any sin while yet in infancy, nor do I believe, on the other hand, that God condemns any soul which He sees to have no sin.

— Letter 166, §21, source. The argument is conditional: it holds that creationism, inherited condemnation and divine justice cannot all three stand without some further account of how the guilt is contracted. At §22 he considers a proposal that in the infant the body alone is the cause of sin, so that an unbaptised infant's soul would be saved while its body is not raised, and answers at §23 that “this opinion I have never heard or read anywhere.”4

He sets out further difficulties for creationism. Infants suffer horribly in this life before they can have done anything; and some souls arrive with so little capacity that their owners “cannot learn even the letters of the alphabet.” If the soul is made to order for the body, someone chose these pairings. Augustine had once answered the suffering-of-infants version of the problem in On Free Will, by appeal to hidden recompense; in Letter 166 he quotes his own earlier answer at length and then withdraws it, because it cannot reach the infants who die unbaptised, for whom there is no recompense to come.5

The consultation of 415

In 415 a young Spanish priest, Orosius, arrived at Hippo from “the remote western coast of Spain.” Augustine sent him on to Bethlehem as a courier. What Orosius carried was not a note but a book: Letter 166, headed in the manuscripts A Treatise on the Origin of the Human Soul, Addressed to Jerome, together with a second long letter on the meaning of James 2:10. The letter opens:

Allow me, therefore, to bring before you a subject which I beseech you not to refuse to open up and discuss with me. Many are perplexed by questions concerning the soul, and I confess that I myself am of this number.

— Letter 166, §3, source. He knows what Jerome holds and says so, referring to the letter Jerome had sent Marcellinus with John 5:17 attached — the letter in which Jerome had told Marcellinus that Augustine, being conveniently in Africa, could explain Jerome's opinion viva voce. Augustine comments on that referral: had he been able to do it, Marcellinus “would not have applied for instruction to you, who were so remote from him.” He offers to be convinced:

Behold, I am willing that the opinion which you hold should be also mine; but I assure you that as yet I have not embraced it.

— Letter 166, §8, source. He repeats the offer later in conditional form: “as to that opinion of yours concerning the creation of new souls, if it does not contradict this firmly grounded article of faith, let it be mine also; but if it does, let it be no longer yours.” He also lists in advance proof-texts — Zechariah 12:1, the psalm about hearts formed severally, Ecclesiastes 12:7 — explaining in each case why, in his view, it does not decide the question, “to prevent you from endeavouring to deliver me from my perplexities by quoting passages such as these.” Jerome had cited these verses for creationism.

Jerome's reply (Letter 134, 416) pleaded the hardness of the times and promised to defend Augustine's opinions as his own; it did not take up the question. The consultation, and the whole long correspondence it belongs to, is narrated on the Jerome–Augustine timeline; Jerome's side is on his page here. A generation later a Marseilles cataloguer, listing the notable Christian writers, still identified Orosius chiefly by this errand: he is “the Orosius who, sent by Augustine to Hieronymus to teach the nature of the soul,” came back with the relics of Stephen.6

The soul's immateriality, and the other options

Augustine held the soul to be immaterial. He states the commitment in the same letter, at length and with a worked example — the whole mind is present at every point of the body at once, which no extended thing can be — and takes trouble to head off a merely verbal dispute about the word:

That the soul is immaterial is a fact of which I avow myself to be fully persuaded, although men of slow understanding are hard to be convinced that it is so.

— Letter 166, §4, source. John Huber, as quoted by Schaff, reports that “as to generationism, he remembers Tertullian, and fears being compelled, like him, to affirm the corporeality of the soul.” Pre-existence Augustine rejects outright — “I do not believe: I reject and protest against such an opinion” — on the ground that if a soul can sin before entering a body there is nothing to stop it sinning in Abraham's bosom after leaving one; the fifth opinion he rejects from the nature of God.

He is reported to have considered, in a later letter to Optatus (Letter 190), a spiritual generationism — one soul kindled from another as one flame is kindled from another without the first being diminished — which would give traducianism's explanatory power without a material transmission. The report is John Huber's nineteenth-century summary of that letter, quoted by Schaff; it has not been checked here against the letter itself (⚠ unverified). The same summary concludes that Augustine “does not get over his indecision,” and the Retractations, written after the letter to Optatus, say the same in his own words.7

The reply to Vincentius Victor (419–420)

Four years after the consultation a young man named Vincentius Victor wrote a creationist account and charged Augustine's hesitation with ignorance. Augustine's reply, On the Soul and Its Origin, states his position at length. Victor held that the soul comes neither from the parents nor from nothing, but “from God”; Augustine answers:

For it is one of two things: if the soul is not derived by natural descent from the parent, it comes out of nothing. To pretend that it is derived from God in such wise as to be a portion of His nature, is simply sacrilegious blasphemy. But we solicit and seek up to the present time some plain passages of Scripture bearing on the point, whether the soul does not come by parental descent…

— On the Soul and Its Origin I.24, source. On this disjunction creationism means creation ex nihilo for each human being, and Augustine asks for “plain passages of Scripture” to decide the point.8

Victor had quoted a psalm at him — man being in honour understands not, and is compared to the senseless cattle. Augustine answers with the mother of the Maccabean martyrs, who told her sons she could not tell how they came into her womb:

Wherefore, I too, on my side, say concerning my soul, I have no certain knowledge how it came into my body; for it was not I who gave it to myself. He who gave it to me knows whether He imparted it to me from my father, or created it afresh for me, as He did for the first man. But even I shall know, when He Himself shall teach me, in His own good time. Now, however, I do not know; nor am I ashamed, like him, to confess my ignorance of what I know not.

— On the Soul and Its Origin I.25, source. To the charge of ignorance he sets the example of the Maccabean mother, who confessed the same ignorance.9

Late in the first book he states the terms on which he would accept the creationist case:

As for the opinion, that new souls are created by inbreathing without being propagated, we certainly do not in the least object to its maintenance — only let it be by persons who have succeeded in discovering some new evidence, either in the canonical Scriptures, in the shape of unambiguous testimony towards the solution of a most knotty question, or else in their own reasonings, such as shall not be opposed to catholic truth, but not by such persons as this man has shown himself to be.

— On the Soul and Its Origin I.33, source. In the chapter that follows he does not forbid creationism; he lists four things a creationist may not affirm:

All, therefore, who wish to maintain that new souls are rightly said to be breathed into persons at their birth, and not derived from their parents, must by all means be cautious on each of the four points which I have already mentioned. That is to say, do not let them affirm that souls become sinful by another's original sin; do not let them affirm that infants who died unbaptized can possibly reach eternal life and the kingdom of heaven by the remission of original sin in any other way whatever; do not let them affirm that souls had sinned in some other place previous to their incarnation, and that on this account they were forcibly introduced into sinful flesh; nor let them affirm that the sins which were not actually found in them were, because they were foreknown, deservedly punished, although they were never permitted to reach that life where they could be committed.

— On the Soul and Its Origin I.34, source. He closes the discussion: if no decisive authority can be found, “it is, indeed, better for a man to confess his ignorance of what he knows nothing about.”10

The Retractations

Going through his own books near the end of his life, he came to On Free Will and the four opinions, and wrote of the soul that “as to its origin… I did not know then, and I still do not know.” The principle behind the confession he had stated years earlier, in the treatise he sent to that same Marcellinus who had written to Jerome: on an obscure subject where no clear scriptural proof can be had, “the presumption of man ought to restrain itself; nor should it attempt anything definite by leaning to either side.” He draws the corollary that if a point were necessary to salvation, Scripture would have made it plain.11

Later Latin witnesses on this timeline state creationism: Cassian (c. 426–429) in passing, Gennadius (c. 470) as the church's teaching, and Anastasius II (498) in a letter against its rival. Gennadius's handbook circulated for centuries under Augustine's name.12
