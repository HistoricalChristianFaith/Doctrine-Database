Rufinus of Aquileia on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 400 · Wikipedia
Summary: Rufinus of Aquileia sets out the question as three opinions, with named partisans, and then declines to choose between them. Writing to Pope Anastasius I under suspicion of Origenism, he declares that after reading each view he cannot hold any of them as certain, and that the only thing the church requires him to believe is that God makes souls as He makes bodies. In 400, answering a charge of Origenism, he treated the origin of the soul as undecided; Jerome answered that the refusal was an evasion.

He stands sixth on this timeline, and unlike the other witnesses on it he does not argue for an answer. His three-way division of the question is also the frame of the modern accounts of Schaff and Dubray.1

How the question came to be put to him

Rufinus had spent some six years at Alexandria under Didymus the Blind and nearly two decades on the Mount of Olives, and had come home to Italy an admirer of Origen. Pressed by the monk Macarius, he put Origen's Peri Archon (On First Principles) into Latin, smoothing as he went what he took to be heretical interpolations, and prefaced it by pointing at Jerome as the translator who had done the same before him. That preface began his quarrel with Jerome. When Pope Siricius died in 399 and Anastasius I succeeded him, Rufinus's accusers pressed their case at Rome, and Rufinus — pleading that he could not come in person — sent a written profession of faith instead.2

The origin of the soul was among the matters raised: Peri Archon teaches that souls are minds that cooled, fell, and were assigned bodies to match what they had become. The relevant paragraph of the Apology is the sixth of eight.

The three-way division

I am next informed that some stir has been made on the question of the nature of the soul. Whether complaints on a matter of this kind ought to be entertained instead of being put aside, you must yourself decide. If, however, you desire to know my opinion on the subject, I will state it frankly. I have read a great many writers on this question, and I find that they express various opinions. Some of those whom I have read hold that the soul is infused together with the material body through the channel of the human seed; and of this they give such proofs as they can. I think that this was the opinion of Tertullian or Lactantius among the Latins, perhaps also of a few others. Others assert that God is every day making new souls, and infusing them into the bodies which have been framed in the womb; while others again believe that the souls were all made long ago, when God made all things of nothing, and that all that he now does is to plant out each soul in its body as it seems good to him. This is the opinion of Origen, and of some others of the Greeks.

— Apology in Defence of Himself (to Pope Anastasius I), §6, source. The list gives three answers, distinguished by where the soul comes from, each with named holders. Jerome's taxonomy in Letter 126 (c. 411) runs to five branches; Gennadius's (c. 470) states the creationist answer as the church's and names the two rejected views. Schaff and Dubray set out the debate in the same three-way division.3

The refusal

Then he declines all three:

For myself, I declare in the presence of God that, after reading each of these opinions, I am up to the present moment unable to hold any of them as certain and absolute; the determination of the truth in this question I leave to God and to any to whom it shall please him to reveal it. My profession on this point is therefore, first, that these several opinions are those which I have found in books, but, secondly, that I as yet remain in ignorance on the subject, except so far as this, that the Church delivers it as an article of faith that God is the creator of souls as well as of bodies.

— Apology in Defence of Himself, §6, source. In the last clause Rufinus draws the line where he holds the church has drawn it: that God is the creator of souls is delivered “as an article of faith”; by what route He makes each soul is not. Origen had made a similar statement in the preface to the book Rufinus had translated, where the soul's origin is listed among the things the church's preaching has not defined.4

The paragraphs before it in the Apology (§§4–5) treat the resurrection — “this very flesh in which we now live … without the loss of a single member” — and the devil's eternal punishment.5

Jerome's answer: a question with a name attached

Two years later Jerome worked through the Apology line by line in Book II of his own Apology against Rufinus, and the paragraph on the soul got a chapter to itself. He turned the question into a Christological one — not “where do souls come from?” but “where did that soul come from?”:

That soul which Jesus took upon him, did it exist before it was born of Mary? Was it created together with the body in that original Virgin nature which was begotten by the Holy Spirit? Or, when the body was already formed within the womb, was it made all at once, and sent down from heaven? I wish to know which one of these you choose as your opinion. If it existed before it was born from Mary, then it was not yet the soul of Jesus; and it was employed in some way, and, for a reward of its virtues, it was made his soul. If it arose by traduction, then human souls, which we believe to be eternal, are subject to the same condition as those of the brutes, which perish with the body. But if it is created and sent into the body after the body has been formed, tell us so simply, and free us from anxiety.

— Jerome, Apology Against Rufinus II.4, source. The three limbs of the question are Rufinus's own three opinions, applied to Christ. Later in the same book Jerome lists the pre-existence of the Saviour's soul among Origen's errors; on his reasoning, Rufinus, having declined to rule out pre-existence in general, has declined to rule it out here. Jerome sums up the reply: “None of these answers will you give us.”6

Later in the book Jerome objects to the choice to publish the confession of ignorance:

If he is ignorant on the subject for which ill-reports are current against him, what need was there for him to send an Apology, which contains no defense of himself, but only a confession of his ignorance? This course is calculated to sow a crop of suspicions, not to calm them.

— Jerome, Apology Against Rufinus II.10, source. He goes on to compare Rufinus to the sceptical Academics, Arcesilaus and Carneades, and to say he outdoes them, since they at least invented a doctrine of probability to temper their agnosticism. Then he suggests that the ignorance is selective:

Take care! You may find people saying that the reason for your confession of your ignorance of the three is that you do not wish to be compelled to condemn one. You spare Tertullian and Lactantius so as not to condemn Origen with them.

— Jerome, Apology Against Rufinus II.10, source. And a theological objection underneath the personal one: “are you astonished if your brethren are scandalized when you swear that you know nothing of a thing which the churches of Christ profess to know?” Rufinus had written that the church delivers as an article of faith only that God is the creator of souls; Jerome holds that the churches “profess to know.”7

Others on the question's standing

Augustine, from 415, also professed in print that he could not decide the question, asked Jerome for help, and wrote in his last years that he did not know. C. Dubray (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that “there are no strict definitions condemning Generationism as heretical,” while holding it “certainly opposed to the doctrine of the Church.”8

Anastasius condemned the translation and disowned the translator, writing to John of Jerusalem that he neither knew nor wished to know what Rufinus was doing or where he was living.9

The attribution to Lactantius

Rufinus assigns the seed-borne soul to “Tertullian or Lactantius among the Latins” (“I think that this was the opinion”). Tertullian argues for it in A Treatise on the Soul; Lactantius, in the chapter in which he treats the question, says souls come from neither parent nor from both. Jerome questioned the attribution — “I am not aware of having read that Lactantius spoke of the soul as planted at the same time as the body. But, as you say that you have read it, please to tell me in what book it is to be found.” The fuller account is on Lactantius's page.10

Reception and debate

  • Rufinus also appears as one of the two principals in the quarrel that produced this document: see the Origenist controversy timeline and his page there, where the translation of Peri Archon, the interpolation thesis behind it, and the war with Jerome are treated at length. The Apology to Anastasius is discussed there as a moment in that quarrel; this page concerns its paragraph on the soul.
  • Rufinus treats the article that God is “the creator of souls as well as of bodies” as compatible with all three opinions, including the traducian one, on which God made the one soul from which the rest descend. He does not say how much the article excludes.
  • Dubray (1912) lists Rufinus, with Gregory of Nyssa, Macarius, and Nemesius, among those who “seem to prefer” generationism, noting that their views “are not always clear.” In the paragraph quoted above Rufinus attributes the traducian view to others and declines to hold it, or either of the other two, as certain.
  • Jerome later told Marcellinus and Anapsychia that his own published opinion on the soul's origin was to be found “in my brief writings against Ruffinus” — that is, in this exchange, where Book II mostly puts questions to Rufinus. Jerome's creationist statements are gathered on his page.
  • Rufinus reports the daily-creation opinion without citing John 5:17.
  • For the rest of the witnesses see the summary timeline.
