Lactantius on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 325 · Wikipedia
Summary: Lactantius gives the earliest Latin argument for creationism among the witnesses on this timeline. In On the Workmanship of God he holds that what the soul is may never be settled, but that where it comes from can be: not from the father, not from the mother, not from both, since nothing can be detached from a simple and imperceptible thing. Souls are therefore made by God alone. He agrees with Tertullian that the soul is present from conception and against the philosophers who have it inhaled at birth — in 400 Rufinus listed him, with Tertullian, among those who hold that the soul comes through the seed.

The treatise is an argument from design: chapter after chapter on the structure of the eye, the hand, the voice, the viscera, each turned into evidence of a maker. The soul comes last, and it comes as the hardest case — the one part of man whose workmanship cannot be inspected. Lactantius stands third on this timeline, roughly a century after Tertullian, whom he does not name.1

What the soul is may never be known

Lactantius opens the subject by granting that the philosophers have not agreed on what the soul is:

It remains to speak of the soul, although its system and nature cannot be perceived… But what the soul is, is not yet agreed upon by philosophers, and perhaps will never be agreed upon. For some have said that it is blood, others that it is fire, others wind, from which it has received its name of anima, or animus, because in Greek the wind is called anemos—and yet none of these appears to have spoken anything.

— On the Workmanship of God, ch. 17, source. He answers the three candidate materials one by one, by the method he also uses on the origin question. He does not claim to know what the soul is; he claims to know what it is not, and to know that from features of the case that any observer can check. The soul is not blood, because a lamp is not oil: “they are plainly different, but the one is the nourishment of the other.” It is not fire, because fire is visible, insensate, and burns to the touch, and the soul is none of the three. It is not breath, because “the soul is produced much before air can be conceived in the mouth.”2

The last of those three answers is a claim about when:

For it is not introduced into the body after birth, as it appears to some philosophers, but immediately after conception, when the divine necessity has formed the offspring in the womb; for it so lives within the bowels of its mother, that it is increased in growth, and delights to bound with repeated beatings. In short, there must be a miscarriage if the living young within shall die.

— On the Workmanship of God, ch. 17, source. The evidence is the same as Tertullian's: the child moves in the womb, and a child that dies there was alive there. So is the target — Varro's definition of the soul as “air conceived in the mouth, warmed in the lungs, heated in the heart, diffused into the body,” and behind it the Stoic doctrine of ensoulment at the first breath. On the timing of ensoulment Tertullian and Lactantius are on the same side, against the same opponents.3

Souls come from neither parent, nor from both

On the origin itself Lactantius gives an answer. He asks whether the soul comes from the father, the mother, or both, and answers by setting aside all three parental options at once:

A question also may arise respecting this, whether the soul is produced from the father, or rather from the mother, or indeed from both. But I think that this judgment is to be formed as though in a doubtful matter. For nothing is true of these three opinions, because souls are produced neither from both nor from either. For a body may be produced from a body, since something is contributed from both; but a soul cannot be produced from souls, because nothing can depart from a slight and incomprehensible subject. Therefore the manner of the production of souls belongs entirely to God alone.

— On the Workmanship of God, ch. 19, source. The middle clause is an argument from the soul's simplicity: generation works by something being detached and contributed, and there is nothing to detach from a thing that has no parts and no bulk. Bodies can shed seed because bodies are quantities; a soul cannot, because it is not. The argument grants that the mechanism of bodily inheritance is intelligible and denies that the soul is the sort of thing it can operate on. Aquinas (c. 1268) argues, in the vocabulary of subsistence, that an immaterial subsistent thing cannot be produced by a power working in matter, so it can only be created (see his page).4

The argument rests on the soul's simplicity. Tertullian holds that the soul possesses body, form, and the three dimensions, and so can be carried by a seed. Neither writer engages the other.

The parents' whole contribution

Lactantius then states the positive doctrine and sets out what he holds human parenthood accomplishes:

From this it is evident that souls are not given by parents, but by one and the same God and Father of all, who alone has the law and method of their birth, since He alone produces them. For the part of the earthly parent is nothing more than with a sense of pleasure to emit the moisture of the body, in which is the material of birth, or to receive it; and to this work man's power is limited, nor has he any further power. Therefore men wish for the birth of sons, because they do not themselves bring it about. Everything beyond this is the work of God—namely, the conception itself, and the moulding of the body, and the breathing in of life, and the bringing forth in safety…

— On the Workmanship of God, ch. 19, source. Two supporting arguments are attached. The first is from ignorance as evidence: a father cannot be said to transmit what he does not know he is transmitting — he “in no way perceives that he has transmitted or breathed a soul from his own; nor, if he perceives it, comprehends in his mind when or in what manner that effect is produced.” The second is from wisdom's not being inherited: God “gives to man wisdom, which no earthly father can by any means give; and therefore it often happens that foolish sons are born from wise parents, and wise sons from foolish parents.” Tertullian's positive case was that children resemble their parents in disposition, so that something of the soul must be handed down; Lactantius's observation is that in wisdom children often do not resemble their parents.5

He closes the passage with a line of Lucretius — “we are all sprung from a heavenly seed, all have that same Father” — and with the gloss that “nothing but what is mortal can be generated from mortals.”6

Rufinus's listing of Lactantius among the traducians

Around the year 400, defending himself before Pope Anastasius I, Rufinus of Aquileia set out the three views on the soul's origin and named Lactantius, with Tertullian, under the first:

Some of those whom I have read hold that the soul is infused together with the material body through the channel of the human seed; and of this they give such proofs as they can. I think that this was the opinion of Tertullian or Lactantius among the Latins, perhaps also of a few others.

— Rufinus, Apology in Defence of Himself (to Pope Anastasius I) §6, source. Rufinus's attribution is hedged: “I think that this was the opinion.” Lactantius's ch. 19, the chapter in which he addresses the question, states that “souls are produced neither from both nor from either.” Jerome, answering Rufinus, writes that he is “not aware of having read that Lactantius spoke of the soul as planted at the same time as the body,” and asks him to “please to tell me in what book it is to be found.”7

Rufinus, Jerome, and Gennadius all set out the options in three or five branches and attach names to each; the modern accounts of Schaff and Dubray draw on these lists.8

Reception and debate

  • Lactantius argues for creationism without a proof-text; his case rests on the soul's simplicity and on what he holds parents cannot do. John 5:17, “my Father worketh hitherto,” is cited for creationism on this timeline by Jerome (c. 411) and Anastasius II (498).
  • Lactantius calls the soul “slight and incomprehensible” and denies it can shed anything; he also says of it that it is “nourished by the moisture of the blood, as light is by oil,” an image he uses to distinguish the soul from blood.
  • Lactantius also appears as a witness on the Old Testament canon, the Nephilim, and purgatory. For the rest of the witnesses on this question, see the summary timeline.
