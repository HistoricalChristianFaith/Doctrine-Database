John of Damascus on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 749 · Wikipedia
Summary: In the chapter “Concerning Man” of John of Damascus's Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, two sentences address the soul's origin: the reasoning soul is God's gift “by His own inbreathing,” and body and soul were formed “at one and the same time, not first the one and then the other, as Origen so senselessly supposes.” The first expounds the making of the first man; the second states, as Gregory of Nyssa had, that soul and body begin together. The chapter does not discuss whether later souls come from the parents or are created afresh.

He stands thirteenth on this timeline, and his entry comes from a manual that sets out doctrine in order, without polemical apparatus.1

“By His own inbreathing”

The account of man's creation follows immediately on the making of the angels and of the sensible world, and its point is that man is the mixture of the two — the connecting link between the visible and invisible natures. The two halves are then assigned to their sources:

Now this being the case, He creates with His own hands man of a visible nature and an invisible, after His own image and likeness: on the one hand man's body He formed of earth, and on the other his reasoning and thinking soul He bestowed upon him by His own inbreathing, and this is what we mean by after His image. For the phrase after His image clearly refers to the side of his nature which consists of mind and free will, whereas after His likeness means likeness in virtue so far as that is possible.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.12, “Concerning Man”, source. This is an exposition of Genesis 2:7, and the sentence concerns the first man; it identifies the image of God with the mind and the free will. The chapter does not say whether later souls are likewise breathed in by God.2

“At one and the same time”

Then, in a single sentence:

Further, body and soul were formed at one and the same time, not first the one and then the other, as Origen so senselessly supposes.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.12, source. The doctrine and the shape of the denial match those of Gregory of Nyssa, who in 379 had set the two rival opinions against each other in On the Making of Man and rejected both: “as man is one, the being consisting of soul and body, we are to suppose that the beginning of his existence is one, common to both parts, so that he should not be found to be antecedent and posterior to himself.” Gregory reasoned from the unity of the man, from the impropriety of a maker who does the halves in turn, and from the wheat-grain that contains the whole plant potentially; John's sentence gives the conclusion without the reasoning.3

Gregory had described the pre-existence doctrine without naming its author, gesturing only at “those … who have dealt with the question of principles.” John names Origen; Justinian's edict of 543 had anathematized the doctrine and Origen by name. Pre-existence is the only error on this subject that the chapter mentions.4

Simultaneity elsewhere on this timeline

On this timeline the simultaneity of soul and body is held by a traducianist and by a creationist.

Tertullian argued it about 170 years before Gregory (c. 210–213), in a chapter headed “Soul and Body Conceived, Formed and Perfected in Element Simultaneously”: “We indeed maintain that both are conceived, and formed, and perfectly simultaneously, as well as born together; and that not a moment's interval occurs in their conception, so that, a prior place can be assigned to either.” His reason was that death is the separation of soul and body, so life must be their conjunction, and the conjunction must therefore be as instantaneous as the separation. On that basis he concluded to traducianism: if the soul begins when the body begins, and the body comes from the parents, the soul comes the same way. Simultaneity was his premise for the propagation, not an alternative to it.5

Among creationists, Gennadius has the body sown first and the soul “created and infused” into a body already formed, and Aquinas places the creation of the intellectual soul at the end of human generation, after a succession of prior forms; Lactantius, also a creationist, holds against the philosophers that the soul is not introduced after birth “but immediately after conception.”6

John's chapter has no sentence on whether a soul now beginning comes from its parents' souls or is made afresh, no proof-text on either side, and no opponent named except Origen.

The soul the handbook describes

John does supply, at length, a definition of what a soul is:

The soul, accordingly, is a living essence, simple, incorporeal, invisible in its proper nature to bodily eyes, immortal, reasoning and intelligent, formless, making use of an organised body, and being the source of its powers of life, and growth, and sensation, and generation, mind being but its purest part and not in any wise alien to it … further it enjoys freedom and volition and energy, and is mutable, that is, it is given to change, because it is created.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.12, source. Tertullian's definition makes the soul “immortal, possessing body, having form … evolved out of one (archetypal soul)”; John's soul is incorporeal and formless. John qualifies the incorporeality: angels, demons, and souls are incorporeal “by free gift, and in comparison with the denseness of matter,” while God alone is so by nature.

Aquinas (c. 1268) argues that an immaterial effect cannot be produced by an active power existing in matter; John draws no conclusion about generation from his definition.7
