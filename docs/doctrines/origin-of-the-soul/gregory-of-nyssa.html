Gregory of Nyssa on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 395 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory of Nyssa addresses the question of when the soul begins. In On the Making of Man he sets the two current opinions against each other — souls living in a society of their own before they fall into bodies, and souls formed after the clay figure was moulded — rejects both, and puts in their place a single beginning for soul and body together, on the model of a seed that already contains the whole plant. Whether he also takes a side on where the soul comes from is read differently: C. Dubray (1912) lists him among those who “seem to prefer” generationism; William Moore and H. A. Wilson (1893) summarize the chapters as maintaining that body and soul come into existence together.

He stands fifth on this timeline. The relevant chapters are xxviii and xxix of a treatise Gregory wrote to complete his brother Basil's unfinished work on the six days of creation.1

The question as the churches were putting it

Gregory opens by naming the dispute as a live one and stating both sides. The first is pre-existence, and he describes it in the language of a settled society with its own moral order:

Some of those before our time who have dealt with the question of principles think it right to say that souls have a previous existence as a people in a society of their own, and that among them also there are standards of vice and of virtue, and that the soul there, which abides in goodness, remains without experience of conjunction with the body; but if it does depart from its communion with good, it falls down to this lower life, and so comes to be in a body.

— On the Making of Man, xxviii.1, source. Nobody is named. The phrase “those … who have dealt with the question of principles” echoes the title of Origen's On First Principles, and the doctrine described — equality before the fall, descent from the good, embodiment as the consequence — corresponds to the account in that work.2

The second opinion is the literalist reading of Genesis 2:7 — God made the clay figure first and breathed into it afterwards — and Gregory states it with its conclusion:

Others, on the contrary, marking the order of the making of man as stated by Moses, say, that the soul is second to the body in order of time, since God first took dust from the earth and formed man, and then animated the being thus formed by His breath: and by this argument they prove that the flesh is more noble than the soul; that which was previously formed than that which was afterwards infused into it: for they say that the soul was made for the body, that the thing formed might not be without breath and motion.

— On the Making of Man, xxviii.1, source. The argument he reports is that what exists for the sake of something else is the lesser thing, as raiment is less than the body it clothes. Gregory does not dispute the inference; he rejects its premise, that either part came first at all.3

Against pre-existence: the slide into transmigration

His case against the first opinion is that its logic does not stop. Once a soul can fall from a higher state into a body by wickedness, the same logic keeps working: the embodied life offers more occasions of sin, so the next descent is lower still, into the brute, then the insensible, then the inanimate, and finally into nothing — while the return journey the doctrine also requires would have to make the life of a bush better than the incorporeal state it fell from. Along the way he pauses to say what he thinks of the pagan metempsychosis-tales the doctrine keeps company with:

They tell us that one of their sages said that he, being one and the same person, was born a man, and afterwards assumed the form of a woman, and flew about with the birds, and grew as a bush, and obtained the life of an aquatic creature — and he who said these things of himself did not, so far as I can judge, go far from the truth: for such doctrines as this of saying that one soul passed through so many changes are really fitting for the chatter of frogs or jackdaws, or the stupidity of fishes, or the insensibility of trees.

— On the Making of Man, xxviii.3, source. Gregory reaches transmigration from the fall of souls through the several steps of descent set out above.4

Neither first: one man, one beginning

Having rejected both, he states a middle position:

The doctrine of both, then, is equally to be rejected; but I think that we ought to direct our own doctrine in the way of truth between these theories.

— On the Making of Man, xxviii.8, source. The positive doctrine follows in the next chapter, and its argument is from the unity of the man rather than from any verse. A human being is one thing made of two; if the two have different starting-dates then the one thing is older than itself:

Nor again are we in our doctrine to begin by making up man like a clay figure, and to say that the soul came into being for the sake of this; for surely in that case the intellectual nature would be shown to be less precious than the clay figure. But as man is one, the being consisting of soul and body, we are to suppose that the beginning of his existence is one, common to both parts, so that he should not be found to be antecedent and posterior to himself, if the bodily element were first in point of time, and the other were a later addition.

— On the Making of Man, xxix.1, source. A second argument is added from the maker's side: a God who had to do the halves in turn would be “in some way imperfect, as not being completely sufficient for the whole task at once.”5

The wheat-grain

The image Gregory uses is agricultural; with it he explains how the soul can be present from the first without being visible, and excludes any later arrival:

But just as we say that in wheat, or in any other grain, the whole form of the plant is potentially included — the leaves, the stalk, the joints, the grain, the beard — and do not say in our account of its nature that any of these things has pre-existence, or comes into being before the others, but that the power abiding in the seed is manifested in a certain natural order, not by any means that another nature is infused into it — in the same way we suppose the human germ to possess the potentiality of its nature, sown with it at the first start of its existence, and that it is unfolded and manifested by a natural sequence as it proceeds to its perfect state … so that it is not true to say either that the soul exists before the body, or that the body exists without the soul, but that there is one beginning of both.

— On the Making of Man, xxix.3, source. Gregory continues in the same chapter: the powers of the soul are there from the beginning and grow with the instrument, nutrition first, then sensibility, then reason “like a fruit” — not because a new thing has arrived, but because the recipient has grown large enough to show what was in it.6

Nothing dead comes out of a living body

Then comes the argument from the living seed:

For since it is not from a dead body that the potentiality for conception is secreted, but from one which is animate and alive, we hence affirm that it is reasonable that we should not suppose that what is sent forth from a living body to be the occasion of life is itself dead and inanimate; for in the flesh that which is inanimate is surely dead; and the condition of death arises by the withdrawal of the soul. Would not one therefore in this case be asserting that withdrawal is antecedent to possession — if, that is, he should maintain that the inanimate state which is the condition of death is antecedent to the soul?

— On the Making of Man, xxix.5, source. He backs it with an empirical test — what is warm and in motion is alive, what is chill and motionless is dead, and the thing in question is warm and operative — and with a second horticultural case: kernels and torn-off roots put into the ground are “not deprived by death of the vital power which naturally resides in them,” and the earth does not implant that power from outside but only feeds it, “for surely then even dead wood would proceed to growth.”7

Comparison with Tertullian, and how Gregory has been classified

Tertullian also reasons from a living seed to a living product and holds that soul and body begin together at conception. Two differences appear in the texts.

First, Tertullian's living seed carries a shoot of Adam's soul, so that the race is one stock; in these chapters Gregory speaks of a potentiality that unfolds, and does not say that it is a portion of a parent's soul. He places the pre-existence of the race in the divine foreknowledge, in which “all the fullness of human nature had pre-existence.”8

Second, Tertullian holds the soul to have body, form, and dimension, so that it can be carried in a seed. Gregory's human germ possesses “the potentiality of its nature,” a power manifested in order, on the analogy of a grain that contains the whole plant potentially.

C. Dubray's article “Traducianism” in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1912) lists Gregory — with Macarius, Rufinus, and Nemesius — among those who “seem to prefer” generationism, adding that their views “are not always clear.” The translators of the NPNF edition, William Moore and H. A. Wilson (1893), summarize the chapters as maintaining that body and soul come into existence together, “potentially in the Divine will, actually at the moment when each individual man comes into being by generation.”9

John of Damascus (c. 743) writes in the Exact Exposition that body and soul were formed “at one and the same time, not first the one and then the other, as Origen so senselessly supposes” (see his page).10

Reception and debate

  • The opinions Gregory sets against each other in these chapters are pre-existence and a literal reading of the order of Genesis 2:7; he does not discuss Tertullian's traducianism by name.
  • Gregory does not discuss creationism as a stated position. xxix.3 says that in the human germ no other nature “is infused into it,” and xxix.1 places the whole race's pre-existence in God's foreknowledge.
  • The chapters are also an argument about embryology: the argument from the living seed draws on an ancient view of generation in which the seed is a portion secreted from a living body.
  • Justinian anathematized the pre-existence of souls taught by Origen in 543. John of Damascus (c. 743) states the simultaneity of soul and body; Aquinas (c. 1268) called the transmission of the intellectual soul with the semen heretical.
