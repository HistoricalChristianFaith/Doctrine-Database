Pope Anastasius II on the origin of the soul

Dates: 498 · Wikipedia
Summary: Anastasius II wrote against traducianism. His letter Bonum atque iucundum of 23 August 498, addressed to the bishops of Gaul, describes people there who reason that parents give their children souls as they give them bodies, calls the reasoning carnal, and answers it from the verse Jerome had used — the Father who “works up to this time.” The Catholic Encyclopedia (1907) records that “He also condemned Traducianism.”

He stands eleventh on this timeline. His entry is a letter from the bishop of Rome to the bishops of Gaul; the earlier entries — Tertullian, Lactantius, Jerome, Augustine — are treatises, commentaries and letters of individual writers.1

The teaching in Gaul

The letter opens the subject by describing the position it condemns:

… Certain heretics in Gaul think that by a rational assertion they are persuaded of this, that just as the parents transmit bodies to the human race from material dregs, so also they bestow the vital principle of the living souls. …

— Bonum atque iucundum, as excerpted in Denzinger, source. The letter describes its opponents as persuaded “by a rational assertion”; Tertullian's case had likewise rested on family likeness of character and on the symmetry of conception and death rather than on a verse. The parallelism “just as … so also” presents the Gallic argument as one from a single generative act: whatever accounts for the body accounts for the soul.2

The excerpt names no one. The nearest contemporary witness to the same milieu is Gennadius of Massilia, writing in Gaul a generation earlier, who lists the error alongside Origen's and attributes it to “the Luciferians, Cyril, and certain presumptuous Latins.”3

The argument: a work that has not ceased

The reply is short and uses the verse Jerome had used:

… How (therefore) do they, contrary to God's will, with a very carnal mind think that the soul made to the image of God is diffused and insinuated by the mixture of human beings, when that very action by Him, who did this in the beginning, has not ceased even today, just as He Himself said: My Father works up to this time, and I work? …

— Bonum atque iucundum, as excerpted in Denzinger, source. The sentence makes two moves. The first appeals to the image of God: on the letter's account a soul made to that image is not “diffused and insinuated” by a bodily mixture. The second is the proof-text, John 5:17, used as Jerome used it: the Father's work did not stop at the end of the six days, and He is still making souls.4

The letter then answers an objection to its use of the verse — that Genesis has God resting on the seventh day — with a distinction. God established every creature “by single species” potentially and causally in the simultaneous work of creation, from which He rested; He operates visibly now in the work that belongs to the passage of time. The conclusion follows in the form of a positive doctrine:

… let the sound doctrines then rest, namely, that He, who calls those, which are not, just as those that are, imparts souls.

— Bonum atque iucundum, as excerpted in Denzinger, source. The clause borrowed at the end is Romans 4:17, God “who calleth those things which be not as though they were” — so that the letter presents God as originating each soul.5

Guilt without soul-stock

The collection files the excerpt under the heading “The Origin of Souls and Original Sin.” The second half of the excerpt takes up the transmission of sin: Anastasius holds that guilt passes from parents to children while denying that souls do:

… they ought to be separated from them by this wise sundering, because nothing else can be transmitted by them than what has been brought to pass by their own evil presumption, that is, guilt and the punishment of sin …

— Bonum atque iucundum, as excerpted in Denzinger, source. On the letter's account, what parents hand on is guilt and its penalty, not substance. What descends by the vine-branch — the image the letter uses, and the image that gives traducianism its name — descends as inherited liability, so that men “are born vicious and distorted,” while God has “no communion” in the wrong.6

Catholic reception and the text

Reception. The Catholic Encyclopedia article on the pope (1907) records that “He also condemned Traducianism.” C. Dubray (1912) cites the letter as condemning generationism and writes in the same paragraph that, “although there are no strict definitions condemning Generationism as heretical, it is certainly opposed to the doctrine of the Church, and could not be held without temerity.”7

The text. The critical edition of the Latin is Thiel's (1868), from the manuscript tradition of the papal letters, which Dubray and the Catholic Encyclopedia cite for this letter. The passages quoted above are from Denzinger's excerpt; the ellipses in them are the collection's own.8

A tag that is not his

The Latin tag quotidie Deus fabricatur animas, “God fashions souls daily,” sometimes quoted in connection with this letter, is not Anastasius's. It is Jerome's, from the tract to Pammachius against John of Jerusalem, written about a century before this letter, where it appears with the same proof-text:

Surely (as the Church teaches in accordance with the Saviour's words, My Father works hitherto and I work; and the passage in Isaiah, Who makes the spirit of man in him; and in the Psalms, Who fashions one by one the hearts of them) God is daily making souls— He, with whom to will is to do, and who never ceases to be a Creator.

— Jerome, To Pammachius Against John of Jerusalem 22, source. Jerome's sentence and the papal letter share the argument and the proof-text.9

Reception and debate

  • How a newly created soul stands under inherited guilt is taken up by Aquinas (c. 1268); see his page.
  • Jerome (c. 411) reported traducianism as the view of “the majority of the Western divines”; Gennadius (c. 470) named it an error; Anastasius wrote to Gaul against it in 498.
  • Tertullian, whose treatise argues for traducianism from inherited character and the simultaneity of conception, is not named in the excerpt.
  • The letter does not mention pre-existence; Justinian anathematized it in 543. For the rest of the witnesses see the summary timeline.
  • His pontificate (496–498) is also remembered for his dealings over the Acacian schism, the subject of a later medieval tradition about him that Richard McBrien (Lives of the Popes, 1997), as summarized on Wikipedia, calls a “confused tradition” and “manifestly unjust.”10
