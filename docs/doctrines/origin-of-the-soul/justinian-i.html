Justinian I on the origin of the soul

Dates: 543 · Wikipedia
Summary: Justinian's tract against Origen, closing in a list of nine anathemas, begins with the pre-existence of souls and condemns a mechanism — souls that were once spirits, cooled from the vision of God, and were sent down into bodies as a punishment. The anathema follows the account in Origen's De Principiis, including its etymology.

He stands twelfth on this timeline, and is its one imperial witness. The first list of anathemas on this subject was issued by an emperor, in a tract of his own composition, addressed to the patriarch of Constantinople and taken up by a local synod of the bishops under him.1

The first anathema

The list opens on the soul, with the doctrine Origen had put forward three centuries earlier:

Whoever says or thinks that human souls pre-existed, i.e., that they had previously been spirits and holy powers, but that, satiated with the vision of God, they had turned to evil, and in this way the divine love in them had died out (ἀπψυγείσας) and they had therefore become souls (ψυχάς) and had been condemned to punishment in bodies, shall be anathema.

— The Anathematisms of the Emperor Justinian Against Origen, i, source. The sentence includes the steps of Origen's derivation: the original state (spirits and holy powers), the cause of the fall (satiety with the vision of God), the mechanism (the divine love cooling), the result (they became souls), and the purpose of embodiment (punishment).2

The condemned etymology

The text preserves two Greek words. Origen's account uses an etymology: a mind that grows cold — the verb is psychesthai — becomes a psychē, a soul. The name of the thing records its fall. The anathema keeps both terms in the same clause, so that the condemned proposition carries the derivation that generated it:

… the divine love in them had died out (ἀπψυγείσας) and they had therefore become souls (ψυχάς) …

— The Anathematisms of the Emperor Justinian Against Origen, i, source. The anathema thus includes the derivation in the condemned proposition; the corresponding passage is De Principiis II.viii.3, where the cooling of the mind into a soul is set out.3

The same etymology appears earlier in a Stoic argument that Tertullian answered: that the newborn receives its soul when cold air strikes it. He replied that peoples born under a broiling sun have souls too.

The rest of the list

The nine anathemas do not stay on the soul. They run through the pre-existence of Christ's soul, the doctrine that the Word became like each order of heavenly beings, spherical resurrection bodies, ensouled stars, and a future crucifixion for demons; and they close on the restoration:

If anyone says or thinks that the punishment of demons and of impious men is only temporary, and will one day have an end, and that a restoration (ἀποκατάστασις) will take place of demons and of impious men, let him be anathema.

— The Anathematisms of the Emperor Justinian Against Origen, ix, source. The fifteen anathemas transmitted with the council of 553 (below) join the two doctrines explicitly, calling the restoration the one “which follows from” pre-existence. The list of 543 then names Origen:

Anathema to Origen and to that Adamantius, who set forth these opinions together with his nefarious and execrable and wicked doctrine and to whomsoever there is who thinks thus, or defends these opinions, or in any way hereafter at any time shall presume to protect them.

— The Anathematisms of the Emperor Justinian Against Origen, conclusion, source. “Adamantius” was Origen's byname; the text as transmitted reads as though it names two people, and the NPNF editor flags the Greek and Latin as disagreeing at this point. The closing clause extends the anathema to anyone who “shall presume to protect them.”4

The fifteen anathemas, and the dispute over their origin

A second and longer list, of fifteen anathemas, is transmitted with the Fifth Ecumenical Council of 553. It opens with the same subject in a single line:

If anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema.

— The Anathemas Against Origen, i, source. The two doctrines are here bound together explicitly — the restoration “which follows from” the pre-existence — and the fourteen that follow work out the fallen-minds cosmology in far more detail than Justinian's nine: intelligences without bodies, differentiated into angelic orders and human and demonic bodies by the degree of their cooling; a final state in which “the end and the beginning shall be alike.”

Whether these fifteen belong to the acts of the council of 553 is disputed. The NPNF excursus records three points. First, the council's own capitula number fourteen and are entirely concerned with Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret, and the letter of Ibas — the Three Chapters — not with Origen's cosmology. Second, the fifteen anathemas as we have them surfaced in a Vienna manuscript found by the librarian Peter Lambeck in the seventeenth century, headed as canons of the fifth synod, and a long line of scholars — Walch, Döllinger, Hefele among them — judged that heading untrustworthy and assigned the fifteen instead to a local synod at Constantinople, while Cardinal Noris, the Ballerini brothers and Harnack held that the fifth council “condemned Origen, as Justinian desired.” Third, a great deal of material later than the council does attribute a detailed examination and condemnation of Origen to it.5

The editor of the NPNF volume wrote:

It would seem that with the evidence now in our possession it would be the height of rashness to give a dogmatic answer to this question. Scholars of the highest repute have taken, and do take to-day, the opposite sides of the case, and each defends his own side with marked learning and ability.

— Excursus on the XV. Anathemas Against Origen, NPNF² vol. 14, source. Hefele, who assigned the fifteen to a synod at Constantinople, wrote: “We certainly possess no strong and decisive proof that the fifteen anathematisms belong to the Constantinopolitan synod of the year 543; but some probable grounds for the opinion may be adduced.” The same editor adds that even if the fifteen were adopted before the ecumenical council opened, the council named Origen among the heretics in its eleventh capitulum, and so “practically ratified and made its own the action of that Synod.”6

The edict of 543, whose first anathema concerns the origin of the soul, is Justinian's on either reconstruction. Schaff counts pre-existence among “the Origenistic heresies, which were condemned under Justinian.”7

Origen's framing and the anathema's

Origen had marked the discussion as exploratory. Twice in the same book of De Principiis he says that what he is offering is investigation and not dogma — “not to be regarded as advanced by us in a dogmatic manner, but simply as opinions, treated in the style of investigation and discussion” — and in the preface he had said explicitly that the church's rule of faith left the soul's origin undetermined. The anathema of 543 states the doctrine as theses held and taught. Whether the anathemas reach Origen's own inquiry or a sixth-century Origenist teaching is the subject of disputed-reading pages on the condemnation-of-Origen timeline, among them whether his condemned opinions were offered as inquiry and whether the anathemas of 553 condemned sixth-century Evagrian Origenism.8

Neither list addresses traducianism or creationism; the anathemas concern where souls were before they were embodied and where they will be afterwards. John of Damascus (c. 743), stating that body and soul were formed together, names pre-existence as the error, as Gregory of Nyssa had rejected it in 379.

Reception and debate

  • Earlier arguments against pre-existence on this timeline are Tertullian's (c. 210), that a soul which began cannot have had a career before its body, and Gregory of Nyssa's (379), that a doctrine of descent by demerit cannot stop descending.
  • The anathemas are the conclusion of a longer tract by Justinian against Origen; the NPNF editor directs readers to “the entire tractate of the Emperor against Origen of which these anathematisms are the conclusion.”
  • For Origen's own statement of the doctrine and his caveats about it see his page; for the rest of the witnesses, the summary timeline.
