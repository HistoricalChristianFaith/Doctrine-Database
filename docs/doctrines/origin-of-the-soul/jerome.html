Jerome on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 411 · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome holds creationism and rests it on a verse. God, he says, is daily making souls and sending them into bodies, on the authority of “my Father worketh hitherto”; the rival view he states as a doctrine on which the soul is bred like the soul of a beast. He held the position for at least fifteen years and stated it three times. When Marcellinus and Anapsychia asked him to expound it, he sent them to Augustine instead; his reply to Augustine's own long letter on the question (415) did not take the question up.

He stands seventh on this timeline. He rests his answer on proof-texts, and he pressed the question in controversy with John of Jerusalem and with Rufinus.1

“God is daily making souls” (c. 396)

Jerome's fullest statement is the earliest, and it comes out of a quarrel. About 396 he answered John of Jerusalem's profession of faith with a tract objecting that John had not answered the question he was asked. John had said the soul is not part of God's essence and that angels do not become souls; Jerome wanted to know when the soul was made:

I now ask: When was that soul made, which is the work of God, which is distinguished by free will and reason, and is not of the essence of the Creator? Was it made at the same time that man was made out of the slime, and the breath of life was breathed into his face? Or, having previously existed, and having associated with reasonable and incorporeal creatures as well as lived, was it afterwards gifted with the inbreathing of God? Here you are silent.

— Against John of Jerusalem, §21, source. The next paragraph runs the question forward from Adam to everyone else — “whence had Eve her soul, seeing that God did not breathe into her face?”, and then “I ask whence Cain and Abel, who were the firstborn of our first parents, had their souls? And the whole human race downwards, what, are we to think, was the origin of their souls?” — and lays out two answers in a single sentence each. The first is traducianism: “Did they come by propagation, like brute beasts? So that, as body springs from body, so soul from soul.” The second is Origen's. Then Jerome gives his own:

Surely (as the Church teaches in accordance with the Saviour's words, My Father works hitherto and I work; and the passage in Isaiah, Who makes the spirit of man in him; and in the Psalms, Who fashions one by one the hearts of them) God is daily making souls — He, with whom to will is to do, and who never ceases to be a Creator.

— Against John of Jerusalem, §22, source. Jerome presents it as the church's teaching. It carries three proof-texts, and adds a metaphysical ground: that a Creator does not stop creating, and that in God willing and doing are the same thing.2

He then answers an objection to daily creation. If God makes each soul to order, what is He doing at a conception produced by adultery or incest — supplying a soul on demand to a crime? Jerome's answer is that the guilt attaches where the choice was:

Birth from adultery imputes no blame to the child, but to the father. As in the case of seeds, the earth which cherishes does not sin, nor the seed which is thrown into the furrows, nor the heat and moisture, under whose influence the grain bursts into bud, but some man, as for example, the thief and robber, who, by fraud and violence, plucks up the seed …

— Against John of Jerusalem, §22, source. The womb receives, nourishes, forms, and divides into members what it has received; “and among those secret recesses of the belly the hand of God is always working, and there is the same Creator of body and soul.” The reply distinguishes God's working in nature from what men do with it. The section closes: “Suppose I am wrong, I openly say what I think. Do you, then, likewise either freely profess our opinions, or firmly maintain your own.”3

The commentaries: what returns to God must have come from God

The same doctrine surfaces twice in the commentaries, both times as an aside, and both times resting on a verse about the spirit's origin or destination. On the last chapter of Ecclesiastes he takes the return of the spirit as evidence of where it came from:

From which there is enough to smile at in those who think that spirits are produced with bodies, not from God, but are made from the parent's body. For when the flesh is returned to the earth, and the spirit goes back to God, who gave it; it is obvious that God is the parent of all spirits, not man.

— Commentary on Ecclesiastes, on Ecclesiastes 12:7, source. The inference is from a symmetry: the flesh goes back where it came from, therefore so does the spirit, and the text says the spirit goes back to God, “who gave it.”4

On Zechariah the point is made in passing, expounding a verse that calls God the one who forms the spirit of man within him:

Therefore the Lord who stretched out the heavens like a skin, and solidified the earth with a high mass, and formed the spirit of man within it, is the same Creator of all souls, so that he might form one living being from two substances, the soul and the body.

— Commentary on Zechariah, on Zechariah 12:1, source. The prophet, on Jerome's reading, names God as the maker of the spirit in each man, in the present tense, which is the daily creation of the earlier tract.5

The taxonomy of 410

Then, some fifteen years after the tract against John, two correspondents in Africa — Marcellinus, the imperial commissioner who had presided over the conference with the Donatists, and his wife Anapsychia — wrote to Bethlehem asking where the soul comes from. Jerome's reply opens by restating the question in five branches:

I have not forgotten the brief query, or rather the very important theological question, which you propounded in regard to the origin of the soul — does it descend from heaven, as the philosopher Pythagoras and all the Platonists and Origen think? Or is it part of the essence of the Deity, as the Stoics, Manichæus, and the Priscillianists of Spain imagine? Or are souls kept in a divine treasure house wherein they were stored of old as some ecclesiastics, foolishly misled, believe? Or are they daily created by God and sent into bodies, according to what is written in the gospel, My Father works hitherto, and I work? or are souls really produced, as Tertullian, Apollinaris, and the majority of the Western divines conjecture, by propagation, so that as the body is the offspring of body, the soul is the offspring of soul, and exists on conditions similar to those regulating the existence of the inferior animals.

— Letter 126 (to Marcellinus and Anapsychia), §1, source. Jerome does not say in this passage which branch is his. As in 396, the fourth is given a proof-text, John 5:17, and the fifth is likened to the propagation of “the inferior animals.” Rufinus's division of ten years earlier had three branches.6

The referral to Augustine

Having stated the question, he declined to answer it. He referred his correspondents to his published exchange with Rufinus — “I know that I have published my opinion on this question in my brief writings against Ruffinus” — and then handed them on:

… you have in Africa that holy man and learned bishop Augustine, who will be able to teach you on this subject viva voce, as the saying is, and expound to you his opinion, or, I should rather say, my own opinion stated in his words.

— Letter 126, §1, source. The Apology against Rufinus states Jerome's position on the soul mostly in the form of questions put to Rufinus. Augustine, to whom he referred them, had left the question undecided in On Free Will and wrote in his last years that he still did not know (see his page).

In the same letter Jerome describes his state after the sack of Rome:

… at the time when I had just begun to dictate the proposed exposition, my mind was so much agitated by the devastation of the western provinces of the empire, and especially by the sack of Rome itself by the barbarians, that, to use a common proverbial phrase, I scarcely knew my own name; and for a long while I was silent, knowing that it was a time for tears.

— Letter 126, §2, source. In 415 Augustine sent Jerome a whole treatise in the form of a letter — Letter 166, On the Origin of the Human Soul, carried east by the priest Orosius — confessing his own perplexity and pressing the one difficulty he could not get past: if God makes each soul new and sinless, where did an infant snatched away before baptism contract the guilt for which it is condemned? Jerome's reply (Letter 134, 416) pleaded “a most trying time” and promised to “defend your opinions as my own”; it did not take up the question.7

“The majority of the Western divines”

One claim in the taxonomy is a historical assertion. Jerome says traducianism is the view of Tertullian, Apollinaris, and maxima pars occidentalium — most Westerners. C. Dubray (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) cites this sentence and judges that the testimony “seems exaggerated, as no other writer of prominence is found to advocate Generationism as certain.” Of the Latins on this timeline, Tertullian argues for traducianism, and Lactantius, whom Rufinus had added to the list, denies it.

Jerome had lived in the Latin West and in the Greek East. In 498 Anastasius II wrote to the bishops of Gaul against people there who held that parents transmit souls. Dubray renders Jerome's phrase “the majority of Oriental writers,” where the Latin has occidentalium and the English translation used here has “Western.”8

Jerome's stated grounds

Jerome gives two grounds.

The first is John 5:17, “my Father worketh hitherto, and I work,” the verse he attaches to the doctrine in 396 and again in 410; Anastasius II cited it against the traducianists of Gaul in 498. Jerome adds texts from Isaiah and the Psalms on the forming of the human spirit and heart.

The second is that traducianism makes the human soul the offspring of a soul as a beast's is the offspring of a beast. He puts it that way in the letter of 410, and had put it more sharply against Rufinus in 402 — “If it arose by traduction, then human souls, which we believe to be eternal, are subject to the same condition as those of the brutes, which perish with the body.” Tertullian had held that a propagated soul is still immortal; Aquinas (c. 1268) argued from the soul's subsistence that a soul caused by the begetter would perish with the body.9

Jerome stated creationism three times as the church's teaching, over fifteen years, and named Tertullian and Apollinaris for the alternative. Later on this timeline Cassian (c. 426–429) states the creationist answer in passing, and Gennadius (c. 470) states it as the church's teaching.10

Reception and debate

  • Jerome also appears on two other timelines whose quarrels touch this page. The Origenist controversy is where he demanded an answer on the soul from Rufinus and got none — see his page there — and the correspondence with Augustine is where Augustine put the question to him; see that page.
  • Augustine's difficulty for creationism — if each soul is made new, how does an infant contract the guilt baptism removes? — was put to Jerome directly in Letter 166; it is set out on Augustine's page.
  • Jerome's taxonomies set Origen's pre-existence and Tertullian's traducianism side by side as rivals to daily creation. Gregory of Nyssa, writing in Greek in 379, had set pre-existence against a body-first reading of Genesis 2:7.
