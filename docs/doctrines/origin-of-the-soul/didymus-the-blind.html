Didymus the Blind on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 380 (Didymus the Blind, c. 313–398, blind from early childhood and for some half-century head of the catechetical school of Alexandria — a teacher who learned Scripture by ear, and under whom Rufinus, Palladius, and, by his own report, Jerome sat) · Wikipedia
Summary: Didymus is the fourth-century witness on this timeline to Origen's third option — the pre-existence of souls — at Alexandria. Reading God's question in the garden, “Adam, where are you?”, he takes the soul to have been “free from every place” in its incorporeal state and to have “come into a place” only when it attached itself to a body; and his pupil Jerome, writing against Rufinus, reports a treatise in which Didymus held that the pre-existent souls “had not sinned much” and so were only lightly punished by being lodged in bodies. Ilaria Ramelli (2013) reads his teaching as Origen's version: not a pre-existence of bare souls but of rational creatures who once wore incorruptible bodies that became corruptible after the fall. He died about 398, never censured in his lifetime; the councils of 680–681 and 787 anathematized “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus.”

The evidence

The soul was “free from every place” and “came into a place” only in the body

In the Commentary on Genesis — one of the works recovered in 1941 among the Tura papyri — Didymus glosses the Lord's question to the fallen Adam, “Where are you?” (Genesis 3:9), by turning it from a question about a body in a garden into a question about a soul in a condition. The soul that has fallen is now somewhere; before, being incorporeal, it was nowhere in particular:

You are in a place, though you were free from every place through incorporeality; since you did not preserve it and you have become attached to the body, you have come into a place.

— Didymus the Blind, Commentary on Genesis 91 (on Genesis 3:9), in Daria Szymańska-Kuta's translation of the Greek.1 The passage follows the pattern of Origen's account: a prior condition “beyond every place,” a lapse of watchfulness, and a descent into embodiment and so into location. Szymańska-Kuta notes that Didymus introduces the sharpest form of the scheme — that the soul's “first substrate” was an incorporeal substance “beyond every place” — as the opinion of unnamed “some authors,” and does not there state in his own voice whether he holds it. Elsewhere in the commentary he describes the fall as the soul's passage into a “dense and perishable” body.

Jerome's report: the pre-existent souls “had not sinned much”

Jerome, who had studied under Didymus and called him “my seer,” attests in the Apology against Rufinus, written during the Origenist controversy, that this was Didymus's own teaching. He distinguishes Didymus's faith in the Trinity from the point on which, he says, Didymus erred in trusting to Origen:

As to Didymus, however, we extol both his powers of memory, and the purity of his faith in the Trinity, while on the other point in which he erred in trusting to Origen we withdraw from him.

And he names the very treatise, addressed to Rufinus, in which Didymus had set out the point at issue — why souls are lodged in bodies at all:

There exists a treatise of Didymus addressed to you, in which he meets this inquiry of yours, with the answer, that they had not sinned much, and therefore it was enough punishment for them just to have touched their bodily prisons.

— Jerome, Apology against Rufinus III.27–28.2 In Jerome's report, as in Origen's account, the body is a penalty proportioned to a fault committed before birth, and a light fault earns a light one. Jerome wrote as a party to the quarrel with Rufinus; he had heard Didymus in person, and he names the book and its dedicatee.

Ramelli's account: rational creatures with incorruptible bodies

Ilaria Ramelli (2013), surveying the Tura commentaries, reads Didymus as teaching not a pre-existence of bare, bodiless souls but, like Origen, the prior existence of rational creatures with bodies that were not corruptible, which became corruptible and mortal after the fall. On her account he read the “garments of skin” God made for Adam and Eve (Genesis 3:21) as the present heavy body, put on after the fall. She writes that Didymus “supported the so-called ‘preexistence of souls,’” yet “exactly like Origen, did not conceive of a preexistence of bare souls proper, but of rational creatures endowed with bodies that were not corruptible.”3

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. On this timeline Didymus is the fourth-century witness to the pre-existence view: Origen proposed it as one option among three around 253; Gregory of Nyssa, his near-contemporary, rejected it as sliding into the transmigration tales; and Justinian anathematized it in 543. Didymus headed the catechetical school of Alexandria, in which Origen had earlier taught.
  • Death and posthumous condemnation. Didymus was never tried or censured in his lifetime. Jerome, writing about 393, still lists his commentaries with admiration and notes that he “is still living, and has already passed his eighty-third year”; he died about 398. His name was joined to Origen's and Evagrius's only posthumously — reported by Cyril of Scythopolis among those the fathers of 553 condemned, then fixed by name in the definitions of Constantinople III (680–681) and Nicaea II (787), which anathematize “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus.” He is commemorated as “St. Didymus the Blind” on 18 October in the Orthodox Prologue of Ohrid.4 His case is among those on the condemnation-of-Origen timeline's page on who else held the condemned views and what became of them.
  • The recovered text. Almost nothing of Didymus's exegesis was known at first hand until 1941, when a cache of papyrus codices was found in a quarry at Tura, south of Cairo, preserving his commentaries on Genesis, Job, Zechariah, and the Psalms — the base on which his teaching can now be read rather than merely reported. How the transmission of Origenist texts, largely through excerpts made by their opponents, bears on reading them is the subject of a disputed-reading page on the transmission.
  • Other timelines. Didymus's teaching on the other doctrine he was condemned for — the final restoration of all rational creatures — is set out on the apokatastasis timeline; and he appears as well as a witness to Mary's perpetual virginity and on the woman-taken-in-adultery pericope.
