John Cassian on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 435 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cassian argues for creationism from Hebrews 12:9, which distinguishes “fathers of our flesh” from “the Father of spirits,” and on his reading the distinction is a doctrine of origins: men father bodies, God alone fathers souls, and He “makes them out of nothing whenever He pleases.” He denies the mechanism of propagation — spirit does not beget spirit. He makes the argument not in a treatise on the soul but in passing, inside a night-long conference about demons.

He stands ninth on this timeline, after Jerome, who stated creationism as the church's teaching and attached it to John 5:17, and before Gennadius, who in the same city a generation later set it down as the church's teaching with the rival views named as errors. Cassian uses creationism as a premise in an argument about something else.1

The setting: a night conference on demons

The Conferences record conversations with the Egyptian fathers. The eighth is the second conference of Abbot Serenus, and its subject is announced in the title — On Principalities. Germanus and Cassian have asked where the hostile powers came from and why they differ in rank, and the discussion, which has run “nearly two whole nights,” works through the fall of the angels, the sons of God in Genesis 6, the antediluvian judgment, and finally the hardest of the questions put: what does the Lord mean by calling the devil a liar “and the father of it”? Is the devil's own father also a liar?

Serenus's answer requires a rule about what spirits can and cannot generate, and he has already laid one down earlier the same night. Asked whether angels could really have fathered children on the daughters of men, he had denied it on grounds of what would follow: “We cannot possibly believe that spiritual existences can have carnal intercourse with women. But if this could ever have literally happened how is it that it does not now also sometimes take place”? — and had reread Genesis 6 as the intermarriage of the line of Seth with the line of Cain. The reading is recorded on the Nephilim timeline, where Cassian has his own page. The doctrine of the soul is introduced as a corollary of it.2

“Spirit does not beget spirit”

The rule, when it comes, is stated as something already agreed:

For as we said a little while ago spirit does not beget spirit just as soul cannot procreate soul, though we do not doubt that the compacting of flesh is formed from man's seed, as the Apostle clearly distinguishes in the case of both substances; viz., flesh and spirit, what should be ascribed to whom as its author, and says: Moreover we have had fathers of our flesh for instructors, and we reverenced them: shall we not much more be in subjection to the Father of spirits and live? What could show more clearly than this distinction, that he laid down that men were the fathers of our flesh, but always taught that God alone was the Father of souls.

— Conferences VIII.25 (the Second Conference of Abbot Serenus), source, citing Hebrews 12:9. Two claims are packed into this. One is negative and about capacity: a spirit is not the kind of thing that can generate another spirit, whatever it can do with flesh. The other is positive and exegetical: on his reading the apostle has sorted the two paternities, assigning flesh to men and spirit to God. Cassian does not make even the body simply the parents' work — in its formation men have “a ministerial office alone,” the chief part belonging to God, and he stacks up Job's milk and cheese, the psalm's “Your hands have made me and fashioned me,” and the word to Jeremiah before birth to say so. On his account God forms both, and parents have a ministerial part in one.3

Ecclesiastes, Adam's breath, and creation out of nothing

The second text is the one Jerome had used the same way, and Cassian reads it in the same direction — from where a thing goes back to, infer where it came from:

But what could be said with greater plainness than that he declares that the matter of the flesh which he styled dust, because it springs from the seed of man, and seems to be sown by his ministration, must, as it was taken from the earth, again return to the earth, while he points out that the spirit which is not begotten by intercourse between the sexes, but belongs to God alone in a special way, returns to its creator?

— Conferences VIII.25, source, on Ecclesiastes 12:7. He adds the breathing into Adam as confirmation, and then draws the conclusion:

And so from these passages we clearly infer that no one can be called the Father of spirits but God alone, who makes them out of nothing whenever He pleases, while men can only be termed the fathers of our flesh.

— Conferences VIII.25, source. Augustine had stated the same disjunction a few years earlier — if the soul is not derived from the parent, it comes out of nothing. Cassian wrote at Marseilles in the years of the debate there over Augustine's teaching on grace.4

The verse in its context

In Hebrews 12 the contrast between “fathers of our flesh” and “the Father of spirits” stands in a passage on discipline: we submitted to human fathers who chastened us for a few days after their own pleasure, so how much more should we submit to the Father of spirits, who chastens us for our profit. In the conference Cassian uses the verse to argue that the devil had no father but God, so that the “spirits” in view include angels; he then applies the same distinction to human souls. Augustine reports that a traducianist can read Ecclesiastes 12:7, the companion text, as consistent with descent of the soul from the first man, and Tertullian holds that “from the one (primeval) man comes the entire outflow and redundance of men's souls.”5

Placement on the timeline

A generation earlier Jerome argued for creationism against named opponents, naming Tertullian and Apollinaris for the rival view and likening it to the propagation of beasts; some three decades before Cassian, Rufinus set out three views and declined all of them. Cassian uses the doctrine as a step in a proof about something else, in a work written for monks, and does not stop to defend it.

Gennadius (c. 470), writing in Marseilles, catalogues Cassian's works — the eighth conference is listed there under its own title, On principalities — and in his handbook sets down creationism as what the church teaches, with the alternatives named as errors.6

Reception and debate

  • Jerome (c. 411) and Anastasius II (498) cite John 5:17 for creationism; Cassian cites Hebrews 12:9.
  • Cassian introduces the rule “spirit does not beget spirit” to settle a question about angels and applies it to human souls.
  • Cassian offers no argument from original sin in either direction — neither the traducian's explanation of it nor Augustine's objection to the creationist alternative.
  • Cassian also appears as a witness on the Nephilim timeline, where the same conference supplies his denial that angels could father children — the premise from which this page's doctrine is drawn as a corollary. See that page, and the summary timeline for the rest of the witnesses here.
