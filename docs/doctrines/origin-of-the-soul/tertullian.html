Tertullian on the origin of the soul

Dates: c. 220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian's A Treatise on the Soul is the earliest extant sustained argument for traducianism, and Schaff and Dubray name him as the theory's author. In it he argues that soul and body are conceived at the same instant, out of a single generative act, so that every human soul is a shoot from the one soul God breathed into Adam. His chief evidence is an observation — children resemble their parents in character, not only in feature — and he holds the soul to have body, form, and dimension.

He stands first on this timeline as its earliest extant witness. The treatise is written against two opponents at once: the philosophers, who make the soul unborn and eternal, and those who have the soul arrive from outside at the moment of birth.1

Against Plato: the soul had a beginning

Tertullian's starting point is not the mechanism but the fact of a beginning. If the soul comes from God's breath, then it is a thing that started, and a thing that started is a creature, not a fragment of divinity:

After settling the origin of the soul, its condition or state comes up next. For when we acknowledge that the soul originates in the breath of God, it follows that we attribute a beginning to it. This Plato, indeed, refuses to assign to it, for he will have the soul to be unborn and unmade. We, however, from the very fact of its having had a beginning, as well as from the nature thereof, teach that it had both birth and creation.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 4, source. Tertullian is arguing against the soul's pre-existence before he is arguing for its propagation, and the two moves are connected: a soul that is born cannot have had a career before its body. He spends two chapters on Plato's recollection-doctrine, objecting that a soul which can forget a previous life is not the divine, impassible thing Plato needs it to be, and setting his own estimate against it — the soul is far below God, “the breath (of God), though not His Spirit.”2

We, however, who allow no appendage to God (in the sense of equality), by this very fact reckon the soul as very far below God: for we suppose it to be born, and hereby to possess something of a diluted divinity and an attenuated felicity, as the breath (of God), though not His spirit…

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 24, source. Origen's account of pre-existence was written after this treatise; Justinian's edict of 543 anathematized the pre-existence of souls.3

Not a moment's interval: soul and body conceived together

The other rival is the view that the soul is inhaled at birth — the Stoic position, with Plato's assent, that the infant receives its soul with its first breath of cold air. Tertullian states it and then answers it:

… such of them as begin by maintaining that the soul is not conceived in the womb, nor is formed and produced at the time that the flesh is moulded, but is impressed from without upon the infant before his complete vitality, but after the process of parturition.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 25, source. His reply is empirical: he calls mothers as witnesses. The child kicks, turns, is quieted and disturbed, shares its mother's cravings and even her bruises; stillbirths happen, and nothing can die that has not lived. He also takes up the etymology offered for it — that the Greek psychē was thought to name a cooling, so that the soul is what the cold air imparts — and answers it by geography: peoples born under a torrid sun have souls too, and nobody's wits are sharper for being born in Scythia.4 (Origen uses the same etymology in his own account.)

The positive thesis follows in the chapter whose heading states it — “Soul and Body Conceived, Formed and Perfected in Element Simultaneously”:

How, then, is a living being conceived? Is the substance of both body and soul formed together at one and the same time? Or does one of them precede the other in natural formation? We indeed maintain that both are conceived, and formed, and perfectly simultaneously, as well as born together; and that not a moment's interval occurs in their conception, so that, a prior place can be assigned to either.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 27, source. The argument for it is symmetry with death. If death is by definition the separation of soul and body, then life is their conjunction; and since the separation is simultaneous, so must the conjunction be. He grants that there are two seeds, but refuses to let them come apart in time: “although we shall allow that there are two kinds of seed — that of the body and that of the soul — we still declare that they are inseparable, and therefore contemporaneous and simultaneous in origin.”5 Gregory of Nyssa (379) and John of Damascus (c. 743) also hold that soul and body begin together, without holding that the soul is propagated.

A sprout from Adam's root

Given simultaneity, the mechanism follows. If the soul begins when the body begins, and the body comes from the parents, the soul comes the same way — and the whole race is one growth from one stock:

Even the infancy of a log, then, may have an intellect (suitable to it): how much more may that of a human being, whose soul (which may be compared with the nascent sprout of a tree) has been derived from Adam as its root, and has been propagated among his posterity by means of woman, to whom it has been entrusted for transmission, and thus has sprouted into life with all its natural apparatus, both of intellect and of sense!

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 19, source. The context is an argument that infants are not mindless: trees have their proper vitality while still twigs, the young vine knows to reach for a prop, and so the newborn's cry, its recognition of its mother, its refusal of a stranger's breast are evidence of an intellect present from the start rather than acquired later. The Latin word for the doctrine, tradux, is the vine-shoot layered into the ground to raise a new plant from the old.6

Tertullian then grounds the picture in Genesis. The two ingredients of the first man — the clay and the breath — are, he says, the archetypes of the two seeds, and the propagation of the race is simply their continued joint flow:

Most true are the examples of the first creation. Adam's flesh was formed of clay. Now what is clay but an excellent moisture, whence should spring the generating fluid? From the breath of God first came the soul. But what else is the breath of God than the vapour of the spirit, whence should spring that which we breathe out through the generative fluid? Forasmuch, therefore, as these two different and separate substances, the clay and the breath, combined at the first creation in forming the individual man, they then both amalgamated and mixed their proper seminal rudiments in one, and ever afterwards communicated to the human race the normal mode of its propagation…

Accordingly from the one (primeval) man comes the entire outflow and redundance of men's souls — nature proving herself true to the commandment of God, Be fruitful, and multiply.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 27, source. Tertullian reads the act of Genesis 2:7 as the pattern of an ongoing natural process; Lactantius, holding that the breathing-in of life at each conception is God's, reads it as the pattern of a repeated divine act. Tertullian acknowledges that the argument requires him to talk about the mechanics of generation: “Nature should be to us an object of reverence, not of blushes.”7

The evidence: children inherit character, not only features

The positive evidence Tertullian offers is family likeness in temper and disposition, which he takes to be a likeness of soul:

Pray, whence comes it that from similarity of soul we resemble our parents in disposition, according to the testimony of Cleanthes, if we are not produced from this seed of the soul?

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 25, source. The source Tertullian cites used the observation for a related purpose: Cleanthes the Stoic argued from family likeness of character that the soul is a body, since only bodies can resemble and differ from one another — and Tertullian reproduces that inference approvingly, in the chapter where he enlists the Stoics against Plato:

Cleanthes, too, will have it that family likeness passes from parents to their children not merely in bodily features, but in characteristics of the soul; as if it were out of a mirror of (a man's) manners, and faculties, and affections, that bodily likeness and unlikeness are caught and reflected by the soul also. It is therefore as being corporeal that it is susceptible of likeness and unlikeness.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 5, source. In Tertullian's treatise the likeness argument and the corporeality thesis are joined.8

A soul with body, form, and dimension

Tertullian asserts the soul's corporeality. His formal definition of the soul places “possessing body” and “having form” among its properties, alongside immortality and freedom, and he ascribes to it the three dimensions by which philosophers measure any body:

The soul, then, we define to be sprung from the breath of God, immortal, possessing body, having form, simple in its substance, intelligent in its own nature, developing its power in various ways, free in its determinations, subject to be changes of accident, in its faculties mutable, rational, supreme, endued with an instinct of presentiment, evolved out of one (archetypal soul).

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 22, source. “Evolved out of one” states the traducianism; “possessing body, having form” states the corporeality. Philip Schaff's History of the Christian Church names Tertullian as the theory's author and links the two: “with Tertullian this theory was connected with a materializing view of the soul.”9

Aquinas (c. 1268) argued that a subsistent immaterial soul cannot be produced by any power acting in matter, and concluded that to hold that the soul is caused by the begetter “is nothing else than to hold the soul to be non-subsistent and consequently to perish with the body. It is therefore heretical to say that the intellectual soul is transmitted with the semen.” Tertullian's definition, by contrast, makes the soul both bodily and immortal.10

Eve's soul from Adam, and Ambrose's reading

The most exposed application of the theory is Eve. If souls pass with the flesh, and Eve's flesh came out of Adam, then Eve's soul came out of Adam too — and God's separate in-breathing was not needed for her:

So that her flesh was for a long time without specific form (such as she afterwards assumed when taken out of Adam's side); but she was even then herself a living being, because I should regard her at that time in soul as even a portion of Adam. Besides, God's afflatus would have animated her too, if there had not been in the woman a transmission from Adam of his soul also as well as of his flesh.

— A Treatise on the Soul, ch. 36, source. The argument rests on Genesis recording no second in-breathing for Eve. Ambrose of Milan, expounding the same verses, draws the opposite conclusion: what was taken from Adam was a rib and not a portion of his soul.

Considera quia ex corpore costam sumpsit, non ex anima portionem; hoc est, non anima ex anima, sed os de ossibus meis et caro de carne mea haec vocabitur mulier.

— Ambrose, De paradiso xi.50, Latin text: “Consider that he took a rib from the body, not a portion from the soul; that is, not soul from soul, but bone from my bones and flesh from my flesh — she shall be called woman.”11

Reception and debate

  • Tertullian does not argue from the transmission of original sin as a reason for the theory. Augustine later set out, as his difficulty with creationism, how a freshly made soul could contract Adam's guilt. Tertullian does assert an inherited fault in the treatise’s closing chapters: every soul “has its nature in Adam until it is born again in Christ,” unclean and “actively sinful” until regeneration (ch. 40), carrying “an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin” (ch. 41) — a tradux peccati that travels with the propagated soul. How that inherited fault relates to later doctrines of original sin is the subject of a disputed-reading page on the Tertullian timeline; Augustine's difficulty for the creationist view is set out on his page.
  • Tertullian's case rests on observation and on an extension of Genesis 2:7 rather than on a single proof-text. Creationist writers on this timeline cite John 5:17, “my Father worketh hitherto”: Jerome (c. 411) and Anastasius II (498).
  • Later writers on this timeline take up the two parts of his position separately. The simultaneity of soul and body at conception is stated, without a material soul, by Gregory of Nyssa (379) and John of Damascus (c. 743). The propagation of the soul is opposed by Anastasius II in his letter to the bishops of Gaul (498) and called heretical by Aquinas (c. 1268).
  • Tertullian insists the soul's body is “of a quality and kind peculiar to itself”; he does not work out how that body differs from the stuff of the flesh. Whether his corporealism is materialism or a substance-realism directed against Platonic and Gnostic immaterialism is the subject of a disputed-reading page under the timeline on Tertullian's standing.
  • Tertullian also appears as a witness on the Nephilim timeline, the intermediate state, and infant baptism — where he counsels delaying the baptism of little children. See the summary timeline for the rest of the witnesses.
