Judas the Galilean and the Fourth Philosophy

Dates: c. AD 6 (the revolt at the census of Quirinius) · Judas “the Galilean” = Judas of Gamala, a Gaulonite
Summary: When Judaea became a Roman province and Quirinius held the census that assessed it for tax, Judas of Gamala “prevailed with his countrymen to revolt,” teaching that to pay tribute to Rome was to accept mortal men as lords in the place of God. Josephus makes him the founder of a “fourth philosophy” whose watchword was that God alone is Israel’s ruler. Gamaliel names him in Acts among the failed insurgents. But he is a hard case for this list: Josephus never puts a diadem on him or calls him king. He is the ideologue of the resistance, not one of its acclaimed popular kings — the teacher whose doctrine his sons and grandson would carry to the throne and the dagger.

Where he sits

In AD 6 Rome deposed Herod’s son Archelaus, annexed Judaea to the province of Syria, and sent the legate Publius Sulpicius Quirinius (Josephus’s “Cyrenius”) to take a census of the new territory for taxation. To a people who held that the land and its increase belonged to God, a Roman assessment of it was not a fiscal measure but a confession of servitude. Out of that crisis rose Judas of Gamala — a town in the Gaulanitis, east of the Sea of Galilee, which is why Josephus can call the same man both a “Gaulonite” and “the Galilean.” With him stood a Pharisee named Sadduc. Their teaching was that submission to the census was apostasy, and that Israel owed allegiance to no lord but God.

Modern scholarship (Richard Horsley and John Hanson) uses Judas precisely to show why the insurgents of the period must be sorted rather than lumped into one “messiah movement.” They class him not with the popular kings (the men acclaimed king by their followers — the 4 BC pretenders, Menahem, Simon bar Giora) but as the founder of the “fourth philosophy,” an ideological strand of resistance whose distinctive was a theological principle, not a crown. He belongs on the timeline because Gamaliel put him there; he qualifies its very premise because nothing in the sources makes him a would-be king.1

Claims & evidence

“They were cowards if they would endure to pay a tax to the Romans” — the Wars

Under his administration it was that a certain Galilean, whose name was Judas, prevailed with his countrymen to revolt, and said they were cowards if they would endure to pay a tax to the Romans, and would, after God, submit to mortal men as their lords. This man was a teacher of a peculiar sect of his own, and was not at all like the rest of those their leaders.

— Josephus, Wars 2.8.1 (§118), trans. Whiston. This is the compact notice, set at the point where Judaea passes under direct Roman rule. Two things in it govern how Judas should be read. First, his cause is defined by a theological antithesis — to pay the tax is to “after God, submit to mortal men as their lords” — not by any royal ambition of his own. Second, Josephus flags him as sui generis: “a teacher of a peculiar sect of his own … not at all like the rest of those their leaders.” He is a founder of a school, which is exactly not what a popular king is.2

Judas of Gamala and Sadduc the Pharisee — the Antiquities account

Yet was there one Judas, a Gaulonite, of a city whose name was Gamala, who, taking with him Sadduc, a Pharisee, became zealous to draw them to a revolt, who both said that this taxation was no better than an introduction to slavery, and exhorted the nation to assert their liberty.

— Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.1 (§4), trans. Whiston. The fuller version names his partner, Sadduc the Pharisee, and sharpens the argument to a slogan: the census-tax “was no better than an introduction to slavery.” Josephus goes on to blame the movement, in his bitter retrospect, for the whole train of national disaster: it “laid the foundations of our future miseries,” the seed of the seditions that would end at the Temple’s destruction. The rhetoric is Josephus’s own hostile hindsight, but it registers how consequential the “fourth philosophy” had become — a doctrine, not a single revolt, that outlived its founder by two generations.3

“God is to be their only Ruler and Lord” — the creed of the sect

These men agree in all other things with the Pharisaic notions; but they have an inviolable attachment to liberty, and say that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord.

— Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.6 (§23), trans. Whiston. Here Josephus gives the philosophy its positive content, and it is telling for the messianic question. The sect’s defining tenet is a claim about God’s kingship, not a man’s: an “inviolable attachment to liberty” grounded in the confession that “God is to be their only Ruler and Lord.” They “do not value dying any kinds of death, nor indeed do they heed the deaths of their relations and friends, nor can any such fear make them call any man Lord.” A movement whose whole point is that no man may be called lord is not one that would crown a king — which is precisely why Judas himself never wears a diadem in Josephus.4

“After this man rose up Judas of Galilee” — Gamaliel in Acts

After this man rose up Judas of Galilee in the days of the taxing, and drew away much people after him: he also perished; and all, even as many as obeyed him, were dispersed.

— Acts 5:37 (KJV). In the Sanhedrin, the Pharisee Gamaliel counsels patience with the apostles by citing two recent movements that came to nothing — Theudas, and “Judas of Galilee in the days of the taxing.” The reference is unmistakably our Judas: “the taxing” is the census of AD 6, and the collapse Luke reports (“he also perished; and all … were dispersed”) matches Josephus’s picture of a teacher whose immediate rising failed even as his doctrine survived. (Luke names Theudas before Judas, reversing the historical order, since the Theudas of Josephus belongs to the AD 40s — a well-known chronological crux discussed on the summary timeline.) That the church’s own short list of failed messianic-style insurgents includes Judas is the reason he sits on this page at all.5

The line continues — his sons crucified, his house at Masada

And besides this, the sons of Judas of Galilee were now slain; I mean of that Judas who caused the people to revolt, when Cyrenius came to take an account of the estates of the Jews, as we have shown in a foregoing book. The names of those sons were James and Simon, whom Alexander commanded to be crucified.

— Josephus, Antiquities 20.5.2 (§102), trans. Whiston. Under the procurator Tiberius Alexander (c. AD 46–48), two sons of Judas, James and Simon, were crucified for continuing his agitation — proof that the “fourth philosophy” was a family inheritance as much as a school. A third of his line, Menahem, whom Josephus calls “the son of Judas that was called the Galilean,” would seize the armoury at Masada at the outbreak of the great revolt (AD 66) and enter Jerusalem “in the state of a king” — the crown Judas’s doctrine had never claimed for himself. And it was a kinsman of Judas, Eleazar ben Jair, who held Masada to the last. The Sicarii — the dagger-men who became the hard core of the resistance — are the movement Judas founded, carried through to its end at Masada.6

Is he a “messianic” claimant at all?

This is the honest crux, and the answer is: only in the loosest, list-membership sense. Weigh it both ways.

  • What tells against calling him messianic. Josephus — who is not shy about noting when a man put on a diadem or was “declared to be a king” (he says exactly that of Simon of Peraea and Athronges in 4 BC, and of Menahem in 66) — assigns Judas no royal act, no acclamation, no crown, no Davidic claim. His creed makes a positive point of it: God alone is Lord, and no man may be called lord. On Horsley and Hanson’s social typology he is therefore not a “popular king” but the founder of the “fourth philosophy,” a distinct, ideological category; and on John Collins’s strict philological definition — “messiah” as an anointed eschatological deliverer tied to specific texts — he does not qualify at all. He is a revolutionary theologian, not a claimant.
  • What can be said for his place on the list. He is on it because the earliest witnesses put him there: Gamaliel, in Acts, files him among the risen-and-fallen insurgents alongside Theudas, and Josephus makes his movement the tap-root of the whole revolt. His teaching — that the tribute is apostasy because God is Israel’s only king — is the theological engine underneath the royal pretenders: if God alone may rule, then the man God raises up to enact that rule is a king by divine and not Roman warrant. Judas supplies the ideology of which the acclaimed kings are the enactment. His own house proves the link, producing in Menahem a claimant who did wear the royal state.

So the fair verdict is the one the summary timeline draws: Judas sits at the far ideological end of the spectrum, not the royal end. He is not a messianic claimant in his own person — no source crowns him — but he is the indispensable ideologue whose doctrine the crown-wearers of 66–70 would act out. To flatten him into “a messiah” is exactly the error the standard scholarship warns against; to drop him from the story is to lose the idea that made the later kings thinkable.7

Notes

  • “Galilean” vs. “Gaulonite.” Josephus calls the same man a “Galilean” in the Wars and a “Gaulonite … of Gamala” in the Antiquities. Gamala lay in the Gaulanitis (the Golan), just across the border from Galilee; the double label is a geographic near-synonym, not two different rebels. Older writers sometimes further confused him with Judas son of Hezekiah, the 4 BC pretender at Sepphoris — a distinct man of the earlier crisis.
  • Sadduc the Pharisee. That Judas’s co-founder was a Pharisee, and that the sect “agrees in all other things with the Pharisaic notions,” matters: the fourth philosophy is not a fringe cult but a Pharisaic sub-school radicalized on the single point of God’s exclusive kingship into armed non-cooperation with Rome.
  • “Zealots” and “Sicarii.” Later tradition, and Josephus himself in places, traces the wartime Zealots and the dagger-wielding Sicarii back to Judas’s founding; Martin Hengel’s standard study treats the “fourth philosophy” as the ideological seedbed of the whole zealot movement, while cautioning that “Zealot” as an organized party is a phenomenon of the war years, not already of AD 6.
