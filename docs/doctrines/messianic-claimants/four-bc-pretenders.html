The royal pretenders at Herod’s death: Judas, Simon, and Athronges

Dates: c. 4 BC (the turmoil after Herod the Great’s death, the “War of Varus”) · Simon of Peraea · Athronges (Wikipedia)
Summary: When Herod died and his kingdom fell into disorder, at least three men reached for the crown — Judas son of Hezekiah at Sepphoris, Simon a slave of Herod in Peraea, and Athronges a shepherd. Two of them physically put a diadem on their own heads and “were declared to be a king” by their followers. These are the purest royal-pretender (“popular king”) cases in the whole record — men made kings by acclamation on the ancient charismatic pattern — and the type they set at the very start of the Roman period would recur, from Menahem to Bar Kokhba, for the next century and a half.

Where they sit

Herod the Great died in 4 BC. His will divided the kingdom among his sons, but the succession had to be ratified at Rome, and while Archelaus was away pressing his claim before Augustus, Judaea, Galilee, and Peraea erupted. The Syrian legate Varus eventually marched south and crushed the risings — the episode is remembered as the “War of Varus” — but not before a swarm of local strongmen had reached for royal power. Josephus narrates the season twice, in the Antiquities and the Wars, and singles out three men who did not merely rob or riot but aimed at the crown itself. Modern scholarship (Richard Horsley and John Hanson) reads these three as the textbook cases of the “popular king” — a charismatic leader acclaimed king by his followers, reviving Israel’s old tradition of non-dynastic, Saul-and-David-style kingship rather than claiming Herodian or Hasmonean descent.1

Claims & evidence

Judas son of Hezekiah — the armoury at Sepphoris

There was also Judas, the son of that Ezekias who had been head of the robbers; which Ezekias was a very strong man, and had with great difficulty been caught by Herod. This Judas, having gotten together a multitude of men of a profligate character about Sepphoris in Galilee, made an assault upon the palace [there,] and seized upon all the weapons that were laid up in it, and with them armed every one of those that were with him, and carried away what money was left there; and he became terrible to all men, by tearing and rending those that came near him; and all this in order to raise himself, and out of an ambitious desire of the royal dignity; and he hoped to obtain that as the reward not of his virtuous skill in war, but of his extravagance in doing injuries.

— Josephus, Antiquities 17.10.5 (§§271–272), trans. Whiston. The son of a bandit chief whom Herod had earlier hunted down, Judas turned the death of Herod into an opportunity: he stormed the royal arsenal at Sepphoris to arm a following and reached, in Josephus’s own words, “out of an ambitious desire of the royal dignity.” The Wars gives the same picture in brief — “this man got no small multitude together, and broke open the place where the royal armor was laid up, and armed those about him, and attacked those that were so earnest to gain the dominion.” Josephus does not say Judas put on a diadem, but the motive he assigns is explicitly royal, which is why he belongs among the pretenders and not the mere bandits. (This is a different man from Judas the Galilean, the census-rebel of AD 6, though older scholarship sometimes conflated the two.)2

Simon of Peraea — the slave who put on a diadem

There was also Simon, who had been a slave of Herod the king, but in other respects a comely person, of a tall and robust body; he was one that was much superior to others of his order, and had had great things committed to his care. This man was elevated at the disorderly state of things, and was so bold as to put a diadem on his head, while a certain number of the people stood by him, and by them he was declared to be a king, and thought himself more worthy of that dignity than any one else.

— Josephus, Antiquities 17.10.6 (§§273–277), trans. Whiston. Simon is the sharpest image of the popular king: a royal slave who crowned himself in the vacuum and was “declared to be a king” by the crowd around him. He “burnt down the royal palace at Jericho, and plundered what was left in it,” along with other royal residences, until the Roman commander Gratus caught him in flight: “Gratus overtook him, and cut off his head.” The Wars parallel adds only that he was “the tallest” of Herod’s freedmen and met the same end. Simon’s self-diademing is the single clearest act of royal usurpation in the whole 4 BC season — the gesture Menahem and Simon bar Giora would echo in the great revolt sixty years later.3

Athronges the shepherd — “he put a diadem about his head”

But because Athronges, a person neither eminent by the dignity of his progenitors, nor for any great wealth he was possessed of, but one that had in all respects been a shepherd only, and was not known by any body; yet because he was a tall man, and excelled others in the strength of his hands, he was so bold as to set up for king. … He had also four brethren, who were tall men themselves, and were believed to be superior to others in the strength of their hands … He also, as well as his brethren, slew a great many both of the Romans and of the king’s forces … This man retained his power a great while … he put a diadem about his head, and assembled a council to debate about what things should be done, and all things were done according to his pleasure.

— Josephus, Antiquities 17.10.7 (§§278–284), trans. Whiston. Athronges is the popular king in his most striking form — an obscure shepherd with nothing to recommend him but stature and strength, who “set up for king,” wore a diadem, and held out with his four brothers longer than any of them, until Archelaus and Gratus wore the movement down. The Wars introduces him in a single line that has become the type-phrase for the whole phenomenon: “a certain shepherd ventured to set himself up for a king; he was called Athrongeus.” That a shepherd should presume to the throne is not random: the shepherd-to-king arc is David’s own, and Josephus’s readers would not have missed it.4

“He was created a king immediately” — Josephus’s summary of the season

And now Judea was full of robberies; and as the several companies of the seditious lighted upon any one to head them, he was created a king immediately, in order to do mischief to the public.

— Josephus, Antiquities 17.10.8 (§285), trans. Whiston. This one sentence is the theme of the whole messianic-claimants timeline: in the crisis of 4 BC, kingship was something a following could confer — any leader they lit upon “was created a king immediately.” That is exactly what marks these three as royal pretenders rather than bandits or prophets, and it is the pattern the rest of the timeline traces to its most explicit end in Bar Kokhba.5

Notes

  • Why these three count as “messianic.” None is called anointed in the sources, so on John Collins’s strict philological definition they are royal insurgents only loosely “messianic.” But on Horsley and Hanson’s social typology they are the clearest instances of the popular king — the one category that is messianic in a defensible sense — because their followers acclaimed them king in the old charismatic pattern, two of them with an actual diadem. They sit at the strong-royal, weak-anointing end of the spectrum the summary timeline lays out.
  • The diadem is the tell. Simon and Athronges physically put on a diadem; Judas “only” reached for the royal dignity. The self-diademing gesture is what the later revolt-era claimants repeat — Menahem entering Jerusalem “in the state of a king,” Simon bar Giora in his white-and-purple — making the 4 BC trio the template for the whole series.
  • Corroboration from Rome. Tacitus, looking back on the province, notes that on Herod’s death “without waiting for Caesar’s decision” one Simon seized the title of king (a compressed Roman memory of exactly this turmoil), confirming that the post-Herodian scramble for the crown was known outside the Jewish sources.
