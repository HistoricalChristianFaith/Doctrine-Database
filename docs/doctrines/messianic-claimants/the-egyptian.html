“The Egyptian” false prophet and the Mount of Olives

Dates: c. AD 56 (under the procurator Antonius Felix, c. AD 52–60)
Summary: An unnamed man from Egypt whom Josephus brands “a false prophet” led a great crowd out of the wilderness to the Mount of Olives, promising — in the Antiquities version — that at his word the walls of Jerusalem would fall down and let his followers into the city, a new Joshua before a new Jericho. Felix marched out and scattered them; the Egyptian himself escaped and vanished. He is the second great sign-prophet of this timeline, larger in scale than Theudas a decade before, and his notoriety gave him a vivid afterlife in the Book of Acts, where a Roman tribune mistakes the arrested Paul for him. Two source-problems attach to him — a wild discrepancy in the numbers, and a detail (the falling walls) that stands in only one of Josephus’s two accounts — and both are set out below.

Where he sits

Antonius Felix governed Judaea c. AD 52–60, and Josephus files the Egyptian among the disorders of his procuratorship; the episode is conventionally dated to the middle of his term, c. AD 56. On this timeline the Egyptian is the second of the sign-prophets, following Theudas under Fadus a decade earlier. Like Theudas he claims no crown and gathers no diadem: his bid is prophetic, not royal. He leads a crowd out to a charged place and promises a miracle of deliverance that would re-enact Israel’s founding conquest. Richard Horsley and John Hanson, in the standard sorting of the period’s unrest, class the Egyptian with Theudas as an “action prophet” and warn against folding either into the box of the acclaimed popular kings (the 4 BC pretenders, Menahem, Simon bar Giora) — they revive a distinct prophetic tradition, the tradition of Moses and Joshua and Elijah, not the tradition of charismatic kingship.1

Claims & evidence

“An Egyptian false prophet… thirty thousand men” — Josephus, Wars

But there was an Egyptian false prophet that did the Jews more mischief than the former; for he was a cheat, and pretended to be a prophet also, and got together thirty thousand men that were deluded by him; these he led round about from the wilderness to the mount which was called the Mount of Olives, and was ready to break into Jerusalem by force from that place; and if he could but once conquer the Roman garrison and the people, he intended to domineer over them by the assistance of those guards of his that were to break into the city with him. But Felix prevented his attempt, and met him with his Roman soldiers, while all the people assisted him in his attack upon them, insomuch that when it came to a battle, the Egyptian ran away, with a few others, while the greatest part of those that were with him were either destroyed or taken alive; but the rest of the multitude were dispersed every one to their own homes, and there concealed themselves.

— Josephus, Wars 2.13.5 (§§261–263), trans. Whiston. This is the earlier of Josephus’s two notices. It gives the vast round number — “thirty thousand men” — and the military shape of the affair: a wilderness gathering, a march to the Mount of Olives, a planned assault on the city, and Felix’s decisive counter-attack. What it does not mention is any miracle: here the Egyptian means to “break into Jerusalem by force,” not to topple its walls by a prophet’s word.2

“At his command, the walls of Jerusalem would fall down” — Josephus, Antiquities

Moreover, there came out of Egypt about this time to Jerusalem one that said he was a prophet, and advised the multitude of the common people to go along with him to the Mount of Olives, as it was called, which lay over against the city, and at the distance of five furlongs. He said further, that he would show them from hence how, at his command, the walls of Jerusalem would fall down; and he promised them that he would procure them an entrance into the city through those walls, when they were fallen down. Now when Felix was informed of these things, he ordered his soldiers to take their weapons, and came against them with a great number of horsemen and footmen from Jerusalem, and attacked the Egyptian and the people that were with him. He also slew four hundred of them, and took two hundred alive. But the Egyptian himself escaped out of the fight, but did not appear any more.

— Josephus, Antiquities 20.8.6 (§§169–172), trans. Whiston. Written some three decades later, the second notice supplies the detail that makes the Egyptian a true sign-prophet: he promised “at his command, the walls of Jerusalem would fall down” and that the crowd would then walk in through the breach. This is the gesture the earlier account leaves out entirely — a deliberate re-staging of Jericho, where the walls fell at Joshua’s trumpet. The Antiquities also gives concrete casualty figures (four hundred slain, two hundred taken) in place of the Wars’ sweeping “thirty thousand,” and both agree on the one memorable outcome: the leader himself got away.3

“Art not thou that Egyptian?” — the tribune to Paul, in Acts

And as Paul was to be led into the castle, he said unto the chief captain, May I speak unto thee? Who said, Canst thou speak Greek? Art not thou that Egyptian, which before these days madest an uproar, and leddest out into the wilderness four thousand men that were murderers?

— Acts 21:37–38 (KJV). This is the Egyptian’s afterlife, and it is the strongest independent confirmation that Josephus’s figure was a household name in Jerusalem within a few years. When Paul is seized in the Temple and rescued from the mob by the Roman garrison, the tribune (Claudius Lysias) assumes he has caught the notorious rebel come back: “Art not thou that Egyptian…?” The tribune expected the Egyptian to reappear — exactly as Josephus says he “did not appear any more” — and took Paul, until he heard him speak Greek, for the vanished prophet. Luke’s notice gives the man’s following as “four thousand men that were murderers,” and the last phrase is a technical term: the Greek is sikarioi, the “dagger-men,” the assassins Josephus describes elsewhere — so the tribune has folded the sign-prophet’s crowd together with the terrorist faction of the day.4

A sign-prophet, not a king — the new Joshua before a new Jericho

The gesture is the interpretation, and the Antiquities makes it plain. To lead a crowd to the Mount of Olives, in full view of the city, and promise that the walls of Jerusalem will collapse at a prophet’s word is to cast oneself as a new Joshua before a new Jericho: at Jericho the walls fell flat when Israel shouted at the prophet’s signal, and the city was given into their hand without a siege. The Egyptian offered the same wonder against Roman-held Jerusalem — deliverance by miracle, not by arms. It is the twin of Theudas’s promise a decade earlier to part the Jordan (a new exodus and entry into the land): both men re-enact the founding conquest, one at its river-crossing, the other at its first walled city. Horsley and Hanson read the two together as the clearest cases of the sign-prophet type — leaders who claimed to be the prophet through whom God would act, whose followers enacted their hope bodily by streaming out to the wilderness and the appointed place to await the sign. This is why the Egyptian belongs on a “messianic claimants” timeline only under a careful label: he is part of the same ferment of end-time deliverance-hope, but he claims no throne. He is a prophet of the coming redemption, not a would-be king.5

Notes & source problems

  • The numbers. The three figures do not agree. Wars says the Egyptian “got together thirty thousand men”; Antiquities gives no total but reports four hundred killed and two hundred captured; Acts puts the following at “four thousand.” Josephus’s “thirty thousand” is almost certainly a rhetorical inflation of the sort common in the Wars (his later casualty figures in the Antiquities imply a far smaller body), so Luke’s “four thousand” is not obviously the outlier — if anything it sits closer to the more sober Antiquities account. The discrepancy is between Josephus’s two works as much as between Josephus and Acts, and does not touch the core fact all three attest.
  • The falling walls stand in one account only. The miracle motif — “at his command, the walls of Jerusalem would fall down” — appears only in the Antiquities (20.170). The earlier Wars has the Egyptian simply “ready to break into Jerusalem by force.” This is a genuine difference in Josephus’s own telling: the later, fuller account preserves the sign-prophet colouring that the first, more military summary flattens into a straightforward assault. It is the Antiquities detail that fixes the Egyptian’s type; the reader should know it is not in both.
  • “Murderers” = sikarioi. The KJV’s “four thousand men that were murderers” renders Greek sikarioi, the “dagger-men” — the assassins who mingled with festival crowds and knifed their victims, whom Josephus treats as a distinct faction. Strictly, the sign-prophet’s unarmed pilgrims and the sicarii are two different groups; the tribune’s question conflates them, which is itself evidence of how the Egyptian was remembered — as a byword for insurrection rather than for prophecy.
  • “False prophet” is Josephus’s framing. As with the goēs (“magician”) he pins on Theudas, Josephus’s “false prophet” and “cheat” are hostile labels for this whole class of leader, not neutral description. His followers plainly took him for a true prophet; the tens of thousands (or thousands) who left their homes for the Mount of Olives are the measure of that.
