Menahem son of Judas — “in the state of a king”

Dates: AD 66 (the outbreak of the great revolt) · son (or grandson) of Judas the Galilean, founder of the Sicarii line
Summary: At the outset of the war against Rome, Menahem seized the arsenal of Herod the Great at Masada, armed his followers, and “returned in the state of a king to Jerusalem.” He took command of the revolt, went up to the Temple “adorned with royal garments,” and grew, in Josephus’s words, into “an insupportable tyrant.” A rival faction under Eleazar — son of the very high priest Menahem’s men had killed — fell on him in the Temple, tortured him, and put him to death. He is the clearest royal claimant of the revolt’s opening: the diadem-tradition of 4 BC now worn in the Temple itself, and the first of the would-be kings the revolt would devour.

Where he sits

Menahem belongs to the purest of the categories on this timeline — the royal pretender, the man “made a king” by his followers on the ancient popular pattern. Where Judas the Galilean, his father (Josephus) or grandfather (as some reconstruct the line), had been the ideologue whose creed was that God alone is Israel’s ruler and that no man may be called lord, Menahem is that doctrine turned into a crown: he does exactly what his house’s theology had refused to do, entering Jerusalem and its Temple in the state of a king. Richard Horsley and John Hanson class him, with the 4 BC pretenders and Simon bar Giora, among the “popular kings” — the peasant risings that acclaimed their own charismatic ruler — and he is the first such king of the war of 66–70. His story also opens the war’s recurring, grim theme: the revolt’s kings were destroyed less by Rome than by their own rivals, and Menahem is the first of them to be killed by other Jews.1

Claims & evidence

The armoury at Masada and the return “in the state of a king”

In the meantime, one Manahem, the son of Judas, that was called the Galilean, (who was a very cunning sophister, and had formerly reproached the Jews under Cyrenius, that after God they were subject to the Romans,) took some of the men of note with him, and retired to Masada, where he broke open king Herod’s armory, and gave arms not only to his own people, but to other robbers also. These he made use of for a guard, and returned in the state of a king to Jerusalem; he became the leader of the sedition, and gave orders for continuing the siege.

— Josephus, Wars 2.17.8 (§§433–434), trans. Whiston. Every element of the royal type is here in one sentence. Menahem is identified by his lineage — “the son of Judas, that was called the Galilean” — which ties him to the founder of the fourth philosophy and the census-revolt of AD 6 (the “reproach … under Cyrenius” is Judas’s teaching against the tax). He seizes the old Herodian arsenal at Masada — arming a following, the concrete first act of a pretender — and then, decisively, “returned in the state of a king to Jerusalem.” Josephus, who is careful to record when a man “put a diadem on his head” or “was declared to be a king,” here says Menahem bore himself as a king and took command of the whole revolt.2

The Temple, “adorned with royal garments”

for he went up thither to worship in a pompous manner, and adorned with royal garments, and had his followers with him in their armor.

— Josephus, Wars 2.17.9 (§444), trans. Whiston. This is the scene that makes Menahem the sharpest royal claimant of the war’s opening. He does not merely wear a diadem in a village or a captured fort; he goes up to the Temple — the seat of Israel’s worship and the natural stage for a Davidic king — “in a pompous manner, and adorned with royal garments,” his armed men around him. The gesture is unmistakable: the man who would rule Israel presenting himself as king before the God in whose name the fourth philosophy had refused every earthly lord. It is the diadem-tradition of the 4 BC pretenders — Simon of Peraea and Athronges putting crowns on their own heads — carried into the sanctuary itself.3

“An insupportable tyrant”

Now the overthrow of the places of strength, and the death of the high priest Ananias, so puffed up Manahem, that he became barbarously cruel; and as he thought he had no antagonist to dispute the management of affairs with him, he was no better than an insupportable tyrant.

— Josephus, Wars 2.17.9 (§442), trans. Whiston. Menahem’s brief reign turned on two victories that undid him. His Sicarii had taken the fortified positions in the city and had hunted down and killed the former high priest Ananias (father of the Eleazar who would kill Menahem). The double triumph “so puffed up” Menahem that he “became barbarously cruel” and “an insupportable tyrant.” Josephus, hostile to every rebel leader, casts this as the pride that invites a fall — but the detail matters for the messianic reading too: a claimant behaving as an autocrat, brooking “no antagonist,” is a man acting the part of a king, and it is precisely his royal pretension that his rivals could not stomach.4

Killed by Eleazar’s faction — “to bear a lord”

it was not proper, when they revolted from the Romans, out of the desire of liberty, to betray that liberty to any of their own people, and to bear a lord.

— Josephus, Wars 2.17.9 (§443), trans. Whiston. The reasoning Eleazar’s party gave for striking down Menahem is the most telling evidence of what he had made himself. They rose against Rome “out of the desire of liberty”; they would not now “betray that liberty … and bear a lord” of their own nation. In other words, the charge against Menahem was that he was setting himself up as king — a claim the very slogan of the fourth philosophy (God alone is Lord) turned into sacrilege. The son’s crown was killed by his father’s creed. They “made an assault upon him in the temple,” where he had gone in his royal robes:

they took him alive, and drew him out before them all; they then tortured him with many sorts of torments, and after all slew him, as they did by those that were captains under him also, and particularly by the principal instrument of his tyranny, whose name was Apsalom.

— Josephus, Wars 2.17.9 (§§447–448), trans. Whiston. Menahem was seized in the Temple, dragged out, tortured, and killed, along with his officers. His kinsman Eleazar ben Jair escaped the massacre and fled to Masada with the surviving Sicarii, where he would command the fortress to its famous end in AD 73–74. Thus the first royal claimant of the great revolt died within weeks of donning his robes — not at Roman hands, but at the hands of fellow rebels who would not “bear a lord.”5

Is he a “messianic” claimant?

Of the figures on this timeline, Menahem is among the strongest cases for the royal reading — and one of the clearest illustrations of why the strict label still needs care. On the side of it: Josephus, who elsewhere flags a diadem or an acclamation explicitly, describes Menahem acting as a king — returning to Jerusalem “in the state of a king,” taking command of the revolt, and worshipping in the Temple “adorned with royal garments”; and his rivals killed him precisely for making himself a “lord.” On Horsley and Hanson’s social typology he is a textbook “popular king.” The caution is the one that governs the whole list: Josephus never binds Menahem to the specific furniture of anointed Davidic messianism — no prophet anoints him, no Scripture is quoted over him, no title “Messiah” is used. On John Collins’s tight philological definition he is therefore a royal insurgent who is “messianic” only in the broad, popular-kingship sense, not the narrow textual one that is unambiguous only of Bar Kokhba. The honest placement is high on the royal end of the spectrum: a man who wore the state of a king and was killed for it, short of a documented Davidic anointing.6

Notes

  • Son or grandson of Judas the Galilean? Josephus calls Menahem “the son of Judas, that was called the Galilean.” Since Judas led the census-revolt in AD 6 and Menahem acts in AD 66 — sixty years later — many historians reckon “son” loosely and take him for a grandson (or a still later descendant); others keep the literal “son” and note that a man born around the time of the census could just be alive, if elderly, in 66. Either way the point that matters for this page holds: Menahem is of the house of Judas, and his kinsman Eleazar ben Jair, who held Masada to the end, is of the same line.
  • “Manahem” / “Menahem.” Whiston’s translation spells the name “Manahem”; the Hebrew name is Mĕnaḥem (as of the last king of that name in 2 Kings 15). The quotations above keep Whiston’s spelling verbatim; the prose uses the more familiar “Menahem.”
  • Eleazar the killer. The Eleazar whose party killed Menahem is Eleazar ben Ananias, the Temple captain who had begun the war by halting the sacrifices offered for Rome and the emperor — and whose father, the high priest Ananias, Menahem’s Sicarii had just murdered. His revenge on Menahem is thus also a blood-feud, not only a doctrinal quarrel over kingship. He is a different man from Eleazar ben Jair, Menahem’s kinsman, who escaped to Masada.
