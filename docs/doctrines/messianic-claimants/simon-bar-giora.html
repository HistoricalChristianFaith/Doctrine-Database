Simon bar Giora — the general Rome executed as Israel’s king

Dates: c. AD 68–70 (commander in Jerusalem to the fall; executed at Rome AD 71)
Summary: Simon, the son of Gioras, rose during the great revolt to command the largest faction in Jerusalem, entering the city on invitation and hailed by its people “as their savior and their preserver.” He held the upper city to the end. When Jerusalem fell he hid in the tunnels beneath it, then staged a last theatre: he “put on a white frock, and buttoned upon him a purple cloak, and appeared out of the ground in the place where the temple had formerly been” — the white and purple of royalty, at the Temple’s ruins. Taken to Rome, he was singled out from every other captive in the triumph of Vespasian and Titus for the death Roman custom reserved for the enemy’s king: dragged with a rope about his head to the forum and executed there while the crowd shouted for joy. He is the great royal figure of the revolt, and the one claimant whom Rome itself treated as a king.

Where he sits

Simon bar Giora belongs, with the 4 BC trio and Menahem, to the purest category on this timeline — the royal pretender, the “popular king” acclaimed by his followers on the ancient charismatic pattern rather than crowned by descent. Richard Horsley and John Hanson class the three groups of would-be kings (the pretenders at Herod’s death, Menahem, and Simon) as the “messianic” movements proper — peasant risings that raised up their own king — and among them Simon is the most formidable: not a village diadem-wearer put down in weeks, but the commander who ruled the greater part of Jerusalem through the siege and fought Rome to the fall. What makes his case distinctive is not only how he bore himself but how Rome answered: of all the captives paraded in the triumph, it was Simon whom the Romans reserved for the ritual death of a conquered king. His story closes the revolt’s grim theme that Menahem opened — the war’s would-be kings destroyed one after another — but where Menahem fell to a rival faction, Simon fell to Rome, and fell precisely as the enemy sovereign.1

Claims & evidence

The rise: received into Jerusalem “as their savior and their preserver”

Accordingly, he, in an arrogant manner, granted them his lordly protection, and came into the city, in order to deliver it from the zealots. The people also made joyful acclamations to him, as their savior and their preserver; but when he was come in, with his army, he took care to secure his own authority.

— Josephus, Wars 4.9.11 (§§575–576), trans. Whiston. Simon’s power was not seized by a coup but conferred by acclamation. The city’s leaders, desperate to unseat the zealot John of Gischala, sent the high priest Matthias to invite Simon in; and when he entered, “the people also made joyful acclamations to him, as their savior and their preserver.” The language is the language of deliverance — the crowd greeted him as the man who would rescue Israel — and Josephus, hostile as ever, notes that Simon promptly “granted them his lordly protection” and “took care to secure his own authority.” From that entry (in the third year of the war) he held the upper city and much of the lower against both his Jewish rivals and, at last, against Titus.2

The last appearance: white and purple at the Temple ruins

And now Simon, thinking he might be able to astonish and delude the Romans, put on a white frock, and buttoned upon him a purple cloak, and appeared out of the ground in the place where the temple had formerly been.

— Josephus, Wars 7.2.2 (§29), trans. Whiston. This is the scene on which the royal reading turns. When Jerusalem fell, Simon took his most faithful men and some stone-cutters down into the tunnels beneath the city, hoping to dig his way out to safety; when the provisions failed and the escape did not, he made a last, calculated appearance. The costume is deliberate and unmistakable: a white frock and a purple cloak — the colours of kingship — and the stage he chose for it was “the place where the temple had formerly been,” the very seat of Israel’s worship and the natural ground for a Davidic king to appear. He did not surrender as a beaten general; he rose from the earth robed as a king. Josephus reads the whole gesture as a last bid to “astonish and delude the Romans,” and it worked at least so far as to stop the soldiers in their tracks: “At the first, indeed, those that saw him were greatly astonished, and stood still where they were.” They then asked who he was; Simon would not say, but demanded their captain, and Terentius Rufus, left in command, took him in bonds.3

Singled out at the triumph: the death of a conquered king

Now the last part of this pompous show was at the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, whither when they were come, they stood still; for it was the Romans’ ancient custom to stay till somebody brought the news that the general of the enemy was slain. This general was Simon, the son of Gioras, who had then been led in this triumph among the captives; a rope had also been put upon his head, and he had been drawn into a proper place in the forum, and had withal been tormented by those that drew him along; and the law of the Romans required that malefactors condemned to die should be slain there.

— Josephus, Wars 7.5.6 (§§153–154), trans. Whiston. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that Simon had been made a king — and it comes not from his own followers but from Rome’s own ceremony. In a Roman triumph the culminating act was the execution of the enemy general, the defeated king or commander, at the forum while the procession halted at the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus to await the news of his death. Of the many thousands taken at Jerusalem — John of Gischala among them, who was condemned only to perpetual imprisonment — it was Simon whom the Romans marked out for this death: dragged by a rope, tormented along the way, and killed as the war’s defeated sovereign. When his death was announced:

Accordingly, when it was related that there was an end of him, and all the people had sent up a shout for joy, they then began to offer those sacrifices which they had consecrated, in the prayers used in such solemnities.

— Josephus, Wars 7.5.6 (§155), trans. Whiston. Rome, in other words, staged Simon’s death as the death of the enemy’s king, the fitting climax of the triumph over Judaea. That the victors treated him as the sovereign of the defeated nation is powerful indirect testimony that his own people had already made him one.4

Is he a “messianic” claimant?

Simon is, with Menahem, one of the two strongest royal cases of the great revolt — and, like the whole list, a case for care about the strict label. The case for reading him as a royal, even messianic, claimant is unusually well-anchored. First, his followers received him with the acclamations of a deliverer, “as their savior and their preserver.” Second, his own last act was a self-presentation as king — white and purple, rising at the Temple’s ruins — not a detail Josephus invents for hostile purposes but one he plainly finds strange and theatrical. Third, and most tellingly, Rome executed him as the enemy’s king in the triumph, singling him out from every other captive. Martin Hengel, in the standard study of the revolt’s ideology, and Horsley and Hanson, in their social typology, both read this convergence — popular acclamation, royal robing, and Rome’s treatment of him as the defeated sovereign — as the mark of a royal/messianic bid in the popular-kingship tradition.

The honest caution is the same one that governs the timeline. Josephus never calls Simon “messiah” or “king” in his own voice; no prophet anoints him, and no Scripture is quoted over him as it would be, sixty years later, over Bar Kokhba. On John Collins’s strict philological definition — “messiah” as an anointed eschatological figure bound to specific texts — Simon is a royal insurgent who is “messianic” only in the broad, popular-kingship sense, not the narrow Davidic-anointing one. The white-and-purple robing and Rome’s ritual are strong evidence of a claim to kingship; whether that kingship carried the freight of the anointed Davidic deliverer is exactly what the sources leave open. The fair placement is high on the royal end of the spectrum: a man his people hailed as their savior, who robed himself as a king at the Temple and whom Rome killed as the enemy’s sovereign — short, still, of a documented Davidic anointing.5

Notes

  • “Bar Giora” / “the son of Gioras.” Josephus (in Whiston’s translation) calls him “Simon, the son of Gioras”; the Aramaic bar Giora means “son of the proselyte,” suggesting a convert’s family. The quotations above keep Whiston’s “Gioras” verbatim; the prose uses the familiar “bar Giora.”
  • Simon and John of Gischala. Through the siege Jerusalem was split between Simon bar Giora, who held the upper city and the larger force, and John of Gischala, who held the Temple and lower city; their feud weakened the defence. At the triumph the two men’s fates diverged sharply: John was sentenced to life imprisonment, while Simon alone was reserved for execution as the enemy general — itself a sign that the Romans regarded Simon, not John, as the war’s sovereign figure.
  • The date of the execution. Simon was captured after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, kept “against that triumph which he was to celebrate at Rome,” and killed at the triumph of Vespasian and Titus in AD 71.
