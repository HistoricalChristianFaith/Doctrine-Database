Augustine of Hippo on the composite Old Testament citations

Dates: c. 354–430 (the Harmony of the Gospels c. 400) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gives the fullest single engagement with the charge in his Harmony of the Gospels — on Matthew’s “Jeremiah” for words that are Zechariah’s, the case the tradition treats as paradigmatic. He does two distinct things: he weighs the text-critical escape (follow the manuscripts that read simply “the prophet”) and deliberately declines it, judging the harder “Jeremiah” the original reading; and he then explains the name providentially, as a Spirit-guided memory that Matthew left uncorrected. Where he himself quotes Mark 1:2, by contrast, he reads “Isaiah” and passes the Malachi composite by in silence.

Claims & evidence

Matthew 27:9 — the harder reading “Jeremiah” is original

Augustine states the difficulty plainly and lays out the tempting solution: some manuscripts omit the name and read only “the prophet,” so one could simply follow them. He refuses — on grounds that are recognizably text-critical, weighing manuscript weight, age, and the direction of change:

Now, if any one finds a difficulty in the circumstance that this passage is not found in the writings of the prophet Jeremiah… let him first take notice of the fact that this ascription of the passage to Jeremiah is not contained in all the codices of the Gospels, and that some of them state simply that it was spoken “by the prophet.” It is possible, therefore, to affirm that those codices deserve rather to be followed which do not contain the name of Jeremiah… However, let others adopt this method of defence, if they are so minded. For my part, I am not satisfied with it; and the reason is, that a majority of codices contain the name of Jeremiah, and that those critics who have studied the Gospel with more than usual care in the Greek copies, report that they have found it stand so in the more ancient Greek exemplars. I look also to this further consideration, namely, that there was no reason why this name should have been added [subsequently to the true text], and a corruption thus created; whereas there was certainly an intelligible reason for erasing the name from so many of the codices.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels III.7.29 (local text Book 3). The reasoning is a clean lectio-difficilior argument twelve centuries before the term: a scribe would erase the puzzling name, not invent it, so the harder “Jeremiah” is the source of the variants.1

The providential-memory explanation

Having fixed “Jeremiah” as what Matthew wrote, Augustine still has to explain why the wrong prophet stands. His answer: even granting that Matthew’s memory first supplied “Jeremiah” for “Zechariah,” he would have corrected it had he not judged the substitution itself to be Spirit-guided and purposeful:

…it may have been the case, that when Matthew was engaged in composing his Gospel, the word Jeremiah occurred to his mind, in accordance with a familiar experience, instead of Zechariah. Such an inaccuracy, however, he would most undoubtedly have corrected… had he not reflected that [perhaps] it was not without a purpose that the name of the one prophet had been suggested instead of the other in the process of recalling the circumstances (which process of recollection was also directed by the Holy Spirit)… there is this first and most serviceable reason… namely, that some idea was thus conveyed of the marvellous manner in which all the holy prophets, speaking in one spirit, continued in perfect unison with each other in their utterances.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels III.7.30. The unity of the prophets in one Spirit is, for Augustine, the point the “error” is permitted to teach.2

Mark 1:2 — quoted as “Isaiah,” the composite passed by

When Augustine himself has occasion to quote Mark’s prologue — introducing the four Evangelists’ accounts of John the Baptist — he cites it as an Isaiah testimony and says nothing of the Malachi half of the blend:

And Mark, who has told us nothing of the nativity or infancy or youth of the Lord, has made his Gospel begin with the same event… For it is thus that he sets out: The beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God; as it is written in the prophet Isaiah, Behold, I send a messenger before Thy face, which shall prepare Thy way before Thee. The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make His paths straight.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels II.6.18 (local text Book 2). In the same passage he introduces Matthew’s “he shall be called a Nazarene” simply as “the testimony from the prophet.” It is Augustine’s Matthew 27:9 discussion, not this one, that the later tradition (Bede especially) carries back to the Mark composite.3

Notes

  • Two moves, kept distinct. Augustine’s text-critical judgment (the harder reading is original) and his theological explanation (the name is providential) are independent, and the first is the one modern scholarship endorses: the United Bible Societies’ committee reaches Augustine’s conclusion at Mark 1:2 by the same logic, printing the harder “in Isaiah” and treating the softening to “the prophets” as the scribal correction. See the summary’s Arguments and Why Mark names Isaiah.
  • He does not harmonize the Mark composite himself. Augustine’s fullest treatment is of Matthew’s “Jeremiah”; the transfer of his reasoning to Mark 1:2 is Bede’s work — see Bede on these citations, who takes over the providential-memory argument almost verbatim.
  • The paradigm case. The full adversarial weighing of the “Jeremy the prophet” crux is hosted with Judas’s biographical timeline, where the words of Zechariah 11 are read as wrapped in Jeremiah’s imagery of the potter and the purchased field.
