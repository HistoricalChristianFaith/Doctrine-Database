Augustine of Hippo on the composite Old Testament citations

Dates: c. 354–430 (the Harmony of the Gospels c. 400) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Harmony of the Gospels Augustine discusses at length Matthew’s “Jeremiah” for words drawn largely from Zechariah 11:12–13. He considers following the manuscripts that read simply “the prophet” and declines, judging “Jeremiah” the original reading; and he explains the name providentially, as a Spirit-guided memory that Matthew left uncorrected. Where he himself quotes Mark 1:2, he reads “Isaiah” and does not comment on the Malachi half of the quotation.

Claims & evidence

Matthew 27:9 — the reading “Jeremiah” judged original

Augustine states the difficulty and the solution some offered: some manuscripts omit the name and read only “the prophet,” so one could follow them. He declines, on grounds of the number and age of the codices and of the likely direction of change:

Now, if any one finds a difficulty in the circumstance that this passage is not found in the writings of the prophet Jeremiah… let him first take notice of the fact that this ascription of the passage to Jeremiah is not contained in all the codices of the Gospels, and that some of them state simply that it was spoken “by the prophet.” It is possible, therefore, to affirm that those codices deserve rather to be followed which do not contain the name of Jeremiah… However, let others adopt this method of defence, if they are so minded. For my part, I am not satisfied with it; and the reason is, that a majority of codices contain the name of Jeremiah, and that those critics who have studied the Gospel with more than usual care in the Greek copies, report that they have found it stand so in the more ancient Greek exemplars. I look also to this further consideration, namely, that there was no reason why this name should have been added [subsequently to the true text], and a corruption thus created; whereas there was certainly an intelligible reason for erasing the name from so many of the codices.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels III.7.29 (local text Book 3). Augustine reasons that copyists had cause to erase the name and none to add it.1

The providential-memory explanation

Having judged “Jeremiah” original, Augustine then explains why Matthew’s text names Jeremiah. His answer: even granting that Matthew’s memory first supplied “Jeremiah” for “Zechariah,” he would have corrected it had he not judged the substitution itself to be Spirit-guided and purposeful:

…it may have been the case, that when Matthew was engaged in composing his Gospel, the word Jeremiah occurred to his mind, in accordance with a familiar experience, instead of Zechariah. Such an inaccuracy, however, he would most undoubtedly have corrected… had he not reflected that [perhaps] it was not without a purpose that the name of the one prophet had been suggested instead of the other in the process of recalling the circumstances (which process of recollection was also directed by the Holy Spirit)… there is this first and most serviceable reason… namely, that some idea was thus conveyed of the marvellous manner in which all the holy prophets, speaking in one spirit, continued in perfect unison with each other in their utterances.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels III.7.30. The unity of the prophets in one Spirit is, for Augustine, the purpose Augustine assigns to the name’s standing.2

Mark 1:2 — quoted as “Isaiah”

When Augustine himself quotes Mark’s prologue, introducing the four Evangelists’ accounts of John the Baptist, he cites it as an Isaiah testimony and does not comment on the Malachi half of the quotation:

And Mark, who has told us nothing of the nativity or infancy or youth of the Lord, has made his Gospel begin with the same event… For it is thus that he sets out: The beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God; as it is written in the prophet Isaiah, Behold, I send a messenger before Thy face, which shall prepare Thy way before Thee. The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make His paths straight.

— Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels II.6.18 (local text Book 2). In the same passage he introduces Matthew’s “he shall be called a Nazarene” simply as “the testimony from the prophet.” Bede (c. 730) later applies Augustine’s Matthew 27:9 explanation to the Mark passage.3

Reception and debate

  • Two arguments. Augustine’s judgement on the text (that “Jeremiah” is original) and his explanation of the name (that it is providential) are separate arguments. At Mark 1:2 the United Bible Societies’ committee prints the singular “in Isaiah” and holds that copyists changed it to “the prophets”; John Gill, Ellicott’s Commentary and James Snapp Jr. hold the plural, the reading of the Byzantine text, original and the singular a copyists’ specification; the readings of that verse are set out at How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read?
  • Bede and the Mark passage. Augustine’s fullest treatment is of Matthew’s “Jeremiah”; Bede later applies the providential-memory argument, in nearly the same words, to Mark 1:2.
  • The Matthew 27:9 crux. Matthew’s “Jeremy” is also treated on Judas’s biographical timeline.
