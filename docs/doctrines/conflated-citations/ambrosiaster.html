Ambrosiaster on Mark’s “Isaiah” for Malachi’s words

Dates: c. 380 (fl. c. 366–384, Rome) · Wikipedia
Summary: The anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster is the first writer to pose the Mark 1:2 crux as a formal question and give it the classic answer. His Questions on the Old and New Testaments asks outright why Mark credits to Isaiah words that stand in Malachi, and replies that Mark, no ignorant man, deliberately headed the blended quotation with the name of the earlier prophet — the one who had “first conveyed this meaning.” Naming the senior prophet for a composite citation is thus stated, at the outset of the tradition, as a considered convention rather than a mistake.

Claims & evidence

Question 57 — naming the earlier prophet for a blended citation

Ambrosiaster frames the difficulty as a set question and answers it in full. Mark knew his Scriptures — he “could not have been unaware of what he was writing” — and referred the whole composite back to its original author, Isaiah, then immediately joined the Isaiah line to the Malachi one so that both should stand under the earlier prophet’s name:

Mark could not have been unaware of what he was writing; for he was not lacking in learning, since from childhood he had been immersed in the divine Scriptures and trained in the Law, especially as he had followed the Apostles. Knowing that all sayings must be referred back to their original author, he attributed these words to Isaiah, who had first conveyed this meaning when he said, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight the paths of our God” (Isa. 40:3). Accordingly, after the words from Malachi, the Evangelist immediately added, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness,” so that he might unite the words of both prophets, which relate to one and the same sense, under the name of the earlier prophet.

— Ambrosiaster, Questions on the Old and New Testaments, Question 57 (“What does this mean, that although it is written in the prophet Malachi… the Evangelist Mark claims that this is written in the prophet Isaiah?”). The answer supplies the reasoning — deliberate design, senior prophet — that Jerome, Bede, and the scholastics all inherit.1

Notes

  • One witness, not several. Ambrosiaster’s Questions circulated for centuries under Augustine’s name, so this answer surfaces in Thomas Aquinas’s Catena Aurea as “Augustine, Quaest. nov. et vet. Test. lvii” (i.e. Pseudo-Augustine). It is one voice — Ambrosiaster’s — not an independent Augustinian testimony.
  • The earliest formal answer. Irenaeus already reads Mark’s opening in the plural (“the prophets”), a scribal “correction” of the difficulty; Ambrosiaster is the first to keep the singular “Isaiah,” state the problem as a question, and defend the attribution as intended. See the summary timeline, Naming the Wrong Prophet.
