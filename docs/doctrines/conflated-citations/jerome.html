Jerome on the composite Old Testament citations

Dates: c. 347–420 (the works cited here fall c. 395–398) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome is the pivot of the whole tradition on these “wrong prophet” citations: he is the one father who addresses all four loci — Mark 1:2, Matthew 2:23, Matthew 13:35, and Matthew 27:9 — and supplies the governing principle the later readers inherit. His answer is a method, not an excuse: the Evangelists cite the Old Testament “sense for sense and not word for word,” and a blended quotation is fairly headed with the name of its senior or governing prophet. Only for Matthew 13:35 does he break the pattern and diagnose a genuine scribal slip — the exception that shows he was not reading every case as design.

Claims & evidence

Mark 1:2 — “made up from two prophets, Malachi and Isaiah”

Defending his own method of translation against a critic, Jerome turns to the Evangelists’ practice with the Old Testament and states the Mark crux as bluntly as any modern skeptic. He first quotes Mark’s opening, then names the two sources and poses the difficulty head-on:

Mark, the disciple of Peter, begins his gospel thus:—“The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, as it is written in the prophet Isaiah: Behold I send my messenger before thy face which shall prepare thy way before thee. The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.” This quotation is made up from two prophets, Malachi that is to say and Isaiah. For the first part: “Behold I send my messenger before thy face which shall prepare thy way before thee,” occurs at the close of Malachi. But the second part: “The voice of one crying, etc.,” we read in Isaiah. On what grounds then has Mark in the very beginning of his book set the words: “As it is written in the prophet Isaiah, Behold I send my messenger,” when, as we have said, it is not written in Isaiah at all, but in Malachi the last of the twelve prophets?

— Jerome, Letter 57 to Pammachius §9 (local text Letter 57).1

The governing principle — “the sense we have to look to, and not the words”

Jerome does not resolve the Mark difficulty as an isolated puzzle; he sets it inside a wider thesis he presses through the whole letter, that the Apostles and Evangelists paraphrase Scripture and cite its meaning rather than its wording. He states the rule as a demand on his critics:

…in dealing with the scriptures it is the sense we have to look to and not the words.

— Jerome, Letter 57 §10. He applies exactly this to a citation of Isaiah in Paul: “the apostle has not rendered his original word for word, but, using a paraphrase, he has given the sense in different terms.” The point of gathering these cases — he is explicit — is “not to convict the evangelists of falsification — a charge worthy only of impious men like Celsus, Porphyry, and Julian — but… that they may concede to me in the case of a simple letter what… they will have to concede to the Apostles in the Holy Scriptures.”2

Matthew 2:23 — the plural “prophets” signals meaning, not a verse

On the hardest of the four loci — a saying (“he shall be called a Nazarene”) found in no verse at all — Jerome reads Matthew’s grammar as the key. Matthew wrote the plural “prophets” on purpose, to signal that he is giving the Old Testament’s meaning and not quoting a text:

If he had set a fixed example from the Scriptures, he would never have said, what was spoken by the prophets; but simply, what was spoken by the prophet: but now, calling them prophets in the plural, he shows that he did not take words from the Scriptures, but the meaning. Nazarene, interprets as holy. But all Scripture testifies that the Lord would be holy. We can also say in another way, that even in the same words, according to the Hebrew truth, it is written in Isaiah: There shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Nazarene shall rise out of his roots.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, on 2:23. The second resort points to Isaiah 11:1’s netzer (“branch,” “rod out of the stem of Jesse”) as a possible verbal echo behind “Nazarene.” The same reading recurs in Lives of Illustrious Men 3, where Jerome notes that Matthew “does not follow the authority of the translators of the Septuagint but the Hebrew,” “wherefore these two forms exist ‘Out of Egypt have I called my son,’ and ‘for he shall be called a Nazarene.’”3

Matthew 27:9 — “more like a testimony taken from Zechariah”

On the thirty-pieces oracle credited to “Jeremy the prophet,” Jerome is careful and candid. He knows an apocryphal book of Jeremiah that contained the words, but he does not lean on it; he judges the real source to be Zechariah, cited by the Evangelists’ usual method:

This testimony is not found in Jeremiah. But in Zechariah, who is almost the last of the twelve prophets, a certain similarity is mentioned. And although the meaning does not differ much, nevertheless both the order and the words are different. I recently read in a certain Hebrew volume, which a Hebrew of the Nazarene sect offered to me, an apocryphal book of Jeremiah, in which I found the following words written verbatim. However, it seems to me more like a testimony taken from Zechariah: just like the Evangelists and Apostles commonly do, who, omitting the order of words, only present the meaning from the Old Testament as an example.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, on 27:9–10. The full adversarial weighing of the “Jeremy” crux — where the words are Zechariah’s but the imagery of potter and purchased field is Jeremiah’s — is set out with Judas’s biographical timeline.4

Matthew 13:35 — a scribe’s error, not a composite

Here alone Jerome does not harmonize. The quoted words are wholly Asaph’s (Psalm 78:2), a single text; some early manuscripts read “Isaiah,” and Jerome judges that reading a scribal blunder for the original “Asaph the prophet”:

Because it was not found in Isaiah, I believe it was later removed by prudent men. But it seems to me that it was originally written like this: ‘What is written by the prophet Asaph, saying. The seventy-seventh psalm, from which this testimony is taken, is inscribed with the title of Asaph the prophet.’ And the first scribe did not understand Asaph, and thought it was a mistake of the scribe, and corrected it to the more obvious name of Isaiah. It must be known, therefore, that in the psalms and hymns and songs of God, not only David, but also the others whose names are written, are to be called prophets: namely, Asaph and Idithun, and Heman the Ezrahite, and Ethan, and the sons of Korah…

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, on 13:35. This is the honest counter-example in the set: no composite is in play, so the “name the senior prophet” principle cannot apply, and Jerome reaches for a textual explanation instead. The crux is weighed at Why Matthew 13:35 names Isaiah for Asaph’s psalm.5

Notes

  • The one father who sees the whole phenomenon. Ambrosiaster answers Mark, Chrysostom answers Matthew 2:23, Augustine answers Matthew 27:9; Jerome is the only witness who treats all four together and derives a single rule from them — which is why the later Latin tradition (Bede, the scholastics) speaks in his idiom of the Evangelists giving “the sense, not the words.”
  • Not a universal solvent. Jerome’s willingness to call Matthew 13:35 a scribal error, rather than force it into the composite pattern, is what keeps his method honest. He harmonizes where a composite is really present and diagnoses a slip where it is not.
  • His anti-pagan motive. Jerome frames the whole discussion against Celsus, Porphyry, and Julian — pagan critics who used exactly these citations to charge the Evangelists with error. His interest is apologetic, but the principle he states (paraphrase, not misquotation) is the one modern scholarship recovers as “composite citation.”
