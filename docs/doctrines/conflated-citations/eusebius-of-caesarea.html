Eusebius of Caesarea on Matthew’s “Jeremiah” — the copyist-error conjecture

Dates: c. 315 (the Proof of the Gospel / Demonstratio Evangelica, Book 10, composed at Caesarea c. 314–318; Eusebius c. 260–340) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius is the writer whom the reference literature credits with the “copyist error” answer to Matthew 27:9. Expounding Zechariah 11 in the tenth book of his Proof of the Gospel, he reaches Matthew’s account of Judas and the potter’s field, observes that the oracle Matthew credits to “Jeremiah” is not in Jeremiah, and proposes that a careless scribe wrote “Jeremiah” where the Evangelist had written “Zechariah.” He supposes that the same scribe also altered the wording of the quotation. On this timeline he stands between Origen (c. 246) and Ambrosiaster (c. 380).

Claims & evidence

The oracle not found in Jeremiah: the copyist conjecture

Eusebius has been reading Zechariah 11’s prophecy of the thirty pieces of silver “weighed” for the Lord and “cast… to the potter” in the house of the Lord (in the Septuagint, “into the furnace, the house of God”), and matching it clause by clause against the passion narrative. When he quotes Matthew 27:3–10 in full and reaches the fulfilment formula, he finds that the name Matthew gives is not that of the prophet whose words these are, and turns to the transmission of the text:

But as this passage is not found in the prophecy of Jeremiah, you must consider whether it is to be supposed that they have been removed through any evil intention, or whether there has been an error in copying, through the mistake of some careless transcriber of the Holy Gospels, who wrote Jeremiah instead of Zechariah, where he ought to have copied, “Then was fulfilled that which was written by Zechariah the prophet,” and instead of, “And they cast them into the house of the Lord, into the furnace,” wrote in error, “And they bought with them the field of the potter.”

Two conjectures, then — deliberate tampering (“removed through any evil intention”) or a copyist’s slip — and he develops the second. He extends it beyond the prophet’s name, supposing that the scribe also replaced “they cast them into the house of the Lord, into the furnace” with “they bought… the field of the potter,” so that on Eusebius’s reconstruction Matthew’s text originally tracked Zechariah’s wording exactly.1

His ground: the money cast into the Temple

Eusebius rests the emendation on a correspondence he has just traced: in his reading both the oracle and the narrative put the silver into the sanctuary:

For the prophecy explicitly states that the money was cast into the Temple of the Lord, so does the Gospel: for, “Judas,” it says, “cast the money into the Temple, and departed.” And perhaps it was through this money that the Temple was rendered profane, and the words, “Behold, your house is left unto you desolate,” were fulfilled.

— Eusebius, Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book 10 (on Zechariah 11 and the passion). Because Judas “cast the money into the Temple” (Matthew 27:5), Eusebius reasons the fulfilment clause should have kept the silver in the Temple too — and reads its being cast there as itself the defilement that left the house “desolate.”2

Reception and debate

  • The copyist theory. The explanation that a scribe wrote Jeremiah for Zechariah at Matthew 27:9 is attributed to Eusebius in the reference article on the verse, which also reports Raymond Brown’s statement that the earliest copies read “Jeremiah” and that copies omitting the name are later corrections. Origen (c. 246) had raised “Jeremiah is put for Zechariah” as one of two conjectures; Eusebius develops it at length and extends it to the wording of the citation. On the summary timeline he follows Origen (c. 246) and precedes Ambrosiaster (c. 380). See Naming the Prophet.
  • A different course in Augustine. Augustine (c. 400) considers following the codices without the name and declines, judging “Jeremiah” the original reading on the report that a majority of codices and “the more ancient Greek exemplars” have it.
  • The Matthew 27:9 crux. Matthew’s “Jeremy” is also treated on Judas’s biographical timeline.
