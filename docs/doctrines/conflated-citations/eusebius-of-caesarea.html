Eusebius of Caesarea on Matthew’s “Jeremiah” — the copyist-error conjecture

Dates: c. 315 (the Proof of the Gospel / Demonstratio Evangelica, Book 10, composed at Caesarea c. 314–318; Eusebius c. 260–340) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius is the writer to whom reference works usually credit the “copyist error” answer to Matthew 27:9 — and his own words bear the attribution out. Expounding Zechariah 11 in the tenth book of his Proof of the Gospel, he reaches Matthew’s account of Judas and the potter’s field, observes that the oracle Matthew credits to “Jeremiah” is not in Jeremiah at all, and proposes that a careless scribe wrote “Jeremiah” where the Evangelist had written “Zechariah.” He presses the text-critical route further than anyone before him — suggesting the same scribe also corrupted the wording of the quotation — but in doing so he treats the harder name as a corruption to be undone, the opposite of the direction the manuscripts actually run. He is the early systematizer between Origen (c. 246) and Ambrosiaster (c. 380) on this crux.

Claims & evidence

The oracle is not in Jeremiah — so a scribe must have blundered

Eusebius has been reading Zechariah 11’s prophecy of the thirty pieces of silver “weighed” for the Lord and “cast… to the potter” in the house of the Lord (in the Septuagint, “into the furnace, the house of God”), and matching it clause by clause against the passion narrative. When he quotes Matthew 27:3–10 in full and reaches the fulfilment formula, he cannot square the name Matthew gives with the prophet whose words these are, and turns to the text itself for a remedy:

But as this passage is not found in the prophecy of Jeremiah, you must consider whether it is to be supposed that they have been removed through any evil intention, or whether there has been an error in copying, through the mistake of some careless transcriber of the Holy Gospels, who wrote Jeremiah instead of Zechariah, where he ought to have copied, “Then was fulfilled that which was written by Zechariah the prophet,” and instead of, “And they cast them into the house of the Lord, into the furnace,” wrote in error, “And they bought with them the field of the potter.”

Two conjectures, then — deliberate tampering (“removed through any evil intention”) or a copyist’s slip — and he develops the second. Notably he does not stop at the prophet’s name: he supposes the scribe also replaced “they cast them into the house of the Lord, into the furnace” with “they bought… the field of the potter,” so that on Eusebius’s reconstruction Matthew’s text originally tracked Zechariah’s wording exactly.1

His ground: prophecy and Gospel agree that the money went into the Temple

Eusebius rests the emendation on a genuine convergence he has just traced — that both the oracle and the narrative put the silver into the sanctuary, not (in his proposed original) into a field:

For the prophecy explicitly states that the money was cast into the Temple of the Lord, so does the Gospel: for, “Judas,” it says, “cast the money into the Temple, and departed.” And perhaps it was through this money that the Temple was rendered profane, and the words, “Behold, your house is left unto you desolate,” were fulfilled.

— Eusebius, Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book 10 (on Zechariah 11 and the passion). Because Judas “cast the money into the Temple” (Matthew 27:5), Eusebius reasons the fulfilment clause should have kept the silver in the Temple too — and reads its being cast there as itself the defilement that left the house “desolate.”2

Notes

  • The theory the reference works credit to him — now in his own words. The “a scribe wrote Jeremiah for Zechariah” explanation of Matthew 27:9 is regularly attributed to Eusebius; the attribution holds. He does not originate it — Origen (c. 246) had already floated “Jeremiah is put for Zechariah” as one of two conjectures two generations earlier — but Eusebius is the first to work it up into a sustained text-critical argument, and to extend it from the prophet’s name to the wording of the citation. On the summary timeline he fills the gap between Origen (c. 246) and Ambrosiaster (c. 380). See Naming the Wrong Prophet.
  • Where he overreaches — the correction runs the wrong way. Eusebius treats “Jeremiah” as a corruption to be undone, restoring “Zechariah.” But the manuscripts point the other way: “Jeremiah” is the lectio difficilior, read by the earliest and best witnesses, and the copies that drop the name or substitute “Zechariah” are the later softenings — scribes doing exactly what Eusebius proposes the original scribe did, but in reverse and demonstrably after the fact. His second, bolder emendation — deleting the potter’s field for “the furnace, the house of God” — has no manuscript support at all and cuts against Matthew’s narrative, where the priests’ purchase of the field to bury strangers, and its renaming as the Field of Blood, are integral (and independently attested at Acts 1:18–19). So his conjecture, taken as a positive account, does not hold: the difficulty is real, but the text he wants to restore is the one the copyists made, not the one Matthew wrote.
  • The crux itself. The full adversarial weighing of Matthew 27:9 — why the deliberate-composite reading is sound, and why the copyist conjecture (Origen, Eusebius) is a difficulty-driven fallback rather than a reading with evidence of its own — is argued with Judas’s biographical timeline (Why does Matthew credit the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet”?).
