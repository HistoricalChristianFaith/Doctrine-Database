Where does Matthew 2:23’s “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” come from?

Disputed reading · on Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations · witness: the Gospel of Matthew

Related readings: How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read? · Did Matthew 13:35 originally name “Isaiah,” “Asaph,” or simply “the prophet”?

The passage

And he came and dwelt in a city called Nazareth: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophets, He shall be called a Nazarene.

— Matthew 2:23. The words “He shall be called a Nazarene” do not stand verbatim in any Old Testament verse. The texts most often proposed as their background are Isaiah 11:1 (the netzer, “branch”), Judges 13:5 and 13:7 (Samson “a Nazarite unto God”), and Isaiah 4:3 (“shall be called holy”).1

The question

Matthew names no single prophet here and quotes words found in no extant prophetic book. What is disputed is where the saying comes from: a prophetic writing no longer extant, the general sense of the prophets, or a particular word in one or more prophetic texts. The answers show how readers from the fourth century onward have understood the Gospel’s fulfilment formulas.

A saying from prophetic writings no longer extant

In brief: Matthew quotes a prophecy that once stood in writing, or in tradition, and has since been lost.

Held by: John Chrysostom, Homilies on Matthew 9 (c. 390); Theophylact of Ohrid, Commentary on Matthew (c. 1100), who adds an unwritten prophecy as an alternative; and the twelfth-century Byzantine commentator Euthymius Zigabenus, as quoted in the Expositor’s Greek Testament.2

On this reading Matthew is quoting, and the source is simply no longer in our hands. Chrysostom answers the question directly: “And what manner of prophet said this? Be not curious, nor overbusy. For many of the prophetic writings have been lost; and this one may see from the history of the Chronicles.” He grounds the loss in Israel’s own history: “being negligent, and continually falling into ungodliness, some they suffered to perish, others they themselves burnt up and cut to pieces,” as Jeremiah relates, and the book of Deuteronomy itself was found only after it had lain “buried somewhere and lost.” On Chrysostom’s account, a prophecy known to Matthew and absent from the surviving books is what that history leads one to expect. Theophylact repeats the answer, “Which prophet said this? It is not to be found now. For many books have been lost, because of both the carelessness of the Hebrews and the frequent captivities,” and adds that “perhaps this was an unwritten prophecy among the Jews.” Euthymius Zigabenus, after Chrysostom, gives the same counsel: “Don’t ask… in what prophets; you will not find: many prophetic books were lost.” On this reading the formula “spoken by the prophets” reports a real prophetic utterance, and the Gospel preserves a word of prophecy that would otherwise be unknown.3

The prophets’ sense gathered, not a single verse

In brief: the plural “prophets” tells the reader that Matthew is summing up what the prophets said, not quoting one text.

Held by: Jerome, Commentary on Matthew (c. 398), as his first account; R. T. France, The Gospel of Matthew (2007); and Jared M. August, “The Non-Citation of Matthew 2:23,” Tyndale Bulletin (2018).4

On this reading the plural is the key. Jerome put it first: “If he had set down a fixed example from the Scriptures, he would never say: what was spoken through the prophets, but simply: what was spoken through the prophet; but as it is, by naming the prophets in the plural, he shows that he took from the Scriptures not the words but the sense.” August finds the same signal across the whole Gospel. Matthew has fourteen fulfilment formulas; the ten that quote a specific verse use a singular noun and the participle “saying” (legontos), while the four that develop a theme (2:23; 5:17; 26:54, 56) use a plural or collective noun and drop the participle. At 27:9, where Matthew draws on more than one text, he keeps the singular “Jeremy the prophet,” so the plural at 2:23 marks a different kind of citation. August holds that the formula itself signals a theme to a Greek reader, for whom the Septuagint renders the netzer of Isaiah 11:1 with rhiza, “root.” France names the theme: the prophets’ expectation of a lowly, rejected Messiah, the humbled shepherd of Zechariah 9–14, the sufferer of Psalms 22 and 69, the despised servant of Isaiah 52–53. Nazareth was an obscure village, and “it is Nazareth’s very absence from the Old Testament which makes it a fitting fulfilment of this Old Testament theme.” On this reading Matthew’s “Nazarene” gathers the prophets’ witness to a Messiah from a place of no account, and the plural formula is how he says so.5

A play on a particular prophetic word

In brief: “Nazarene” echoes a specific Hebrew word in the prophets: the branch, the holy one, or the Nazirite.

Held by: Jerome, Commentary on Matthew (c. 398), as his second account (Isaiah 11:1); Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Matthew (“holy” or “flower”); W. D. Davies and Dale C. Allison, Matthew (1988), with Isaiah 4:3 primary; and Maarten J. J. Menken (2001), with the Nazirite of Judges 13.6

On this reading “He shall be called a Nazarene” is built on a word the prophets actually used, and the town’s name answers it. Jerome gives the Hebrew link: “it is written in Isaiah even in the same words according to the Hebrew truth: A rod shall go forth from the root of Jesse, and a nazarene shall rise up from the root.” The netzer, the branch of Jesse, carries a messianic sense and gives a consonantal play on Nazōraios; D. A. Carson reads Matthew’s phrase as a “discreet allusion to the neṣer” of Isaiah 11:1. A second line reads the name as “holy.” Jerome reports that “Nazarene is interpreted holy,” Theophylact and Aquinas say the same, and Aquinas also offers “abounding with flowers” from Isaiah 11:1. Davies and Allison make Isaiah 4:3, “shall be called holy,” the primary text, with Isaiah 11:1 secondary. A third line hears nazir: the angel tells Samson’s mother that the child will be “a Nazarite unto God from the womb” (Judges 13:5, 7; 16:17). Menken takes this as the primary source, the plural “prophets” pointing to the Former Prophets, which include Judges, so that Jesus appears as a second Samson. Each of these proposals gives the formula a real prophetic text and a word Matthew could expect a Hebrew-reading audience to hear.7
