Why does Mark say “in Isaiah the prophet” for a Malachi–Isaiah blend?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — Mark’s opening citation is best read as a deliberate composite: a fusion of Malachi 3:1 (itself conformed to the wording of Exodus 23:20) with Isaiah 40:3, headed with the name of the major and thematically governing prophet, Isaiah. Naming one prophet for a blended quotation was a recognized ancient convention, not a blunder; the harder attribution “in Isaiah” is the original text, and the softening to “in the prophets” in the later manuscripts is itself the surest sign that scribes felt — and “corrected” — exactly this difficulty. What remains genuinely open is narrower: why Isaiah is named rather than Malachi (the more prominent prophet, the governing text, or a pre-formed source), and whether “in Isaiah” is even the reading Mark wrote — a minority still defends the plural, on which route there is no misattribution at all.

This is one instance of a recurring pattern — the Gospels naming one prophet, or “the prophets,” for a composite or unlocatable Old Testament citation (so too Matthew 27:9, Matthew 2:23, and Matthew 13:35). The reception of that whole phenomenon, and the shared principle the fathers used to answer it, is traced on the Naming the Wrong Prophet timeline; the parallel Matthew 27:9 crux is weighed at Why Matthew credits the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet.”

Related arguments: naming the prophet — see also Why Matthew says “the prophets” for the Nazarene saying (Matthew 2:23) · Why Matthew names Jeremiah (Matthew 27:9) · Why Matthew 13:35 names Isaiah for Asaph’s psalm (Matthew 13:35)

The claim

The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in Isaiah the prophet, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, who shall prepare thy way; the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.

— Mark 1:2–3 (in the reading of the earliest manuscripts; the King James, following the later Byzantine text, reads “in the prophets”). Of the two quoted lines, only the second — “The voice of one crying in the wilderness” — is from Isaiah 40:3. The first — “Behold, I send my messenger before thy face” — is Malachi 3:1, its wording drawn toward Exodus 23:20 (“Behold, I send an Angel before thee… to bring thee into the place which I have prepared”). The charge: Mark has credited to Isaiah words that are Malachi’s, a plain error at the very head of the earliest Gospel.1

Who advances it

The reading that the ascription is deliberate, not a slip, is the ancient and mainstream one. Ambrosiaster (c. 380) is the first to pose the crux as a formal question and answer it; Jerome (c. 398) states the governing principle; Bede (c. 730) gives it its fullest harmonizing form; and the Greek catena tradition (Victor of Antioch, transmitted as Pseudo-Chrysostom) lays the two live options side by side. Modern text-criticism — Metzger’s United Bible Societies committee — vindicates “in Isaiah” as the original wording, and modern exegesis (the two-volume survey of Sean Adams and Seth Ehorn) recovers “composite citation” as a documented convention across ancient Jewish, Greco-Roman, and Christian writing. The dissenting minority — that the plural “in the prophets” is the original text, so that no misattribution ever stood — is defended in our day by James Snapp Jr., against the committee’s near-certain rating.2

The evidence for a deliberate composite

No single passage contains what Mark quotes. The opening line fuses two texts before it ever reaches Isaiah: Malachi’s “Behold, I will send my messenger, and he shall prepare the way before me” is recast into the second-person “before thy face… prepare thy way,” borrowing the frame of Exodus 23:20’s angel “sent before thee.” Only then comes Isaiah 40:3. So the citation is already a mosaic of three passages, and the question is not whether Mark blended texts — plainly he did — but why he headed the blend with one name. Naming the senior or governing prophet was an accepted convention. Ancient authors regularly combined testimonies and attributed the whole to the more prominent source; Matthew does the same at 27:9 (Zechariah’s words under Jeremiah’s name) and at 21:4–5 (Isaiah + Zechariah). Isaiah is the fittest head for this composite on every count: he is the major prophet against Malachi’s minor, and Isaiah 40:3 — the herald’s “voice in the wilderness” — is the text that all four Gospels make the governing prophecy of John the Baptist’s ministry, the passage the whole citation exists to reach. The best readers, ancient and modern, saw design here. Ambrosiaster argued that Mark “could not have been unaware of what he was writing… Knowing that all sayings must be referred back to their original author, he attributed these words to Isaiah, who had first conveyed this meaning… so that he might unite the words of both prophets… under the name of the earlier prophet.” Jerome, treating the same crux, granted flatly that the quotation “is made up from two prophets, Malachi that is to say and Isaiah,” and defended it by the evangelists’ settled method of citing “sense for sense and not word for word.” And modern scholarship classifies precisely this kind of citation — combined, conflated, condensed — as a normal ancient technique rather than a copyist’s failure.3

Is “in Isaiah the prophet” even the original reading?

The crux exists only if Mark wrote “in Isaiah” in the first place, and the manuscripts divide. The earliest and weightiest witnesses — Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, the papyrus P76, and the Caesarean and Western strands — read “in Isaiah the prophet”; the great mass of the later Byzantine manuscripts (with Alexandrinus and Washingtonianus) read the general “in the prophets,” and it is this plural that stands in the Textus Receptus and so in the King James. The United Bible Societies’ committee prints “in Isaiah the prophet” with an {A} rating (virtually certain), on a clean lectio-difficilior argument: the composite Malachi–Isaiah quotation gave scribes an obvious motive to soften the singular, mistaken-looking name to the safe plural “the prophets,” whereas no scribe would create the difficulty by narrowing an accurate “prophets” to a wrong “Isaiah.” The change runs one way, and Irenaeus already quotes the plural before 200 — the “correction” began early. The minority case cuts the other way and deserves stating. James Snapp Jr. argues that early copyists had a documented itch toward specificity — supplying a prophet’s name where the text ran generic (so at Matthew 1:22, 2:5, and 13:35) — which, if it operated here, could have generated “Isaiah” out of an original “prophets.” The point is real but does not overturn the committee’s verdict: the parallels Snapp cites are scattered scribal additions, not the concerted, majority-becoming movement his reading needs, and the internal logic still favours the harder name as the seedbed of the change. Either way, the result defeats the bare-error charge from both directions at once: on the near-certain original “in Isaiah,” the ascription is a deliberate composite; and on the minority “in the prophets,” there is simply no misattribution to explain.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That Mark’s citation is a composite is not in dispute — the words are demonstrably Malachi’s and Isaiah’s, with an Exodus 23:20 cast — and the convention of heading a composite with the major or governing prophet is well attested, both in Matthew and in the wider ancient practice catalogued by Adams and Ehorn. Isaiah is the natural head: the greater prophet, and the source of the very line (40:3) the whole citation is built to deliver. The fathers who felt the difficulty most sharply (Ambrosiaster, Jerome, Bede) all read it as design, and modern text-criticism confirms that the “wrong” name is the original wording — which is decisive, because a mistaken-looking original that scribes then smoothed is exactly what a genuine authorial composite would leave behind. This is the mainstream explanation, and the flat charge of a blunder is the weaker verdict.

What it cannot reach. The account fixes that the citation is a deliberate composite more securely than why Isaiah in particular is named — the “major prophet,” the “governing text,” and the possibility that Mark drew the pairing from a pre-formed collection of testimonies (in which case the grouping, not the naming, is what he inherited — a mechanism weighed on its own at Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection?) all fit, and none is singly decisive. Bede himself conceded the softer possibility that “Isaiah occurred to the mind of Mark… instead of Malachi, as happens,” uncorrected because he took even the lapse to be Spirit-guided — a candid admission that pure design cannot be strictly proved against an inadvertent but harmless substitution. And the whole crux rests on a contested text: the minority reading, if right, dissolves it. But on the mainstream text and reading alike, the interpretation stands; the residue is the motive, not the fact.5

Assessment

Sound. Mark’s “in Isaiah the prophet” is best read as a deliberate composite citation — Malachi 3:1 (in an Exodus 23:20 frame) fused with Isaiah 40:3 and headed with the name of the major, governing prophet — not as a misremembered source. The reading is coherent, matches a documented ancient convention and Matthew’s own practice, and is confirmed by the text-critical finding that the harder “Isaiah” is original and the plural a later softening. The bare-error charge is the weaker alternative, and it fails whether one takes the near-certain “in Isaiah” (a deliberate composite) or the minority “in the prophets” (no misattribution at all). What stays genuinely open is only why Isaiah is named — a question underdetermined among good answers, not a crack in the reading.
