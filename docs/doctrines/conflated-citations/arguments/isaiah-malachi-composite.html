How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read?

Disputed reading · on Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations · witness: the Gospel of Mark

Related readings: Where does Matthew 2:23’s “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” come from? · Did Matthew 13:35 originally name “Isaiah,” “Asaph,” or simply “the prophet”? · Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection?

The passage

The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in Isaiah the prophet, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, who shall prepare thy way; the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.

— Mark 1:1–3, in the wording of Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus; the King James Version, following the Byzantine text, reads “in the prophets.” Of the two quoted lines, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness” is Isaiah 40:3; “Behold, I send my messenger before thy face” is Malachi 3:1, its wording close to Exodus 23:20 (“Behold, I send an Angel before thee… to bring thee into the place which I have prepared”).1

The question

The manuscripts divide between “in Isaiah the prophet” and “in the prophets,” and Irenaeus (c. 180), in the Latin translation in which Against Heresies III survives, quotes the verse both ways.2 What is disputed is which wording Mark wrote and, if he wrote “Isaiah,” whether the name heads a deliberate composite, came by a lapse of memory, or is an inexact ascription. The answers given since the third century show how readers of each period handled the heading of a two-prophet quotation. Academic literature defending the plural beyond the commentaries cited was not located.

An inexact ascription

In brief: Mark wrote “Isaiah,” and the name does not fit the first line he quotes.

Held by: Porphyry (third century), as reported in the notes to Bengel’s Gnomon; Victor of Antioch, a contemporary of John Chrysostom, as the first of two options; the Expositor’s Greek Testament; and J. Rendel Harris, Testimonies, Part I (1916).3

On this reading the name in the earliest Greek text is the place to begin, and it does not cover the words that follow it: “Behold, I send my messenger” stands in Malachi, not in Isaiah. Porphyry, on the report of Bengel’s editors, used the verse in charging Mark with a false ascription, and the editors take the charge as evidence that “Isaiah” stood in the Greek or Syriac copies of his day. Victor of Antioch, in the Greek catena tradition, gives as his first option: “it may be said that it is a mistake of the writer.” The Expositor’s Greek Testament calls it “an inaccuracy doubtless, but not through an error of memory (Meyer and Weiss), but through indifference to greater exactness, the quotation from Isaiah being what chiefly occupied the mind.” Harris gives the inexactness a source. In a written collection of proof-texts, titles stood beside the extracts, and a sequence headed “Malachi. Behold, I send my messenger. / Isaiah. The voice of one crying” invites exactly this error: “Either the title of a quotation has slipped… or the evangelist himself has let his eye wander from one marginal ascription to another in the sequence.” He holds the singular to be the primitive text and the plural its early correction: “The revised text is therefore wrong in fact but right in tradition”; it was “probably a wrong text in the Testimony Book before it became a wrong text in Mark.” Read this way, the verse preserves an ancient citing habit in which a familiar prophet’s name could travel with a cluster of texts, and its correction in later copies shows how early readers noticed the mismatch.4

A deliberate composite under the earlier prophet’s name

In brief: Mark joined Malachi’s words to Isaiah’s on purpose and headed the whole with Isaiah’s name.

Held by: Ambrosiaster, Questions on the Old and New Testaments 57 (c. 380); Victor of Antioch, as his second option; Bede, Commentary on Mark (c. 730), as his first account; and the Lutheran scholar Johann Albrecht Bengel, Gnomon Novi Testamenti (1742).5

On this reading Mark knew what he was quoting and chose the heading. Ambrosiaster argues that Mark “could not have been unaware of what he was writing”: “Knowing that all sayings must be referred back to their original author, he attributed these words to Isaiah, who had first conveyed this meaning,” and set Isaiah’s line straight after Malachi’s “so that he might unite the words of both prophets, which relate to one and the same sense, under the name of the earlier prophet.” Victor of Antioch puts it as a method: Mark “has compressed into one, two prophecies delivered in different places by two prophets.” Bede finds the sense of the Malachi line in Isaiah’s own: the messenger sent before the Lord’s face and the voice crying in the desert announce the same preparation, so “the evangelist should not be thought to have erred.” Bengel sets the verse among parallel cases: Matthew 21:4–5 quotes Zechariah “under the title of one prophet” while blending in Isaiah 62:11, and Romans 9:27 names Isaiah while interweaving Hosea, “so Mark quotes two prophets, and yet mentions by name only the one, the prophet Isaiah.” He gives the reason for the choice: “Isaiah is more copious and better known, and his testimony… used to be read in public on the Sabbath,” and Malachi’s line comes in “in a kind of parenthetic way.” Isaiah 40:3 is the line all four Gospels apply to John the Baptist, and on this reading it is the text the whole citation is built to reach. The survey of composite citation edited by Sean Adams and Seth Ehorn sets such combined and conflated quotations within the citing practice of Jewish, Greco-Roman and Christian authors.6

A name supplied by memory and left standing under the Spirit’s guidance

In brief: Isaiah’s name may have come to Mark’s mind in place of Malachi’s, and he let it stand because the Spirit governed his memory.

Held by: Bede, Commentary on Mark (c. 730), as his second account, applying to Mark the explanation Augustine gave for Matthew 27:9 in the Harmony of the Gospels (c. 400).7

On this reading the name came to Mark as names come to any writer, and its standing in the Gospel is a matter of providence. Bede allows that “it could have happened that in the mind of Mark writing the Gospel, Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi, as happens.” Mark could have corrected it, “at least being admonished by others who could read this while he was still living in the flesh,” and did not, because “the name of another prophet occurred to his memory, which was governed by the Holy Spirit, not without reason, because the Lord ordained it to be written thus.” The purpose Bede names is the unity of the prophets: “all the holy prophets spoke by one spirit in wonderful agreement,” so that “what has been said through Isaiah is as much Malachi’s as Isaiah’s.” Augustine had reasoned the same way about Matthew’s “Jeremiah,” holding that the Evangelist would have corrected the name “had he not reflected that… it was not without a purpose that the name of the one prophet had been suggested instead of the other.” On this reading the attribution teaches that every prophet’s word belongs to all of them, which is why the Evangelist could leave it as it came.8

The original wording is “in the prophets”

In brief: Mark wrote the plural, and “Isaiah” was supplied later by copyists.

Held by: the Particular Baptist scholar John Gill, Exposition of the New Testament (1746–48); Ellicott’s Commentary for English Readers; and the text critic James Snapp Jr. (2017).9

On this reading Mark’s heading matched his quotation from the start. Gill judges the plural “the better reading, since two prophets are cited, and Isaiah is the last; to which agree the Arabic and Ethiopic versions, and the greater number of Greek copies.” Ellicott’s Commentary grants that “the better MSS. give the more accurate reference, ‘in Esaias the prophet,’” but holds that “on general grounds… it seems more probable that the general reference should have been specialised by a transcriber than the reverse.” Snapp gives that general ground its evidence: early copyists show “the tendency to make non-specific quotations of Old Testament prophets more specific.” Witnesses add Isaiah’s name at Matthew 1:22; Codex Vercellensis reads “through Isaiah the prophet” for Micah’s words at Matthew 2:5; and copies in the Caesarean stream name Isaiah for a psalm at Matthew 13:35. The plural is read by Codex Alexandrinus, Codex Washingtonianus, family 13 and the Byzantine text, and Irenaeus (c. 180) quotes it, in the Latin translation in which Against Heresies III survives. On this reading the plural is the Evangelist’s accurate heading for a two-prophet quotation, and the singular belongs to the history of copying, as an addition early hands made at more than one fulfilment formula.10
