Did Paul combine the scriptural texts in Romans 3:10–18, 9:33 and 11:8 himself, or receive them already combined?

Disputed reading · on Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations · witness: Paul, Epistle to the Romans

Related readings: Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection?

The passage

Behold, I lay in Sion a stumblingstone and rock of offence: and whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed.

— Romans 9:33, joining Isaiah 28:16 (the foundation-stone, whose believer “shall not be ashamed”) with the “stone of stumbling, and… rock of offence” of Isaiah 8:14. The same two texts, with Psalm 118:22, stand together at 1 Peter 2:6–8. Under one “as it is written,” Romans 3:10–18 strings Psalms 14:1–3, 5:9, 140:3, 10:7, Isaiah 59:7–8 and Psalm 36:1; Romans 11:8 joins the “spirit of deep sleep” of Isaiah 29:10 to the unseeing eyes and unhearing ears of Deuteronomy 29:4. None of these names a prophet. Hebrews 1 builds a related chain of seven citations, each with its own introductory formula (“And again”).1

The question

That these citations join several texts under one formula is common ground among their readers. What is disputed is whether Paul joined the texts himself, in his own reading and writing, or took them over already joined in an earlier Christian (or Jewish) collection or tradition. The answer bears on where composite citation, including the Gospel cases on this timeline, came from.

Paul’s own combining of texts from his reading

In brief: Paul gathered these texts himself, from Scripture or from his own excerpts, and joined them to make his argument.

Held by: Origen, Commentary on Romans (third century); Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Romans (thirteenth century); Dietrich-Alex Koch, Die Schrift als Zeuge des Evangeliums (1986); and Christopher D. Stanley, Paul and the Language of Scripture (1992).2

On this reading the seams in these citations are the marks of Paul’s own work. Origen follows the Romans 3 chain source by source: “Their throat is an open sepulchre” “you will find in the fifth psalm,” the next line he thinks “was itself also taken from some psalm, the wording being changed,” and the cursing line “seems to be received from the ninth psalm”; returning to the verse, he says of Paul, “taking from the fifth psalm, as we said above, he says: ‘Their throat is an open sepulchre.’” Aquinas names the act as the Apostle’s. At Romans 9:33, “the Apostle gathers together the words of Isaiah found in various places”; at 11:8, “the Apostle bases himself on two passages from Isaiah,” and to the second “the Apostle adds on his own: until this present day.” Koch and Stanley give the practice a setting. Koch proposed that Paul, in the course of his own reading of Scripture, made excerpts for himself and drew on them when writing his letters; Stanley elaborated this: “This growing collection of biblical excerpts would then have become his primary resource for meditation and study,” and “the great majority of Paul’s quotations would have come directly from this Pauline anthology.” Such a collection, Stanley argues, explains why “the close integration of many of Paul’s biblical citations into their present argumentative contexts becomes more comprehensible if the verses were selected from the start for their value in addressing a recurring problem.” Stanley sets this beside the widespread ancient practice of making excerpts from one’s reading. On this reading the combined citations of Romans are deliberate citation technique, the work of a writer who chose, cut and joined his texts for the argument in hand.3

Combinations received from earlier testimony collections

In brief: some of Paul’s combined texts, the stone texts above all, came to him already joined in a collection of proof-texts.

Held by: J. Rendel Harris, Testimonies (1916–20); Martin C. Albl, “And Scripture Cannot Be Broken” (1999); and David Lincicum (2008), for Romans 9:33 only.4

On this reading a combination that appears in more than one writer points to a source older than any of them. The stone texts are the leading case. Isaiah 28:16 and 8:14 stand joined in Romans 9:33 and again, with Psalm 118:22, in 1 Peter 2:6–8, and Cyprian’s Testimonies 2.16 pairs Isaiah 28:16 with Psalm 118:22. Harris drew the conclusion: “we suggested the dependence both of Peter and of Paul upon a primitive collection of Testimonies against the Jews, from which they had without collusion derived the doctrine that Christ was foretold in the Scriptures to be the Stone laid in Zion for a sure foundation, and equally laid for a stumbling stone on the part of the unbelieving Jews.” Working through the whole letter, he found that “it is surprising to find how little is left of scriptural quotation in the Epistle after this test is applied, and we may affirm, at all events for Romans, that St Paul was a traditionalist, operating with conventional and approved matter, to a degree far beyond what we should a priori have expected.” Albl, acknowledging Koch’s and Stanley’s account, holds to a loose collection of testimonia that Paul inherited and incorporated into his letters, and finds Jewish traditions taken up into his parenesis and his versions of the Decalogue. Lincicum, who holds that collections cannot account for Paul’s engagement with Scripture as a whole, grants the point for the stone citation: “No doubt some of Paul’s quotations of the OT were traditional in nature (possibly Isa 28:16/8:14 in Rom 9:33).” On this reading Paul’s combined citations carry forward the shared proof-texts of the churches before him, and Romans 9:33 preserves one of the oldest of them.5
