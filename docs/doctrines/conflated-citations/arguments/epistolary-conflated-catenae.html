Do Paul’s and Hebrews’ unattributed conflated catenae show composite citation was a workaday convention?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that the epistles’ unattributed catenae (the strung proof-texts of Romans 3:10–18, the two-Isaiah “stone” of Romans 9:33, the Deuteronomy–Isaiah splice of Romans 11:8, and the “string of pearls” that opens Hebrews 1) are genuine instances of the same combining-and-fusing technique the Gospel cruxes turn on is well-grounded and uncontested. These are the New Testament’s purest specimens of composite citation, because no one is misnamed: the writer fuses several texts under one formula, or clusters them into one argument, without pinning the result on a single wrong prophet. So they cannot be dismissed as blunders — there is no attribution to be “wrong” about — and they show the same authors’ milieu handling the Old Testament by combination as a matter of course. That is exactly the “settled floor” the Naming the Wrong Prophet timeline rests on: composite citation is a documented ancient convention, not a Gospel accident. What the comparanda establish is the conflation half of the phenomenon only; they do not, by themselves, license the wrong-prophet half (the misattribution), which still needs the “name the senior prophet” convention or a genuine slip — the same limit the testimonia-source argument reaches from the other direction.

This argument weighs a body of comparanda that cut across the whole doctrine rather than any one crux. The four contested Gospel cases all pair a conflation with a single (allegedly wrong) prophet’s name; strip the name away and what remains — the bare act of welding two or more scriptural texts into one citation — is on open display all through the epistles, unattributed and unchallenged. If that act is a recognized convention where nobody cries “error,” then the burden on the Gospel cruxes is not “is fusing texts a thing an ancient author would do?” (plainly it is) but only “why this name for the blend?” The comparanda do not sit on the timeline (they name no prophet, so they are not members of the “Naming the Wrong Prophet” set) and they are not a text-form question (their Vorlage is adjudicated verse by verse on Which text did the New Testament quote?); they belong here, under the composite-citation doctrine, as the convention’s clearest evidence.

The claim

Modern citation-technique scholarship treats composite citation as a normal feature of the way Second-Temple and early-Christian writers used Scripture, and the Pauline and Hebrews catenae are its stock New Testament examples. Sean Adams and Seth Ehorn, whose two-volume Composite Citations in Antiquity is the standard survey, define the category and sort it into combined (two or more texts joined under one citation), conflated (texts interwoven so their wording merges), and condensed citations, and show the technique running across Jewish, Greco-Roman, and Christian authors alike — so that a fused quotation is a craft, not a mistake.1 On that account the epistles’ catenae are the cleanest demonstrations available: the writer signals “as it is written” once and then gives a mosaic, and the early readers who noticed treated the mosaic as ordinary exegesis, not as a fault to be excused.

The evidence for

Romans 3:10–18 — the textbook combined citation. Under a single “as it is written” Paul strings six separate texts into one continuous indictment: Psalm 14:1–3 (“there is none righteous…”), Psalm 5:9 (“their throat is an open sepulchre”), Psalm 140:3 (“the poison of asps”), Psalm 10:7 (“full of cursing”), Isaiah 59:7–8 (“their feet are swift to shed blood”), and Psalm 36:1 (“no fear of God before their eyes”). No prophet is named, no seam is hidden, and the result reads as one text. That this was recognized as a chain and not a single quotation is visible already in Origen, whose Commentary on Romans tracks the catena source by source — noting, at 3:13, that Paul is here “taking from the fifth psalm, as we said above” — treating the compositeness as plain fact.2 (A curiosity confirms the direction of borrowing: the Septuagint of Psalm 14:3 later absorbed Paul’s whole 3:13–18 chain, so a Greek psalter copy came to quote Paul rather than the reverse — adjudicated as a text-form row on the catalog.)

Romans 9:33 — a conflation of two Isaiah “stone” texts. “Behold, I lay in Sion a stumblingstone and rock of offence: and whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed” fuses Isaiah 28:16 (the tried foundation-stone, whose believer “shall not be ashamed”) with the “stone of stumbling, and… rock of offence” of Isaiah 8:14, wrapping the second inside the first. Thomas Aquinas sets the mechanism out cleanly: “the Apostle gathers together the words of Isaiah found in various places” — the opening from Isaiah 28:16, the middle (“a stone that will make men stumble”) from Isaiah 8:14, the close (“shall not be confounded”) from Isaiah 28:16 again.3 This is the most instructive comparandum, because the same fused two-Isaiah unit reappears at 1 Peter 2:6–8 — a “fixed testimonium” Paul and Peter share (so the catalog’s Isaiah 8:14 row) — which points past either author to a pre-formed stone-testimony they both drew on: exactly what the testimonia-source hypothesis predicts, and a conflation demonstrably older than its two New-Testament uses.

Romans 11:8 — a splice across the Law and the Prophets. “God hath given them the spirit of slumber, eyes that they should not see, and ears that they should not hear” joins the “spirit of deep sleep” of Isaiah 29:10 to the unseeing eyes and unhearing ears of Deuteronomy 29:4 — a Prophets-and-Torah blend under one formula. Aquinas again names the seam without embarrassment: “the Apostle bases himself on two passages from Isaiah” (he reads the second half as Isaiah 6:10), and notes what Paul “adds on his own” (“until this present day”) — the editorial welding a composite requires.4

Hebrews 1–2 — the catena as florilegium. The epistle’s opening argument for the Son’s superiority to the angels is a chain of seven scriptural citations — Psalm 2:7 and 2 Samuel 7:14 (Heb 1:5), Deuteronomy 32:43 / Psalm 97:7 (1:6), Psalm 104:4 (1:7), Psalm 45:6–7 (1:8–9), Psalm 102:25–27 (1:10–12), and Psalm 110:1 (1:13) — extended in chapter 2 with Psalm 8:4–6, Psalm 22:22, and Isaiah 8:17–18. Here the register is slightly different, and honesty requires the distinction: Hebrews 1 is a catena (or florilegium) — discrete citations, each with its own introductory formula (“And again,” “But unto the Son he saith”) — not a single fused quotation like Romans 9:33. It is the same scribal-exegetical culture of clustering proof-texts, but the clustering is additive, not welded; the composite-citation surveys treat Hebrews’ catena as a related, adjacent phenomenon rather than an identical one. Its evidential force here is therefore modest and correctly stated: it shows the habit of building an argument out of a strung ensemble of texts was native to the same first-century milieu — not that every such ensemble is a “conflation” in the technical sense.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The compositeness of Romans 3:10–18, 9:33, and 11:8 is not in dispute: they are among the standard textbook examples of combined and conflated citation, they were recognized as chains and splices by early readers (Origen, Aquinas) who saw no fault in them, and the technique is documented across the ancient corpus by the field’s standard survey. As comparanda for the convention — the claim that fusing Scripture under one citation was ordinary craft — they are decisive. And because these catenae name no prophet, they carry the point the Gospel cruxes cannot carry cleanly: that combination as such attracts no charge of error. The “settled floor” the timeline asserts is, on this evidence, genuinely settled.

What the comparanda do not reach.

  1. They evidence the conflation, not the wrong prophet. This is the sharpest limit, and it is the same one that bounds the testimonia-source argument. The epistolary catenae are unattributed: Paul writes “as it is written” and names no author, so nothing in them explains how a single, wrong prophet’s name comes to head a Gospel blend. They establish that fusing texts is ordinary; the misattribution of Mark 1:2 (“Isaiah”) or Matthew 27:9 (“Jeremy”) is a second phenomenon that still needs the “name the senior or governing prophet” convention — set out at Why Matthew names Jeremiah — or a copyist’s slip. The comparanda strengthen the floor; they do not collapse the four cruxes into “solved.”

  2. Hebrews’ catena is not a conflation in the strict sense. Folded in above: the Hebrews 1 chain is a florilegium of separately-introduced citations, so it is comparanda for the clustering habit, not for the welding that defines a conflated citation. Counting it as a “conflation” would overstate; it is included as the adjacent, additive register of the same proof-text culture, and rated for exactly that.

  3. Whether Paul fused these himself or inherited them fused is a separate, open question. The shared Romans 9:33 / 1 Peter 2:6–8 stone-testimonium suggests at least one of these composites is pre-formed — older than either apostle’s use of it. That does not weaken the point (the convention is real either way) but it relocates the compositional act, exactly as the testimonia-source argument works out; the two arguments are companions, not rivals.

Assessment

Sound. The unattributed conflated catenae of Paul — Romans 3:10–18 (a six-text combined citation), 9:33 (a two-Isaiah conflation), and 11:8 (a Deuteronomy–Isaiah splice) — are well-grounded, uncontested instances of composite citation, recognized as chains by their earliest careful readers and catalogued as the technique’s stock examples by modern scholarship; Hebrews’ opening catena is the same proof-text culture in its additive, florilegium register. Together they are the New Testament’s clearest demonstration that combining and fusing Scripture under one citation was workaday craft, not a Gospel blunder — which is precisely the floor the Naming the Wrong Prophet doctrine rests on, here corroborated from outside the four disputed cruxes and with no misattribution in play to muddy it. The one thing to hold firmly is the scope: these comparanda prove the conflation was ordinary, not that the wrong prophet is thereby explained — that remains the work of the “name the senior prophet” convention and, in the one genuine counter-case (Matthew 13:35), of a scribal slip. Read with that scope kept honest, the epistles settle the question the Gospel cruxes only raise: fusing texts is what these writers did.
