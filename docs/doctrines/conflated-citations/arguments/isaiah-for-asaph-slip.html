Did Matthew 13:35 originally name “Isaiah,” “Asaph,” or simply “the prophet”?

Disputed reading · on Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations · witness: the Gospel of Matthew

Related readings: How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read? · Where does Matthew 2:23’s “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” come from?

The passage

All these things spake Jesus unto the multitude in parables; and without a parable spake he not unto them: That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, saying, I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things which have been kept secret from the foundation of the world.

— Matthew 13:34–35 (King James, following the majority text’s “by the prophet”). The quoted words are Psalm 78:2 (“I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old”), a psalm headed “Maschil of Asaph.” The first hand of Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ*), Codex Koridethi (Θ), minuscule families 1 and 13 and codex 33 read “by Isaiah the prophet.”1

The question

The quotation comes from a single psalm, so no second text is blended in, and the manuscripts divide between “by Isaiah the prophet” and “by the prophet.” What is disputed is what Matthew wrote: Isaiah’s name, Asaph’s, or no name. The answer bears on how early the attribution of Psalm 78 to Isaiah entered the Gospel tradition, and on whether Matthew 13:35 belongs with the other composite citations.2

Matthew wrote “Isaiah”

In brief: “by Isaiah the prophet” is the original wording, and later copies dropped the name.

Held by: Porphyry (third century), as reported in Ellicott’s Commentary for English Readers; Constantin von Tischendorf, Novum Testamentum Graece, 8th ed. (1869); F. J. A. Hort, in Westcott and Hort’s Appendix; and Eberhard Nestle, Introduction to the Textual Criticism of the Greek New Testament (1901).3

On this reading the name stood in the earliest text, and the manuscript tradition shows its removal. “By Isaiah the prophet” is read by early and widely spread witnesses: the first hand of Sinaiticus, Koridethi, the families of minuscules 1 and 13, and codex 33, and Jerome had “read in some codices” the name Isaiah. It was current in the third century, when Porphyry, on the report of Ellicott’s Commentary, used it “as a proof of St. Matthew’s ignorance.” Hort stated the transcriptional argument: “It is difficult not to think Ἠσαίου genuine. There was a strong temptation to omit it… and, though its insertion might be accounted for by an impulse to supply the name of the best known prophet, the evidence of the actual operation of such an impulse is much more trifling than might have been anticipated.” Jerome reports that the name was later removed: “Because this was nowhere found in Isaiah, I think it was afterwards removed by prudent men.” Tischendorf printed “Isaiah” in his eighth edition, and Nestle judged it “certainly… original.” On this reading the bare “by the prophet” of the majority text is the later form, and Matthew 13:35 stands with Matthew 27:9 as a second place where the Gospel names a prophet other than the author of the text it quotes.4

Matthew wrote “Asaph,” and a copyist changed it to “Isaiah”

In brief: the original read “Asaph the prophet”; an early copyist replaced the unfamiliar name with Isaiah’s, and later copyists removed it.

Held by: Jerome, Commentary on Matthew (c. 398), and Homilies on the Psalms 11.5

On this reading each stage of the manuscript history has a cause. Jerome had “read in some codices” the words “through Isaiah the prophet, saying,” and found the common edition reading only “through the prophet.” He accounts for both. The psalm Matthew quotes “is inscribed with the title of Asaph the prophet,” so the Evangelist wrote “through Asaph the prophet”; then “the first copyist did not understand Asaph, thought it a copyist’s fault, and corrected it to the name of Isaiah, whose name was better known”; and because the words were nowhere found in Isaiah, “prudent men” afterwards removed the name. On Jerome’s reading Asaph’s title as prophet is scriptural, and he draws the rule: “in the psalms and hymns and songs of God not only David but also the others whose names stand at their head are to be called prophets, namely Asaph and Jeduthun and Heman the Ezrahite and Ethan and the sons of Korah.” Chronicles calls him “Asaph the seer” and says his sons “prophesied with harps.” Jerome made the same point in his homily on the psalm, where he reports that it stood under Asaph’s name in the ancient copies. On this reading Matthew named the psalm’s own author, and the two later readings are a substitution and a correction of it.6

Matthew wrote “the prophet,” and copyists added “Isaiah”

In brief: the majority text’s “by the prophet” is original, meaning Asaph, and the name Isaiah is an early scribal addition.

Held by: the Particular Baptist scholar John Gill, Exposition of the New Testament (1746–48); Ellicott’s Commentary for English Readers; Bruce M. Metzger for the United Bible Societies committee, A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament (2nd ed., 1994); and the text critic James Snapp Jr. (2019).7

On this reading Matthew wrote the bare formula he uses elsewhere, and the prophet he meant is the psalm’s author. Gill reads the verse “Not Isaiah, as some copies in the times of Jerom read, but Asaph, who is called Asaph the seer,” and since Asaph and his sons prophesied with harps, “he is very rightly called a prophet by the evangelist.” Ellicott’s Commentary grants the age of the variant and denies its originality: “Old as it is, however, there is no reason for receiving it as the original reading. The mistake was probably that of a transcriber, misled by the word ‘prophet,’ and writing the name after the precedent of Matthew 8:17; Matthew 12:17.” Snapp generalizes the mechanism: copyists in the Western and Caesarean streams showed a strong tendency to make unnamed prophetic citations specific, so that “some early copyists occasionally added Isaiah’s name where it didn’t belong, and… Mark 1:2 and Matthew 13:35 are two of those places.” Snapp places the copies that name Isaiah here in the Caesarean stream, where he finds that habit at work. Metzger’s committee prints “the prophet,” rating the decision {C}. On this reading Matthew 13:35 is an exact citation of a psalm by its prophet, and the name Isaiah belongs to the history of copying.8
