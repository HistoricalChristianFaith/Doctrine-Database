Is Matthew 13:35’s “Isaiah” a scribal slip for Asaph, not a composite citation?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · primarily a textual-criticism argument · Assessment: sound — this is the honest counter-example in the set. Unlike the sibling cruxes, Matthew 13:35 is not a composite of two prophets headed with the senior name: its quotation comes wholly from a single text, Asaph’s Psalm 78:2. So the “name the governing prophet” convention has nothing to work on here, and the case is best read as a genuine mislabelling — a scribal slip, not authorial design. On the majority text, which reads simply “by the prophet,” there is no error at all: Asaph is himself named a “seer” and one who “prophesied.” On the harder early text, which names “Isaiah,” there is a misattribution — and the best diagnosis, Jerome’s, is that an early copyist who did not recognize “Asaph the prophet” corrected it to the familiar “Isaiah.” Either way the reading that governs the other three cases — deliberate composite citation — is exactly what this case is not. What stays genuinely open is the text: whether “Isaiah” or the bare “the prophet” is original is a decision the United Bible Societies’ committee rates only {C}.

This is one instance of a recurring pattern — the Gospels naming one prophet, or “the prophets,” for a composite or unlocatable Old Testament citation (so too Mark 1:2, Matthew 27:9, and Matthew 2:23). The reception of that whole phenomenon, and the shared principle the fathers used to answer it, is traced on the Naming the Wrong Prophet timeline. This page weighs the one member the fathers and the manuscripts alike treat differently: the case that keeps the doctrine honest, where the answer is not “design” but “slip.”

Related arguments: naming the prophet — see also Why Mark names Isaiah (Mark 1:2–3) · Why Matthew says “the prophets” for the Nazarene saying (Matthew 2:23) · Why Matthew names Jeremiah (Matthew 27:9)

The claim

All these things spake Jesus unto the multitude in parables; and without a parable spake he not unto them: That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, saying, I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things which have been kept secret from the foundation of the world.

— Matthew 13:34–35 (King James, following the majority text’s “by the prophet”). The quoted words are Psalm 78:2 (“I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old”) — and Psalm 78 is headed, in the Hebrew, “Maschil of Asaph,” not a psalm of Isaiah. In a strong line of early manuscripts the verse names the prophet outright: “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet.” That reading is a plain error — Psalm 78 is not Isaiah’s — and, unlike Mark 1:2 or Matthew 27:9, there is no second text blended in to make “name the senior prophet” even a candidate explanation: the whole quotation is Asaph’s single psalm. So this crux poses two questions in turn, and the honest answer is to keep them apart: is “Isaiah” the original reading at all?, and if it is, whose slip is it — Matthew’s or an early scribe’s?1

Who advances it

The reading that this is a mislabelling rather than a designed composite is Jerome’s, and it has become the common judgement. Commenting on the verse, Jerome reports that the Vulgate copies before him actually read “through the prophet Isaiah,” found the name impossible (“because it was not found in Isaiah”), and gave a transmissional diagnosis: the original was “Asaph the prophet” — Psalm 78 “is inscribed with the title of Asaph the prophet” — and “the first scribe did not understand Asaph, and thought it was a mistake of the scribe, and corrected it to the more obvious name of Isaiah.” He drives the point home with a rule the other cases never needed: in the Psalms, hymns, and songs “not only David, but also the others whose names are written, are to be called prophets: namely, Asaph and Idithun, and Heman the Ezrahite, and Ethan, and the sons of Korah” — so a bare “the prophet” for Asaph is not an error needing a harmonization. Among the modern critical editors the harder “Isaiah” has strong defenders as the original text: Tischendorf printed it in his eighth edition, and Nestle judged it “certainly… original,” both on a lectio-difficilior argument. Against them the United Bible Societies’ committee prints the bare “the prophet” and rates the decision only {C} — a candid admission of doubt, and the mark of a case where the answer is genuinely open in a way the sibling cruxes are not.2

Is “Isaiah the prophet” even the original reading?

The manuscripts divide, and the split is unusual: here the “wrong” name sits in some of the earliest witnesses. “By Isaiah the prophet” is read by the first-hand scribe of Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ*), by Codex Koridethi (Θ), and by the minuscule families 1 and 13 and codex 33 — a scattered but old and geographically broad group — and Jerome reports it standing in the Latin copies of his day. The great majority of Greek manuscripts (and so the Textus Receptus and the King James) read simply “by the prophet.” The transcriptional argument for “Isaiah” as original is the same lectio difficilior that decides Mark 1:2: no scribe would create the difficulty of a wrong name, whereas the obvious motive runs the other way — strike the impossible “Isaiah” and leave the safe “the prophet.” That reasoning persuaded Hort (“it is difficult not to think Ἑσαίου genuine”), Tischendorf, and Nestle. But it does not carry the day as it does at Mark 1:2, and the committee’s {C} says why: early copyists had a documented itch to supply a prophet’s name where the text ran generic (the same hands add “Isaiah” at other fulfilment formulae), so “Isaiah” could as easily be an early intrusion as an original that was later purged — and once so recognized, resisted. The evidence is real on both sides, and unlike the Mark case there is no convention (no composite) to tip the internal balance. The one firm result is that the decision is genuinely undetermined — which is itself the point: this is the member of the set where text-criticism does not hand back a clean {A}.3

Why this is a slip, not a composite

The decisive difference from the sibling cases is that there is nothing composite here to head with a name. Mark 1:2 fuses Malachi 3:1 (in an Exodus 23:20 frame) with Isaiah 40:3; Matthew 27:9 wraps Zechariah’s words in Jeremiah’s imagery; Matthew 2:23 gathers a whole theme that sits in no one verse. In each, naming one prophet for a blend or a summary is a coherent, documented convention — the “combined,” “conflated,” and “condensed” citation the ancient authors used deliberately. Matthew 13:35 is not of that kind: the quotation is drawn from a single passage, Psalm 78:2, with no second source folded in. So the machinery that acquits the others cannot even be invoked: if “Isaiah” stood in the original, it names the wrong prophet for a plain single-source quotation — an error, whether Matthew’s own memory-slip or (with Jerome) an early copyist’s substitution for “Asaph.” And on the majority text the difficulty simply evaporates: Matthew wrote “the prophet,” and Asaph is a prophet — Scripture calls him “Asaph the seer” and lists his sons among those who “prophesied with harps” — so a psalm of Asaph is rightly “spoken by the prophet.” The reading that keeps the doctrine honest, then, is not to over-explain 13:35 as one more clever composite, but to say plainly that it is the exception: a mislabelling if “Isaiah” is original, and no mislabelling at all if it is not — and in neither case a designed composite. The fathers who read the other three as design read this one as a copyist’s stumble, and they were right to sort it apart.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That Matthew 13:35 is not a composite citation is not seriously disputable — the words are wholly from Psalm 78:2, a single text, so the “head it with the senior prophet” convention that carries Mark 1:2 and Matthew 27:9 has no purchase. If “Isaiah” is the original reading, it is therefore a genuine mislabelling, not a design; and Jerome’s account of how it arose — a scribe replacing an unfamiliar “Asaph the prophet” with the famous name — is a concrete, transmissionally plausible mechanism, not a harmonizing evasion. If instead the bare “the prophet” is original, there is no error to explain, since Asaph is himself a prophet on the Old Testament’s own terms. On every route, the “deliberate composite” verdict that the sibling arguments earn is the one verdict this case does not: it is the honest counter-example, and reading it as anything else would be over-reaching the pattern.

What it cannot reach. The account does not settle the underlying text, and should not pretend to: the committee’s {C} is right that “Isaiah” vs. “the prophet” is genuinely undetermined, so whether there is a mislabelling here at all stays open. Nor can it decide, if “Isaiah” is original, between Matthew’s own slip and an early scribe’s — Jerome’s Asaph-diagnosis is the best single proposal, but it rests on inference from the psalm-title, not on a manuscript that actually reads “Asaph.” What the argument does establish is the negative that matters for the doctrine: this is not a designed composite, and the cluster’s explanation of the other three cases is not a universal solvent to be poured over this one.5

Assessment

Sound. Matthew 13:35 is best read as the counter-example in the set: not a deliberate composite citation but, at most, a genuine mislabelling — and quite possibly no error at all. The quotation is from a single text (Asaph’s Psalm 78:2), so the “name the governing prophet” convention cannot apply; on the harder early reading “Isaiah” the name is a slip, most plausibly (with Jerome) an early scribe’s substitution for “Asaph,” and on the majority reading “the prophet” there is no misattribution, since Asaph is a prophet in Scripture’s own idiom. The interpretation is robust across both readings of a genuinely {C}-rated text, and it is the honest brake on the doctrine: the fathers were reading the other three cases as design precisely because this one, which is a slip, does not fit that shape.
