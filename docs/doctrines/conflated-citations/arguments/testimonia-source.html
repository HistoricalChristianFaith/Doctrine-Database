Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection?

Disputed reading · on Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations · witness: the Gospels of Mark and Matthew

Related readings: How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read? · Did Paul combine the scriptural texts in Romans 3:10–18, 9:33 and 11:8 himself, or receive them already combined?

The passage

Then was fulfilled that which was spoken by Jeremy the prophet, saying, And they took the thirty pieces of silver, the price of him that was valued, whom they of the children of Israel did value; And gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord appointed me.

— Matthew 27:9–10 (King James Version), the words drawn largely from Zechariah 11:12–13; cited beside it, Mark 1:2–3, which in the critical text heads a Malachi–Isaiah quotation “in Isaiah the prophet.”1

The question

In the critical text Mark 1:2 heads a blend of Malachi and Isaiah with Isaiah’s name, and Matthew 27:9 credits to Jeremiah words drawn largely from Zechariah. What is disputed is whether the Evangelists, and other early Christian writers, found such blends already joined and labelled in a collection of proof-texts (testimonia), or drew them from their own reading of Scripture. The answer bears on where the composite citations, and their headings, came from.

A written collection of testimonies behind the citations

In brief: the Evangelists copied grouped and labelled proof-texts from a written collection, and its errors came with them.

Held by: Edwin Hatch, Essays in Biblical Greek (1889); J. Rendel Harris, Testimonies (1916–20); and Daniel Plooij, Studies in the Testimony Book (1932), as quoted by David Lincicum.2

On this reading the citations carry the marks of a book. Hatch reasoned that Greek-speaking Judaism, with its habit of collecting excerpts and its regard for its scriptures, would have had manuals of extracts, and that “the existence of composite quotations in the New Testament, and in some of the early Fathers suggests the hypothesis that we have in them relics of such manuals.” Harris named the tokens by which such a book could be traced through early Christian writing: peculiar texts, recurrent sequences, editorial comments, controversialist matter, and “a recurrence of erroneous ascriptions of authorship, by which a wrong title is assigned to a passage taken from the Old Testament.” The stone texts are his example. 1 Peter 2:6–8 joins Isaiah 28:16, Psalm 118:22 and Isaiah 8:14; Romans 9:33 joins Isaiah 28:16 and 8:14; Cyprian’s Testimonies 2.16 pairs Isaiah 28:16 with Psalm 118:22. Such shared groupings, he held, point to a common source older than the letters. Mark’s “Isaiah” for a Malachi–Isaiah sequence is, on this reading, a slipped title in such a sequence, and Harris read Matthew’s “Jeremy” the same way:

The suggestion then arises (and it will be a startling one to those to whom the subject is altogether new) that the Gospel of Matthew has been using a Book of Testimonies, in which the history and tragic end of Judas was explained as a fulfilment of ancient prophecy, and that the mistake which has vexed so many righteous souls was not necessarily even an original one in the Gospel, but one which either existed in the Book of Testimonies, or was accidentally made by the Evangelist in using such a book.

Harris concluded that “the Testimony Book is one of the earliest Christian documents, and… the earliest books of the New Testament must be interpreted in the light of such a document.” Plooij agreed: “We find the Testimony Book quoted over and over again in the pages of the New Testament.” On this reading a fixed wording under an unstable name is what citation from an anthology looks like, and Cyprian’s third-century Testimonies to Quirinus is a surviving Christian example of the genre.3

A shared reading of whole scriptural contexts, with collections as its later result

In brief: the New Testament writers drew on whole passages of Scripture read in common, and testimony books came later.

Held by: N. J. Hommes, Het Testimoniaboek (1935), as reported by Lincicum; C. H. Dodd, According to the Scriptures (1952); and Krister Stendahl, The School of St. Matthew (2nd ed., 1968), for Matthew.4

On this reading the agreements among early Christian citations come from a common way of reading Scripture, not from a common book. Dodd, while acknowledging Harris’s “immense and curious learning,” wrote: “I have come to think that his theory outruns the evidence, which is not sufficient to prove so formidable a literary enterprise at so early a date.” He judged the shared variants and merged citations within the New Testament too few to carry it, and held that a book so central should have survived, or been mentioned before the third century. In its place he described a method: the New Testament writers “often quoted a single phrase or sentence not merely for its own sake, but as a pointer to a whole context,” drawing again and again on the same blocks of Scripture, which he grouped as “the Bible of the Early Church.” The anthologies that do survive are, on this account, the deposit of that work: “The composition of ‘testimony-books’ was the result, not the presupposition, of the work of early Christian biblical scholars.” Hommes had concluded of Paul that “with a Testimony Book, as it has been construed by Rendel Harris and Plooy, he has not worked—just like the other NT writers,” and Stendahl rejected a Testimony Book behind Matthew’s fulfilment citations. On this reading a blended citation such as Mark 1:2 is the work of a writer who knew both passages in their settings and joined them himself.5

Looser testimony traditions, oral or in small written collections

In brief: early Christians used groupings of proof-texts that grew up in preaching and in small, changing collections, not one fixed book.

Held by: T. W. Manson, “The Argument from Prophecy” (1945); Robert A. Kraft (1960); Oskar Skarsaune, The Proof from Prophecy (1987) ⚠ unverified; and Martin C. Albl, “And Scripture Cannot Be Broken” (1999).6

On this reading testimony collections were real, but varied and changing. Manson proposed that “we should think of the ‘Testimony Book’, not as something that was turned out in written form in the earliest days of the Church, but rather as a collection of proof-texts assembled in the course of preaching, and forming part of the primitive kerygma.” Kraft found the Isaiah texts of the Epistle of Barnabas too varied for one source, and read Barnabas instead as “one early stage in the adaptation and modification of late Jewish testimony literature by Christian authors.” Skarsaune reconstructs the written testimony-sources that Justin Martyr drew on, and Albl defends a loose collection of testimonia that Paul inherited, defining the core of every such hypothesis as “the claim that early Christians did not use the Jewish scriptures as an undifferentiated whole, but rather selected, shaped, and interpreted certain passages in support of emerging Christian beliefs.” David Lincicum (2008), citing J. A. Fitzmyer (1957), holds that 4QTestimonia (4Q175), published by John Allegro in 1956, has, beside 4Q174 (4QFlorilegium), which he regards as “probably better explained as a fragmentary eschatological midrash,” “a much stronger claim to represent such an excerpta collection.” It is a single sheet copied about 90 BC that strings together Deuteronomy 5:28–29 and 18:18–19, Numbers 24:15–17, Deuteronomy 33:8–11 and Joshua 6:26, and its first block already joins Exodus 20:21, Deuteronomy 5:28–29 and 18:18–19 into one citation. On this reading the Evangelists could receive a composite already formed in the church’s preaching or in a small collection, and pass it on.7
