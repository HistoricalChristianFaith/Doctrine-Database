Bede on the Mark 1:2 composite citation

Dates: c. 672/673–735 (the Commentary on Mark c. 720–730) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bede takes Augustine’s reasoning about Matthew’s “Jeremiah” and transfers it to Mark’s “Isaiah,” giving the Mark crux its fullest and most settled harmonizing form for the Latin West. His answer has two layers: first, that the words drawn from Malachi are found in Isaiah “in the same sense,” so the Evangelist has not erred; and second — borrowed almost verbatim from Augustine — that even if “Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi” in Mark’s mind, he left it uncorrected because the name was “governed by the Holy Spirit, not without reason.”

Claims & evidence

“The evangelist should not be thought to have erred”

Bede states the difficulty precisely — one of Mark’s two testimonies is from Isaiah, the other from Malachi — and answers that the sense of the Malachi line is itself found in Isaiah, so the attribution stands:

Indeed it must be noted that from the prophetic testimonies which Mark has placed, one is only found in Isaiah, while the other is found in Malachi. Yet the evangelist should not be thought to have erred or been deceitful, who says this was written in Isaiah which Isaiah did not write, but rather it should be understood that even if the words placed from Malachi are not found in Isaiah, the sense of them is found in Isaiah, and in several other places, and more clearly in what he himself added here: A voice crying out in the desert: Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths.

— Bede, Commentary on Mark, on 1:2–3. He then draws the two prophets together: Malachi’s messenger “sent before the face of the Lord who would prepare his way” is “the same as what Isaiah said, that a voice crying out should be heard in the desert” — a single agreed prophecy under one name.1

The providential-memory argument, taken from Augustine

Bede then reproduces Augustine’s explanation of the “wrong” name almost word for word — the one difference being that Augustine applied it to Matthew’s “Jeremiah,” and Bede applies it to Mark’s “Isaiah”:

However, it could have happened that in the mind of Mark writing the Gospel, Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi, as happens. But he would without any doubt correct this, at least being admonished by others who could read this while he was still living in the flesh, unless he thought that the name of another prophet occurred to his memory, which was governed by the Holy Spirit, not without reason, because the Lord ordained it to be written thus.

— Bede, Commentary on Mark, on 1:2–3. As in Augustine, the purpose the Lord intends is that “all the holy prophets spoke by one spirit in wonderful agreement.” With Bede, Augustine’s Matthew argument becomes settled Latin doctrine for the Mark passage.2

Notes

  • A transfer, not a fresh argument. Bede’s contribution is to move Augustine’s reasoning from Matthew 27:9 to Mark 1:2, so that the composite citation Augustine himself passed by in silence (at Harmony II.6.18) now has the full harmonizing treatment. Compare Augustine on these citations.
  • He concedes the softer possibility. Bede does not insist the substitution was deliberate; he allows that “Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi… as happens,” an inadvertent but harmless lapse left standing because it was Spirit-guided. This candor is noted in the adversarial assessment of the crux at Why Mark names Isaiah, which observes that pure design cannot be strictly proved against such a substitution.
