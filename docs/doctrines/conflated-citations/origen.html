Origen on Matthew’s “Jeremiah” for Zechariah’s words

Dates: c. 246 (the Commentary on Matthew, written at Caesarea late in life; Origen c. 185–254) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen is the earliest known writer to stop over Matthew 27:9’s ascription of a Zechariah oracle to “Jeremiah the prophet” and say plainly that the words are not in Jeremiah. Commenting on the thirty-pieces passage, he candidly notes that the quotation “is not found to have been prophesied by Jeremiah anywhere in his books,” whether the copies read in the churches or those recited among the Jews, and offers two conjectures — either a scribal slip (“Jeremiah is put for Zechariah”) or a lost “secret writing of Jeremiah.” He is thus the fountainhead of both later escape-routes; he does not, however, reach the “deliberate composite” reading the tradition would later settle on, and he leans toward the weaker of his own two options.

Claims & evidence

The words are not in Jeremiah — two candid conjectures

Working through the account of Judas’s remorse and the potter’s field, Origen reaches the fulfilment formula and admits at once that its words cannot be located in the prophet Matthew names. He then does not force a harmonization but sets out two possibilities — and, honestly, invites correction (“if anyone can know, let him show where it is written”):

But since what the evangelist says after these things (then was fulfilled that which had been spoken by Jeremiah the prophet, saying the things which are written) — this is not found to have been prophesied by Jeremiah anywhere in his books, whether those which are read in the churches or those which are recited among the Jews — but if anyone can know, let him show where it is written. I suspect, however, that either it is an error of the Scripture and Jeremiah is put for Zechariah, or that there is some secret writing of Jeremiah in which it is written.

Origen then quotes the Zechariah oracle in full (“they appointed my wages, thirty pieces of silver… and I took the thirty pieces of silver, and put them into the house of the Lord”), so that the reader can see for himself that the wording is Zechariah’s.1

The defence of the “secret writing” option

Of his two conjectures, Origen presses the second — that Matthew drew on a non-canonical “secret writing of Jeremiah” — by appeal to the apostolic precedent of citing sources outside the public canon:

But if anyone saying this supposes that he stumbles, let him see whether this may be prophesied somewhere in the secret writings of Jeremiah, knowing that the Apostle too brings forward certain writings of the secret books, as he says somewhere: “what eye has not seen nor ear heard”; for in no canonical book is this found set down, except in the secret writings of Elijah the prophet. Likewise what he says: “as Jamnes and Mambres withstood Moses” is not found in the public books, but in the secret book which is entitled the book of Jamnes and Mambres.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew, Commentariorum Series §117 (on Matthew 27:3–10). He treats Paul’s citation of non-canonical material at 1 Corinthians 2:9 (which he assigns to a “secret writing of Elijah”) and at 2 Timothy 3:8 (Jannes and Jambres) as warrant that an Evangelist could likewise quote a lost book of Jeremiah.2

Notes

  • The earliest witness to the crux — and to both its stock answers. Origen (c. 246) predates Eusebius (c. 313), to whom the “copyist error” theory is usually credited, by some two generations, and he already holds both of the answers the later tradition would separate: the scribal-slip route (later pressed by Jerome and vindicated by modern text-criticism for the related Matthew 13:35 case) and the lost-book route (revived by Chrysostom and Theophylact). On the summary timeline he is the missing early link between Irenaeus (c. 180) and Ambrosiaster (c. 380). See Naming the Wrong Prophet.
  • Where he overreaches. Origen puts more weight on the weaker of his two conjectures. Defending a lost “secret writing of Jeremiah” by analogy with Paul’s supposed use of an apocryphal “Elijah” source is a less-probable reading than the alternative he raises and passes over: the words are Zechariah’s, cited by sense. His first conjecture — a bare scribal substitution of “Jeremiah” for “Zechariah” — also treats the harder name as a corruption to be undone rather than as the original, whereas the manuscripts point the other way (“Jeremiah” is the lectio difficilior from which the nameless and “Zechariah” readings descend). Origen does not reach the reading the tradition settles on — that Matthew has fused Zechariah’s words with Jeremiah’s potter-and-field imagery and headed the composite with the name of the governing prophet.
  • The crux itself. The full adversarial weighing of Matthew 27:9 — including why the composite reading is sound and the lost-apocryphon route the weakest fallback — is argued with Judas’s biographical timeline (Why does Matthew credit the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet”?).
