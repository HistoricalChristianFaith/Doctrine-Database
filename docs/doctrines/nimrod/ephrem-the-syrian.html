Ephrem the Syrian on Nimrod

Dates: c. 373 (Syriac deacon, poet, and exegete of Nisibis and Edessa, d. 373; his Commentary on Genesis belongs to the Edessa years, c. 363–373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Josephus and the Latin Fathers read “a mighty hunter before the LORD” as defiance, Ephrem reads it as praise: Nimrod fought “according to the will of the Lord,” driving the scattered nations out to “the regions that had been set apart for them by God,” and the proverb “May you be like Nimrod” was a blessing pronounced over a chief or ruler — a “mighty hunter who was victorious in the battles of the Lord.” His is the earliest extant full statement of the favourable reading of the phrase among the witnesses on this timeline.

Placement on the timeline

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373), deacon of Nisibis and then of Edessa, was a poet and exegete of the Syriac church; his prose Commentary on Genesis, composed during the Edessa years, reads the book in a literal-historical key. His note on Nimrod falls in section VIII, on the Table of Nations of Genesis 10. It is roughly contemporary with the Greek and Latin exegesis of the later fourth century (Chrysostom, Jerome) but reads the phrase the other way. Ephrem read Genesis in the Syriac (Peshitta) tradition, and takes the phrase “before the LORD” in the honourable sense, where Josephus, Jerome, and Bede take it in the hostile one.1

Claims & evidence

The hunter who fought “according to the will of the Lord”

Concerning Nimrod, Moses said, “He was a mighty hunter before the Lord,” because, according to the will of the Lord, it was he who fought with each of these nations and chased them out from there, so that they would go out and settle in the regions that had been set apart for them by God. “Therefore it is said, like Nimrod a mighty hunter before the Lord.” One used to bless a chief or a ruler by saying, “May you be like Nimrod, a mighty hunter who was victorious in the battles of the Lord.”

— Commentary on Genesis 8. In Ephrem’s reading the “hunting” is not the pursuit of beasts, nor the tyrant’s conquest, but a divinely-willed driving of the nations to their allotted homelands — Nimrod as the agent by whom the post-Babel dispersal of Genesis 10:5, 32 is carried out. “Before the Lord” means at and by the Lord’s will; the “battles” are the Lord’s battles; and the proverb that the adversative tradition treats as a byword for infamy, Ephrem treats as a benediction, the thing one wished upon a good ruler. On this reading the “mighty one” (gibbor) of the Table of Nations is a hero, and Ephrem does not connect Nimrod with the Babel rebellion, as Josephus had done.2

Reception and debate

  • The favourable reading of the phrase. Later readers divide over whether “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH) is praise or indictment. Ephrem takes it as praise: the hunter is God’s instrument, his campaigns are “the battles of the Lord,” and the proverb is a blessing. Josephus’s tyrant and Jerome’s “I have never read favourably about a hunter” take the other side of the “before vs. against the LORD” question.
  • Dispersal. Ephrem’s Nimrod settles the nations in the lands “set apart for them by God” — the opposite of the Babel-builders who tried not to be “scattered abroad” (Genesis 11:4). On this reading he is the executor of the dispersal God decreed rather than the ringleader of the Tower; the allotment of the nations to their divinely-fixed boundaries is the same motif Scripture sounds at Deuteronomy 32:8.
  • A Syriac witness. Ephrem read Genesis in Syriac, in the Peshitta tradition, rather than in Greek or Latin, and he takes “before the LORD” as honour.
  • Text and edition. The English is Edward G. Mathews Jr.’s translation, in St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works (Fathers of the Church 91, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr. and Joseph P. Amar; Washington, D.C.: Catholic University of America Press, 1994), Commentary on Genesis section 8, digitised at the Internet Archive.
