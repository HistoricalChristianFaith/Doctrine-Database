Ephrem the Syrian on Nimrod

Dates: c. 373 (Syriac deacon, poet, and exegete of Nisibis and Edessa, d. 373; his Commentary on Genesis belongs to the Edessa years, c. 363–373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem is the great counter-pole to the villain tradition. Where Josephus and the Latin Fathers read “a mighty hunter before the LORD” as defiance, Ephrem reads it flatly as praise: Nimrod fought “according to the will of the Lord,” driving the scattered nations out to “the regions that had been set apart for them by God,” and the proverb “May you be like Nimrod” was a blessing pronounced over a chief or ruler — a “mighty hunter who was victorious in the battles of the Lord.” His is the strongest patristic case for the favourable reading of the crux, and the earliest voice on this timeline to give it in full.

Where it sits

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373), deacon of Nisibis and then of Edessa, is the pre-eminent poet-theologian of the Syriac church; his prose Commentary on Genesis, composed during the Edessa years, reads the book in a sober, literal-historical key. His note on Nimrod falls in section VIII, on the Table of Nations of Genesis 10. Two things make it decisive for this timeline. First, it is early — roughly contemporary with the Latin and Greek exegesis of the later fourth century, but reaching an opposite verdict. Second, it draws on the Syriac (Peshitta) tradition, close kin to the Hebrew, and yet takes the ambiguous phrase “before the LORD” in the honourable sense that the Hebrew equally allows — making Ephrem the head of the pole from which Josephus, Jerome, and Bede stand on the far side.1

Claims & evidence

The hunter who fought “according to the will of the Lord”

Concerning Nimrod, Moses said, “He was a mighty hunter before the Lord,” because, according to the will of the Lord, it was he who fought with each of these nations and chased them out from there, so that they would go out and settle in the regions that had been set apart for them by God. “Therefore it is said, like Nimrod a mighty hunter before the Lord.” One used to bless a chief or a ruler by saying, “May you be like Nimrod, a mighty hunter who was victorious in the battles of the Lord.”

— Commentary on Genesis 8. Every move here pulls against the villain-reading. The “hunting” is not the pursuit of beasts, nor the tyrant’s conquest, but a divinely-willed driving of the nations to their allotted homelands — Nimrod as the agent by whom the post-Babel dispersal of Genesis 10:5, 32 is carried out. “Before the Lord” means at and by the Lord’s will; the “battles” are the Lord’s battles; and the proverb that the adversative tradition treats as a byword for infamy, Ephrem treats as a genuine benediction — the very thing one wished upon a good ruler. On this reading the “mighty one” (gibbor) of the Table of Nations is a hero, and the fusion of Nimrod with the Babel-rebellion that Josephus had already made simply does not arise.2

Notes

  • The favourable pole of the crux. The whole later tradition divides over whether “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH) is praise or indictment. Ephrem takes it as praise without hesitation, and supplies the strongest positive gloss any Father gives: the hunter is God’s instrument, his campaigns are “the battles of the Lord,” and the proverb is a blessing. He is the direct counter-witness to Josephus’s tyrant and to Jerome’s “I have never read favourably about a hunter” — the two poles of the “before vs. against the LORD” reading laid side by side.
  • Dispersal, not defiance. Ephrem’s Nimrod settles the nations in the lands “set apart for them by God” — the opposite of the Babel-builders who tried not to be “scattered abroad” (Genesis 11:4). Far from being the ringleader of the Tower, on this reading he is the executor of the dispersal God decreed; the allotment of the nations to their divinely-fixed boundaries is the same motif Scripture sounds at Deuteronomy 32:8.
  • A Syriac, not a Greek or Latin, witness. Ephrem read Genesis in Syriac, in the Peshitta tradition that stands closest of the versions to the Hebrew. That a reader so near the Semitic text takes “before the LORD” as honour is itself evidence that the favourable sense is native to the phrase and not a mere apologetic softening — the ambiguity is real, and the older, kinder reading has good philological standing.
  • Text and edition. The English is Edward G. Mathews Jr.’s translation, in St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works (Fathers of the Church 91, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr. and Joseph P. Amar; Washington, D.C.: Catholic University of America Press, 1994), Commentary on Genesis section 8, digitised at the Internet Archive.
