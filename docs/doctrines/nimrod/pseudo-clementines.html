The Pseudo-Clementine literature on Nimrod

Dates: c. 3rd–4th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single author: the Greek Clementine Homilies and the Latin Recognitions in their surviving form date to the fourth century, but rest on a lost early-third-century source, sometimes called the Circuits or Periodoi of Peter) · Wikipedia
Summary: This pseudonymous romance, fathered on Clement of Rome, gives the fullest ancient version of a distinct strand of the Nimrod legend — not the Babel-tyrant of Josephus but the founder of magic, star-worship, and idolatry. In the Homilies (IX.4–6) the man is named “Nebrod” — the Septuagint’s spelling of Nimrod — and flatly identified with Zoroaster: a giant who “chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God,” a great magician who forced a star to grant him sovereignty, was struck dead by the fire he called down, and was thereafter buried, given a temple, and worshipped as a god — the origin, in this telling, of Persian fire-worship and of idolatry itself. The Recognitions (IV.27–29) tell the same origin-story but assign the Zoroaster role to Ham’s son Mesraim and keep Nimrod as a separate figure (“a certain king Nimrod… whom the Greeks also called Ninus”). So the explicit Nimrod = Zoroaster equation lives in the Homilies; the Recognitions supply the same “first magician consumed by star-fire” myth but stop short of the identification. Either way, this is a principal ancient root of the later “Nimrod founded astrology and idolatry” tradition.

Where it sits

The “Pseudo-Clementine literature” is a pair of overlapping fourth-century works — the Greek Homilies (twenty books) and the Latin Recognitions (ten books, surviving in Rufinus of Aquileia’s translation of c. AD 406) — both purporting to be the memoirs of Clement of Rome and both elaborating a lost common source (a Jewish-Christian romance of the early third century, conventionally the Grundschrift or Circuits of Peter). Their Nimrod material comes in a set speech, put in the mouth of the Apostle Peter, on how idolatry and false worship first entered the post-Flood world. On this timeline the Pseudo-Clementines are the earliest surviving witness to the tradition that makes Nimrod not the builder of Babel’s tower but the first magus — the fountainhead of the astrological and idolatrous arts — a strand distinct from, and older in its literary form than, the Latin Fathers who would carry it forward.1

Claims & evidence

The Homilies: “Nebrod, whom the Greeks have called Zoroaster”

Of this family there was born in due time a certain one, who took up with magical practices, by name Nebrod, who chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God. Him the Greeks have called Zoroaster. He, after the deluge, being ambitious of sovereignty, and being a great magician, by magical arts compelled the world-guiding star of the wicked one who now rules, to the bestowal of the sovereignty as a gift from him. But he, being a prince, and having authority over him who compelled him, wrathfully poured out the fire of the kingdom, that he might both bring to allegiance, and might punish him who at first constrained him.

— Clementine Homilies IX.4 (“Zoroaster”). The name “Nebrod” is simply the form the Septuagint gives Nimrod (Greek Nebrōd, Genesis 10:8), so the identification is unambiguous: Nimrod is Zoroaster, the founder of the magic art. Two biblical threads are woven in. The verb “chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God” echoes both the “mighty one” (gibbor) of the Table of Nations and the giants of Genesis 6; and “in opposition to God” takes the adversative side of the “before / against the LORD” crux for granted. Just before this, the Homilies place Nebrod in the line of Ham: after Noah’s death his descendants grew “ambitious of the kingdom,” and “one of whom was of the family of Ham, whose descendant was Mestren, from whom the tribes of the Egyptians and Babylonians and Persians were multiplied” — matching Genesis, where Nimrod is son of Cush son of Ham (Genesis 10:6–8).2

Struck dead by the star-fire, then worshipped as a god

Therefore the magician Nebrod, being destroyed by this lightning falling on earth from heaven, for this circumstance had his name changed to Zoroaster, on account of the living (ζῶσαν) stream of the star (ἀστέρος) being poured upon him. But the unintelligent amongst the men who then were, thinking that through the love of God his soul had been sent for by lightning, buried the remains of his body, and honoured his burial-place with a temple among the Persians, where the descent of the fire occurred, and worshipped him as a god.

— Clementine Homilies IX.5 (“Hero-Worship”). The moral engine of the passage is a fabricated etymology: Zoro-aster is derived, wrongly, from Greek zōsan astera, “living star,” the flowing star-fire that killed him — a folk-etymology, not a real derivation of the Iranian name. The theory of idolatry that follows is the point: men saw a man killed by lightning, mistook the death for a divine translation, gave him a tomb-temple, and worshipped him — and “by this example also, others … bury those who die by lightning as beloved of God, and honour them with temples, and erect statues of the dead in their own forms.” Idolatry is thus traced to a single first case, and that first case is Nimrod/Zoroaster.3

The origin of fire-worship and of the pagan kingdoms

The Persians, first taking coals from the lightning which fell from heaven, preserved them by ordinary fuel, and honouring the heavenly fire as a god, were honoured by the fire itself with the first kingdom, as its first worshippers. After them the Babylonians, stealing coals from the fire that was there… they themselves also reigned in order. And the Egyptians, acting in like manner, and calling the fire in their own dialect Phthaë, which is translated Hephaistus or Osiris…

— Clementine Homilies IX.6 (“Fire-Worship”). From the one death at Babylon the author derives the whole apparatus of pagan religion: the Persians preserve the lightning-fire and reign first; the Babylonians and Egyptians copy them; the gods of the nations (Hephaistus, Osiris) are the same deified fire under other names. This is a Jewish-Christian “euhemerist” account of idolatry — the gods are dead men mistaken for gods — with Nimrod planted at its root as the first of the deified dead.4

The Recognitions: the same myth, but Zoroaster is Ham’s son, and Nimrod is Ninus

One of these, by name Ham, unhappily discovered the magical act, and handed down the instruction of it to one of his sons, who was called Mesraim, from whom the race of the Egyptians and Babylonians and Persians are descended. Him the nations who then existed called Zoroaster, admiring him as the first author of the magic art… until he was set on fire, and consumed by the demon himself…

[And in the next generation:] First among whom is named a certain king Nimrod, the magic art having been handed down to him as by a flash, whom the Greeks also called Ninus, and from whom the city of Nineveh took its name.

— Recognitions of Clement IV.27, 29. The parallel version keeps every structural element of the myth — the first magician who draws sparks from the stars, is consumed by his own demon-fire, is buried, and is worshipped as “a friend of God… removed to heaven in a chariot of lightning” (IV.28) — but assigns the Zoroaster role to Mesraim (the Mizraim of Genesis, eponym of Egypt), Ham’s son, and places him in the fourteenth generation, dead in the fifteenth “in which the tower was built.” Nimrod then appears one step down the chain, in chapter 29, as the first of the Persian kings who inherit the magic art — and is glossed not as Zoroaster but as Ninus, the legendary founder of Nineveh. So within the one Pseudo-Clementine tradition the Nimrod = Zoroaster equation is made explicitly by the Homilies and left unmade by the Recognitions: a fault-line worth marking, because later writers who cite “the Clementines” for “Nimrod the first astrologer” are drawing specifically on the Homilies’ recension.5

Notes

  • Which recension makes the equation? The lead question for this witness — verified above — is that the explicit “Nimrod = Zoroaster” identity is the Homilies’ (IX.4, “by name Nebrod… Him the Greeks have called Zoroaster”). The Recognitions (IV.27–29) tell the same first-magician myth but identify Zoroaster with Mesraim/Mizraim and make Nimrod a separate, later figure equated with Ninus. Both, however, root the origin of magic and idolatry in the family of Ham, and both stand behind the medieval “Nimrod invented astrology” commonplace.
  • “Nebrod” = Nimrod. The Greek Nebrōd (Νεβρώδ) is the Septuagint’s standard rendering of the Hebrew Nimrōd at Genesis 10:8, so a reader of the Greek Bible would recognise the identification at once; it is not a different person.
  • A folk-etymology, not linguistics. The Homilies’ derivation of “Zoroaster” from zōsan astera (“living star”) is a Greek pun with no basis in the Iranian name Zaraϑuštra; it is a storyteller’s etymology built to carry the star-fire moral, and should be read as such — part of the legend, not evidence for it.
  • Nimrod = Ninus. The Recognitions’ gloss “whom the Greeks also called Ninus” is an independent equation with its own long afterlife — Ninus being the mythical founder-king of Nineveh in Greek historiography (Ctesias, Diodorus). It later becomes load-bearing in Alexander Hislop’s The Two Babylons (Nimrod = Ninus = Tammuz), assessed on this timeline’s Hislop argument page; the Pseudo-Clementine Recognitions is one of its ancient footholds.
  • Text and editions. The English quoted here is the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation (T. Smith, P. Peterson, and J. Donaldson; vol. 8, 1886, public domain): the Clementine Homilies IX.4–6 and the Recognitions of Clement IV.27–29, as digitised at New Advent (linked in the sources below). The two works survive independently — the Homilies in Greek, the Recognitions only in Rufinus’s Latin — and both descend from a lost common source; the datings above follow the standard account of the corpus.
  • The idolatry-from-deified-men theory here belongs to the wider ancient “euhemerist” polemic the Fathers used against the pagan gods; on Nimrod specifically as the alleged founder of star-worship, cf. this wiki’s astrology timeline, where the origin of the astral arts is traced (with Tertullian) to the rebel angels of Genesis 6 and the Watcher-lore of 1 Enoch — the same primeval matrix into which the Pseudo-Clementines set their first magician.
