The Aramaic Targums on Nimrod

Dates: c. 2nd–8th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Aramaic synagogue paraphrases of the Torah, whose written redaction spans several centuries — Onkelos the earliest, Pseudo-Jonathan the latest — while the traditions they carry are older still) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Torah Targums — the Aramaic renderings read alongside the Hebrew in the synagogue — split cleanly on the crux Scripture leaves open. The restrained Babylonian Targum, Onkelos, keeps the neutral proverb: Nimrod is simply “mighty” and a “mighty hunter before the Lord,” with no note of rebellion. Pseudo-Jonathan, the latest and most expansive, does the opposite: it reads “before the LORD” adversatively and writes the villain into the translation itself — Nimrod “began to be mighty in sin, and to rebel before the Lord,” a “mighty rebel” the like of whom the world has not seen since it was made. This is the earliest place the Hebrew hint of defiance becomes the plain sense of the verse, textualizing the pun on marad (“he rebelled”) that the rabbis would make explicit. Yet even Pseudo-Jonathan is not of one mind: at Genesis 10:11 it has Nimrod leave Babel and reign in Assyria “because he would not be in the counsel of a divided generation” — a stray positive note surviving inside the arch-rebel portrait. So the two Targum families preserve, side by side, the two Nimrods the whole tradition divides into: Onkelos’s neutral strong man and Pseudo-Jonathan’s first rebel against heaven.

Where it sits

A Targum (Aramaic for “translation”) is the running Aramaic rendering of Scripture that grew up in the synagogue once Aramaic had displaced Hebrew as the everyday language of Jewish Palestine and Babylonia; read aloud after the Hebrew, it was never a bare translation but a paraphrase that folded interpretation, expansion, and homily into the text. Two Targums to the Torah matter most for Nimrod. Onkelos is the authoritative Babylonian Targum — the most literal and restrained, its core reaching back to the second century and its final form fixed in Babylonia by roughly the fifth. Pseudo-Jonathan (Targum Yerushalmi I) is the latest and by far the most elaborate, belonging to the Palestinian family; its final redaction is dated to the seventh or eighth century (it betrays a post-Islamic horizon elsewhere), though it preserves much older haggadah. Because a Targum’s wording may be centuries younger than the tradition it records, this page is best read not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum — the deposit of what the synagogue said about Nimrod across the whole span — slotted on the timeline after Josephus, whose villain-portrait it shares and sharpens, and read together with the fuller rabbinic legend on which it draws.1

Claims & evidence

Onkelos keeps the neutral proverb (Genesis 10:8–9)

The Hebrew of the Table of Nations is capable of either sense: “he began to be a mighty one (gibbor) in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Genesis 10:8–9). Onkelos, true to its habit of literal restraint, renders the phrase with no editorial hostility at all: Nimrod “began to be mighty (powerful) on the earth,” and “was a mighty hunter before the Lord; therefore it is said, Like Nimrod, a mighty hunter before the Lord.” The Babylonian Targum simply carries the proverb across, “before the Lord” (Aramaic qŏdām YY) and all, without deciding whether the might is blessed or damned. On the interpretive crux that divides the tradition, Onkelos stands with the earliest and the Syriac readers at the favourable pole — or at least the neutral one: a strong man and a great hunter, no more.2

Pseudo-Jonathan writes the rebel into the verse (Genesis 10:8–9)

Pseudo-Jonathan does the reverse of Onkelos: where Onkelos leaves the ambiguity standing, Pseudo-Jonathan resolves it against Nimrod inside the translation itself. Its Genesis 10:8 reads:

And Kush begat Nimrod: he began to be mighty in sin, and to rebel before the Lord in the earth.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 10:8 (Etheridge). The neutral “mighty one in the earth” has become “mighty in sin, and to rebel before the Lord.” The next verse presses the point and turns the proverb inside out:

He was a mighty rebel before the Lord; therefore it is said, From the day that the world was created there hath not been as Nimrod, mighty in hunting, and a rebel before the Lord.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 10:9 (Etheridge). The Hebrew byword “none like Nimrod, a mighty hunter before the LORD” — which in Onkelos and the plain text is praise, or at worst neutral — is here recast as a superlative of wickedness: no rebel like him since the world began. This is the earliest surviving witness to read “before the LORD” flatly as “against the LORD” in the wording of the verse itself, and it textualizes the midrashic pun that ties the very name Nimrod to Hebrew marad, “he rebelled” — the etymology the rabbis spell out at b. Eruvin 53a (“he caused the entire world to rebel against God”). Pseudo-Jonathan thus stands squarely at the adversative pole of the crux, alongside Josephus, Bede, and Jerome, and against the neutral Onkelos and the positive Ephrem.3

The stray positive note: Nimrod refuses “the divided generation” (Genesis 10:11)

For all its hostility, Pseudo-Jonathan preserves one older tradition that cuts the other way. Where the Hebrew of Genesis 10:11 is famously ambiguous — “out of that land went forth Asshur” can mean either that Asshur the man departed or that Nimrod went out into Assyria — the Targum makes the subject Nimrod and, remarkably, gives him a creditable motive:

From that land went forth Nimrod, and reigned in Athur, because he would not be in the counsel of a divided generation. And he left those four cities; and the Lord thereupon gave him a place; and he builded four other cities, Nineveh and Pelatiath, Kartha and Parioth.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 10:11 (Etheridge). The “divided generation” is the generation of the dispersion — the Babel-builders whose tongues God confused — and here Nimrod is said to have withdrawn from their “counsel,” for which “the Lord thereupon gave him a place” to found Nineveh. This is the same old reading the rabbis attached to the departing Asshur (who “went out from the counsel of the generation of division” and was rewarded), here transferred to Nimrod — and it sits awkwardly beside the arch-rebel of the two preceding verses. It is worth flagging precisely because it survives inside a hostile text: even the Targum that brands Nimrod the first rebel could not fully erase an inherited strand in which he keeps clear of Babel’s folly. The tension is the tradition in miniature — two Nimrods, never quite reconciled.4

Notes

  • Two families, two Nimrods. Onkelos (Babylonian) and Pseudo-Jonathan (Palestinian) stand at opposite poles of the “before vs. against the LORD” crux: the one keeps the neutral hunter of the proverb, the other writes “mighty in sin… a mighty rebel” into the verse. That the same Hebrew phrase yields both readings within the synagogue’s own translations is the clearest single illustration of how a lone ambiguous preposition (lifnê YHWH) generated the two opposed Nimrods of the later tradition.
  • The marad pun made grammatical. The rabbinic etymology deriving “Nimrod” from marad, “he rebelled,” is a wordplay, not a philology (the name is more probably Mesopotamian); but Pseudo-Jonathan is where that pun stops being a homiletical aside and becomes the actual rendering of the text — “to rebel before the Lord.” The fuller etymology and the Amraphel identification are set out on the rabbinic-tradition page.
  • Neofiti and the Fragment Targums. The other Palestinian witnesses (Codex Neofiti and the Fragment Targums) belong to Pseudo-Jonathan’s family and its expansive habit, but the two poles are already fully drawn by the restrained Onkelos and the elaborate Pseudo-Jonathan quoted here, which is why those two carry this page.
  • Text and editions. Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan are quoted here in J. W. Etheridge’s translation (The Targums of Onkelos and Jonathan Ben Uzziel on the Pentateuch, London, 1862–65), as reproduced at Sefaria; the bracketed clarifications in some printed Onkelos editions (“mighty and powerful”) are the modern translators’, not the Aramaic.
