The medieval reception of Nimrod

Dates: c. 7th–14th century (the Latin Middle Ages: Isidore of Seville, d. 636, through Peter Comestor, d. 1178, to Dante’s Inferno, c. 1308–1320) · Isidore · Dante
Summary: The Latin Middle Ages received the patristic Nimrod — the “against the Lord” giant-tyrant, founder of Babylon and its tower — and fixed him as a settled type: the proud giant-king and the author of Babel’s presumption. Isidore of Seville hands the figure on in the age’s great encyclopedia (Nimrod = “tyrant,” the giant who founded Babylon and raised the tower “against God”); the schools and chronicles (Peter Comestor’s Historia Scholastica, and the Syriac Book of the Bee) carry the giant-tyrant-astrologer, adding the legend that Nimrod compelled fire-worship and was schooled in the stars. The strand climaxes as poetry in Dante, who lodges Nimrod among the giants ringing the pit of hell, his mind babbling the unintelligible line “Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi” — punished by the very confusion of tongues his tower provoked. This block gathers the Latin-medieval reception as one reception-tail, earliest conduit first, ending in the poem that fixed the Babel-Nimrod identification in the Western imagination.

Where it sits

By the seventh century the two great strands of the Nimrod tradition were long set: the Babel-tyrant portrait, from Josephus through Augustine and Jerome, of the giant who “first seized an unprecedented tyranny” and raised the tower against heaven; and the founder-of-magic portrait of the Pseudo-Clementine literature, where Nimrod is the first astrologer and idolater. The Latin Middle Ages did not so much reinterpret Nimrod as consolidate him — digesting the Fathers into encyclopedias, school-texts, and chronicles until the figure hardened into a fixed emblem, and then handing that emblem to the vernacular poets. This page follows the Latin conduit at its two most consequential points, the encyclopedia that fed the later Middle Ages and the poem that fixed the image for good: Isidore of Seville, whose Etymologies passed the patristic Nimrod to every medieval library, and Dante, who set him in hell.1

Claims & evidence

Isidore of Seville — the encyclopedic conduit (the Etymologies, c. 615–630)

Isidore, bishop of Seville (c. 560–636), compiled the Etymologies (Etymologiae, or Origines) — the encyclopedia that was the standard reference of the Latin West for the next thousand years — on the principle that the origin of a word unlocks the nature of the thing. His Nimrod is the patristic one, compressed to a definition. Treating the names of the patriarchs and peoples, he glosses:

Nembroth interpretatur tyrannus. Iste enim prior arripuit insuetam in populo tyrannidem, et ipse adgressus est adversus Deum impietatis aedificare turrem.

— Etymologies 7.6.22: “Nembroth is interpreted ‘tyrant.’ For he first seized an unaccustomed tyranny among the people, and he himself undertook, in impiety, to build a tower against God.” The gloss is almost verbatim Jerome (“first seized an unprecedented tyranny among the people”) welded to Augustine’s reading of the tower as raised adversus Deum, “against God” — the two Latin authorities fused into one lapidary entry. In the book on cities Isidore supplies the other half, the founding of Babylon:

Primus post diluvium Nembroth gigans Babylonem urbem Mesopotamiae fundavit.

— Etymologies 15.1.4: “First after the flood, Nembroth the giant founded the city of Babylon in Mesopotamia” (which, Isidore adds, Queen Semiramis of Assyria later enlarged, and whose name “Babel” comes from the confusion of tongues at the tower). So the encyclopedia carries every element of the settled portrait — the name meaning “tyrant,” the giant stature (gigans), the first tyranny, the founding of Babylon, and the tower raised “against God” — in two dictionary sentences that the whole later Middle Ages would consult as fact.2

The schools and the chronicles — the giant-tyrant-astrologer (Comestor and the Book of the Bee, 12th–13th c.)

Through the high-medieval school-texts the compressed Isidorean Nimrod expanded again, now gathering to itself the astrological strand the Pseudo-Clementines had carried. Peter Comestor’s Historia Scholastica (c. 1173) — the “Medieval Popular Bible,” a core text of the Paris, Oxford, and Bologna curricula — retold sacred history with the extra-biblical legends folded in: its Nimrod is the tyrant who compelled his people to worship fire, and who as a young man was schooled in astronomy by “Jonitus,” a legendary fourth son of Noah. The Syriac Book of the Bee (Solomon of Akhlat, 13th c.) preserves the cognate Eastern legends binding Nimrod to the stars and to the origins of fire-worship. Neither is Scripture; both are the medieval reception building the encyclopedic Nimrod out into the giant-tyrant-astrologer who founded not only Babel but idolatry and the reading of the heavens — a charge the wiki weighs on its own account alongside the origin of the astral arts.3

Dante — Nimrod among the giants, babbling in the pit of hell (Inferno 31, c. 1308–1320)

The strand climaxes as poetry. In Inferno 31 Dante and Virgil reach the well that sinks to the frozen bottom of hell, ringed by the giants who warred on heaven; the first of them, towering so that his face is “as long and large / As is at Rome the pine-cone of Saint Peter’s,” suddenly bellows a line of pure gibberish:

“Raphel mai amech izabi almi,”
Began to clamor the ferocious mouth,
To which were not befitting sweeter psalms.
… “Soul idiotic,
Keep to thy horn, and vent thyself with that,”

— Inferno 31.67–71 (Longfellow), rendering Dante’s Italian «Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi». Virgil then names him, and turns the punishment into its own poetic justice — the man whose tower shattered the one human language is himself locked out of language forever:

“He doth himself accuse;
This one is Nimrod, by whose evil thought
One language in the world is not still used.
… For even such to him is every language
As his to others, which to none is known.”

— Inferno 31.77–81 (Longfellow). Dante takes the whole medieval inheritance as given: Nimrod is a literal giant (Isidore’s gigans), the author of Babel “by whose evil thought” the world lost its one tongue (Josephus’s and Augustine’s tower-builder), and a figure of pride punished in the lowest circle. The horn slung across his chest is the hunter’s horn of “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Genesis 10:9), and his gibberish is the confusion of tongues turned inward — the contrapasso that makes his sin his sentence. More than any school-text, it was Dante’s three tercets that lodged the Babel-Nimrod identification permanently in the Western imagination.4

Notes

  • Consolidation, not reinterpretation. The medieval contribution to the Nimrod tradition is not a new reading of Genesis 10 but the fixing of the patristic one. Isidore turns the Fathers’ readings into dictionary definitions; the schools turn the definitions into narrated history with the legends attached; Dante turns the history into unforgettable poetry. By the fourteenth century “Nimrod” simply means the proud giant-king of Babel — the “before the LORD” ambiguity that Ephrem and Bede had still felt is gone, closed toward “against the LORD” without remainder.
  • Isidore quotes Jerome and Augustine. The two Etymologies sentences are a fair specimen of Isidore’s method: 7.6.22 lifts Jerome’s “first seized an unprecedented tyranny” (Hebrew Questions on Genesis) and fuses it with the adversus Deum reading of the tower Augustine had fixed in the City of God. The medieval Nimrod is, quite literally, the patristic Nimrod copied out.
  • The astrological legend is extra-biblical. The fire-worship and the tutelage in astronomy by “Jonitus” are nowhere in Scripture; Comestor drew them from the wider (ultimately Syriac and pseudo-Clementine) legend-pool. They belong to the reception, and the underlying claim that Nimrod founded astrology is weighed on its own alongside the origin of the astral arts — presented here as tradition, not established history.
  • Dante’s gibberish. The line «Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi» (Inferno 31.67) is deliberate nonsense — its spelling varies across the manuscripts, fittingly, since it is meant to be untranslatable. Early commentators already agreed it could not be construed; it is the confusion of tongues made audible, spoken by the man the tradition blamed for it.
  • Nimrod the giant. The medieval sources take the biblical “mighty one” (gibbor, Greek gigas) as literal bodily giantism — Isidore’s gigans, Augustine’s giant “of body,” Dante’s pine-cone-faced colossus — the same word used of the “mighty men… of old” before the Flood.
  • Texts and editions. Isidore, Etymologiae 7.6.22 and 15.1.4, Latin at The Latin Library (Book 7) and Book 15 (English translations rendered here; the standard English is Barney, Lewis, Beach & Berghof, The Etymologies of Isidore of Seville, Cambridge, 2006). Peter Comestor, Historia Scholastica: see the British Library’s Egerton Genesis Picture Book (whose Nimrod cycle is captioned from Comestor). Dante, Inferno 31, in Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s 1867 translation, at Wikisource; text and commentary also at Digital Dante (Columbia).
