The medieval reception of Nimrod

Dates: c. 7th–14th century (the Latin Middle Ages: Isidore of Seville, d. 636, through Peter Comestor, d. 1178, to Dante’s Inferno, c. 1308–1320) · Isidore · Dante
Summary: The Latin Middle Ages received the patristic Nimrod — the “against the Lord” giant-tyrant, founder of Babylon and its tower — and repeated him as a type: the proud giant-king and the author of Babel’s presumption. Isidore of Seville hands the figure on in his encyclopedia (Nimrod = “tyrant,” the giant who founded Babylon and raised the tower “against God”); the schools and chronicles (Peter Comestor’s Historia Scholastica, and the Syriac Book of the Bee) carry the giant-tyrant-astrologer, adding the legend that Nimrod compelled fire-worship and was schooled in the stars. Dante, in verse, lodges Nimrod among the giants ringing the pit of hell, his mouth babbling the unintelligible line “Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi” — punished by the confusion of tongues his tower provoked. This page gathers the medieval reception as one block, earliest source first, ending with Dante.

Placement on the timeline

By the seventh century two strands of the Nimrod tradition were in circulation: the Babel-tyrant portrait, from Josephus through Augustine and Jerome, of the giant who “first seized an unprecedented tyranny” and raised the tower against heaven; and the founder-of-magic portrait of the Pseudo-Clementine literature, where Nimrod is the first astrologer and idolater. The medieval writers on this page carry the Fathers’ Nimrod into encyclopedias, school-texts, chronicles, and vernacular poetry. The page follows the Latin sources from Isidore of Seville, whose Etymologies gives the patristic Nimrod in two entries, to Dante, who set him in hell, with the Syriac Book of the Bee alongside.1

Claims & evidence

Isidore of Seville — the encyclopedia (the Etymologies, c. 615–630)

Isidore, bishop of Seville (c. 560–636), compiled the Etymologies (Etymologiae, or Origines), a reference work of the Latin West, on the principle that the origin of a word unlocks the nature of the thing. His Nimrod is the patristic one, compressed to a definition. Treating the names of the patriarchs and peoples, he glosses:

Nembroth interpretatur tyrannus. Iste enim prior arripuit insuetam in populo tyrannidem, et ipse adgressus est adversus Deum impietatis aedificare turrem.

— Etymologies 7.6.22: “Nembroth is interpreted ‘tyrant.’ For he first seized an unaccustomed tyranny among the people, and he himself undertook, in impiety, to build a tower against God.” The gloss is almost verbatim Jerome (“first seized an unprecedented tyranny among the people”) joined to Augustine’s reading of the tower as raised adversus Deum, “against God,” in one entry. In the book on cities Isidore supplies the other half, the founding of Babylon:

Primus post diluvium Nembroth gigans Babylonem urbem Mesopotamiae fundavit.

— Etymologies 15.1.4: “First after the flood, Nembroth the giant founded the city of Babylon in Mesopotamia” (which, Isidore adds, Queen Semiramis of Assyria later enlarged, and whose name “Babel” comes from the confusion of tongues at the tower). So the encyclopedia carries each element of the patristic portrait — the name meaning “tyrant,” the giant stature (gigans), the first tyranny, the founding of Babylon, and the tower raised “against God” — in two dictionary sentences.2

The schools and the chronicles — the giant-tyrant-astrologer (Comestor and the Book of the Bee, 12th–13th c.)

In the high-medieval school-texts Nimrod is joined to the astrological strand earlier found in the Pseudo-Clementines. Peter Comestor’s Historia Scholastica (c. 1173) — the “Medieval Popular Bible,” a core text of the Paris, Oxford, and Bologna curricula — retold sacred history with the extra-biblical legends folded in: its Nimrod is the tyrant who compelled his people to worship fire, and who as a young man was schooled in astronomy by “Jonitus,” a legendary fourth son of Noah. The Syriac Book of the Bee (Solomon of Akhlat, 13th c.) preserves the cognate Eastern legends binding Nimrod to the stars and to the origins of fire-worship. Both are extra-biblical legends that make Nimrod a giant-tyrant-astrologer, founder not only of Babel but of idolatry and the reading of the heavens; see the page on Nimrod as founder of astrology and the astrology timeline.3

Dante — Nimrod among the giants, babbling in the pit of hell (Inferno 31, c. 1308–1320)

In Inferno 31 Dante and Virgil reach the well that sinks to the frozen bottom of hell, ringed by the giants who warred on heaven; the first of them, towering so that his face is “as long and large / As is at Rome the pine-cone of Saint Peter’s,” suddenly bellows a line of gibberish:

“Raphel mai amech izabi almi,”
Began to clamor the ferocious mouth,
To which were not befitting sweeter psalms.
… “Soul idiotic,
Keep to thy horn, and vent thyself with that,”

— Inferno 31.67–71 (Longfellow), rendering Dante’s Italian «Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi». Virgil then names him and states the punishment: the man held responsible for the loss of the one human language is himself locked out of language:

“He doth himself accuse;
This one is Nimrod, by whose evil thought
One language in the world is not still used.
… For even such to him is every language
As his to others, which to none is known.”

— Inferno 31.77–81 (Longfellow). In Dante, Nimrod is a literal giant (Isidore’s gigans), the author of Babel “by whose evil thought” the world lost its one tongue (Josephus’s and Augustine’s tower-builder), and a figure of pride punished in the lowest circle. The horn slung across his chest is the hunter’s horn of “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Genesis 10:9), and his gibberish is the confusion of tongues turned inward — the contrapasso that makes his sin his sentence.4

Reception and debate

  • Medieval forms. Isidore gives the Fathers’ readings as dictionary definitions; the schools give narrated history with the legends attached; Dante gives poetry. None of the medieval sources on this page takes up the favourable reading of “before the LORD” that Ephrem gave, or the ambiguity Bede named; each reads Nimrod as the proud giant-king of Babel.
  • Isidore quotes Jerome and Augustine. Etymologies 7.6.22 takes Jerome’s “first seized an unprecedented tyranny” (Hebrew Questions on Genesis) and joins it to the adversus Deum reading of the tower given in Augustine’s City of God.
  • The astrological legend is extra-biblical. The fire-worship and the tutelage in astronomy by “Jonitus” are nowhere in Scripture; Comestor drew them from the wider (ultimately Syriac and pseudo-Clementine) legend-pool. How the claim that Nimrod founded astrology has been read is set out on the founder-of-astrology page; see also the astrology timeline.
  • Dante’s gibberish. The line «Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi» (Inferno 31.67) is presented as nonsense; its spelling varies across the manuscripts. Virgil’s words that Nimrod’s language is “to none… known” make it the confusion of tongues made audible, spoken by the man the tradition blamed for it.
  • Nimrod the giant. The medieval sources take the biblical “mighty one” (gibbor, Greek gigas) as literal bodily giantism — Isidore’s gigans, Augustine’s giant “of body,” Dante’s pine-cone-faced colossus — the same word used of the “mighty men… of old” before the Flood.
