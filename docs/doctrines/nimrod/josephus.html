Josephus on Nimrod

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (Jewish priest, soldier, and historian; the Antiquities of the Jews completed c. AD 93–94) · Wikipedia
Summary: Josephus is the first extra-biblical witness to give Nimrod a full character, and the portrait he draws is the seed of the whole villain tradition. In Antiquities of the Jews I.4 (§§113–114) Nimrod is “a bold man, and of great strength of hand” who turned the post-Flood generations away from the fear of God, “gradually changed the government into tyranny,” and — to be revenged on God for the Flood and put himself beyond His reach — incited them to raise the Tower of Babel “in contempt of God.” The confusion of tongues follows as God’s answer to that defiance. Two moves here are decisive for everything after: Josephus is where the Bible’s ambiguous “mighty hunter before the LORD” becomes flatly a rebel against the LORD, and where Nimrod — whom Genesis 11 never names at the Tower — is first fused with its builder.

Where it sits

Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100), a priest of Jerusalem and general in the Jewish revolt who became a client of the Flavian emperors at Rome, completed his twenty-book Antiquities of the Jews about AD 93–94 — a paraphrase of Israel’s history from creation to his own day, written for a Graeco-Roman readership. His Nimrod falls in Book I, chapter 4 (Whiston’s sections; §§113–114 in the Niese/Loeb numbering), in the chapter titled “Concerning the Tower of Babylon, and the Confusion of Tongues.” It comes immediately after the dispersal-notice of the nations, so that on this timeline Josephus is the earliest interpretive voice after the biblical portrait itself — the source that turns the genealogical “mighty one” of the Table of Nations into a named and motivated tyrant.1

Claims & evidence

Nimrod the tyrant who turned men from the fear of God

Now it was Nimrod who excited them to such an affront and contempt of God. He was the grandson of Ham, the son of Noah, a bold man, and of great strength of hand. He persuaded them not to ascribe it to God, as if it was through his means they were happy, but to believe that it was their own courage which procured that happiness. He also gradually changed the government into tyranny, seeing no other way of turning men from the fear of God, but to bring them into a constant dependence upon his power.

— Antiquities I.4.2 (§113, William Whiston). Genesis gives Nimrod only might and a kingdom; Josephus supplies the motive and the method. The “bold man… of great strength of hand” renders the biblical “mighty one” (gibbor), but Josephus reads that strength as an instrument of impiety: Nimrod teaches the survivors of the Flood to credit their own courage rather than God, and converts their gratitude into dependence on himself. This is the first appearance of the reading that would dominate the tradition — Nimrod not as a hunter blessed “before the LORD” but as the archetype of the God-defying despot, the first man to turn a people against heaven.2

The Tower raised “to be revenged on God”

He also said he would be revenged on God, if he should have a mind to drown the world again; for that he would build a tower too high for the waters to be able to reach! and that he would avenge himself on God for destroying their forefathers!

Now the multitude were very ready to follow the determination of Nimrod, and to esteem it a piece of cowardice to submit to God; and they built a tower, neither sparing any pains, nor being in any degree negligent about the work…

— Antiquities I.4.2–3 (§§113–114, Whiston). Here Josephus makes the move the later tradition never lets go of: he identifies Nimrod as the instigator of the Tower of Babel and gives the building a defiant motive found nowhere in Scripture — not the vague “let us make us a name, lest we be scattered” of Genesis 11:4, but open war on God: a structure engineered to rise above any second Flood, so that heaven could never again reach mankind, and built expressly to avenge the generation the Flood had drowned. The people follow because to submit to God now looks like cowardice. Josephus corroborates the story from the pagan record too, quoting a line he ascribes to the Sibyl — “When all men were of one language, some of them built a high tower, as if they would thereby ascend up to heaven; but the gods sent storms of wind and overthrew the tower” — and a notice of the building from Hestiaeus.3

The confusion of tongues at Babylon

When God saw that they acted so madly, he did not resolve to destroy them utterly, since they were not grown wiser by the destruction of the former sinners; but he caused a tumult among them, by producing in them divers languages, and causing that, through the multitude of those languages, they should not be able to understand one another. The place wherein they built the tower is now called Babylon, because of the confusion of that language which they readily understood before; for the Hebrews mean by the word Babel, confusion.

— Antiquities I.4.3 (§§116–117, Whiston). The confusion of tongues is God’s measured answer to Nimrod’s defiance — not a second annihilation (God has bound Himself against that), but a scattering that breaks the rebellion by breaking its common speech. Josephus takes the Genesis etymology (Babel = “confusion,” Genesis 11:9) as read. What he has added is the causal spine the biblical text leaves loose: a named ringleader, a rebel’s motive, and a single arc running Nimrod → Tower → scattering — the shape every later retelling inherits.4

Notes

  • The fusion Genesis does not make. Scripture keeps Nimrod (Genesis 10:8–12) and the Tower (Genesis 11:1–9) in two separate pericopes: the Table of Nations makes Babel merely “the beginning of his kingdom,” and the Tower narrative names no builder at all. Reading Nimrod as the architect of the Tower is Josephus’s (and the wider Jewish tradition’s) inference, not the plain text — a link the Targums and the rabbis would harden into the standard story and the Fathers would take up. It is worth marking as an interpretive move, however early and influential: the “Nimrod built Babel’s tower” that most readers assume is biblical is first of all Josephan.
  • “Before” becomes “against.” Genesis calls Nimrod “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (lifnê YHWH) — a phrase the earliest readers took as praise and the Syriac tradition of Ephrem the Syrian read as blessing. Josephus never quotes the hunter-phrase, but his whole portrait presupposes the hostile reading of it: his Nimrod is the man who raised his strength “in contempt of God.” He thus stands at the head of the adversative pole of the “before vs. against the LORD” crux that divides the whole tradition, on the far side from Ephrem’s hero.
  • “Grandson of Ham.” Whiston’s “grandson of Ham, the son of Noah” is not a discrepancy with Genesis but agrees with it: Nimrod is son of Cush, and Cush is son of Ham (Genesis 10:6–8), so Nimrod is Ham’s grandson and Noah’s great-grandson — the third generation after the Flood, which fits Josephus’s placing him among those who defied the memory of it.
  • Text and edition. The English quoted here is William Whiston’s translation (1737), The Antiquities of the Jews I.4, as digitised at the University of Chicago’s Penelope archive; Whiston’s chapter-and-section divisions (I.4.1–3) run alongside the Niese/Loeb continuous numbering §§109–121, of which the Nimrod material is §§113–117. Greek in B. Niese, Flavii Iosephi Opera, vol. I (Berlin, 1887).
  • Josephus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the first language, Nephilim, Flood, Pillars of Seth, Old Testament canon, and Septuagint origin timelines — several of them, like this one, drawing on his primeval history in Antiquities Book I.
