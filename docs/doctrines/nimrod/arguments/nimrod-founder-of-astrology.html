Did Nimrod found astrology, idolatry, and the pagan cultus?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Nimrod? · witness: the Pseudo-Clementine literature

Related readings: Did Nimrod build Babel and its Tower? · Is Nimrod the god behind the pagan religions, as the “Two Babylons” thesis holds?

The passage

Of this family there was born in due time a certain one, who took up with magical practices, by name Nebrod, who chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God. Him the Greeks have called Zoroaster. He, after the deluge, being ambitious of sovereignty, and being a great magician, by magical arts compelled the world-guiding star of the wicked one who now rules, to the bestowal of the sovereignty as a gift from him. But he, being a prince, and having authority over him who compelled him, wrathfully poured out the fire of the kingdom, that he might both bring to allegiance, and might punish him who at first constrained him.

— Clementine Homilies IX.4, New Advent (Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 8).1

The question

Genesis gives Nimrod a kingdom and a name as a hunter; a body of later sources makes him, in addition, the first magician, the founder of fire-worship and star-worship, and the first of the deified dead. What is disputed is whether that figure is the record of an origin or a post-biblical aetiology, and the question matters for how the history of the Nimrod tradition is told. Other ancient accounts place the origin of astral learning before the Flood, among Seth’s descendants (Josephus, Antiquities I.2.3), on the pillars of Seth and astrology timelines.2

Nimrod the first magus and founder of the star-cult

On this reading Nimrod began magic, fire-worship and star-worship, and was the first man worshipped as a god.

Held by: the Clementine Homilies (IX.4–6, 3rd–4th c.); Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 1.3), as quoted by van der Horst; Peter Comestor (Historia Scholastica, c. 1173); the Syriac Book of the Bee (Solomon of Akhlat, 13th c.).3

The Homilies name “Nebrod,” the Septuagint’s spelling of Nimrod, as the giant who “chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God,” a great magician who compelled a star to grant him sovereignty and was struck dead by the fire he called down. Men, “thinking that through the love of God his soul had been sent for by lightning,” buried him, gave him a temple among the Persians, and worshipped him as a god; the Persians kept the heavenly fire and reigned first, and the Babylonians and Egyptians followed. The account binds the opposition to God of the “mighty one” to the origin of idolatry: the first empire-builder is the first magus, the first idol, and the first teacher of the stars, and the gods of the nations are the dead mistaken for gods. The Recognitions tell the same story of the first magician consumed by star-fire and have the magic art “handed down” to “a certain king Nimrod.” Epiphanius, as quoted by van der Horst, writes that “Nimrod was an originator of wrong teaching, astrology and magic,” while denying that he was Zoroaster. The rabbinic sources make him the idol-king who bids Abraham worship fire (Genesis Rabbah 38:13). The medieval schools give the same figure: Peter Comestor’s Nimrod compels his people to worship fire and is taught astronomy by “Jonitus,” a fourth son of Noah, and the Book of the Bee binds Nimrod to the stars and to the origin of fire-worship. Read together, these sources give a single account of how paganism began, placed at the head of the post-Flood kingdoms.

A post-biblical aetiology joined to Zoroaster

On this reading the founder-of-astrology Nimrod grew out of Jewish and Christian identification of Nimrod with Zoroaster.

Held by: Wilhelm Bousset (Hauptprobleme der Gnosis, 1907), as quoted by van der Horst; K. van der Toorn & P. W. van der Horst (critical scholarship, Harvard Theological Review, 1990).4

Genesis links Nimrod to empire and hunting; the astral and idolatrous charge comes from other sources. Van der Horst notes that in Genesis Rabbah 38:13 Nimrod proposes to Abraham, “Let us worship the fire,” and that “Nimrod was sometimes identified with Zoroaster who was regarded as the one who introduced the worship of fire,” so that “Nimrod-Zoroaster is viewed as the originator of astrology and magic.” He holds that although these testimonies are found chiefly in Christian sources — the Pseudo-Clementine Homilies 9.4–5 and the Cave of Treasures — “there can be little doubt that the identification of Nimrod and Zoroaster had a Jewish origin.” He quotes Bousset’s account: to the Jews of the Babylonian lowlands Zoroaster came to seem “the creator of the pagan religion that dominated the region,” and “from there it was only a step to the identification Zoroaster-Nimrod,” in which Bousset saw “a last distant echo” of “the incursion of the Persian religion into the Babylonian lowlands.” Van der Horst adds that “the image of Nimrod as the archrebel against God lent itself easily to identification with a person who in a certain religio-historical constellation could be regarded as the founder of paganism par excellence.” The recensions show the identification still taking shape: the Homilies make Nebrod Zoroaster, while the Recognitions give the Zoroaster role to Ham’s son Mesraim and call Nimrod Ninus. On this reading the charge belongs to the history of what was said about Nimrod, a tradition whose growth can be dated and whose sources can be named.5
