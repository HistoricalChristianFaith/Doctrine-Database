The “Two Babylons” thesis: is Nimrod the hidden god behind every pagan religion — Tammuz, Ninus, the husband of Semiramis, the “Babylon mystery religion” smuggled into Rome?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: unsupported — Alexander Hislop’s The Two Babylons (1853/1858) is the fullest form of the modern claim that Nimrod is the historical seed of all paganism: husband and son of Semiramis, one and the same as Ninus, Tammuz, Baal, and a dozen other gods, a slain-and-risen fertility deity whose cult — the “Babylon mystery religion” — survived Babel and was later smuggled whole into the Roman Church. A defensible kernel exists (Nimrod really is tied to Babylon in Genesis; syncretism among ancient Near-Eastern fertility cults is real; Revelation really does call Rome “Babylon”), and that kernel is steelmanned below. But the specific Hislop system has no basis: no ancient source makes Semiramis Nimrod’s wife or mother, or equates Nimrod with Tammuz or Ninus; the only historical Semiramis (Sammu-ramat) lived more than a millennium after any Nimrod horizon; and the chain of god-identifications rests on free-associative etymology and doctored citations (the notorious “IHS = Isis-Horus-Seb” is a plain error — the letters are the Greek abbreviation of Jesus). The thesis was popularized, then publicly retracted, by its best-known modern proponent, Ralph Woodrow. Whether Rome is “Babylon” is a separate, legitimate question that does not need — and is not helped by — Hislop’s genealogy of gods.

The claim

The thesis is Alexander Hislop’s, set out in The Two Babylons; or, The Papal Worship Proved to Be the Worship of Nimrod and His Wife — a pamphlet of 1853 expanded to a book in 1858, and revived in the twentieth century by Jack Chick, by Dave Hunt’s A Woman Rides the Beast, and across a wide field of anti-Catholic and King-James-only media. In its strong form it runs: Nimrod founded Babylon and its mystery-cult; on his death his widow Semiramis deified him and bore a son, Tammuz, whom she declared to be Nimrod reborn — so that Nimrod is also Tammuz, and the two are worshipped across the nations under scores of names (Ninus, Baal, Molech, Osiris, Bacchus, and the rest, all “one and the same”). This Babylonian mother-and-child, Semiramis-and-Tammuz, is the archetype of every pagan madonna-and-child; and the whole cult, dispersed at Babel, was carried into the Roman Church, where (Hislop argues) it survives disguised as the round wafer of the Mass, the mitre, Christmas and Lent, the Madonna, and the monogram “IHS” — read as “Isis, Horus, Seb.” The system leans on Revelation 17’s “MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT” to identify the papacy as the end-time Babylon whose religion Nimrod began.1

The steelman: the defensible kernel

Three of the thesis’s starting-points are genuinely sound, and a fair hearing must grant them. First, Nimrod really is bound to Babylon in the text. Genesis makes “the beginning of his kingdom” Babel in the land of Shinar (Genesis 10:10), and the tradition — from Josephus to Augustine — ties him to the tower on that same plain (weighed on the Babel argument). A Nimrod-and-Babylon link is not invented. Second, syncretism among ancient Near-Eastern fertility cults is real. The dying-and-rising vegetation god, the mourned young consort, the mother-goddess — Dumuzi/Tammuz, Ishtar/Inanna, Baal, Adonis, Osiris — do share genuine morphological kinship, and cults did borrow imagery across cultures; comparative religion did not begin with Hislop. Third, Revelation really does call a great world-city “Babylon.” The Apocalypse names “MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS” (Revelation 17:5), and the mainstream reading takes that Babylon to be Rome — the seven-hilled city (Revelation 17:9) that ruled the earth when John wrote. So “Rome is Babylon” is a serious biblical proposition, not a fever-dream. The steelman is that Hislop merely joins three true things: Nimrod founded Babylon, Babylon’s cult was pagan and syncretistic, and Rome inherited the name Babylon — therefore Rome inherited Nimrod’s religion.2

Weighing it: where the system breaks

No ancient source makes Semiramis Nimrod’s wife — and the only historical Semiramis lived a millennium too late. The linchpin of the whole construction is the marriage of Nimrod and Semiramis, and it has no ancient attestation whatever: not Genesis, not Josephus, not the rabbis, not the Fathers, not any Greek or Mesopotamian text pairs them. The one Semiramis known to history is Sammu-ramat, a real Neo-Assyrian queen — wife of Shamshi-Adad V and regent for her son Adad-nirari III, dominant at Calah c. 811–806 BC. Whatever date one assigns Nimrod (the tradition puts him at the founding of Babel, in the third millennium or earlier), Sammu-ramat postdates it by well over a thousand years; the fifth-century romancers Ctesias and Diodorus Siculus then spun her into the legendary queen who married the (equally legendary) king Ninus and built Babylon. To make her Nimrod’s wife, Hislop must first identify Nimrod with Ninus — a bare conjecture of his own — and then attach to that pairing a whole biography (the deified husband, the reborn son) that no source supplies. The keystone is a modern invention.3

The god-identifications are free association, not evidence. Hislop’s method is to chain unrelated deities across languages and centuries on the strength of similar-sounding names and shared attributes, so that (as Woodrow tallies it) “Nimrod, Adonis, Apollo, Attes, Baal-zebub, Bacchus, Cupid, Dagon, Hercules… Osiris, Pluto, Saturn… and Zoroaster were all one and the same.” But phonetic resemblance is not descent: by the same reasoning one could prove Gerald Ford and Henry Ford the same man. Tammuz (Sumerian Dumuzi) is a distinct, independently attested shepherd-god of the third-millennium Sumerian pantheon — not a byname for Nimrod, and nowhere his son. Nor do the “Roman survivals” hold: the monogram “IHS” is not “Isis-Horus-Seb” but the first three letters — iota, eta, sigma — of ΙΗΣΟΥΣ, the Greek spelling of Jesus, a standard nomen sacrum in Christian manuscripts centuries before any of Hislop’s Egyptian gloss. And the citations that seem to anchor the argument repeatedly misrepresent their sources: where Hislop cites Wilkinson for “round” Egyptian cakes to prime the round wafer, Wilkinson in fact describes cakes “oval and triangular… shaped like leaves, animals, and a crocodile’s head.”4

Its best-known modern champion retracted it. The thesis reached its widest twentieth-century audience through Ralph Woodrow’s Babylon Mystery Religion (1966), a readable digest of Hislop that sold for decades. Woodrow then checked Hislop’s sources, found the pattern of error, and in The Babylon Connection? (1997) publicly repudiated both Hislop and his own earlier book — withdrawing it from print at real cost, because “I could not in good conscience continue to publish a book against pagan mixture knowing that it contained a mixture itself of misinformation about Babylonian origins.” When the scholar who spent thirty years popularizing a thesis dismantles it on inspecting the footnotes, that is telling evidence about the thesis.5

Keep the Revelation-Babylon question separate. None of this touches whether Rome is the “Babylon” of Revelation — a real and long-debated question, weighed under the papal reading of the beast. That identification (if sound) rests on Rome’s seven hills and its first-century dominion, not on any genealogy of gods reaching back to Nimrod. Hislop’s system does not establish the Rome-Babylon reading; it burdens it with a mass of false etymology that a careful case is better off without. The two must not be confused: one can hold that John’s Babylon is Rome and still reject, wholesale, the claim that the Mass is Nimrod-worship.

Assessment

Unsupported. The defensible kernel is real — Nimrod is tied to Babylon, ancient fertility cults did syncretize, and Revelation does call a great city Babylon — but the Hislop system built on it has no basis. Its keystone marriage of Nimrod and Semiramis is attested by no ancient source and is chronologically impossible (the historical Semiramis, Sammu-ramat, postdates any Nimrod horizon by more than a millennium); its equation of Nimrod with Tammuz and Ninus is unfounded; its chain of “one and the same” gods is free-associative etymology; and its Roman “survivals” rest on demonstrable errors, the “IHS = Isis-Horus-Seb” gloss being the plainest (the letters spell Jesus in Greek). Its most influential modern advocate retracted it after checking the sources. This is not a case of a strong reading marred by a weak proponent — there is no stronger version of “Nimrod is the god of every pagan religion” waiting to be steelmanned; the interpretation itself has nothing under it. The separate and legitimate question — is Rome the Babylon of Revelation? — is better pursued on its own terms, without Hislop’s genealogy of gods.
