Is Nimrod the god behind the pagan religions, as the “Two Babylons” thesis holds?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Nimrod?

Related readings: Did Nimrod build Babel and its Tower? · Did Nimrod found astrology, idolatry, and the pagan cultus?

The passage

And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.

— Genesis 10:10 (KJV); with the name of the woman in Revelation, “MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS” (Revelation 17:5), the two texts on which the thesis builds.1

The question

Alexander Hislop’s The Two Babylons; or, The Papal Worship Proved to Be the Worship of Nimrod and His Wife (1853; expanded 1858) holds that Nimrod, deified with his wife Semiramis and their son Tammuz, is the god behind the pagan religions, and that his Babylonian cult survives in Roman Catholic worship. What is disputed is whether the ancient sources bear out that chain of identifications. Both readings below are held chiefly in popular and polemical literature; an academic literature defending the thesis was not located. Whether Rome is the “Babylon” of Revelation is a separate question, recorded on the mark-of-the-beast timeline.

Nimrod’s Babylonian mystery-cult survives in Rome

On this reading Nimrod, Semiramis and Tammuz are the originals of the pagan gods, and their worship passed into the Roman Church.

Held by: Alexander Hislop (The Two Babylons, 1853/1858); Ralph Woodrow (Babylon Mystery Religion, 1966, later withdrawn by him); Dave Hunt (A Woman Rides the Beast); Jack Chick.2

The thesis starts from Genesis 10:10, which makes Babel “the beginning of his kingdom”; Hislop reads this as Nimrod’s founding of Babylon. In Hislop’s account Nimrod founded Babylon’s mystery-cult; on his death his widow Semiramis deified him and bore a son, Tammuz, whom she declared to be Nimrod reborn, so that Nimrod is also Tammuz, and the pair are worshipped across the nations under many names — Ninus, Baal, Molech, Osiris, Bacchus, and others. The Nimrod–Ninus equation already appears in the Pseudo-Clementine Recognitions (IV.29, “a certain king Nimrod… whom the Greeks also called Ninus”), and the dying-and-rising young god, the mourned consort and the mother-goddess of the ancient Near East — Dumuzi/Tammuz, Ishtar/Inanna, Baal, Adonis, Osiris — share features that Hislop reads as one original cult dispersed from Babel. The Babylonian mother-and-child, Semiramis and Tammuz, is on this reading the archetype of every pagan madonna-and-child. Revelation names a city “MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT,” and Hislop identifies its religion with the one Nimrod began, carried into Rome and preserved there in the round wafer of the Mass, the mitre, Christmas and Lent, the Madonna, and the monogram “IHS,” which he reads as “Isis, Horus, Seb.” On this reading the subtitle states the thesis in brief: the papal worship is “the worship of Nimrod and his wife.”

The equations have no ancient basis, and the Roman usages have their own origins

On this reading the marriage, the god-chain and the “survivals” are Hislop’s constructions.

Held by: Ralph Woodrow (Protestant evangelist, The Babylon Connection? and “The Two Babylons: A Case Study in Poor Methodology,” Christian Research Journal); René Maere (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “IHS,” 1910), on the monogram.3

Woodrow, who had popularized the thesis, checked Hislop’s sources and reports that of the ancient histories he consulted “not one said anything about Nimrod and Semiramis being husband and wife,” adding, “They did not even live in the same century,” and “Nor is there any basis for Semiramis being the mother of Tammuz.” The one Semiramis known from inscriptions is Sammu-ramat, wife of Shamshi-Adad V and regent for her son Adad-nirari III at Calah, c. 811–806 BC; the legendary queen who married the eponymous king Ninus and built Babylon is the elaboration of Ctesias and Diodorus Siculus. Tammuz (Sumerian Dumuzi) is an independently attested shepherd-god of the Sumerian pantheon. Woodrow describes Hislop’s method as joining deities on the strength of similar names and attributes, so that “Nimrod, Adonis, Apollo, Attes, Baal-zebub, Bacchus, Cupid, Dagon, Hercules… Osiris, Pluto, Saturn… and Zoroaster were all one and the same,” and answers it with an analogy: Gerald Ford, the president, might be confused with Henry Ford, the car manufacturer. Checking the citations, he found that Wilkinson, cited for “round” Egyptian cakes, also describes cakes “oval and triangular.” Maere explains “IHS” as a monogram of the name of Jesus: in Christian inscriptions “from the beginning” the nomina sacra were shortened by contraction, “IHS and XPS for Iesous Christos.” Woodrow withdrew Babylon Mystery Religion from print and replaced it with The Babylon Connection?, because “I could not in good conscience continue to publish a book against pagan mixture knowing that it contained a mixture itself of misinformation about Babylonian origins.” On this reading Nimrod’s place in the record is the one Genesis and its interpreters give him, and the Roman usages are explained from their own Christian history.4
