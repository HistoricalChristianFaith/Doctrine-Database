Did Nimrod build Babel and its Tower?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Nimrod? · witnesses: Josephus, the Targums, Theophilus of Antioch

Related readings: Did Nimrod found astrology, idolatry, and the pagan cultus? · Is Nimrod the god behind the pagan religions, as the “Two Babylons” thesis holds?

The passage

And Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one in the earth… And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.

— Genesis 10:8, 10 (KJV).

And it came to pass, as they journeyed from the east, that they found a plain in the land of Shinar; and they dwelt there… And they said, Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven… Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth.

— Genesis 11:2–9 (KJV).1

The question

Genesis 10 makes Babel in Shinar “the beginning of his kingdom”; Genesis 11 tells of a city and tower built at Babel in Shinar by builders it does not name. Whether Nimrod built or led the building of the tower, opposed it, or is joined to it only by later exegesis is disputed, and the answer shapes the later portrait of Nimrod as the rebel of Babel.

Nimrod led the building of Babel and its Tower

On this reading the founder of Babel in chapter 10 is the leader of the tower-project in chapter 11.

Held by: Josephus (Jewish historian, Antiquities I.4, c. 93); Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 24 (rabbinic, 8th–9th c.); Augustine (City of God 16.4, c. 420); Isidore of Seville (Etymologies, c. 615–630).2

The city is the same city. Genesis 10:10 names Babel, in the land of Shinar, as the first seat of Nimrod’s kingdom; Genesis 11:2–9 sets the tower on a plain in the land of Shinar and names that city Babel. The Table of Nations already lists the peoples “after their tongues” (Genesis 10:5, 20, 31) before chapter 11 tells how the one tongue was confounded, so chapter 11 can be read as the account that explains chapter 10, and the Babel Nimrod founded and the tower-Babel as one place at one moment. Nimrod is the only founder of cities named in the surrounding text — “the beginning of his kingdom” is a list of foundations — and the empire-builder introduced just before the tower is the natural candidate to have led its building. Josephus names him the instigator who raised it “too high for the waters to be able to reach,” to be “revenged on God” for the Flood; Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer has him propose to his people that they build a great city and a tower ascending to heaven. Augustine argues it from the names: because the city called Confusion is Babylon, and Babylon was the beginning of Nimrod’s kingdom, “we conclude that the giant Nimrod was its founder,” the “proud and impious founder” whose plan was to reach the sky. Isidore states it in two entries: Nimrod “undertook, in impiety, to build a tower against God,” and “first after the flood, Nembroth the giant founded the city of Babylon.” Read as one connected narrative, the two chapters give the kingdom of Nimrod rising into the tower’s presumption.

Nimrod opposed the tower-builders and left Babel

On this reading Nimrod kept apart from the Babel project, or fought against it, by God’s will.

Held by: Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 10:11 (Palestinian Targum, final redaction 7th–8th c.), as read by P. W. van der Horst (1990); Ishodad of Merv (Syriac commentator), as reported by van der Horst.3

The Hebrew of Genesis 10:11 can be read “out of that land went forth Asshur” or “out of that land he [Nimrod] went forth to Asshur.” Taking Nimrod as the subject, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders: “From that land went forth Nimrod, and reigned in Athur, because he would not be in the counsel of a divided generation. And he left those four cities; and the Lord thereupon gave him a place.” Nimrod leaves Babel before the tower narrative begins, and God rewards him with a new place; van der Horst reads the Targum to mean that, “because he left before chapter 11, he must have been opposed to the building of the tower.” The Syriac commentators carry the same tradition further. Van der Horst reports Ishodad of Merv’s comment that Nimrod became famous by God’s will “because he had combatted the builders of the tower of Babel and driven them away from the city,” and a Syriac manuscript quoted by A. Levene says that “he made war on those who built the tower and he first captured Babylon.” Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373), whom van der Horst places among these witnesses, has Nimrod fight the nations “according to the will of the Lord” and drive them out “to the regions that had been set apart for them by God” — the dispersal the tower-builders sought to avoid (“lest we be scattered abroad,” Genesis 11:4). On this reading the proverb “like Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD” is a blessing, and Nimrod founds Nineveh and its cities as the servant of the dispersal God decreed.

Genesis keeps the notices apart; the link is later exegesis

On this reading Genesis makes Nimrod the founder of the city Babel and leaves the tower’s builders unnamed.

Held by: K. van der Toorn & P. W. van der Horst (critical scholarship, Harvard Theological Review, 1990).4

The tower narrative (Genesis 11:1–9) has a collective subject — “they said one to another… let us build… which the children of men builded” — and a stated motive, “let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad.” It names no king, and the “revenge on God” motive is Josephus’s. Genesis 10 asserts that Babel was the beginning of Nimrod’s kingdom; that much this reading takes as the text’s own statement. An early Christian narrator kept the two apart: Theophilus of Antioch (c. 185) makes Nimrod king of the cities of Shinar and builder of Nineveh, then ascribes the tower to “men” who “took counsel of their own judgment,” naming no ringleader. Van der Toorn and van der Horst begin from the observation that “the biblical information concerning the figure of Nimrod is scarce” and that “post-biblical tradition has added supplementary details that cannot be found in the biblical text, however much they may be presented as results of exegesis of this text.” Van der Horst traces how the link arose: the gibbor/gigas of Genesis 10 joined Nimrod to the giants of Genesis 6, and his kingdom in Babel in Shinar, “where also the tower was built according to Genesis 11,” “seemed to leave no other possibility than that Nimrod built the tower”; he finds the connection already implied in Philo, early in the first century AD. On this reading the attribution is a coherent exegetical synthesis with a datable history, and the text’s own claim is Nimrod’s founding of the city.5
