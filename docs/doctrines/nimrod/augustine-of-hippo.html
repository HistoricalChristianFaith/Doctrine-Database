Augustine on Nimrod

Dates: c. 420 (the City of God Book 16; Augustine of Hippo, 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading the Table of Nations and Babel together in the City of God, Augustine makes Nimrod the head of the earthly city — the society founded on domination and pride, against the pilgrim city of God traced through Shem to Abraham. He calls Nimrod “the founder” of Babylon, the “proud and impious” builder whose tower rose “against the Lord,” and fixes the adversative reading of Genesis 10:9: he names the ambiguity of the Greek enantion (“before” or “against”) only to resolve it firmly — those who render “before the Lord” have “misunderstood” the word — and glosses “hunter” as “deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer.” This is the locus classicus for Nimrod as founder of Babylon-the-earthly-city and for the “against the Lord” reading in the Latin West.

Where it sits

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) devotes Books 15–18 of the City of God (finished c. 420) to the parallel histories of the two cities — the earthly city of self-love and domination and the heavenly city of the love of God — traced from the Flood to Christ. In Book 16 he works through the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) and the confusion of tongues (Genesis 11) as a single movement, and it is there, in chapters 3 and 4, that Nimrod appears — not as an incidental name in a genealogy but as the emblem of the earthly city at the moment it becomes visible in history: the founder of Babylon, whose very name means “Confusion.” The reading is brief but decisive, and because of Augustine’s authority it became the standard Latin account, digested a century and more later by Bede.1

Claims & evidence

The two cities, and Nimrod at the head of the earthly one

We must therefore introduce into this work an explanation of the generations of the three sons of Noah, in so far as that may illustrate the progress in time of the two cities… “Cush begat Nimrod; he began to be a giant on the earth. He was a giant hunter against the Lord God: wherefore they say, As Nimrod the giant hunter against the Lord. And the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.”… he either begat this giant after his grandsons were born, or, which is more credible, Scripture speaks of him separately on account of his eminence; for mention is also made of his kingdom, which began with that magnificent city Babylon.

— City of God 16.3. The framing is the whole point: Augustine reads the genealogy of Noah’s sons “in so far as that may illustrate the progress in time of the two cities,” and Nimrod is where the earthly city surfaces. Two things are already settled in his citation of the verse. First, he quotes Genesis in the form “a giant hunter against the Lord” — the Old Latin rendering of the Greek — not “before” the Lord. Second, he reads “giant” (Greek gigas, for Hebrew gibbor) as Nimrod’s literal eminence, the reason Scripture “speaks of him separately.” Nimrod is singled out because his kingdom — “that magnificent city Babylon” — is the head of the earthly city itself.2

Nimrod the founder of Babylon-the-Confusion

This city, which was called Confusion, is the same as Babylon… For Babylon means Confusion. Whence we conclude that the giant Nimrod was its founder, as had been hinted a little before, where Scripture, in speaking of him, says that the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon… although it did not rise to the grand dimensions designed by its proud and impious founder. The plan was to make it so high that it should reach the sky.

— City of God 16.4. Genesis itself never names Nimrod as the builder of the Tower — it keeps the “mighty one” of chapter 10 and the nameless builders of chapter 11 in separate pericopes, saying only that Babel was “the beginning of his kingdom.” Augustine draws the identification out as an inference (“whence we conclude…”): because the city called Confusion is Babylon, and Babylon was the head of Nimrod’s kingdom, Nimrod is Babylon’s “proud and impious founder” and the architect of the tower that would “reach the sky.” He thus fuses Nimrod with the Tower as Josephus had, but grounds it in the logic of the text rather than in a legend of revenge on God.3

The crux named — and resolved toward “against the Lord”

The safe and true way to heaven is made by humility, which lifts up the heart to the Lord, not against Him; as this giant is said to have been a “hunter against the Lord.” This has been misunderstood by some through the ambiguity of the Greek word, and they have translated it, not “against the Lord,” but “before the Lord;” for ἀναντίον means both “before” and “against.” In the Psalm this word is rendered, “Let us weep before the Lord our Maker.” The same word occurs in the book of Job, where it is written, “Thou hast broken into fury against the Lord.” And so this giant is to be recognized as a “hunter against the Lord.” And what is meant by the term “hunter” but deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer of the animals of the earth? He and his people therefore, erected this tower against the Lord, and so gave expression to their impious pride.

— City of God 16.4. This is the earliest statement on this timeline to name the interpretive crux by the Greek word that carries it — enantion (Genesis 10:9’s “before / against the LORD”) “means both ‘before’ and ‘against.’” But where Bede three centuries later would concede that “both can be interpreted” and leave the grammar genuinely open, Augustine names the ambiguity only to close it: those who read “before the Lord” have “misunderstood” the word, and the giant “is to be recognized as a ‘hunter against the Lord.’” He backs the adversative sense with the same pair of proof-texts Bede would later reuse — “before the Lord” in Psalm 95:6 (“Let us weep before the Lord our Maker”) versus “against the Lord” in Job — and then supplies the moral gloss that fixes “hunter” in the Latin tradition as a term of opprobrium: a hunter is a “deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer,” and Nimrod “and his people… erected this tower against the Lord” out of “impious pride.”4

Notes

  • The Latin locus classicus. More than any other Father, Augustine fixes two readings for the West at once: that Nimrod is the founder of Babylon — the earthly city of domination — and that his epithet is “mighty hunter against the Lord.” Both feed directly into the two live questions the wiki weighs on their own argument pages: was the biblical Nimrod a hero or a rebel, and did he found Babel. On both, Augustine stands squarely at the adversative, founder-of-Babel pole.
  • He names the crux, but does not balance it. Augustine is the source of the philological observation that enantion bears both senses — the very point Bede would later develop — but he deploys it one-directionally: to explain why some wrongly read “before the Lord,” not to leave the reading open. Where Bede concedes “both can be interpreted” and Ephrem takes “before the Lord” as outright blessing, Augustine treats the favourable rendering as a straightforward mistake. His block is thus the strongest statement of the adversative case that still, in passing, admits the grammar that undoes its certainty.
  • The literal “giant.” Augustine reads Nimrod’s gibbor (Greek gigas) as bodily as well as political: later in the same book he says Scripture named Nimrod separately “on account of the extraordinary dimensions of his empire and of his body” (City of God 16.11). The giant-hunter of the earthly city is, for Augustine, a giant in the flesh — the same word used of the “mighty men… of old” before the Flood.
  • Nimrod and the two cities. The framing is what medieval writers inherit: Nimrod is not merely a wicked king but the type of the earthly city — “the society of men living according to man, not according to God” — whose emblem is Babylon/Confusion and whose punishment (the scattering of tongues, pride broken in “the instrument of domination”) is the counter-image of the pilgrim city of God gathered from every tongue. This two-cities reading of Nimrod is the frame Dante and the later Latin tradition apply.
