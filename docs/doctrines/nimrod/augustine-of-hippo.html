Augustine on Nimrod

Dates: c. 420 (the City of God Book 16; Augustine of Hippo, 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading the Table of Nations and Babel together in the City of God, Augustine makes Nimrod the head of the earthly city — the society founded on domination and pride, against the pilgrim city of God traced through Shem to Abraham. He calls Nimrod “the founder” of Babylon, the “proud and impious” builder whose tower rose “against the Lord,” and takes the adversative reading of Genesis 10:9: he names the ambiguity of the Greek enantion (“before” or “against”) and resolves it — those who render “before the Lord” have “misunderstood” the word — and glosses “hunter” as “deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer.” On this timeline, Isidore (early 7th century) has Nimrod build a tower “against God,” and Bede (c. 720) repeats the enantion observation and its proof-texts but writes that “both can be interpreted.”

Placement on the timeline

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) devotes Books 15–18 of the City of God (finished c. 420) to the parallel histories of the two cities — the earthly city of self-love and domination and the heavenly city of the love of God — traced from the Flood to Christ. In Book 16 he works through the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) and the confusion of tongues (Genesis 11) as a single movement, and it is there, in chapters 3 and 4, that Nimrod appears — not as an incidental name in a genealogy but as the emblem of the earthly city at the moment it becomes visible in history: the founder of Babylon, whose very name means “Confusion.” The reading is brief; three centuries later Bede repeats its enantion observation and its proof-texts.1

Claims & evidence

The two cities, and Nimrod at the head of the earthly one

We must therefore introduce into this work an explanation of the generations of the three sons of Noah, in so far as that may illustrate the progress in time of the two cities… “Cush begat Nimrod; he began to be a giant on the earth. He was a giant hunter against the Lord God: wherefore they say, As Nimrod the giant hunter against the Lord. And the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.”… he either begat this giant after his grandsons were born, or, which is more credible, Scripture speaks of him separately on account of his eminence; for mention is also made of his kingdom, which began with that magnificent city Babylon.

— City of God 16.3. Augustine reads the genealogy of Noah’s sons “in so far as that may illustrate the progress in time of the two cities,” and Nimrod is where the earthly city surfaces. Two things appear already in his citation of the verse. First, he quotes Genesis in the form “a giant hunter against the Lord” — the Old Latin rendering of the Greek — not “before” the Lord. Second, he reads “giant” (Greek gigas, for Hebrew gibbor) as Nimrod’s literal eminence, the reason Scripture “speaks of him separately.” Nimrod is singled out because his kingdom — “that magnificent city Babylon” — is the head of the earthly city itself.2

Nimrod the founder of Babylon-the-Confusion

This city, which was called Confusion, is the same as Babylon… For Babylon means Confusion. Whence we conclude that the giant Nimrod was its founder, as had been hinted a little before, where Scripture, in speaking of him, says that the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon… although it did not rise to the grand dimensions designed by its proud and impious founder. The plan was to make it so high that it should reach the sky.

— City of God 16.4. Genesis names Nimrod in chapter 10, where Babel is “the beginning of his kingdom,” and names no builder in the tower narrative of chapter 11. Augustine draws the identification out as an inference (“whence we conclude…”): because the city called Confusion is Babylon, and Babylon was the head of Nimrod’s kingdom, Nimrod is Babylon’s “proud and impious founder” and the architect of the tower that would “reach the sky.” He thus joins Nimrod to the Tower as Josephus had, arguing from the names of the city rather than from a story of revenge on God.3

The crux named, and read as “against the Lord”

The safe and true way to heaven is made by humility, which lifts up the heart to the Lord, not against Him; as this giant is said to have been a “hunter against the Lord.” This has been misunderstood by some through the ambiguity of the Greek word, and they have translated it, not “against the Lord,” but “before the Lord;” for ἀναντίον means both “before” and “against.” In the Psalm this word is rendered, “Let us weep before the Lord our Maker.” The same word occurs in the book of Job, where it is written, “Thou hast broken into fury against the Lord.” And so this giant is to be recognized as a “hunter against the Lord.” And what is meant by the term “hunter” but deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer of the animals of the earth? He and his people therefore, erected this tower against the Lord, and so gave expression to their impious pride.

— City of God 16.4. This is the earliest extant statement among this timeline’s witnesses to name the interpretive crux by the Greek word that carries it — enantion (Genesis 10:9’s “before / against the LORD”) “means both ‘before’ and ‘against.’” Augustine names the ambiguity and then decides it: those who read “before the Lord” have “misunderstood” the word, and the giant “is to be recognized as a ‘hunter against the Lord.’” He backs the adversative sense with a pair of proof-texts that Bede also uses — “before the Lord” in Psalm 95:6 (“Let us weep before the Lord our Maker”) versus “against the Lord” in Job — and then glosses “hunter” as a term of opprobrium: a hunter is a “deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer,” and Nimrod “and his people… erected this tower against the Lord” out of “impious pride.”4

Reception and debate

  • Two readings in one passage. Augustine holds both that Nimrod is the founder of Babylon, the earthly city of domination, and that his epithet is “mighty hunter against the Lord.” Both bear on questions recorded on the Nimrod timeline: whether the biblical Nimrod was a hero or a rebel, and whether he built Babel and its Tower. On both, Augustine takes the adversative, founder-of-Babel side.
  • He names the crux and decides it. Augustine records the observation that enantion bears both senses, which Bede also makes, and uses it to explain why some read “before the Lord,” a rendering he calls a misunderstanding. Bede writes “both can be interpreted” before choosing the same sense; Ephrem takes “before the Lord” as blessing.
  • The literal “giant.” Augustine reads Nimrod’s gibbor (Greek gigas) as bodily as well as political: later in the same book he says Scripture named Nimrod separately “on account of the extraordinary dimensions of his empire and of his body” (City of God 16.11). The giant-hunter of the earthly city is, for Augustine, a giant in the flesh — the same word used of the “mighty men… of old” before the Flood.
  • Nimrod and the two cities. In this framing Nimrod is not merely a wicked king but the type of the earthly city — “the society of men living according to man, not according to God” — whose emblem is Babylon/Confusion and whose punishment (the scattering of tongues, pride broken in “the instrument of domination”) is the counter-image of the pilgrim city of God gathered from every tongue.
