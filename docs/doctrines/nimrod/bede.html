Bede on Nimrod

Dates: c. 720 (the Commentary on Genesis / In Genesim; the Venerable Bede, c. 672/673–735, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bede gathers the whole adversative tradition into one careful synthesis and then adds the allegory that would carry it through the Middle Ages. He names the interpretive crux by its Greek term: “before the Lord” renders enantion, which “can be interpreted both” ways, and another version reads “a giant hunter against the Lord” — the phrase, he says, “was added to magnify the crime.” He makes Nimrod the first tyrant who, “neglecting heavenly matters,” sought “lowly things,” reads his very name as “tyrant, or fugitive, or transgressor,” and turns him into a figure of the devil — the hunter who “sets traps… in the forest of this world” and hunts souls to their death, the dark mirror of the apostles who were made “fishers of men.”

Where it sits

The Venerable Bede (c. 672/673–735), monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow in Northumbria and the foremost scholar of the early-medieval Latin West, wrote his four-book Commentary on Genesis (In Genesim) in stages, the whole complete by about 720. His treatment of Nimrod, on Genesis 10:8–10, is the fullest patristic discussion on this timeline: it digests Jerome and the earlier exegesis, spells out the Greek philology of the crux by name, and then supplies the tropological (moral-allegorical) reading — Nimrod as a type of the devil — that the medieval schools inherit.1

Claims & evidence

The first tyrant, “mighty on earth” by neglecting heaven

While the progeny of Shem and Japheth remained in the innocence of a simple life, a cursed offspring was born of the lineage of Ham… living first by hunting; then, having gathered an army, he endeavored to exercise unusual tyranny over peoples… He began indeed because he was the first to do it. Moreover, he was mighty on the earth because, neglecting heavenly matters, in which the just are mighty, he learned to seek lowly things and place his hope in them.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Genesis 10:8. Bede reads the whole trajectory into the one verse: hunter → warlord → tyrant → king. Two touches are his own emphasis. First, the moral inversion of “mighty”: Nimrod is “mighty on the earth” precisely because he is not mighty in the heavenly things “in which the just are mighty” — earthly greatness bought by neglect of God. Second, the note that Nimrod was “the first to do it” matches Jerome’s “first seized an unprecedented tyranny.”2

The crux named: enantion, “before” or “against” the Lord

…the phrase before the Lord was added to magnify the crime, since it was evidently quite reckless and arrogant for a man on earth to dare live in such a way before the Lord… although another version has it that Nimrod was a giant hunter against the Lord. From the ambiguous Greek term, enantion, both can be interpreted… he was a mighty hunter of brute animals, as a powerful man; but because he was an impious man, he led a proud life against the will of the Creator.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Genesis 10:9. This is the most valuable single patristic statement of the crux, because Bede does not simply take a side — he names the ambiguity. The Greek enantion (which the Septuagint uses for “before the LORD”) can mean “in the presence of” or “over against / in opposition to,” and Bede shows the range from other texts: it renders “before the Lord who made us” in Psalm 95:6, but also “you burst forth in rage against the Lord” in Job. He concludes that Nimrod was a mighty hunter and an impious man “who led a proud life against the will of the Creator” — adopting the adversative reading while conceding, more honestly than most, that the grammar itself will bear either.3

The name and the allegory: Nimrod as a figure of the devil

…the name Nimrod, which sounds like tyrant, or fugitive, or transgressor, suits well. For he is a tyrant because, rebelling against the Creator, he strives to obtain the height of divinity… a fugitive, because, having fallen from heaven… a transgressor, because he disdained to obey the will of the Creator… he sets traps of his ambushes in the forest of this world, and hunts men who are clean in nature and wit… deceiving them for death: surely opposed to those hunters who seek to catch the souls of men by their teaching so that they may lead them to eternal life; to whom the Lord speaks, saying, “Come after me, and I will make you become fishers of men.”

— Commentary on Genesis, on Genesis 10:10. Reading Nimrod’s kingdom — “Babel… in the land of Shinar” — Bede turns the whole figure into allegory. Babel means “confusion,” so its builder “figuratively indicates the devil, who is the head of all evils,” and the three senses of the name (tyrant, fugitive, transgressor) map onto the devil’s revolt, fall, and disobedience. The hunter-image, which Ephrem had made a blessing, Bede makes the very emblem of damnation: Nimrod-the-devil “sets traps… in the forest of this world” and hunts clean souls “for death” — the exact inversion of the apostles, the good hunters who “catch the souls of men… for life” (Matthew 4:19; Bethsaida, he adds, means “the House of Hunters”).4

Notes

  • The most careful witness to the crux. Bede is the one Father who states the ambiguity of “before / against the LORD” explicitly, by its Greek term, and demonstrates the range of enantion from other passages before choosing. That makes his block the natural anchor for the “before vs. against the LORD” argument: he shows that the adversative reading is a choice the grammar permits, not the only thing the text can mean — the very point Ephrem’s favourable reading depends on.
  • From tyrant to devil. Bede completes the villain-arc the tradition had been building: Josephus gave the rebel, Chrysostom the moral tyrant, Jerome the reprobate hunter, and Bede the diabolical type — Nimrod as a figure of Satan himself, “fallen from heaven,” hunting souls. This tropological Nimrod is what the medieval schools and preachers inherit.
  • Devil-hunter and Christ’s fishers. The elegant hinge of Bede’s allegory is that “hunting” can be salvific or damning: the devil hunts souls to death, the apostles “hunt men for life.” It is the same ambivalence the crux turns on — a hunter can be “before” the Lord or “against” Him — now moralized into two opposite kinds of hunter.
  • Text and edition. Bede, Commentary on Genesis (In Genesim), on Genesis 10:8–10 (English from the digitised translation of Bede’s Genesis commentary). The work was completed c. 720.
