Theophilus of Antioch on Nimrod

Dates: c. 185 (Ad Autolycum, c. 180–185) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian narrative to name Nimrod, one generation after Josephus. Working through the history after the Flood, Theophilus lists Nimrod as king of the first cities of Shinar and credits him with building Nineveh in the north — the Mesopotamian empire-builder of Genesis 10. Two things are notable. He treats the notice as plain history, drawing no moral verdict on Nimrod (neither the tyrant of Josephus nor a hero) — and, unlike Josephus, he keeps Nimrod and the tower of Babel apart: the tower is raised by "men" who "took counsel of their own judgment," with no named ringleader and no revenge-on-God motive. He is thus an early Christian witness who does not fuse the "mighty one" of Genesis 10 with the nameless builders of Genesis 11.

Claims & evidence

Nimrod, king of Shinar and builder of Nineveh

After the flood was there again a beginning of cities and kings, in the following manner:—The first city was Babylon, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar. And their king was called Nebroth [Nimrod]. From these came Asshur, from whom also the Assyrians receive their name. And Nimrod built the cities Nineveh and Rehoboth, and Calah, and Resen, between Nineveh and Calah; and Nineveh became a very great city.

— Ad Autolycum, Book II, ch. 31 ("The History After the Flood"), newadvent.org / historicalchristian.faith. Theophilus follows the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:8–12) closely, but resolves one of its cruxes in a particular direction: where the Hebrew of Genesis 10:11 can be read "out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh," Theophilus keeps "Asshur" as the eponym of the Assyrians yet makes Nimrod the builder of Nineveh, Rehoboth, Calah, and Resen — the Nimrod-subject construal that gives him the whole imperial sweep, Babylon in the south and Nineveh in the north. This is the reading later welded to the "founder of Babylon" tradition, but Theophilus states it flatly, without a word of praise or blame.

The tower — built by "men," not by Nimrod

But now we will mention the remaining facts both concerning cities and kings, and the things that happened when there was one speech and one language. Before the dividing of the languages these fore-mentioned cities existed. But when men were about to be dispersed, they took counsel of their own judgment, and not at the instigation of God, to build a city, a tower whose top might reach into heaven, that they might make a glorious name to themselves. Since, therefore, they had dared, contrary to the will of God, to attempt a grand work, God destroyed their city, and overthrew their tower.

— Ad Autolycum, Book II, ch. 31, newadvent.org. The point for the Nimrod question is what Theophilus does not do. Josephus, a generation earlier, had made Nimrod the instigator of the tower — the tyrant who raised it "too high for the waters to reach" to be "revenged on God." Theophilus tells the same sequence — the cities of Shinar, then the tower — but the tower is the work of a nameless collective ("men… took counsel of their own judgment"), its motive their own pride ("a glorious name to themselves"), not a king's war on heaven. Nimrod, named a few lines earlier as king and city-builder, is not carried over into the tower episode. Theophilus thus reads Genesis 10 and 11 as the plain text keeps them — the "mighty one" of the one and the anonymous builders of the other left apart — making him an early Christian witness against the fusion the later tradition would treat as settled.

Notes

  • Theophilus draws no moral verdict on Nimrod — he is neither the arch-tyrant of Josephus and the rabbis nor the blessed hunter of Ephrem, but simply the first king of the post-Flood cities. On the interpretive crux of "a mighty hunter before the LORD" he is silent: he never quotes Genesis 10:9, taking up only the kingdom-and-cities notice. His witness bears on what Nimrod founded, not on whether he was hero or rebel.
  • That he keeps Nimrod and the tower distinct is direct evidence for the "Did Nimrod build Babel and its Tower?" argument: the personal attribution of the tower to Nimrod is a synthesis the tradition drew (Josephus, Pseudo-Jonathan, the rabbis, the Latin Fathers), not a datum the text supplies — and here is an early reader who narrates both notices and does not make it.
  • The same passage makes Theophilus the earliest Christian narrator of Babel on the first-language timeline (the confusion of tongues, with the Sibyl quoted as pagan corroboration), and the three-book apology also anchors him on the Flood timeline, where he presses a geographically universal deluge and dates the world by the biblical chronology. The Nimrod-and-Babel notice here is, in his own words, "the history after the flood."
