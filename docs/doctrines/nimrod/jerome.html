Jerome on Nimrod

Dates: c. 391–392 (the Hebrew Questions on Genesis and the Commentary on Micah, written at Bethlehem; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome fixes the Latin villain-portrait and grounds it in the Hebrew. In the Hebrew Questions on Genesis he glosses “he began to be mighty” as Nimrod being the man who “first seized an unprecedented tyranny among the people” — the first tyrant. In the Commentary on Micah, where the prophet calls Assyria “the land of Nimrod,” he presses the name itself: Nimrod means “descending temptation,” and he is “the giant and hunter and one who proudly opposes the Lord,” fallen “like lightning from heaven” and dwelling in the pits. His famous line closes the case for the adversative reading: “As far as I can recall… I have never read favorably about a hunter” — pairing Nimrod with the two other biblical hunters, Ishmael and Esau.

Where it sits

Jerome (c. 347–420), the greatest Latin biblical scholar of the age and translator of the Vulgate, settled at Bethlehem in 386 and there produced, around 391–392, both the Hebrew Questions on Genesis (a philological commentary correcting the Latin from the Hebrew) and the Commentary on Micah. Nimrod appears in each: as the first tyrant in the Genesis notes on Genesis 10:8, and as the proud giant-hunter in the Micah commentary on Micah 5:6, “the land of Nimrod.” Jerome’s command of Hebrew gives his adversative reading unusual weight: he is not merely repeating Josephus but arguing from the name and the versions themselves.1

Claims & evidence

The first tyrant (Hebrew Questions on Genesis)

And Cush begot Nimrod. He began to be powerful in the land… Nimrod, the son of Chus, first seized an unprecedented tyranny among the people.

— Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Genesis 10:8. Jerome reads the biblical “he began to be a mighty one” as the beginning of tyranny: Nimrod is the first man to seize despotic power over others, and the emphasis on “unprecedented” (the thing had never been done before) matches Bede’s later gloss that “he began… because he was the first to do it.” The genealogical notice is thus made the charter of political tyranny — the same reading Chrysostom gives morally and Josephus gives narratively.2

“Descending temptation” and the hunter who opposes the Lord (Commentary on Micah)

And they will feed the land of Nimrod (which means ‘descending temptation’) in its pit; for the land of the giant and hunter and one who proudly opposes the Lord is not in the mountains, but in the pits. He has fallen like lightning from heaven, and he always dwells among beasts; and because he is a hunter, he roams through unfruitful woods and forests. As far as I can recall from my memory, I have never read favorably about a hunter. Ishmael and Esau were hunters…

— Commentary on Micah, on Micah 5:6. Here Jerome loads the whole adversative reading onto the etymology of the name and the trade of hunting. Nimrod means “descending temptation”; he “proudly opposes the Lord” — the plain hostile sense of “before the LORD”; he has “fallen like lightning from heaven” (a phrase that draws Nimrod toward the fallen adversary, as Bede’s allegory later makes explicit); and, decisively for the hunter-question, Jerome cannot recall a single hunter Scripture presents favourably. He names the other two — Ishmael and Esau — and reads all three hunters as types of the reprobate. This is the flat contradictory of Ephrem’s hunter “victorious in the battles of the Lord.”3

Notes

  • The hunter-motif made a rule. Ephrem could bless a ruler as “a mighty hunter”; Jerome makes hunting itself a mark of the reprobate, reading Nimrod, Ishmael, and Esau as a single unfavourable type. The disagreement is not over a fact but over the valence of a metaphor — and it is exactly this split that the “before vs. against the LORD” crux tracks. Jerome, with his Hebrew, throws the full weight of Latin scholarship onto the adversative side.
  • Etymology doing the work. Jerome’s “descending temptation” is a name-gloss (from the sound of the Hebrew), not a translation, and his case leans heavily on it — as the rabbinic tradition’s leans on deriving “Nimrod” from marad, “he rebelled.” Both are midrashic wordplays rather than secure philology; the reading they serve (Nimrod the rebel) may well be right, but the etymological prop is the weaker part of the argument, and is best weighed on the crux argument page rather than taken as proof.
  • Text and edition. The Genesis note is Jerome, Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Genesis 10:8; the Micah passage is Jerome, Commentary on Micah, on Micah 5:6 (English from the digitised translations of Jerome’s commentaries). Both were composed at Bethlehem c. 391–392.
  • Jerome appears on this wiki as a witness across many timelines, including Balaam and Melchizedek among the Hebrew-Scripture figures.
