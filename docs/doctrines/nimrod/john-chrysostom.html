John Chrysostom on Nimrod

Dates: c. 388 (the Homilies on Genesis preached at Antioch; John Chrysostom c. 347–407, later archbishop of Constantinople) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom’s Nimrod is the moralist’s tyrant. Preaching through Genesis at Antioch, he makes Nimrod the man who, “in imitation of his forebear,” abused his natural strength to “hit upon another form of servitude” — kingship itself — “endeavoring to become ruler and king” and building cities “with a view to ruling over the enemy.” Where Josephus gives the outward story of the Tower, Chrysostom reads the inward vice: ambition that “constantly lusts after more and clutches for glory,” the same disordered craving that, in his homily on Babel, drives the whole race to overreach and “topple into the very depths.” He stands firmly on the adversative pole of the crux, but locates Nimrod’s sin in the birth of domination rather than in open war on heaven.

Where it sits

John Chrysostom (c. 347–407), the “golden-mouthed” preacher of Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople, delivered his sixty-seven Homilies on Genesis at Antioch, most likely in the Lent and following months of 388 — a running expository course through the book. Nimrod falls in Homily 29, on Genesis 10:8–10, and the Tower of Babel that Homily fuses to him in Homily 30, on Genesis 11. As an Antiochene, Chrysostom keeps close to the plain narrative sense and turns it to moral exhortation; his Nimrod is less a figure of allegory (as he will later become in Bede) than a case-study in the disorder of ambition.1

Claims & evidence

Nimrod invents “another form of servitude” — kingship

Nimrod too, however, in his turn in imitation of his forebear did not take due advantage of his natural preeminence but hit upon another form of servitude in endeavoring to become ruler and king. You see, there would not ever be a king unless there were people being ruled. But in that case freedom is seen for what it really is, whereas slavery is the most galling obstacle to conditions of freedom, when all the more power is exercised over free people. See what ambition is guilty of. Observe bodily strength not keeping to its limits but constantly lusting after more and clutching for glory. You see, the orders [Nimrod] gave were not those of a leader. Rather, he even builds cities with a view to ruling over the enemy.

— Homilies on Genesis 29. The reading is characteristically Chrysostomic: the fault is not strength as such (a gift of nature) but strength “not keeping to its limits.” Nimrod’s crime is the invention of domination — he discovers that a man can subject free people to his own power, and Chrysostom calls this a “form of servitude” that Nimrod imposes on others. The city-building of Genesis 10:10’s kingdom (“Babel, and Erech, and Accad…”) becomes, on this reading, not civic achievement but the apparatus of conquest, raised “with a view to ruling over the enemy.” The “forebear” whom Nimrod imitates is the line of pride running back through the primeval rebels — ambition reproducing itself down the generations.2

Babel: the race that “always longs for more” and topples into the depths

Notice how the human race, instead of managing to keep to its own boundaries, always longs for more and reaches out for greater things… not being prepared to recognize the limitations of its own condition but always lusting after more, entertaining ambitions beyond its capacity… they aspire to such heights that they topple into the very depths.

— Homilies on Genesis 30, on Genesis 11:2. In the following homily Chrysostom widens Nimrod’s particular vice into the diagnosis of the whole Babel enterprise: the builders’ sin is the refusal to “keep to their own boundaries” — the same phrase he used of Nimrod’s strength that would not keep to its limits. And he presses the lesson home to his own congregation: the men who raise “splendid homes, baths, porches and avenues” so that it may be said “this is the house belonging to so-and-so” are Babel’s heirs, chasing an “undying reputation” that earns not remembrance but “unremitting accusations” — whereas the one who gives his wealth “into the hands of the poor” secures the only lasting memory. Nimrod’s tower and the rich man’s mansion are, for Chrysostom, the same sin in different stones.3

Notes

  • The adversative pole, moralized. Chrysostom reads Nimrod firmly against the LORD, but his indictment is not Josephus’s cosmic war on heaven — it is the birth of tyranny as a disorder of the soul. Ambition “lusting after more” is the engine; kingship-over-the-free is the crime; the cities and the tower are its monuments. He thus supplies the ethical content of the villain-reading that Josephus had supplied the plot for, and stands opposite Ephrem’s hero blessed “in the battles of the Lord.” See the “before vs. against the LORD” crux.
  • “Keeping to one’s boundaries.” The thread binding the two homilies is Chrysostom’s favourite theme of measure: nature assigns each creature its limit, and sin is the reach beyond it. Nimrod’s strength “not keeping to its limits” and the Babel-race that will not “keep to its own boundaries” are one and the same fault, which is why he treats the Tower as the natural sequel to the tyrant — even though, like the other early witnesses, he is supplying the link between chapters 10 and 11 that the plain text leaves open.
  • Text and edition. The English is Robert C. Hill’s translation, St. John Chrysostom: Homilies on Genesis 18–45 (Fathers of the Church 82; Washington, D.C.: Catholic University of America Press, 1990), Homilies 29 and 30, digitised at the Internet Archive. The homilies were preached at Antioch c. 388.
  • Chrysostom appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Melchizedek and Old Testament canon timelines, and as a principal of the Theophilus–Chrysostom quarrel.
