The Holy Name confraternity and feast on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1564 (the arc of institution runs c. 1274–1721) · Wikipedia
Summary: What Bernardine of Siena carried into the public square as a preacher’s devotion, the Western church turned into a standing institution: a church-approved confraternity vowed to reverence the Name and suppress blasphemy (the Dominican “Holy Name Society”), a feast of the Most Holy Name of Jesus that spread from the Franciscans to the whole Latin calendar, and the gilded IHS monogram carried on into the seal of the Jesuits. The devotion was made an order of the church’s common life — and, as at every earlier stage, its object was fixed as honouring the Name and its abuse policed, not the Name worked as a charm.

Claims & evidence

The confraternity of the Holy Name — the devotion vowed against blasphemy

The primary object of the society is to beget due love and reverence for the Holy Name of God and Jesus Christ. Its secondary object is to suppress blasphemy, perjury, oaths of any character that are forbidden, profanity, unlawful swearing, and improper language.

— “Society of the Holy Name,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 7 (1910), source. The organized devotion traces its charge to the Second Council of Lyons (1274), which prescribed a special reverence for the Holy Name in reparation for the blasphemies of the age, and to the letter Nuper in concilio that Pope Gregory X addressed on 20 September 1274 to Blessed John of Vercelli, Master General of the Dominicans, committing the promotion of that reverence to the Order of Preachers. Out of this grew the Confraternity of the Holy Name of God, founded by the Spanish Dominican Didacus of Victoria (d. 1450) expressly “to suppress the horrible profanation of the Divine Name by blasphemers,” approved and enriched with indulgences by Pope Pius IV on 13 April 1564, and reserved to canonical erection by the Dominicans alone in the motu proprio “Decet Romanum Pontificem” of Pope Pius V, 21 June 1571; the confraternity of the Holy Name of God was afterwards merged with the Society of the Holy Name of Jesus and bore both titles.1 Here the note the whole timeline sounds is made a rule of a society: the Name is honoured, and the mouth that would profane it corrected — the same boundary the fathers drew, now written into the by-laws of a lay sodality.

The Feast of the Most Holy Name of Jesus — from the Franciscans to the universal calendar

Pope Clement VII in 1530 allowed the Franciscans to celebrate a feast day in honor of the Holy Name… Pope Innocent XIII extended this to the universal Church in 1721.

— “The Feast of the Holy Name of Jesus,” source. The devotion Bernardine preached received its own place on the church’s calendar. An Office and Mass of the Holy Name — the Franciscan Bernardino de’ Busti’s work — were granted to the Friars Minor by Clement VII in 1530, and the feast spread piecemeal, kept on different days by different orders and dioceses (14 or 15 January by the Franciscans and Dominicans, 7 August in the old English uses of Sarum, York, and Durham). It was made a feast of the whole Latin Church by Innocent XIII in 1721, inserted into the General Roman Calendar by his decree of 20 December of that year.2 So the Name that Diadochos held in the praying heart and Bernardine raised on a tablet became, in the West, an annual solemnity of the Name kept by the entire church.

The IHS monogram carried on — from Bernardine’s tablet to the seal of the Jesuits

St. Ignatius of Loyola adopted the monogram in his seal as general of the Society of Jesus (1541), and thus it became the emblem of his institute.

— “IHS,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 7 (1910), source. The gilded IHS in a sunburst that Bernardine had raised over the cities of Italy did not stop with him. The letters — a transliteration of the first three of ΙΗΣΌΥΣ (IHSOUS, “Jesus,” in Greek capitals), not a Latin acrostic — had grown into a recognized emblem of the Holy Name across the later Middle Ages (worn by Blessed John Colombini and made the sign of Vincent Ferrer and Bernardine alike). Ignatius of Loyola took the monogram, set in its rays and marked with a cross and the three nails, as the seal of the Society of Jesus he founded in 1540 (its rule confirmed by Paul III’s bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiæ, 27 September 1540), and through the worldwide Jesuit missions Bernardine’s emblem of the Name travelled further than his preaching ever had.3

Notes

  • Why this sits after Bernardine. The Dominican charge of 1274 is older than Bernardine (c. 1420), but the organized, church-approved devotion — the confraternity as a standing society, the feast on the universal calendar, the monogram as the badge of a religious order — is the later development his civic preaching fed into, so the block is seated at the mid-sixteenth-century crest of that institution (Pius IV’s approbation, 1564) rather than at the medieval root it looks back to.
  • The affective root. Behind the whole Western devotion stands Bernard of Clairvaux’s sweetness of the Name (“honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart”) and the hymn Jesu dulcis memoria — the lyric strain the confraternity, the feast, and the monogram all institutionalize.
  • The boundary held here too. Making the devotion an institution did not dissolve the doctrine’s recurring tension — where honouring the Name ends and treating it as a talisman begins. The confraternity’s own charter fixes its object as reverence and reparation, not the working of the Name as a charm; the line is the one weighed on the argument apotropaic devotion and the talisman.
  • The Western institutionalization stands beside the Eastern lay reception the next witness records — The Way of a Pilgrim (c. 1860), which carried the Jesus Prayer to a wide readership — the two traditions arriving, by very different routes, at a Name kept and honoured in the common life of the faithful.
