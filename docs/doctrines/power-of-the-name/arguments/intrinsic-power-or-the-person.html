Is the Name’s power intrinsic to the words, or is it the power of the Person invoked?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the efficacy the fathers ascribe to the Name is the power of the Person invoked, laid hold of by faith and relation to him, not a force lodged in the sounds or letters of the word as such. That is the reading the New Testament states outright (the lame man is healed “through faith in his name”; the sons of Sceva, who have the word but not the Lord, are routed) and the reading the fathers press wherever they draw the line against magic (the Name of Jesus crucified, invoked in faith and not as an incantation; “we are not the doers of these things, but it is Christ who worketh them”). The rival — that a real, natural power resides in the very sounds of the divine names (Origen’s name-realism) — fastens on a genuine datum (the felt untranslatability of the holy names, the Name availing even an unworthy user) and is a coherent account of the mechanism; but as a mechanism it is overstated, and it is weighed on its own pages (“the power of names,” contested; the modest name-realism, plausible). · the person-reading advanced by Justin, Irenaeus, and Athanasius; the name-realism by Origen.

Related arguments: the Name and magic — see also the sons of Sceva (Acts 19:13–17): warrant or rebuke?

The claim

This is the doctrine’s core interpretive crux and the hinge that separates Christian devotion from magic. Grant that the Name has power — that demons flee it, the sick are healed by it, prayer is answered in it. The question is where the power sits. On one reading it is a property of the word itself — its very sounds and letters, fitted “by nature” to their object, so that to pronounce the name rightly is to release a force lodged in it (name-realism). On the other it is wholly the power of the One named: the Name is effectual only as the invocation of Christ, laid hold of in faith and in relation to him, and the word apart from the Lord is an empty sound. The page advocates the second reading — the efficacy is the Person’s, not the phonemes’ — and treats name-realism as a coherent but overstated account of the mechanism, weighed in full on its own pages.

And his name through faith in his name hath made this man strong, whom ye see and know: yea, the faith which is by him hath given him this perfect soundness in the presence of you all.

— Acts 3:16 (KJV), historicalchristian.faith. Peter states the mechanism himself: the Name heals through faith in the Name — the power is the living Christ’s, received by faith, not a virtue in the syllables.1

Who advances it

The person-reading is the mainstream and the older layer: it is the plain sense of the apostolic narrative and the explicit teaching of the fathers who define the power of the Name over against sorcery — Justin, who insists it is the name of Jesus crucified under Pontius Pilate that subdues the demon; Irenaeus, who says the church works “not… by incantations” but by “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ”; Athanasius, who makes the Name a sign of the living Christ and has Antony heal “not by commanding, but by prayer and speaking the name of Christ.” The rival name-realism is genuinely advanced — above all by Origen, who argues at length that names are natural, not conventional, and that the Hebrew divine names carry a power in their very sounds that translation destroys. That doctrine is real and is not a distortion foisted on him; it is weighed on its own two pages, and this argument does not re-litigate it but asks the narrower question it raises: is the power the fathers report a property of the word, or of the One named?

The evidence for — the Person, not the phonemes

Scripture lodges the power in the referent and its reception by faith. The lame man at the temple gate is healed “through faith in his name” (Acts 3:16); Peter presses the Name to its farthest reach — “there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” — but the Name saves as the name of a Person, the crucified and risen Jesus whom he has just preached.1 And the two hard warnings the doctrine everywhere carries are precisely warnings against detaching the word from the Lord. The seven sons of Sceva have the formula exact — “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — and the spirit turns on them: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The correct sounds, in the mouth of men with no relation to the One named, avail nothing; they flee “naked and wounded.” And the Lord’s own warning is sharper still: “Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord… in thy name have [we not] cast out devils?… And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you.” Even true works “in thy name” do not avail the one the Lord does not know — which is unintelligible if the power were in the word, and exactly right if it is in the Person and the relation to him.2

The fathers anchor the power in the crucified Christ, invoked in faith, not in an incantation. The witnesses who theorize the power of the Name define it against magic by locating it in the Person. Justin’s whole argument turns on the content of the Name: it is “Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate” — a particular person with a particular history — that subdues the demon, where the bare names of “kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs” do not, and where the exorcists who trade in “fumigations and incantations” are working sorcery, not wielding the Name. Irenaeus draws the same line in a single breath: the church casts out demons “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art,” but by prayer and “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” — and it is this Name, “but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates,” that heals, the difference being not the phonetics but who is named.3

Athanasius makes the point explicit: the Name works because the One named is alive and is God. For Athanasius the Name’s power is a sign pointing to its bearer: “actions, and power over men, belong to the living only,” so the fact that demons flee “on barely hearing” the Name proves that the One named is not dead but risen — “and especially… God.” The efficacy is evidence of the Person, not a property of the syllable. And in the Life of Antony he states the mechanism flatly: “We are not the doers of these things, but it is Christ who worketh them”; Antony “healed not by commanding, but by prayer and speaking the name of Christ.” The word is the occasion; Christ is the agent.4 The same holds for the whole devotional tradition that follows — Diadochos grounds the invocation “Lord Jesus” in the Spirit (“no one can say ‘Lord Jesus’ except in the Holy Spirit”), not in the technique — so that the Jesus Prayer is the presence of the Person, not the working of a formula.

The name-realist counter-case, and where it has a point

The rival reading is not empty, and honesty requires stating its strongest form. Three data pull toward locating some power in the word itself. (1) The felt untranslatability of the holy names. Origen’s theory begins from a real phenomenon: the Hebrew divine names were held too holy to translate, and the tradition felt that rendering them into Greek lost something — “the God of Abraham… Isaac… Jacob” makes “even demons… vanquished,” yet in translation “the mention of the name is attended with no result.” If the power were purely the referent’s, why should the language of the name matter?5 (2) The Name avails even an unworthy user. Origen presses that the name of Jesus is effectual “even… pronounced… by bad men,” and the Lord himself refuses to forbid the stranger “casting out devils in thy name” though he “followeth not us” — “there is no man which shall do a miracle in my name, that can lightly speak evil of me.” The Name seems to work independently of the speaker’s standing, which looks like a power in the word.6 (3) The OT reverence for the Tetragrammaton treats the Name as bearing a weight its substitutes do not carry. Taken together these make name-realism a coherent account of the mechanism — and, in its modest form (that revealed names carry a God-given, non-arbitrary force), one the wiki rates plausible.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The person-reading is the one the sources state in their own voice. Peter names the mechanism — healing “through faith in his name”; the sons of Sceva and Matthew 7:22–23 establish that the correct word without relation to the Lord is inert or worse; and every father who defines the Name against magic locates its power in who is invoked — the crucified Jesus, named in faith — not in the sounds. Athanasius says it outright (“it is Christ who worketh them”), and even the tradition that most exalts the Name in devotion grounds it in the Spirit and the Person, never in a formula. On the central Christian datum — that the Name is not a spell severable from faith and belonging — the phonemic reading simply cannot be the whole account.

What is overstated in the name-realist case — and what survives. The three data do not carry the weight the strong realist reading puts on them. The untranslatability is as well explained by reverence and long usage as by an intrinsic natural power — a point pressed by Gregory of Nyssa’s conventionalism (rated sound), which holds that words are human institutions and cuts against strong name-realism. That the Name avails an unworthy user tells against phonemic magic rather than for it: the strange exorcist and the “bad men” of Matthew 7 are precisely those who have no merit of their own, so the power that works through them cannot be theirs — it is the Person’s, acting on his own name wherever it is truly named, which is why the same Christ who honours the stranger’s invocation disowns the false worker (“I never knew you”). Crucially, even Origen’s realism is Christologically anchored: the power is not any sound but the name of Jesus, and the divine names vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts — so his name-realism is an account of how the Person’s power is mediated, not a rival that displaces the Person. Read that way, the realist datum folds into the person-reading as a claim about mechanism (some revealed names may carry a given, non-arbitrary force), overstated only when it lets the word float free of the One named — the exact drift that shades into magic, and the reason the charge that Origen made names a magical art is itself contested, not sound.

Assessment

sound. The efficacy the fathers ascribe to the Name is the power of the Person invoked, laid hold of by faith and relation to him — not a force resident in the sounds or letters of the word. Scripture states it (Acts 3:16, healing “through faith in his name”; the sons of Sceva and Matthew 7:22–23, the word without the Lord availing nothing), and the fathers who theorize the Name’s power confirm it wherever they draw the line against sorcery (the name of Jesus crucified, invoked in faith and not as an incantation; “it is Christ who worketh them”). Name-realism — a real, natural power in the very sounds of the divine names — is a coherent account of the mechanism and rests on a genuine datum (the felt untranslatability of the holy names, the Name availing an unworthy user); but as a mechanism it is overstated, better explained by reverence and usage, and even in its most serious proponent stays anchored to the name of Jesus rather than floating free of him. Held in its modest form it survives as plausible and folds into the person-reading; pressed to the strong form that lodges the power in the phonemes, it fails — and it is precisely that failure the whole tradition polices as the boundary against magic. (The name-realism itself is weighed on its own pages: the modest form, plausible, and the charge that Origen made names a magical art, contested.)
