Which form of the Name? Is the power in the Hebrew “Yeshua,” or the Person named in any tongue?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the efficacy of “the name of Jesus” does not attach to a particular linguistic form. The claim of the modern Sacred Name Movement — that only the Hebrew Yeshua / Yehoshua (or the coined “Yahshua”) truly carries the power, so that Greek Iēsous and English “Jesus” are defective or even blasphemous — is refuted by the inspired Scripture that states it: the New Testament renders the name in Greek without apology and works the very exorcisms in that form (Acts 3:6; 16:18), sets “the name of Jesus” (Greek Iēsous) at the summit of the cosmos before “every tongue” (Philippians 2:9–11), and carries Joel’s “call on the name of the LORD” onto the Person across languages (Romans 10:13). This is the sharp case of the mechanism question next door (is the power the words’ or the Person’s? — sound: the Person’s): the sons of Sceva had the correct sounds and were routed, and by the same logic the correct Hebrew sounds in a faithless mouth avail nothing while the name in any tongue, laid hold of in faith, avails everything. What survives is only the modest point — that the name’s God-given meaning (“YHWH saves,” Matthew 1:21) and the reverence due it are real — and that folds into the person-reading rather than establishing a sacred phoneme. The strong form also collapses on its own terms: its flagship “Yahshua” is a modern coinage attested in no Hebrew source.

The claim

A reader of this timeline asks a live question the pages so far answer only by implication: which name of Jesus is meant — his name as it was actually spoken in his own tongue? Behind it stands a genuinely-advocated position. The modern Sacred Name Movement holds that the power — and, in its stronger voices, that salvation itself — attaches to the Hebrew form of the name: only Yahweh for the Father and only Yahshua for the Son, so that to say “God” or “Jesus” is at best a loss and at worst blasphemy. The argument runs from an etymology: the angel commands, “thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21) — and the Hebrew behind that name, Yehoshua / Yeshua, means “YHWH saves.” If the name is a revealed compound of the divine Name and salvation, the movement urges, then the Greek Iēsous and the English “Jesus” have buried the very Name the son was given to bear.1

And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins.

— Matthew 1:21 (KJV), historicalchristian.faith. The etymology is real: the name is not an arbitrary label but a God-given word that means “YHWH saves.” The question this page weighs is whether that makes a particular set of phonemes load-bearing — whether the power is in the Hebrew word, or in the Person the word names, laid hold of in any tongue by faith.1

Who advances it

The form-matters position is the doctrine of the twentieth-century Sacred Name Movement, which grew out of the Church of God (Seventh Day) in the 1930s. Clarence O. Dodd, moved by Proverbs 30:4 (“what is his name, and what is his son’s name?”), began the first Sacred Name magazine, The Faith, in 1937 and was rebaptized “in the name of Yahshua” in 1941; his associate Angelo B. Traina produced the first Sacred Name Bibles — the Sacred Name New Testament (1950) and the complete Holy Name Bible (1963), a revision of the King James Version that replaces “God” with Elohim, “LORD” with Yahweh, and “Jesus” with Yahshua throughout. Its stronger heirs press the claim to exclusivity: any other name is illegitimate, and some circulate the “Ea-Zeus” etymology — that “Jesus” smuggles the name of the pagan god Zeus into the New Testament.2 At the father level the one real pressure toward the form mattering is Origen’s name-realism — the argument that the Hebrew divine names are “possessed of great power” and that translating them into Greek leaves “the mention of the name… attended with no result.” That doctrine is weighed in full on its own pages, and the mechanism argument next door adjudicates it (sound: the power is the Person’s, name-realism a coherent but overstated account); this page does not re-litigate it but asks the narrower, sharper question it raises about the Jesus form.3

The evidence for — and where its point is real

Honesty requires the strongest form of the form-matters case. (1) The name is revealed, not arbitrary. Matthew 1:21 does not report a naming choice but a command with a reason: the son is to be called “YHWH saves” because he will save. The meaning is given by God, and it is the Hebrew that carries it — the Greek Iēsous and English “Jesus” preserve the sound but bury the sense. (2) The one father-level datum. Origen, the fullest patristic theorist of the Name, held that the holy Hebrew names carry a force lodged in their very sounds, lost in translation. If that is right for “the God of Abraham,” the same logic would privilege the Hebrew of the Son’s name over its Greek rendering. (3) The reverence for the divine Name. The Old Testament treats the Tetragrammaton as bearing a weight its substitutes do not carry, and Joel’s promise is that “whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD [YHWH] shall be delivered” (Joel 2:32) — so if the Son’s name is built from that Name, recovering the Hebrew looks like recovering the divine Name the apostles say saves.4 The real point buried here — that the name’s God-given meaning and the reverence due it matter — is sound, and the assessment keeps it. What it does not establish is that the phonemes are the locus of the power.

The evidence against — the inspired Scripture is Greek

The New Testament itself renders the name in Greek, and works the Name in that form. This is decisive and internal to the claim. The apostles, native speakers of Aramaic who knew the Hebrew name and used it in daily life, nonetheless wrote the New Testament in Greek and put the Greek Iēsous on every page — and Luke reports the apostolic exorcisms performed in exactly that form. Peter heals the lame man “in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth”; Paul turns on the spirit in the slave-girl, “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.”5 If a particular linguistic form were load-bearing, the Holy Spirit chose the wrong one in inspiring a Greek New Testament that names the Saviour in Greek throughout. The Sacred Name claim is refuted by the very Scripture from which it draws its one proof-text.

The apostolic move is to carry the divine-Name promise across languages onto the Person, not to guard a phoneme. When Peter at Pentecost and Paul to the Romans take up Joel’s oracle — “whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved” — they apply the Hebrew Name of the LORD (YHWH), without apology, to the Greek-named Jesus (Acts 2:21; Romans 10:13). The whole point of the citation is that the promise made to the divine Name now rests on the Person of the Son, reachable in the language of the hearer; it is the opposite of insisting the hearer first recover the Hebrew. And the Philippian hymn sets “the name of Jesus” (Greek Iēsous) at the summit of the cosmos precisely so that “every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord” — universality across languages, not a single sacred sound.6

The mechanism question is already settled, and it transposes exactly. The argument next door establishes (sound) that the power is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith and relation to him, not a force in the sounds — the sons of Sceva had the formula exact and were routed “naked and wounded,” because they had the word but not the Lord. Transpose that to the form-question and it answers it: the correct Hebrew sounds in a faithless mouth would avail nothing, and the name in Greek, Latin, or English — laid hold of in faith — avails everything, exactly as the apostles found. A power that a stranger’s Greek releases and a Hebrew adept’s exactness cannot is manifestly not lodged in the phonemes.7

The strong form collapses on its own terms. The movement cannot even fix the sacred form it says is salvific: its flagship “Yahshua” is a modern coinage with no attestation in any Hebrew source before the twentieth century, and it is linguistically impossible — it requires the waw to fall silent, which Hebrew does not permit. The Hebrew scholar Michael L. Brown, a Ph.D. in Semitic languages, states flatly that there is “absolutely no support for this pronunciation—none at all”; the genuine forms are Yeshua and its longer Yehoshua (the same word the Septuagint renders Iēsous when it names Joshua). A doctrine that the exact Hebrew phonemes carry the power cannot say which phonemes, and its best-known candidate is one no Hebrew ever spoke. The kindred “Jesus = Zeus” etymology is a plain error of the same kind: Iēsous is the ordinary Greek transliteration of Yeshua, not a pagan importation.8

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The name’s meaning is God-given and worth reverencing: Matthew 1:21 grounds the name in “YHWH saves,” and the whole tradition rightly treats the Name as holy, not as a bare label. That modest point — that the meaning and the reverence matter — survives every objection.

What fails. The load-bearing claim — that the power or salvation attaches to a particular linguistic form, and the Hebrew alone truly carries it — does not hold on its best available case. It is refuted by the inspired Greek New Testament, which names and wields the Saviour in Greek; it is the opposite of the apostolic move that carries the divine-Name promise across tongues onto the Person; it folds under the already-settled mechanism (the power is the Person’s, not the phonemes’, as the sons of Sceva show); and it collapses on its own inability to fix or attest its sacred form. Origen’s name-realism — the one father-level pressure the other way — is itself only plausible in its modest form and, even in Origen, stays anchored to the name of Jesus rather than floating free of him; it will not bear the exclusivist weight the Sacred Name claim puts on it. Note the boundary this respects: to say the power is not in the phonemes is not to say the name may be altered or its meaning slighted — it is to say the same thing the whole doctrine says against magic, that the Name is the invocation of a Person and not a formula whose exact sounds compel.

Assessment

weak. The efficacy of “the name of Jesus” does not attach to a particular linguistic form. The strong Sacred-Name claim — Hebrew-only, and in its coined shape “Yahshua”-only — is refuted by the inspired Scripture it appeals to: the New Testament renders the Saviour’s name in Greek and performs the apostolic exorcisms in that form (Acts 3:6; 16:18), exalts “the name of Jesus” (Greek Iēsous) before “every tongue” (Philippians 2:9–11), and applies Joel’s “call on the name of the LORD” to the Person across languages (Acts 2:21; Romans 10:13). The mechanism argument next door already locates the power in the Person laid hold of by faith, not in the sounds — and the sons of Sceva, correct-worded and routed, transpose the point onto the form-question. The claim also collapses on its own terms, unable to fix or attest the sacred form it says is salvific (“Yahshua” is a modern, linguistically impossible coinage; the “Jesus = Zeus” etymology a plain error). Not weak because reverence for the name is misplaced — that modest point (the name’s meaning is God-given, Matthew 1:21, and the Name is holy) is real and folds into the person-reading — but because the load-bearing thesis, that the power lives in the Hebrew phonemes, does not hold. This is the sharp case of the intrinsic-power question, and the answer is the same: the power is the One named’s, reachable in any tongue by faith.
