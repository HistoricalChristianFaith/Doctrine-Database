Is the Name of God God himself? The onomatodoxy (imiaslavie) thesis

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the early-twentieth-century Russian imiaslavie (“Name-glorifying”) thesis that in the Jesus Prayer the Name of God is God himself is the maximal form of this whole doctrine, and it splits along its own wording. In its crude form — that the very letters and sounds of the word are divine, so that the Name works of itself — it collapses into exactly the phonemic name-magic the tradition polices, and is weak (see the intrinsic-power argument). But in the refined form its best proponents actually advocated — the Name as a locus of the uncreated energies (a Palamite grounding), so that “the Name is God” means God is truly present and operative in his Name, not that a created word is the divine essence — it is a coherent, seriously-defended, and never-conciliarly-resolved reading. Condemned as pantheistic by the Russian Holy Synod and the Ecumenical Patriarchate in 1913 (on a procedure its critics grant was defective — the proponents were never interviewed), it was defended by Florensky, Bulgakov, and Losev, left open when the 1917–18 Moscow Council was aborted by the Revolution, and is called “an open question to this day” by its leading modern historian. That is the textbook profile of a contested reading: strong by the lights of mystical/Palamite theology, weak by the lights of the critics who read the bare formula as divinizing a word.

The claim

This is the sharpest thesis the power of the Name ever generated, and the doctrine’s most extreme test case — exactly the point at which reverence for the Name risks becoming what critics call magic. The tradition traced on this timeline had, by the nineteenth century, made the continual invocation of the name of Jesus (“Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me”) the heart of a whole spirituality, carried to the laity by The Way of a Pilgrim. Around 1907 a Russian schema-monk of the Caucasus, Hilarion (Domrachev), drew from that practice of the Jesus Prayer a dogmatic conclusion in his book On the Mountains of the Caucasus: that the felt presence of God in the invoked Name is no metaphor — the Name of God is God himself. The book spread rapidly through the Russian monasteries and above all the Russian sketes of Mount Athos, where the schism it caused (the “Name-glorifiers,” imiaslavtsy) peaked in 1912–1913. The question this page weighs is whether the thesis is a legitimate development of the power of the Name — the Jesus Prayer’s own theology drawn out — or a lapse into name-magic and pantheism.1

(a) the name of God is energy of God, inseparable from the essence of God itself, and therefore is God himself; (b) however, God is distinct from His energies and from His name, and that is why God is not His name or a name in general.

— the philosopher Aleksei Losev’s formulation of the Name-glorifier position, which states its refined form exactly: the identity runs through the energies, not the essence, so that the Name is God-present-and-acting, while God is not reducible to the word.2

Who advances it

The thesis was first put in dogmatic form by Hilarion (Domrachev) (On the Mountains of the Caucasus, 1907) and given its systematic defence by the hieroschemamonk Antony Bulatovich, whose Apology of Faith in the Name of God and in the Name of Jesus became the movement’s central text; Bulatovich argued that his teaching simply drew out the Fathers, above all Gregory Palamas on the uncreated divine energies. After the 1913 condemnations it was taken up, in more careful philosophical form, by three of the leading Russian religious thinkers of the century — Pavel Florensky, Sergei Bulgakov (both of whom advocated for it at the 1917–18 Moscow Council), and Aleksei Losev, whose “absolute symbolism” treated the holy name as a verbal icon that mediates the divine presence as a painted icon does.3 Against it stood the Russian Holy Synod and the Ecumenical Patriarchate (1913), and among individual theologians the future Metropolitan Antony (Khrapovitsky), who denounced imiaslavie as a heresy akin to the ecstatic Khlyst sect.4

The steelman — the Name as a locus of the divine energies

The identity is asserted of the energies, not the essence. The strong form of the Name-glorifier case is not that the phonemes or letters of a word are the divine nature — that would be the crude reading its own best proponents reject. It is the Palamite move applied to the Name: God’s unknowable essence is one thing; his uncreated energies, in which the creature really participates, are another; and both are truly God. The Name of God, invoked in prayer, is a bearer of those energies — a place where God is genuinely present and operative — and so, in the same qualified sense in which the Taboric light or grace is God, the Name is God. Losev’s two-part formula holds the paradox together: the Name is God (through the energy), and God is not the Name (he transcends every energy and every word). Read this way, the thesis is the direct heir of Gregory Palamas’s distinction between essence and energies — the very distinction that had already served as the theological charter of the Jesus Prayer.2

It draws out the Jesus Prayer’s own logic. The whole devotional tradition on this timeline had insisted that to invoke the Name is to lay hold of the One named — that the continual “Lord Jesus” is the presence of Christ in the heart, not the working of a formula. Hilarion’s thesis is that experience raised to a dogma: the hesychast’s felt union with God in the invoked Name is real union with God, because God does not stand apart from the Name by which he gives himself. On this reading imiaslavie is not a novelty but the Jesus-Prayer spirituality made explicit — the reason its proponents could plausibly claim the Fathers and the Philokalic tradition for it, and the reason the whole controversy revived Russian interest in Palamas and hesychasm in the first place.5

The condemnation was procedurally weak and never conciliar. The 1913 verdicts were reached, its critics concede, “without ever interviewing its supporters,” and were enforced by the state: in June 1913 the Imperial Russian Navy vessel Tsar carried Archbishop Nikon (Rozhdestvensky), a professor of theology, and some 120 soldiers to Athos to remove the Name-glorifiers by force. No Ecumenical Council ever ruled on the matter; the Russian Sobor of 1917–18 placed it on its agenda with advocates and opponents both present, but was aborted by the Revolution before it could decide. Its leading modern historian, Metropolitan Hilarion (Alfeyev), concludes that “the Church’s final assessment of Name-worshipping remains an open question to this day.”6

The case against — pantheism and magical automatism

The Synod’s charge is not empty, and honesty requires stating its strongest form. (1) It risks divinizing a created word. Whatever the refined formula intends, the bare slogan “the Name of God is God himself” predicates deity of a human, created, translatable word — and the leap from “God is present in the Name” to “the Name is God” is precisely the equivocation the critics call pantheism: it folds a created sign into the uncreated. (2) The Palamite grounding may prove less than it claims. Opponents pressed that Palamas nowhere calls the energies “God” (Theos) but “Deity” (theotēs) — so even granting the Name is an energy, one does not straightforwardly reach “the Name is God himself.” (This objection is itself contested by recent Palamas scholarship, which finds Palamas applying the word “God” to the energies too — so the lexical rule the critics leaned on is not as clean as they supposed.) (3) It slides toward magical automatism. If the Name is God, then pronouncing it is handling the divine directly — and the reverence owed to God attaches to the syllable, which is the exact drift, policed all down this timeline, from the power of the Name into the presumption that a rightly-spoken word works of itself. The sons of Sceva and “I never knew you” are the standing warning that the word without faith and relation to the Lord avails nothing — a warning the crude form of the thesis cannot easily honour.7

Adversarial assessment

Where it holds. Taken in the refined, energies-mediated form its serious proponents actually advanced, the thesis is coherent and stands within a recognized Orthodox framework: it says no more than that God gives himself really in the Name he reveals, which is the Palamite theology already underwriting the Jesus Prayer. Losev’s formula guards the transcendence the critics feared for — God is not the Name — while affirming the presence the hesychasts reported. It has never been condemned by an Ecumenical Council, it was defended by first-rank theologians, the process that condemned it was admittedly one-sided and state-enforced, and its status was left formally open. On the evidence, the refined reading cannot be dismissed as mere heresy.

Where it fails, and where the line runs. The bare slogan, unqualified, does predicate deity of a created word, and if pressed to mean that the sounds or letters themselves are divine and effectual of themselves, it is the phonemic name-magic the whole tradition rejects — and on that reading it is weak, for the same reasons strong name-realism fails: the power the sources report is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith, and the word apart from the Lord is inert (the sons of Sceva; Matthew 7:22–23). The two readings are not equally defensible, and the assessment must not launder the crude form upward on the refined form’s credit. But neither can it flatten the refined form down to the crude one: the movement’s best minds were making the energies-move, not the phoneme-move, and that move is a live theological option that the church has never adjudicated. The honest verdict is that the interpretation genuinely divides — sound-adjacent by the lights of Palamite mystical theology, weak by the lights of those who read the formula as divinizing a word — with no authoritative resolution and serious advocates on both sides. That is contested, not sound and not weak.

Assessment

contested. The imiaslavie thesis that “the Name of God is God himself” is the maximal form of the power of the Name, and it divides along its own wording. In its refined form — the Name as a bearer of the uncreated divine energies (a Palamite grounding), so that God is truly present and operative in his Name while remaining beyond it (Losev: the Name is God through the energy, yet “God is not His name”) — it is a coherent, seriously-defended reading, condemned in 1913 on an admittedly one-sided procedure, never ruled on by an Ecumenical Council, left open when the 1917–18 Moscow Council was aborted, and called “an open question to this day” by its leading modern historian. In its crude form — that the created word, its very sounds and letters, is itself divine and works of itself — it collapses into the phonemic name-magic the whole tradition polices and is weak (the power is the Person’s, not the word’s: the sons of Sceva; Matthew 7:22–23). Because the interpretation is genuinely disputed, strong by one discipline’s lights and weak by another’s, with real advocates on both sides and no authoritative verdict, it is rated contested — the doctrine’s sharpest test of where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins. (The narrower question of where the power sits — the Person, not the phonemes — is weighed as sound on the intrinsic-power argument, of which this is the extreme case.)
