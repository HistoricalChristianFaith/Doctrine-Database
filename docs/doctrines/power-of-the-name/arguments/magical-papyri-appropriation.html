What do the Greek Magical Papyri’s uses of the name of Jesus show about Christian invocation of the Name?

Disputed reading · on The Power of the Name · witness: the Papyri Graecae Magicae

Related readings: What tradition did the Jewish exorcists of Acts 19:13–16 stand in? · What did canon 35 of the Council of Laodicea forbid?

The passage

The charm of Pibechis, headed “A tested charm of Pibechis for those possessed by daimons,” in the Great Paris codex:

I conjure you by the god of the Hebrews, Jesus, IABA IAE ABRAOTH AIA THOTH ELE ELO AEO IIIBAECH ABARMAS IABARAOU ABELBEL LONA ABRA MAROIA BRAKION, who appears in fire, who is in the midst of land, snow, and fog, TANNETIS…

— PGM IV.3019–3020 (Betz trans.).1

A bilingual exorcism in the same codex:

Hail, God of Abraham; hail, God of Isaac; hail, God of Jacob; Jesus Chrestos, the Holy Spirit, the Son of the Father, who is above the Seven, who is within the Seven. Bring Iao Sabaoth; may your power issue forth from him, NN, until you drive away this unclean daimon Satan, who is in him.

— PGM IV.1231–1234ff (Betz trans.).2

The question

The working handbooks of Greco-Egyptian ritual specialists use the Hebrew divine names and the name of Jesus among their words of power. Whether this shows Christian invocation of the Name to be of one kind with ancient name-magic, or a borrowing of the Name into a technique distinct from Christian invocation, has been disputed since the second century, and bears on how the early Christian claims about the power of the Name are read.

Background: the record

Clinton E. Arnold’s survey of the papyri states that “Egyptian sorcerers had great respect for the names of the supernatural powers in Judaism. References to Iao (for YHWH), Sabaoth, Adonai, Moses, Solomon, Michael, Gabriel, angels, archangels, cherubim and seraphim abound in the texts.”3 Arnold reports W. M. Brashear’s judgement that the repute of Jewish magicians exceeded even that of the Egyptian sorcerers, and P. S. Alexander’s that “magic flourished among the Jews despite strong and persistent condemnation by the religious authority”; Alexander lists more than a dozen texts of the corpus as probably composed by a Jew, the most noteworthy being the charm of Pibechis quoted above.4 The Pibechis charm also adjures the demon “by the great god Sabaoth, through whom the Jordan River drew back,” and is worked over a boiled compound of olive oil, mastic and lotus, with a tin lamella hung on the patient. The Great Paris codex (PGM IV) dates to the early fourth century, but Arnold holds that it preserves magical traditions from much earlier sources, and that magic was widely practised for centuries before the time of Jesus and the apostles, with several papyri datable to the first century B.C./A.D.5

Christian invocation of the Name is of one kind with ancient name-magic

On this reading the Christians’ power over spirits and the magicians’ power-words belong to the same practice, and the papyri show Jesus himself understood in those terms.

Held by: Celsus (pagan philosopher, True Word, 2nd c.), as quoted by Origen; Morton Smith (historian, Jesus the Magician, 1978), as reported by Clinton E. Arnold.6

Celsus, as Origen reports him, “asserts that it is by the names of certain demons, and by the use of incantations, that the Christians appear to be possessed of (miraculous) power,” and charges the Saviour himself with accomplishing his wonders “by means of sorcery.” The papyri supply the documentary record for this classification. The name of Jesus stands in the Pibechis charm in a string of voces magicae, as one more word of power beside IABA and ABARMAS, and “the god of the Hebrews” is its epithet. The bilingual exorcism hails the patriarchs’ God, “Jesus Chrestos, the Holy Spirit, the Son of the Father,” and Iao Sabaoth in one formula of command. Operators of every background reached for these names: Jewish composers wrote some of the most Jewish spells in the corpus, and Greco-Egyptian specialists copied and recopied them. The name of Jesus had a reputation for power over demons among those who were not his followers, and the magicians copied the recipes in which it stood because they held them to work. Smith, by Arnold’s account, made extensive use of the magical papyri to develop the thesis that Jesus’ contemporaries understood him to be a magician. On this reading the papyri place the name of Jesus, and the practice of exorcism in his name, inside the same world of adjuration, power-names and compelled spirits in which Celsus classed them.

The papyri borrow the Name into a technique distinct from Christian invocation

On this reading the magicians borrowed the Name as a reputed power-word, while Christian invocation rests on the Person named and on prayer, not on a formula.

Held by: Irenaeus of Lyons (Against Heresies II.32.5, c. 180); Origen (Against Celsus I.6, c. 248); Clinton E. Arnold (New Testament scholar, Dictionary of New Testament Background, 2000).7

Irenaeus describes the church as working “nor… anything by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art; but, directing her prayers to the Lord… and calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.”8 Origen answers Celsus that “it is not by incantations that Christians seem to prevail (over evil spirits), but by the name of Jesus, accompanied by the announcement of the narratives which relate to Him,” and that the Name has been effectual “when it was pronounced even by bad men,” as Jesus taught in Matthew 7:22.9 On this reading the efficacy belongs to the One named, and the Name is bound to the story of his life and death, not to its syllables. The papyri use the Name in the other mode: compulsive verbs, the planetary “Seven,” manufactured syllables and materia magica, the name of Jesus set between IABA and ABARMAS. Luke narrates the same borrowing at Ephesus, where the sons of Sceva adjure a spirit “by Jesus whom Paul preacheth,” are overpowered, and the converts then burn their books of “curious arts.”10 Arnold stresses “the essential Jewishness of Jesus’ teaching and the many dissimilarities of method and worldview between Jesus and contemporary magicians.”11 On this reading the papyri document the reputation the Name had acquired and the milieu it entered, and the distinction between invocation and incantation that Irenaeus and Origen drew.
