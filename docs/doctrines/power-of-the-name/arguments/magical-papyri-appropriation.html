The Name in the Greek Magical Papyri — the divine names, and Jesus, as voces magicae

Type: argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the boundary the whole doctrine polices, seen from the far side of the line. The Greco-Egyptian magical handbooks (the Papyri Graecae Magicae) folded the Hebrew divine names — Iao, Sabaoth, Adonai, Abrasax — and even Jesus into their spells, wielding the Name as a severable formula to be piled up with a hundred other power-words. That this happened is a matter of near-consensus history and is sound as such. The interpretation weighed here — that this appropriation is exactly the “name as spell in any mouth” Irenaeus (“not… by incantations”) and Origen (effectual by the Person, not the bare sounds) said cannot avail — also holds: read critically, the magicians’ own record confirms the fathers’ boundary rather than breaching it. The papyri show that outsiders believed the Name potent and borrowed it; they do not show that it availed them, and the New Testament’s own verdict on this milieu — the sons of Sceva, heirs of exactly this apparatus — is that the borrowed Name turns on the borrower.

This page weighs the ancient magical handbooks as historical evidence — the documentary record of the name-magic the fathers set the power of the Name against. Their spells, adjurations, and amulets are examined critically, as the practice to be weighed, and never as instruction.

The claim

The doctrine on this timeline draws one line again and again: the Name of Jesus has real power, yet is not a formula severable from faith and from belonging to the One named — it is not a spell workable by any mouth that gets the syllables right. The argument weighed here is that we can watch that exact line being crossed in the surviving record of the ancient magicians themselves. The Papyri Graecae Magicae — the working handbooks of Greco-Egyptian ritual specialists, copied and recopied from the Hellenistic period into late antiquity — are saturated with the Hebrew names of God (Iao for the Tetragrammaton, Sabaoth, Adonai) and the cognate power-word Abrasax, and in their later strata they reach for the name of Jesus too, adjuring demons “by the god of the Hebrews, Jesus.” Here the Name is precisely a vox magica: a sound of power, catalogued alongside Egyptian, Greek, and invented names, to be strung together and pronounced by whoever holds the recipe. The interpretation to weigh is that this appropriation is not a counterexample to the doctrine but its confirmation from the outside — the “name as spell in any mouth” that the fathers said misunderstands where the power sits, documented in the magicians’ own hand.

The record — the divine names as voces magicae

The material fact is not in dispute. The magical papyri are shot through with Jewish divine names used as words of power. As the standard survey puts it, “Egyptian sorcerers had great respect for the names of the supernatural powers in Judaism. References to Iao (for YHWH), Sabaoth, Adonai, Moses, Solomon, Michael, Gabriel, angels, archangels, cherubim and seraphim abound in the texts.”1 The reputation was real and cuts against any tidy “pagans versus Jews” picture: the repute of Jewish magicians was said to exceed even that of the Egyptian sorcerers, and “magic flourished among the Jews despite strong and persistent condemnation by the religious authority” — so much so that the most Jewish spell in the whole corpus, the very exorcism examined below, was likely composed by a Jew steeped in the Scriptures, not by a pagan borrowing from outside.2 The point for this doctrine is not the nationality of the copyists but the mode: whoever wrote them, these handbooks treat the Name as a transferable technology — a sound one owns by knowing the recipe.

The exorcisms that name Jesus

The charm of Pibechis (PGM IV.3007–3086). The single most-cited instance is an exorcism from the Great Paris codex, headed “A tested charm of Pibechis for those possessed by daimons.” Its adjuration reaches for the God of Israel and, in the same breath, for Jesus — as one more name in the string:

I conjure you by the god of the Hebrews, Jesus, IABA IAE ABRAOTH AIA THOTH ELE ELO AEO IIIBAECH ABARMAS IABARAOU ABELBEL LONA ABRA MAROIA BRAKION, who appears in fire, who is in the midst of land, snow, and fog, TANNETIS…

— PGM IV.3019–3020 (Betz trans.).3 The spell’s reservoir is unmistakably biblical — it adjures the demon “by the great god Sabaoth, through whom the Jordan River drew back,” and alludes across the Exodus story — yet the biblical names sit inside a farrago of manufactured voces magicae (IABA, ABARMAS, BRAKION) and are worked over a boiled compound of olive oil, mastic, and lotus, with a tin lamella hung on the patient. The sacred Name has become one ingredient in a technique.

The bilingual exorcism (PGM IV.1227–1264). A shorter rite in the same codex opens with what reads almost as a creed — and then commandeers it:

Hail, God of Abraham; hail, God of Isaac; hail, God of Jacob; Jesus Chrestos, the Holy Spirit, the Son of the Father, who is above the Seven, who is within the Seven. Bring Iao Sabaoth; may your power issue forth from him, NN, until you drive away this unclean daimon Satan, who is in him.

— PGM IV.1231–1234ff (Betz trans.).4 The patriarchs, the Trinity, and the Tetragrammaton-as-Iao are all present — but slotted into a mechanism of the “Seven” (the planetary archons) and a “bring… issue forth… drive away” that compels. The Great Paris codex itself is an early-fourth-century book, but its recipes preserve traditions reaching back to the second century and earlier, and the practice they document was widespread for centuries before the Gospels; as backdrop to the New Testament and the fathers the milieu is contemporary and older, whatever the date of this particular copy.5

The steelman — does this show the Name “worked” for outsiders?

The sharpest form of the challenge to the doctrine runs like this: if pagan and syncretistic magicians reached for the Name of Jesus and the Hebrew divine names, is that not evidence that the Name did avail outside the church — that it really was a power-word anyone could wield, exactly the “name as spell” the fathers denied? The papyri seem to be the magicians voting with their feet: they would not have paid to copy a formula that never worked.

That deserves an honest concession. The papyri are strong evidence that the Name of Jesus had acquired, by the second and third centuries, a formidable reputation for power — potent enough that Jewish, Christian, and pagan operators alike wanted it in their kit, and that a demon might be expected to fear it. This is real historical yield, and it converges with the fathers’ own boast that the Name routed spirits: friend and foe agreed the Name was dreaded. To that extent the appropriation testifies to the very power the doctrine asserts.

Adversarial assessment

What the papyri establish. Reputation and borrowing — not efficacy. That the Name was reached for is documented on every leaf; that it availed in a magician’s mouth is documented nowhere. A recipe copied because it is believed to work is not a recipe shown to work, and the whole apparatus — compulsive verbs, planetary archons, manufactured syllables, materia magica — is built on the assumption the doctrine denies: that supernatural power is a technology the operator commands by procedure. On the wiki’s own reading (argued at whether the power is intrinsic to the words or the Person’s), that is precisely the category error — the efficacy is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith and relation to him, so the Name is borrowable as sound but not commandeerable as power. The papyri are what that reading predicts you would find: the Name floating free as a vox magica, exactly where, on the doctrine’s account, it cannot deliver.

The indiscriminate piling-up is itself the tell. In the Pibechis charm “Jesus” is not invoked as the crucified and risen Lord to whom one belongs; he is stacked between IABA and ABARMAS, one more sound in a chain, and “the god of the Hebrews” is made an epithet of Jesus — a confusion no catechized Christian would commit. That is the signature of the severable-formula approach the fathers diagnosed: the operator wants the syllables, detached from the Person, the faith, and the belonging. It is the written form of the sons of Sceva’s “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — the Name as a word overheard and re-deployed — and the New Testament sets that scene, pointedly, just before the Ephesian magicians burn their own handbooks, the very genre these papyri belong to.6

The fathers drew this line in advance. The appropriation does not ambush the doctrine; the doctrine predicts it. Irenaeus insists the church works “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art,” but by prayer and the plain invocation of Jesus — the exact contrast between a prayer and a spell that the papyri put on display.7 Origen fixes the same point from the other side: Christians prevail over spirits “not… by incantations… but by the name of Jesus, accompanied by the announcement of the narratives which relate to Him” — the Name bound to the story and the Person, not to the sound — and even where it is “effectual… pronounced… by bad men,” that is the Lord’s doing, not the wielder’s craft, and no warrant for the one the Lord does not know (Matthew 7:22–23).8 The papyri are the negative image of that claim: the syllables in the wrong hands, without the narrative, the faith, or the belonging.

The overreach to flag. The strong modern version of “Jesus in the magical papyri” is Morton Smith’s thesis (Jesus the Magician, 1978) that Jesus himself is to be understood as a magician of just this type — the papyri read back onto the founder. That is the sister-charge weighed, and rated weak, on “Jesus worked his signs by name-magic”; here it is enough to note that it has been “rightly rejected” for ignoring “the essential Jewishness of Jesus’ teaching and the many dissimilarities of method and worldview between Jesus and contemporary magicians.”9 The sound use of the papyri is the modest one: they illuminate the milieu the Name entered and the boundary the church policed, not the identity of the One named. (The Iao/Tetragrammaton half of the same record — the guarded Divine Name surfacing as a magical vowel-string — is traced on the Divine Name timeline; the developed Jewish exorcism-by-name apparatus these handbooks belong to, archetypally attached to Solomon, on the Solomon exorcism tradition.)

Assessment

sound. The Greek Magical Papyri really did absorb the Hebrew divine names and the name of Jesus as voces magicae — a near-consensus historical fact, documented in the papyri’s own hand and studied by the standard scholarship. Read critically, that record confirms the doctrine’s boundary rather than breaching it: it attests that the Name had a fearsome reputation (which the fathers also claimed), but it nowhere shows the Name availing in a magician’s mouth, and its whole method — the Name piled indiscriminately among manufactured syllables, worked by compulsive procedure detached from faith and the Person — is exactly the “name as spell in any mouth” that Irenaeus and Origen said cannot lay hold of the power, and that the sons of Sceva embody in narrative. The honest residue is that the papyri cannot prove the spells failed, only that they presuppose the very transferability the doctrine denies; but on attestation and coherence they read as the negative image of the doctrine, not a counterexample to it. Weighed as historical evidence, never as instruction.
