Simon Magus and simony (Acts 8:9–24): is the power of the Name a commodity that can be bought or sold?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — a distinct facet of the boundary the whole doctrine polices: divine power is a gift, not a ware. Simon the magician, who offered the apostles money to buy the power of conferring the Spirit, is the tradition’s settled archetype of the man who would purchase what cannot be purchased — the sin the church named simony after him — and Peter’s “thy money perish with thee” fixes the rule that the power in the Name and the Spirit’s gift are received freely and ministered freely, never held as merchandise or retailed as a technique for hire. Near-consensus history and exegesis; the honest caution is only against collapsing this commodification-boundary into the neighbouring faithless-wielding one.

The claim

And when Simon saw that through laying on of the apostles’ hands the Holy Ghost was given, he offered them money, saying, Give me also this power, that on whomsoever I lay hands, he may receive the Holy Ghost. But Peter said unto him, Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money. Thou hast neither part nor lot in this matter: for thy heart is not right in the sight of God.

— Acts 8:18–21 (KJV), historicalchristian.faith.1 The sub-claim advanced here is not that Simon failed to work the Name (that is the sons-of-Sceva point), but something one step behind it: that the power at issue is of a kind that cannot be bought or sold at all. It is a gift of God — “freely ye have received, freely give” (Matthew 10:8) — and the moment it is approached as a purchasable commodity or a saleable technique, the approacher has already stepped outside the thing he covets. The fathers make Simon the standing type of that error, and the church coined its name for the sin — simony — from him.

Who advances it — the patristic archetype

This is less one author’s thesis than a near-unanimous reading of Acts 8, running from the second century forward and lending its own vocabulary (“simony”) to later canon law. Justin Martyr (c. 155) already knows Simon as the Samaritan magician honoured at Rome “with a statue” who “did mighty acts of magic, by virtue of the art of the devils operating in him.”2 Irenaeus (c. 180) makes him the fountainhead of heresy — “this Simon of Samaria, from whom all sorts of heresies derive their origin” — and retells the money-offer verbatim: he “offer[ed] money to the apostles,” thinking he “might receive this power of bestowing the Holy Spirit on whomsoever he would,” and was met with “Thy money perish with thee.”3 Chrysostom (c. 400) expounds the episode as the exposure of a man who “had imagined” the Spirit “to be altogether a thing of man,” and marvels that the apostles are “on all occasions… clean from money”; and he is the one who explicitly yokes Simon to the vagabond exorcists of Acts 19, so that the purchase and the spell are read as companion vices — “both Simon for the sake of merchandise sought the grace of the Spirit, and these for this object did this.”4 Cyprian (c. 250) states the doctrine the episode teaches as a bare heading — “That the grace of God ought to be without price” — adducing Peter’s rebuke, the Lord’s “Freely ye have received, freely give,” and the cleansing of the temple from “merchandise.”5

The evidence for

The text makes the vice the purchase itself, not the failure. Simon is already a baptized believer (Acts 8:13) who has “wondered, beholding the miracles and signs” — he does not doubt the power, he wants to own it. What Peter condemns is precisely the category-error of the offer: “thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money” (v. 20). The disorder is not that money is evil but that a gift has been mistaken for a ware; the two belong to different orders, and to price the one is to have lost it. Peter’s “thou hast neither part nor lot in this matter” (v. 21) says as much — the man who would buy the power has, by that very intention, no share in it. The gospel supplies the positive rule the episode enforces from the other side: the same power is given by Christ’s own charge “freely ye have received, freely give” (Matthew 10:8), so that gratuity is written into the commission itself.6

The fathers make gratuity the diagnostic mark against the magician. The commodification-boundary is not a stray moral but one of the recurring criteria by which the fathers tell the church’s power from the sorcerer’s. Irenaeus sets the two side by side: the church works “in the name of Jesus Christ… neither practising deception upon any, nor taking any reward,” for “as she has received freely from God, freely also does she minister” — whereas Simon and Carpocrates and their like “perform” their wonders “by means of magical deceptions… for the sake of destroying and misleading mankind,” and it is “the name of our Lord Jesus Christ… but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates,” that truly heals.7 The magician sells a technique; the church ministers a gift — and that is the same line, seen from the commercial side, that Irenaeus draws against the incantation and Origen against the spell. So the venal posture is not an accident of Simon’s biography but the exact inversion of the doctrine’s core: power laid hold of by faith and relation to the One named cannot be a possession transferred for a price.

The tradition’s own coinage confirms the reading. That the church named a sin “simony” — and read Acts 8 as its charter — is itself strong evidence that this facet of the boundary was recognized as distinct and load-bearing. Chrysostom draws the connection the argument turns on: the purchaser of the Spirit (Acts 8) and the traders who would wield the Name as a spell for gain (Acts 19) are two forms of one vice — treating the holy power “by way of trade” — and both are refused by a power that is only ever given (Chrysostom, above).

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The identification of Simon as the archetype of buying spiritual power is early, wide, and effectively unanimous (Justin, Irenaeus, Chrysostom, Cyprian, and the whole later tradition that took its name from him), and it rests on the plain sense of the pericope: Peter condemns the purchase, not a botched performance. The gratuity criterion — grace received and ministered without price — is a genuine and recurring mark by which the fathers separate the Name’s power from the magician’s craft, and it is a different mark from unbelief. As a facet of the doctrine it is well-grounded.

What to keep precise. Three cautions keep the argument from over-reaching:

(1) The proof-text is adjacent to the Name, not squarely on it. Acts 8 is strictly about the gift of the Holy Spirit conferred through the apostles’ laying-on of hands, not about the name of Jesus in exorcism. Its bearing on this doctrine is by the shared boundary (divine power is not venal) and by the fathers’ own bridging — Chrysostom is the one who links Simon to the sons of Sceva. The honest framing is that Simon is the archetype of trafficking in holy power generally, of which the power of the Name is one instance, rather than a witness to the Name’s efficacy as such.

(2) It is a companion of the sons-of-Sceva finding, not a duplicate of it. The two are easy to blur because Chrysostom names them together and both are “trade,” but the sins differ: Sceva’s sons wanted the Name to work for them as a formula in a faithless mouth (a wielding problem); Simon wanted to buy and retail the power itself (a commodification problem). One can commit either without the other — a sincere believer might still try to purchase an office; a scoffer might wield the words for free — so the commodification-boundary is a distinct line, weighed here in its own right and cross-linked, not folded, into the faithless-wielding page.

(3) Later “simony” is a broadening of the Acts-8 kernel. In medieval canon law “simony” came to cover the buying and selling of church offices, ordinations, and sacraments — a real and legitimate extension, but a wider net than the pericope, whose kernel is the attempt to purchase spiritual power / the Spirit’s gift. The argument rates the kernel; the canon-law extension inherits its force without being identical to it.

Assessment

sound. The fathers do make Simon Magus the archetype of the man who would buy the power of God, and Acts 8 does teach, in Peter’s own words, that “the gift of God” cannot “be purchased with money” — a distinct facet of the anti-magic boundary the whole doctrine polices: the Name’s power, and the Spirit’s gift with it, is gratuitous, received and given freely (Matthew 10:8), never a merchandise or a technique for hire. This is near-consensus exegesis, and the church’s own coinage of “simony” is the tradition’s witness to it. The only real cautions are against over-claiming: the proof-text is about the gift of the Spirit and so bears on the Name by extension and the fathers’ bridging; the commodification-vice is the companion, not the twin, of the faithless-wielding vice; and the later canon-law sense of “simony” broadens the Acts-8 kernel rather than reproducing it. Weighed within those bounds the reading holds.
