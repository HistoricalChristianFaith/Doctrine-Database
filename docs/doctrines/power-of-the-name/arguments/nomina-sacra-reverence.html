Are the nomina sacra a scribal witness to reverence for the sacred Name?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the near-universal, early, and distinctively Christian practice of contracting the sacred names (ΘΣ for Theos/God, ΚΣ for Kyrios/Lord, ΙΗΣ/ΙΣ for Iesous/Jesus, ΧΣ for Christos/Christ) and marking them with a horizontal overstroke is a material trace of reverence for those names — the strongest evidence being that scribes contracted the word only when it named the true God, the Lord, or Christ, and wrote the very same word out in full when it meant a pagan god or a human master. That selectivity defeats a purely stenographic explanation: the marked words are exactly the sacred ones. The narrow claim — that the convention reflects a devotional reverence for the sacred names — is well-grounded and mainstream. The overreach to flag is the heavier inference some load onto the same evidence (that the contractions prove a determinate early “binitarian” devotional theology); that further step is genuinely contested, and this rating tracks the narrower, sounder claim.

The claim

The oldest Christian manuscripts do something no Jewish or pagan Greek book does: wherever a handful of sacred words appear, the scribe shrinks them to their first and last letters and draws a horizontal line over the top — ΘΣ (Theos, God), ΚΣ (Kyrios, Lord), ΙΗΣ / ΙΣ (Iesous, Jesus), ΧΣ (Christos, Christ). Ludwig Traube, who in 1907 gave the phenomenon its name, called these the nomina sacra, “sacred names.”1 The sub-claim this page weighs, advanced in its strongest form by C. H. Roberts and Larry Hurtado: the almost universal, early, and distinctively Christian practice of specially writing these four names is a devotional reverence for the sacred names given visible form — a material, scribal trace of the very belief this timeline traces, that the Name is holy and is to be specially handled.

This is distinct from the question weighed on the Howard thesis (assessment: weak), which asks whether the New Testament autographs carried the Tetragrammaton יהוה and reads the nomina sacra as the slots where the erased Name once stood. That is a reconstruction about a lost text. The question here is not the Tetragrammaton at all but the reverence the surviving practice itself expresses — a claim about what the manuscripts we have reveal of the piety of the scribes who wrote them.

Who advances it

Traube, who coined the term, already read the contractions as a Christian analogue to the Hellenistic-Jewish reverence that gave the divine Name special treatment, tracing the system to a reverential handling of Theos and Kyrios.1 A. H. R. E. Paap’s 1959 catalogue of the papyri fixed the evidence and confirmed that the core, most consistently contracted group is precisely these four nomina divina — God, Lord, Jesus, Christ.2 Colin Roberts argued that the earliest of them was ΙΗ (the first two letters of Iesous), treated specially very early — plausibly in the Jewish-Christian community of Jerusalem within a generation of the crucifixion — and that the coordinated marking of the sacred names amounted to a kind of confession of faith written into the very orthography.3 Hurtado, in the fullest modern treatment, presses the point that the convention is a distinctively Christian scribal habit, early and near-universal, and reads it as an artifactual expression of early Christian devotion — and, more ambitiously, of a devotional pattern that gave the name of Jesus the reverence reserved for God.4

The evidence for

The convention is early, near-universal, and distinctively Christian. The contractions are not a fourth-century tidying-up but appear already in our earliest substantial Christian papyri — P46, P66, and P75, all of the later second or early third century — where ΚΣ, ΘΣ, ΙΣ, and ΧΣ are already in place, written the same way across otherwise unrelated copies and text-types. Jewish and pagan Greek books do not contract these words this way; the system is a Christian innovation, and so uniform and so early that it must reflect a shared attitude carried across the churches, not the private quirk of one scriptorium.4

The selectivity defeats mere abbreviation — this is the decisive point. If the contractions were a stenographer’s shortcut, they would fall wherever the word did. They do not. Scribes contracted ΚΣ when kyrios meant the Lord God but wrote kyrios out in full when it meant an ordinary human master; they contracted ΘΣ for the true God but spelled theos in full for a pagan “god”; and when the system later took in pneuma, they marked it for the Holy Spirit but not for an ordinary “spirit.” The abbreviation tracks the referent, not the letters — the mark falls only on the sacred sense.5 This is doubly telling because the contraction saves almost nothing (two letters of a short word) and the overbar, far from hiding the word to spare ink, positively draws the eye to it. A device that costs effort to highlight exactly the divine names is a device for setting them apart — which is what reverence, in a copyist’s hands, looks like.

The words chosen are the timeline’s own. The core four are God, the Lord, Jesus, and Christ — the very names this doctrine holds to be holy and effectual — and the name most plausibly marked first, on Roberts’s reconstruction, is the name of Jesus itself. A scribal reverence that begins with, and centres on, the name of Jesus is the material counterpart of the confession the New Testament makes in words: that God has “given him a name which is above every name” (Philippians 2:9–11). The contraction is the pen’s bow at the Name.3

There is a ready Jewish model for reverent scribal treatment of a divine name. Second-Temple Jewish copyists already set the Tetragrammaton apart on the page — writing it in archaic paleo-Hebrew letters inside a square-script or Greek text, leaving blank space for it, or marking its place with dots (the tetrapuncta). The impulse to give the sacred name a distinctive visual handling is thus not invented by the Christians; what is new is the form (contraction-with-overbar) and the extension of that reverence to the name of Jesus alongside God and Lord.6

Adversarial assessment

Was the motive reverence, or is it irrecoverable? Christopher Tuckett is the sharpest sceptic: he cautions that early scribal practice was less regular than the confident “reverence” reading assumes, and that the original motive of the convention may simply be beyond recovery — a habit that could have begun as a scribal convenience and hardened into a rule.7 The force of this is real and should be conceded at one point: once the convention was fixed, scribes did apply it with some inconsistency, so not every single token can be read as a fresh act of devotion, and the very earliest steps of the system’s birth are not directly observed. But the caution does not overturn the reverential reading, because it does not account for the selectivity. A mere convenience would not have learned to distinguish the divine Kyrios from a human master, or the Holy Pneuma from an ordinary spirit; a reverence for the sacred referent explains exactly that discrimination. Irrecoverability of the first spark is not equivalence of hypotheses: the sacral reading fits the data the “mere shorthand” reading cannot.

Reverence, or sacred-referent marking? Schuyler Brown recast the finding: the contractions, he argued, do not so much express reverence as mark the sacred referent — flagging for the reader that the divine person or thing, not its mundane namesake, is meant.8 This is a refinement of the reverence thesis, not a refutation of it. Setting the sacred apart from the profane on the page is a species of reverence in the copyist’s medium; “this word names the Holy One” and “this word is to be treated as holy” are two descriptions of one gesture. Brown’s correction pulls the claim from a vague piety toward a precise semantic function — but the function it names is the setting-apart of the sacred, which is what the sub-claim asserts.

The later expansion complicates a pure “divine-name” account. From the core four the system grew — to pneuma (Spirit), then across the third and fourth centuries to a standard set of some fifteen words including huios (Son), pater (Father), stauros (cross), anthropos (man), meter (mother), and the sacred place-names David, Israel, Jerusalem, and heaven. Some of these (“man,” “mother”) are not divine names at all, which shows the convention broadened into a general marker of theologically weighty words. But this tells against only a maximal claim that the system was, from first to last, purely a reverence for the divine name. The advocated sub-claim rests on the core group and its priority — God, Lord, Jesus, Christ, present first and marked most consistently — and the later, looser extension does not retroject onto that origin.2

The overreach to flag. Hurtado draws from the same evidence a heavier conclusion: that the nomina sacra are proof of an early, determinate “binitarian” devotional pattern — Jesus receiving, in the scribes’ treatment of his name, the reverence due to God.4 That the name of Jesus stands among the earliest and most consistently marked words is genuine and striking. But the step from “the name of Jesus was reverenced in writing” to “the contractions establish a specific christological theology” is where Tuckett’s caution bites hardest: a scribal convention is thin evidence for a determinate doctrine, and the inference is more than the marks alone can carry. This page rates the narrower claim, and notes the heavier one as a probable-but-contested extension, not a demonstrated result.

Assessment

sound. The advocated claim — that the nomina sacra are a scribal witness to reverence for the sacred names — is well-grounded and mainstream. The convention is early (in place already in P46, P66, P75), near-universal, and distinctively Christian; the core words are the four sacred names; and the decisive datum is the selectivity — the same word contracted for the divine sense and written out for the mundane one — which no purely stenographic account can explain but a reverence for the sacred referent explains exactly. Two honest refinements stand: Brown’s “sacred-referent marking” is a sharpening of the reverence reading, not a rival to it; and the later expansion to non-divine words means the pure divine-name account holds only for the core, not for the whole grown system. One overreach is to be named: the further inference (Hurtado) that the marks prove a specific binitarian devotional theology is a contested extension, on which Tuckett’s scepticism is well taken — the rating here tracks the narrower, sounder claim, not that maximal one. So the material record does carry a real trace of the belief this timeline follows: the earliest Christian copyists reverenced the Name with their pens.
