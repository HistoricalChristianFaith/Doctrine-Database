What do the nomina sacra of the earliest Christian manuscripts express?

Disputed reading · on The Power of the Name · witness: the early Christian scribes

The evidence

In the oldest Christian manuscripts, wherever a handful of sacred words appear, the scribe shrinks them to their first and last letters and draws a horizontal line over the top — ΘΣ (Theos, God), ΚΣ (Kyrios, Lord), ΙΗΣ / ΙΣ (Iesous, Jesus), ΧΣ (Christos, Christ). Ludwig Traube, who in 1907 gave the phenomenon its name, called these the nomina sacra, “sacred names.”1 A. H. R. E. Paap’s 1959 catalogue of the papyri found that the core, most consistently contracted group is these four nomina divina; across the third and fourth centuries the system grew to some fifteen words, including pneuma (Spirit), huios (Son), pater (Father), stauros (cross), anthropos (man), meter (mother), and David, Israel, Jerusalem and heaven.2 Larry Hurtado reports the four core contractions already in place in P46, P66 and P75, of the later second or early third century, written the same way across otherwise unrelated copies, and treats the convention as distinctively Christian.3 Hurtado also documents a selective application: ΚΣ contracted when kyrios means the Lord but written in full for a human master, ΘΣ for the true God but theos in full for a pagan god, pneuma marked for the Holy Spirit but not for an ordinary spirit.4 Second-Temple Jewish copyists set the Tetragrammaton apart on the page — in paleo-Hebrew letters within a square-script or Greek text, as a blank space, or with dots (the tetrapuncta).5

The question

Whether the contractions express a scribal reverence for the sacred names, mark which sense of a word is meant, or rest on a motive that can no longer be recovered is disputed; the answer bears on what the earliest manuscripts can show about how their copyists regarded the name of Jesus. (A separate question, whether the New Testament autographs carried the Tetragrammaton and the nomina sacra mark its erased place, is set out on the Divine Name timeline.)

A scribal reverence for the sacred names

On this reading the special writing of God, Lord, Jesus and Christ is a devotional reverence for those names given visible form on the page.

Held by: Ludwig Traube (Nomina Sacra, 1907); Colin H. Roberts (Manuscript, Society and Belief in Early Christian Egypt, 1979); Larry W. Hurtado (The Earliest Christian Artifacts, 2006).6

Traube read the contractions as a Greek Christian analogue to the Hellenistic-Jewish reverence that gave the divine Name special treatment, tracing the system to a reverential handling of Theos and Kyrios.1 Roberts argued that the earliest of them was ΙΗ (the first two letters of Iesous), treated specially very early, plausibly in the Jewish-Christian community of Jerusalem within a generation of the crucifixion, and that the coordinated marking of the sacred names amounted to a confession of faith written into the orthography.7 On this reading the convention is early, near-universal and distinctively Christian, and so uniform that it reflects a shared attitude across the churches rather than the habit of one scriptorium. The contraction saves only a few letters, and the overbar draws the eye to the word; the mark falls on the sacred sense of a word and not on its ordinary sense. The impulse to give a sacred name distinctive visual handling has its model in the Jewish treatment of the Tetragrammaton; the Christian form is contraction with overbar, and its range takes in the name of Jesus beside God and Lord, as Philippians 2:9–11 confesses “a name which is above every name.” Hurtado reads the practice as an artifactual expression of early Christian devotion and, further, as “a striking expression of the binitarian shape of early Christian devotion,” the name of Jesus receiving the reverence given to God.3

A marker of the sacred referent

On this reading the contractions flag for the reader that the divine person or thing, not its ordinary namesake, is meant.

Held by: Schuyler Brown (“Concerning the Origin of the Nomina Sacra,” 1970).8

Brown, writing against Traube, argued that the contractions do not so much express reverence as mark the sacred referent: a semantic distinction between the divine person or thing and its mundane namesake. The selective application is the core of this case. The same word is contracted in one sense and written in full in another — kyrios for the Lord and for a human master, theos for the true God and for a pagan god, pneuma for the Holy Spirit and for an ordinary spirit — so the device tracks the referent of the word. A reader meeting ΚΣ knows at once that the divine Lord is meant. Brown located the origin of the convention among Jewish Christians. Roberts and Hurtado cite Brown’s critique as “generally persuasive” on the critique of Traube only.8

An early convention whose original motive cannot be recovered

On this reading the evidence does not disclose why the convention began, which may have been as a scribal convenience that hardened into a rule.

Held by: Christopher M. Tuckett (“‘Nomina Sacra’: Yes and No?”, 2003).9

Tuckett cautions that early scribal practice was less regular than the reverence reading assumes, and that the original motive of the convention may be beyond recovery and need not have been devotional. The earliest steps of the system’s birth are not directly observed in any surviving manuscript, and once the convention was fixed scribes applied it with some inconsistency, so individual contractions cannot each be read as a fresh act of devotion. The set also grew to words that are not divine names at all, such as “man” and “mother.”2 A habit that may have begun as a convenience could have hardened into a rule before any theological meaning was attached to it. On this reading the convention’s origin is a scribal practice whose motive the surviving manuscripts do not disclose.
