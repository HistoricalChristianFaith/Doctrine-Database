The Solomon exorcism tradition: the Jewish name-magic milieu the Name of Jesus entered

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the milieu is near-consensus history: before “the name of Jesus” entered the world there was already a developed Jewish apparatus of exorcism-by-name, archetypally attached to Solomon (demons bound and expelled by divine names, a seal-ring, incantations, and roots), attested by Josephus and Wisdom and running on into the Testament of Solomon, the “seal of Solomon” amulets, and the Aramaic incantation bowls. This page weighs a Jewish and pagan name-lore as historical backdrop to the Christian doctrine of the power of the Name — the concrete milieu the New Testament’s Name entered. It is examined critically, as context for what the gospel was held to supersede, and never as instruction.

The claim

The interpretation weighed here is a historical one: that the New Testament’s “name of Jesus” was not launched into a vacuum but into a world that already had a Jewish technology of exorcism-by-name, whose patron and archetype was Solomon. On this reading the “vagabond Jews, exorcists” who tried to wield the Name at Ephesus — the sons of Sceva “chief of the priests” (Acts 19:13–14) — were heirs of a going concern: demons bound and expelled by divine names, a seal-ring, incantations, and prescribed roots, all traced back to Israel’s wisest king. And the further claim is that the fathers read the power of the Name against this backdrop: the one Name does, by the Person it names, what the whole Solomonic apparatus of names and seals only attempts by procedure. So the interpretation has a factual limb (there was such a milieu) and an interpretive limb (the gospel’s Name was held to enter and displace it).

The Jewish milieu — the evidence

The licit root: Solomon’s God-given knowledge (Wisdom 7:17–21). The tradition begins in a wholly respectable place — the wisdom-book put in Solomon’s mouth, where his omni-competence includes a mastery of the natural world that later ages would press toward the occult:

For he hath given me certain knowledge of the things that are… The natures of living creatures, and the furies of wild beasts: the violence of winds, and the reasonings of men: the diversities of plants, and the virtues of roots: And all such things as are either secret or manifest, them I know.

— Wisdom of Solomon 7:17–21 (KJV Apocrypha).1 “The virtues of roots” is the seed of everything that follows: a knowledge of the powers latent in creation, given by God, which in the later legend shades into a knowledge of the spirits and the names that command them.

The historiographic root: Josephus’s Eleazar (Antiquities 8.45–49). By the end of the first century the tradition is fully formed and presented as sober fact. Writing c. 93, Josephus reports that God gave Solomon “that skill which expels demons,” that he composed incantations and left behind “the manner of using exorcisms,” and — crucially — that Josephus had seen the technique worked in his own day, in the name of Solomon, before the future emperor:

God also enabled him to learn that skill which expels demons… He composed such incantations also by which distempers are alleviated. And he left behind him the manner of using exorcisms; by which they drive away demons… For I have seen a certain man of my own countrey, whose name was Eleazar, releasing people that were demoniacal in the presence of Vespasian, and his sons, and his Captains, and the whole multitude of his soldiers: the manner of the cure was this: he put a ring that had a root of one of those sorts mentioned by Solomon to the nostrils of the demoniack: after which he drew out the demon through his nostrils… making still mention of Solomon, and reciting the incantations which he composed.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 8.45–49 (Whiston).2 Here is the whole apparatus in miniature: a name (“making still mention of Solomon”), a seal/ring, a prescribed root, and set incantations — the exorcist a technician executing a transmitted procedure. Eleazar even sets a cup of water for the departing demon to overturn, a public proof of the cure. This is roughly the milieu, and roughly the decade, of the sons of Sceva at Ephesus.

The living magical stream: the Testament of Solomon and the seal-ring. The same tradition, in frankly magical dress, fills the Testament of Solomon — a Greek text whose written form is later (its substance drawn from earlier tradition, its redaction generally placed in the first three Christian centuries, some parts later) but which shows the fully-developed shape the legend took. In it Solomon commands the demons by a seal-ring sent from God through the archangel Michael, and binds each spirit by the name of the angel that thwarts it:

…grace was given to me from the Lord Sabaoth by Michael his archangel. [He brought me] a little ring, having a seal consisting of an engraved stone, and said to me: “Take, O Solomon, king, son of David, the gift which the Lord God has sent thee… With it thou shalt lock up all demons of the earth, male and female.”

— Testament of Solomon 5–6 (Conybeare).3 Throughout, Solomon asks each demon “what angel is it that frustrates thee?” and the demon must confess the binding name — the mirror-image, in a Jewish key, of the demon at Ephesus who would not own the borrowed name of Jesus (“Jesus I know… but who are ye?”). Solomon’s lordship over demons is on this wiki already in its rabbinic form — the Targum Sheni to Esther makes him ruler “over devils, demons, and spirits, whose language he understood”4 — and the material afterlife of the tradition is thick: the Aramaic incantation bowls of Nippur seal demons “by the seal of King Solomon son of David, by which were sealed the Oppressors,” and Montgomery, editing them, traces “Solomon’s magic ring” back to “first mention… by Josephus.”5

The patristic contrast — the one Name against the technique

Justin ranks the names, and Solomon’s is not the one that avails. The earliest father on this timeline draws exactly the line the interpretation needs, though he draws it generically. In Dialogue 85 the Name of Jesus crucified subdues the demon where the names of Israel’s own greats do not: “though you exorcise any demon in the name of any of those who were amongst you—either kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs—it will not be subject to you,” while the Jewish exorcists who work “craft… fumigations and incantations” are doing sorcery, not wielding the Name.6 Solomon is a king — the paradigm king of the exorcism legend — so his name falls under precisely the rank Justin says will not subject the demon: the technique that trades on the great name is exactly what the one Name overtops.

Origen frames the contrast without naming Solomon; Bede supplies the name. Origen states the contrast at its sharpest — “it is not by incantations that Christians seem to prevail… but by the name of Jesus,” a Name so bound to the One named that it is effectual “even… pronounced… by bad men” — and he grants that certain Hebrew divine names “are possessed of great power,” the very premise the name-technique exploits.7 But in the whole of Against Celsus Origen does not name Solomon in the exorcism argument (his Solomon is the sage of the proverbs and the Queen of Sheba). The father who makes the link explicit is Bede, glossing the sons of Sceva: “Josephus reports that King Solomon devised and taught his people modes of exorcism… by which unclean spirits, once expelled from a person, might not dare to return.”8 Bede reads Acts 19 against Josephus’s Solomon — the sons of Sceva as failed heirs of the Solomonic art — which is the interpretation this page advances, stated by a father in so many words.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The factual limb is as secure as this kind of claim gets. That a developed Jewish exorcism-by-name tradition existed, that it was attached to Solomon, and that it was a live practice around the time and place of Acts 19, rests on Josephus — a hostile-to-magic, eyewitness-claiming source writing c. 93 — corroborated upstream by Wisdom’s “virtues of roots” and downstream by the Testament of Solomon, the seal-of-Solomon amulets, and the incantation bowls. Luke’s own detail fits it exactly: the men are “vagabond Jews, exorcists,” sons of a Jewish chief priest, adjuring “by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — i.e. slotting a new powerful name into an existing adjuration-procedure. The interpretive limb — that the gospel’s Name was read as entering and overtopping this milieu — is genuinely in the fathers: Justin ranks the names and puts the king’s below the one Name; Origen sets “not incantations… but the name of Jesus” as the whole difference; Bede names Solomon as the tradition the sons of Sceva botch. The mechanism the contrast turns on is the doctrine’s own: efficacy is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith and relation, not a property of the procedure — so the Solomonic technique, however elaborate, is the very thing the sons of Sceva show cannot be borrowed.

What to be careful of. Two over-specifications must be resisted. First, do not claim the fathers consciously singled out Solomon by name as the thing displaced: with the single exception of Bede (and he only in a verse-note), they arraign the whole name-magic market — “fumigations and incantations,” “curious arts,” the professional exorcist — of which Solomon was the Jewish archetype, not the sole named target. The strong form of the interpretation is “the Name entered a world of name-magic whose Jewish patron was Solomon,” not “the fathers set Jesus against Solomon.” Second, mind the dating: the Testament of Solomon in its written form is later than the New Testament, so it is evidence of the stream, not proof of the exact pre-Christian shape — the pre-Christian existence of the tradition is carried by Josephus and Wisdom, which are contemporary or earlier, not by the Testament. Neither caveat touches the core: they trim an overreach, they do not defeat the reading. (And the whole apparatus is weighed here as backdrop, critically — the tradition is reported, not commended; the seal-ring and the roots are history, not method.)

Assessment

sound. There was a developed Jewish exorcism-by-name tradition archetypally attached to Solomon — a matter of near-consensus history, decisively on Josephus (the God-given “skill which expels demons,” the composed incantations, Eleazar drawing out a demon “making still mention of Solomon” before Vespasian), rooted in Wisdom’s “virtues of roots” and running on into the Testament of Solomon, the seal-of-Solomon amulets, and the incantation bowls — and the New Testament’s “name of Jesus” entered exactly this milieu (the sons of Sceva are its heirs). The fathers read the power of the Name against that backdrop: Justin puts the king’s name below the one Name, Origen makes “not incantations… but the name of Jesus” the whole difference, and Bede names Solomon as the art the sons of Sceva fail to work — the one Name doing, by the Person named, what the technique of names, seals, and roots only attempts by procedure. The honest limits: the fathers arraign the whole name-magic market (Solomon its archetype, not usually its named target — Bede excepted), and the Testament is later evidence of the stream, not proof of its pre-Christian form (Josephus and Wisdom carry that). Weighed as historical backdrop, never as instruction.
