What tradition did the Jewish exorcists of Acts 19:13–16 stand in?

Disputed reading · on The Power of the Name · witnesses: Justin Martyr, John Chrysostom, Bede

Related readings: What do the Greek Magical Papyri’s uses of the name of Jesus show about Christian invocation of the Name? · What did canon 35 of the Council of Laodicea forbid?

The passage

Then certain of the vagabond Jews, exorcists, took upon them to call over them which had evil spirits the name of the Lord Jesus, saying, We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth. And there were seven sons of one Sceva, a Jew, and chief of the priests, which did so. And the evil spirit answered and said, Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye? And the man in whom the evil spirit was leaped on them, and overcame them, and prevailed against them, so that they fled out of that house naked and wounded.

— Acts 19:13–16 (KJV).1

The question

Luke calls the men “vagabond Jews, exorcists” and the sons of a Jewish chief priest, and says nothing more of their methods. Whether they are to be understood as practitioners of a general craft of exorcism worked without faith, or as heirs of a Jewish exorcism tradition attached to Solomon, has been answered differently by Christian commentators, and bears on how the early contrast between the name of Jesus and other exorcisms is read.

Background: the Solomonic exorcism tradition

The Wisdom of Solomon puts in Solomon’s mouth a God-given knowledge of creation that includes “the virtues of roots”:

For he hath given me certain knowledge of the things that are… The natures of living creatures, and the furies of wild beasts: the violence of winds, and the reasonings of men: the diversities of plants, and the virtues of roots: And all such things as are either secret or manifest, them I know.

— Wisdom of Solomon 7:17–21 (KJV Apocrypha).2 Josephus, writing c. 93, reports that God gave Solomon “that skill which expels demons,” and that he had himself seen the technique worked, in Solomon’s name, before Vespasian:

God also enabled him to learn that skill which expels demons… He composed such incantations also by which distempers are alleviated. And he left behind him the manner of using exorcisms; by which they drive away demons… For I have seen a certain man of my own countrey, whose name was Eleazar, releasing people that were demoniacal in the presence of Vespasian, and his sons, and his Captains, and the whole multitude of his soldiers: the manner of the cure was this: he put a ring that had a root of one of those sorts mentioned by Solomon to the nostrils of the demoniack: after which he drew out the demon through his nostrils… making still mention of Solomon, and reciting the incantations which he composed.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 8.45–49 (Whiston).3 Eleazar’s cure joins a name (“making still mention of Solomon”), a ring, a prescribed root and set incantations, and he sets a cup of water for the departing demon to overturn as a proof of the cure. The Testament of Solomon, a Greek text whose written form postdates the New Testament, has Solomon command the demons by a seal-ring sent from God through the archangel Michael and bind each spirit by asking “what angel is it that frustrates thee?”:

…grace was given to me from the Lord Sabaoth by Michael his archangel. [He brought me] a little ring, having a seal consisting of an engraved stone, and said to me: “Take, O Solomon, king, son of David, the gift which the Lord God has sent thee… With it thou shalt lock up all demons of the earth, male and female.”

— Testament of Solomon 5–6 (Conybeare).4 The Targum Sheni to Esther makes Solomon ruler “over devils, demons, and spirits, whose language he understood”;5 an Aramaic incantation bowl from Nippur seals demons “by the seal of King Solomon son of David, by which were sealed the Oppressors,” and J. A. Montgomery, editing the bowls (1913), traces “Solomon’s magic ring” to its “first mention… by Josephus.”6

Practitioners of a craft of exorcism who used the Name without faith

On this reading the exorcists worked by a craft and for gain, and their failure lay in using the name of Jesus without believing in him.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 85, c. 160), on the Jewish exorcists of his own day; John Chrysostom (Homilies on Acts 41, c. 400).7

Justin describes the Jewish exorcists of his time as men who “make use of craft when they exorcise, even as the Gentiles do, and employ fumigations and incantations,” and holds that “though you exorcise any demon in the name of any of those who were amongst you—either kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs—it will not be subject to you,” while the demon exorcised in the name of Jesus crucified “is overcome and subdued.”8 Chrysostom reads Acts 19:13 in the same terms: “So entirely did they do all by way of trade! Observe: vagabond, or, itinerant, Jewish exorcists. And to believe indeed, they had no mind; but by that Name they wished to cast out the demons.” Their failure follows from this: “Then not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith.” He sets them beside Simon Magus: “Both Simon for the sake of merchandise sought the grace of the Spirit, and these for this object did this.”9 On this reading what defines the exorcists is the use of the Name as one more instrument of a trade shared with Gentile exorcists, detached from faith in the One named. Their words — “by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — borrow a name at second hand, and Chrysostom notes that they “must needs add Paul, as thinking him to be something great.” The episode then sets the contrast between craft and faith in narrative form.

Heirs of the exorcism art attributed to Solomon

On this reading the exorcists at Ephesus practised the adjurations that Jewish tradition traced to Solomon, and the name of Jesus was a new name inserted into that procedure.

Held by: Bede (Commentary on Acts, on 19:13, 8th c.).10

Bede glosses the verse directly from Josephus: “Josephus reports that King Solomon devised and taught his people modes of exorcism, that is, adjurations, by which unclean spirits, once expelled from a person, might not dare to return.” On this reading the background supplies the link: a Jewish exorcism by adjuration, carried out in Solomon’s name with ring, root and composed incantations, was a public practice in the generation of Acts, attested by Josephus as an eyewitness before Vespasian, rooted in the Solomon of Wisdom, and continued in the Testament of Solomon, the seal-of-Solomon amulets and the Nippur bowls. Luke’s men are Jewish professional exorcists, sons of a chief priest, who “adjure” the spirit; on this reading they take an existing adjuration-procedure and slot into it the name they have heard Paul preach. Bede adds that such exorcism “sometimes happens even through reprobates, either for the condemnation of those who do such things or for the benefit of those who see and hear, so that although men may despise those who perform the signs, they nevertheless honor God, at whose invocation such great miracles are performed.”10 The demon’s question to the sons of Sceva, “who are ye?”, on this reading answers the Solomonic adjuration in its own terms, in which the demon must confess the binding name.
