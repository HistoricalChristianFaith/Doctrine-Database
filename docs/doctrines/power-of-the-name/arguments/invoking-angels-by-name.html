Invoking angels by name — what did the Council of Laodicea (canon 35) forbid?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the canon condemns a specific heterodox cult of angels — the Phrygian practice of making angels mediators to a too-distant God, bypassing Christ, and adjuring them by name in the manner of the amulet and the spell — and not the honouring or naming of angels as such. That is the reading the earliest interpreter of the canon states outright (Theodoret localizes the abuse to Phrygia and Pisidia and notes that lawful oratories of St Michael stood among the very same people), the reading modern scholarship confirms (Lightfoot ties the canon back to the “worshipping of angels” Paul rebuked at Colossae), and the reading the church’s own practice presupposes (it went on venerating Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael by name while forbidding the invention of unknown angel-names). The rival — that the canon is a blanket ban on all invocation of angels — reads the bare words in isolation from the abuse they targeted and is not the sense the tradition gave them.

The claim

Christians must not forsake the Church of God, and go away and invoke angels and gather assemblies, which things are forbidden. If, therefore, any one shall be found engaged in this covert idolatry, let him be anathema; for he has forsaken our Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, and has gone over to idolatry.

— Council of Laodicea, canon 35 (mid-4th c., Phrygia; Percival’s translation), NPNF2 vol. 14.1 The canon is the church’s one explicit legislative boundary-marker on the invocation of the heavenly names, and its scope is genuinely disputed. It can be read narrowly — as condemning a particular heterodox cult of angels that flourished in Phrygia (angels as intermediary powers whose favour must be won to approach God, invoked by name in the manner of the magical adjuration) — or broadly, as forbidding all invocation and honouring of angels. This page advocates the narrow reading: the canon forbids the angel-cult and its magical name-adjuration, not the naming or veneration of angels as such.

The abuse the canon targeted

The canon does not legislate in a vacuum; it names a concrete practice — forsaking the church’s assembly to “go away and invoke angels and gather assemblies” — and both the earliest interpreters and modern scholarship read it against a documented Phrygian cult. Theodoret of Cyrus, commenting on the “worshipping of angels” Paul had condemned at Colossae (Colossians 2:18), traces the abuse forward to this very synod and gives it a place on the map: the false teachers “were leading to worship angels… for, said they, the Law was given through angels. And this vice lasted for a long time in Phrygia and Pisidia. Therefore it was that the synod which met at Laodicea in Phrygia, prohibited by a canon, that prayer should be offered to angels” — adding, tellingly, “and even to-day an oratory of St. Michael can be seen among them, and their neighbours.”2 The mechanism of the cult is what the Greek fathers describe on Colossians: it made the Only-Begotten too high and remote to be approached directly, and slotted the angels in as the necessary go-betweens. Chrysostom states it flatly — “There are some who maintain that we must be brought near by Angels, not by Christ, that were too great a thing for us” — and Theophylact spells out the same logic: the deceivers held it “unworthy… of the greatness of the Only-Begotten” that he should bring men to the Father, so “it is more reasonable to suppose that angels served in your bringing to the Father… and persuaded the simple-hearted to turn to them, as if to our saviors.”3 This is the “covert idolatry” the canon anathematizes: not the honouring of angels but the transfer to them of a mediatorship that belongs to Christ alone — “he has forsaken our Lord Jesus Christ… and has gone over to idolatry.” The invocation condemned is invocation in place of Christ, of a piece with the magical adjuration of named angels in the amulets and papyri, the very line Irenaeus had drawn against the church working “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations.”

The evidence for the narrow reading

The earliest witness localizes and dates the abuse — and reports lawful angel-devotion surviving alongside it. Theodoret does not read the canon as a universal prohibition; he reads it as aimed at a regional “vice” that “lasted for a long time in Phrygia and Pisidia,” and in the same breath he records that an oratory of St Michael “can be seen among them, and their neighbours” — i.e. the church that forbade the angel-cult was, in the same place, honouring the archangel Michael by name in a licit shrine. The condemnation and the veneration coexist because they are different things: one makes the angel a saviour to be invoked instead of Christ; the other honours a servant of God under God. This is the distinction the whole argument turns on, drawn by the canon’s own earliest interpreter.2

Modern scholarship reads canon 35 as the legislative echo of the Colossian heresy Paul had already rebuked. J. B. Lightfoot, in the classic study of the “Colossian heresy,” renders the canon — Christians must not “invoke angels (&aggr;&ggr;&ggr;&eacgr;&lgr;&ogr;&ugr;&sfgr; &ogr;ν&ogr;&mgr;&aacgr;ζ&egr;&igr;ν) and hold conventicles” — and identifies its target as the same cult that “three centuries before had called forth the Apostle’s rebuke and warning in the Epistle to the Colossians.” The Colossian error (Paul’s “worshipping of angels,” Colossians 2:18) treated angels as intermediary powers whose favour secured access to a transcendent God — approaching God through the angels rather than through Christ — which is precisely what the canon, and Theodoret after it, single out as a distinct heterodox cult localized in the Lycus valley and its hinterland.4

The church’s reception of the canon distinguishes licit angel-names from illicit ones. When the Frankish church took up the canon it read it exactly this way. The Capitulary issued under Charlemagne (789) invokes “that same council” of Laodicea to rule “that angels should not be given unknown names, and that such should not be affixed to them, but that only they should be named by the names which we have by authority. These are Michael, Gabriel, Raphael.” The prohibition, so received, falls on the invention of strange angel-names (the stock-in-trade of the adjuration-magic that filled the amulets with barbarous angel-words) and expressly permits the three names Scripture reveals. This is the same instinct Origen had voiced when he said the angelic names Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael are fitted “appropriately to the duties which they discharge” — a revealed name honoured, not a secret name conjured. The line is not naming-versus-not-naming; it is the revealed name held in reverence against the manufactured name worked as a spell.5

Legitimate Michael-devotion grew up beside the canon, with Christ’s sole mediatorship guarded. Far from extinguishing the honour of angels, the post-Nicene church built the great shrines of Michael — the Michaelion at Sosthenion near Constantinople, the archangel venerated there chiefly as healer, the synaxis of 8 November that spread through the Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic churches. And where the older Phrygian region kept its Michael-cult, the tradition took care to keep Christ central: at the healing spring near Colossae the sick were cured “invoking the Blessed Trinity and St. Michael” — the pairing that honours the archangel while leaving the invocation anchored in the Triune God. The church could forbid the angel-cult of canon 35 and foster the veneration of Michael at once because the two are opposites: one puts the angel in Christ’s place, the other keeps him in his own.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The narrow reading is the one the sources give in their own voice. The canon itself names the abuse — forsaking the church to “invoke angels and gather assemblies,” a rival cult with its own conventicles, which it calls “covert idolatry” because it “forsakes our Lord Jesus Christ.” Theodoret, the earliest interpreter, localizes it to Phrygia and Pisidia, dates it, and reports lawful Michael-oratories surviving among the same people; Lightfoot ties it to the Colossian heresy Paul had condemned; the Carolingian reception permits the revealed names and forbids the invented ones; and the church’s own Michael-devotion, guarded by the Trinity-and-Michael pairing, went on unbroken. Every line of evidence converges: what the canon anathematizes is the treatment of angels as mediators to be invoked in Christ’s stead, and the magical adjuration of them by manufactured names — not the honouring or naming of the angels God has revealed.

The rival reading, and why it is weaker. Taken as a bare sentence — “Christians must not… invoke angels” — the canon can be, and in Reformation polemic often was, pressed as a blanket condemnation of every invocation of angels, and so of the later Catholic and Orthodox cultus of Michael. But this reads the words apart from the abuse they name and against the whole reception. Theodoret, who stood nearest the practice, did not read it so, and set the surviving Michael-oratory beside the very canon; the same council that issued canon 35 approved the commemoration of the martyrs, which the blanket reading would equally have to forbid; and the churches that received the canon (Frankish, Byzantine) simultaneously honoured the archangels by name. A reading that makes a fourth-century Phrygian synod contradict its own age’s settled devotion, and that Theodoret’s eyewitness note directly refutes, is not the sense of the canon but a later use of it. (One genuine uncertainty lies upstream, at Colossae: Paul’s phrase “worshipping of angels” is itself debated — worship offered to angels, or a visionary’s claim to share the worship the angels render — but on either construal the Phrygian cult Theodoret describes, and the canon condemns, is angel-invocation that displaces Christ, which is what this page assesses.)

The wider tension the canon settles. The line canon 35 draws is the same one the whole doctrine polices: the Name (and the names heaven bears) has real power, yet is not a formula severable from faith in, and belonging to, the One named. To invoke an angel as saviour is to make the name a technique and the angel a rival mediator — the drift into magic that the intrinsic-power argument identifies and the sons of Sceva dramatize. To honour Michael under Christ, by the name Scripture gives, is devotion. The canon marks the boundary between them in law, exactly where the fathers had marked it in exegesis.

Assessment

sound. Canon 35 of Laodicea forbids a specific heterodox cult of angels — the Phrygian practice, already rebuked by Paul at Colossae, of making angels intermediary saviours whose favour must be sought to approach a too-distant God, and of invoking them by name in the manner of the adjuration-magic — and not the honouring or naming of angels as such. The earliest interpreter of the canon (Theodoret) localizes and dates the abuse and records lawful Michael-oratories surviving beside it; modern scholarship (Lightfoot) reads the canon as the legislative echo of the Colossian “worshipping of angels”; the church’s reception permits the revealed names Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael while forbidding invented ones; and licit Michael-devotion grew up unbroken, guarded by the pairing of the Trinity with the archangel. The blanket reading — that the canon bans all invocation of angels — lifts the bare words out of the abuse they name and out of the reception the canon actually had, and is the weaker of the two. The line the canon draws in law is the line the whole doctrine draws: the power of the Name is not a technique, and no name in heaven or earth may stand where Christ’s mediation stands. The magical and pagan angel-adjuration is present here only as the historical practice the canon was written to exclude, weighed critically and never as instruction.
