What did canon 35 of the Council of Laodicea forbid?

Disputed reading · on The Power of the Name · witness: the Council of Laodicea (canon 35)

Related readings: What tradition did the Jewish exorcists of Acts 19:13–16 stand in? · What do the Greek Magical Papyri’s uses of the name of Jesus show about Christian invocation of the Name?

The passage

Christians must not forsake the Church of God, and go away and invoke angels and gather assemblies, which things are forbidden. If, therefore, any one shall be found engaged in this covert idolatry, let him be anathema; for he has forsaken our Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, and has gone over to idolatry.

— Council of Laodicea, canon 35 (mid-4th c., Phrygia; Percival’s translation), NPNF2 vol. 14.1

The question

The canon forbids Christians to “invoke angels” (Greek angelous onomazein, “to name angels”) in assemblies apart from the church. Whether it condemns a particular regional cult of angels, the giving of unrevealed names to angels, or every invocation of angels is disputed; the answer bears on how the canon has been cited in later disputes over the honouring of angels.

Background: the practice of invoking angels by name

Phrygia held a large and old Jewish diaspora: Josephus reports that Antiochus III resettled some two thousand Jewish families from Babylonia into Lydia and Phrygia around 200 BC, and J. B. Lightfoot, from Cicero’s account of the gold confiscated at Laodicea c. 62 BC, estimated the adult Jewish freemen of the district at more than eleven thousand. Clinton E. Arnold (1995) holds that the “veneration of angels” in the Colossian region took place “in the context of magic, not only in pagan circles but also within Judaism, where monotheism still prevailed.” F. O. Francis (1962) reads the “worshipping of angels” of Colossians 2:18 as the worship the angels render, which a visionary claims to share, rather than worship offered to them, a reading Larry Hurtado has endorsed.2

The Sepher ha-Razim (“Book of the Mysteries”), a Jewish magical manual of about the fourth century, is built as a tour of the seven heavens, each stocked with ranks of angels, and catalogues the angels of each heaven with the names by which they are to be adjured and the ends they serve: to heal, to divine, to send or read dreams, to overturn a court judgement, to win at the races. Its preface traces the book from the angel Raziel down to Solomon.3 Aramaic amulets (inscribed metal lamellae from Palestine, Syria and Asia Minor) and incantation bowls (from Babylonia) of the fourth to seventh centuries, edited by Joseph Naveh and Shaul Shaked, adjure named angels — Suriel, Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and many lesser and invented names — to heal the wearer, bind a demon or compel a lover, the efficacy resting on knowledge of the powerful name. A large part of the Hekhalot (“palaces”) literature is given over to adjuration of the “Prince of the Torah” (Sar ha-Torah) or the “Prince of the Presence” (Sar ha-Panim) by seals inscribed with divine names, to obtain wisdom or mastery of Torah without the labour of study.4

Devotion to the archangel Michael grew up in the same centuries. The Catholic Encyclopedia (1911) records the Michaelion at Sosthenion near Constantinople, where Michael was venerated chiefly as healer, and the synaxis of 8 November kept in the Greek, Syriac, Armenian and Coptic churches; it reports a healing spring near Colossae where the sick were cured “invoking the Blessed Trinity and St. Michael,” and calls Phrygia “from early times the centre of the true cult of the holy angels.”5

The canon condemns a Phrygian cult that set angels in Christ’s place as mediators

On this reading the canon targets a regional heterodox cult, rooted in the error Paul addressed at Colossae, that treated angels as the necessary go-betweens to God.

Held by: Theodoret of Cyrus (Commentary on Colossians, 5th c.), on the canon’s Phrygian target; J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, St Paul’s Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon, 1875).6

The canon names a concrete practice: forsaking the church’s assembly to “go away and invoke angels and gather assemblies,” a rival cult with its own conventicles, called “covert idolatry” because its adherent “has forsaken our Lord Jesus Christ.” Theodoret, commenting on Colossians 2:18, traces the abuse from Colossae to this synod and places it on the map: the false teachers “were leading to worship angels… for, said they, the Law was given through angels. And this vice lasted for a long time in Phrygia and Pisidia. Therefore it was that the synod which met at Laodicea in Phrygia, prohibited by a canon, that prayer should be offered to angels; and even to-day an oratory of St. Michael can be seen among them, and their neighbours.” The Greek commentators on Colossians describe the mechanism of the cult: Chrysostom, “There are some who maintain that we must be brought near by Angels, not by Christ, that were too great a thing for us”; Theophylact, that the deceivers held it “unworthy… of the greatness of the Only-Begotten” to bring men to the Father, so “it is more reasonable to suppose that angels served in your bringing to the Father… and persuaded the simple-hearted to turn to them, as if to our saviors.”7 Lightfoot renders the canon — Christians must not “invoke angels (&aggr;&ggr;&ggr;&eacgr;&lgr;&ogr;&ugr;&sfgr; &ogr;ν&ogr;&mgr;&aacgr;ζ&egr;&igr;ν) and hold conventicles” — and identifies its target as the cult that “three centuries before had called forth the Apostle’s rebuke and warning in the Epistle to the Colossians.”8 Theodoret’s sentence also records an oratory of Michael standing in the region in his own day, and the Catholic Encyclopedia calls Phrygia “from early times the centre of the true cult of the holy angels.”5

The canon forbids giving angels names that have not been revealed

On this reading the canon’s “naming” of angels means assigning them unknown names, and it leaves the revealed names free for use.

Held by: the Capitulary of Charlemagne (Frankish, 789), as quoted in Percival’s edition.9

The Greek verb is onomazein, “to name,” and the Ancient Epitome of the canon keeps it: “Whoso calls assemblies in opposition to those of the Church and names angels, is near to idolatry.” The Frankish church, receiving the canon, read the naming as the giving of names. The Capitulary issued under Charlemagne in 789 invokes “that same council” to rule “that angels should not be given unknown names, and that such should not be affixed to them, but that only they should be named by the names which we have by authority. These are Michael, Gabriel, Raphael.” The prohibition so received falls on the invention of angel-names and expressly permits the three names Scripture gives. The editorial note in Percival’s edition suggests that the Capitulary’s framers may have had in mind the Roman council under Pope Zachary (745) against Aldebert, who invoked eight angels by name. The invented angel-names of the contemporary adjuration texts (see Background) are the kind of names this reading places under the ban. The three names the Capitulary allows are those Origen had described as given to angels “appropriately to the duties which they discharge.”10

The canon forbids all invocation of angels

On this reading the canon’s words apply to every invocation of angels, and so to any later cultus that prays to them.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.11

The canon’s prohibition is unqualified: Christians must not “invoke angels,” and the one who does is “engaged in this covert idolatry” and “has gone over to idolatry.” It names no region, no particular sect and no particular names. The Ancient Epitome treats the naming of angels itself as “near to idolatry.” Theodoret summarizes the canon as prohibiting “that prayer should be offered to angels,” a description of the act forbidden that is general in form. The canon’s ground is christological: invoking angels is treated as forsaking “our Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God.” On this reading the reason given covers any prayer addressed to angels, since the canon locates the offence in the act of invocation and not in the identity of those who practise it. Percival’s edition reports that “this canon has often been used in controversy as condemning the cultus” of the angels.1
