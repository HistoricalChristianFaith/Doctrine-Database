Gregory Palamas on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1340 (Gregory Palamas, monk of Athos and later archbishop of Thessalonica, c. 1296–1359; his Triads in defence of the hesychasts, c. 1338–1341) · Wikipedia
Summary: When the Calabrian monk Barlaam ridiculed the Athonite monks who invoked the name of Jesus with head bowed and breath controlled, Palamas defended their method — the single-phrased Jesus Prayer, the body drawn into prayer, the vision of the uncreated light — as, in his account, true union with God. In his writings the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” is named and defended as such.

Claims & evidence

The “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” purifies and orders the intellect

In those devoted to prayer, and especially to the single-phrased Jesus Prayer, the intellect’s noetic activity is easily ordered and purified…

— Three Texts on Prayer and Purity of Heart, text 3 (The Philokalia, vol. 4, trans. Palmer, Sherrard & Ware), source.1 Palamas names the practice: in those devoted to the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” — the short, unvarying invocation of the Name — the intellect, he writes, is “easily ordered and purified.”

The body drawn into prayer — the defence of the hesychast method

On the other hand, it is not out of place to teach people, especially beginners, that they should look at themselves, and introduce their own mind within themselves through control of breathing.

— The Triads, I.2 (ed. Meyendorff, trans. Nicholas Gendle, Classics of Western Spirituality), source.2 Against Barlaam’s mockery of the monks who “bring their mind within themselves” by the breath, Palamas insists the body is no obstacle to prayer but is drawn into it — the physical discipline of the Jesus Prayer defended as fitting, “especially for beginners.” In the same passage he quotes the inherited hesychast method he is defending: “Have no other occupation or meditation than the cry of: ‘Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me!’ Under no circumstances give yourself any rest.” Palamas quotes the formula from an earlier text, the Method of Holy Prayer and Attentiveness, which is ascribed to Symeon the New Theologian and to Nikephoros the Hesychast.

Notes

  • Gregory Palamas is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 1340, after Bernard of Clairvaux (c. 1140) and before Bernardine of Siena (c. 1420). The hesychast controversy was taken up at the Councils of Constantinople (1341–1351). Palamas defends the practice against the charge that bodily prayer and the vision of the divine light are illusion, grounding it in his distinction between God’s unknowable essence and His uncreated energies, in which, on his teaching, the one who prays truly shares.
  • The formula “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me” that Palamas quotes also appears in The Way of a Pilgrim (c. 1860).
