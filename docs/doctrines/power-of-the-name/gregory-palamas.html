Gregory Palamas on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1340 (Gregory Palamas, monk of Athos and later archbishop of Thessalonica, c. 1296–1359; his Triads in defence of the hesychasts, c. 1338–1341) · Wikipedia
Summary: The theological vindication of the practice Diadochos and John Climacus had handed down. When the Calabrian monk Barlaam ridiculed the Athonite monks who invoked the name of Jesus with head bowed and breath controlled, Palamas defended the whole method — the single-phrased Jesus Prayer, the body drawn into prayer, the uncreated light — as genuine union with God. In him the Byzantine invocation of the Name receives its dogmatic charter; the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” is now named and defended as such.

Claims & evidence

The “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” purifies and orders the intellect

In those devoted to prayer, and especially to the single-phrased Jesus Prayer, the intellect’s noetic activity is easily ordered and purified…

— Three Texts on Prayer and Purity of Heart, text 3 (The Philokalia, vol. 4, trans. Palmer, Sherrard & Ware), source. Here, in his own voice, Palamas names the practice the tradition had been building toward: the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer” — the short, unvarying invocation of the Name — is set apart as the surest ordering and purifying of the praying mind. The devotional power Diadochos located in “Lord Jesus” and Climacus in the breath is now a settled category of Orthodox spiritual theology.

The body drawn into prayer — the defence of the hesychast method

On the other hand, it is not out of place to teach people, especially beginners, that they should look at themselves, and introduce their own mind within themselves through control of breathing.

— The Triads, I.2 (ed. Meyendorff, trans. Nicholas Gendle, Classics of Western Spirituality), source. Against Barlaam’s mockery of the monks who “bring their mind within themselves” by the breath, Palamas insists the body is no obstacle to prayer but is drawn into it — the physical discipline of the Jesus Prayer defended as fitting, “especially for beginners.” In the same passage he quotes the inherited hesychast method he is defending: “Have no other occupation or meditation than the cry of: ‘Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me!’ Under no circumstances give yourself any rest.” This is the full formula of the Jesus Prayer as it had by now crystallized — the words Palamas received from the earlier masters (the Method of Holy Prayer and Attentiveness ascribed to Symeon the New Theologian and to Nikephoros the Hesychast), not his own coinage.

Notes

  • Gregory Palamas is seated here on the power of the Name timeline as the theological climax of the Byzantine Jesus Prayer: the fourteenth-century defence, at the Councils of Constantinople (1341–1351), of the practice Diadochos and Climacus had transmitted. Where they describe the invocation of the Name, Palamas vindicates it — against the charge that bodily prayer and the vision of the divine light are illusion — grounding it in his distinction between God’s unknowable essence and His uncreated energies, in which the one who invokes the Name truly shares.
  • The “cry of ‘Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me’” that Palamas quotes is the fixed formula that The Way of a Pilgrim would carry to a wide readership five centuries later — the next witness on this timeline.
  • Whether the continual invocation of the Name is a distinct spirituality of the Name or ordinary prayer intensified — and, in the twentieth-century Athonite onomatodoxy (imiaslavie) dispute, whether the Name of God is itself divine — is weighed among this doctrine’s arguments; the practice Palamas defended is the one those later disputes are about.
