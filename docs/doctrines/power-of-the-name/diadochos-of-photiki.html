Diadochos of Photike on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 486 (Diadochos, bishop of Photike in Epirus, c. 400–c. 486; his On Spiritual Knowledge and Discrimination: One Hundred Texts was written mid-fifth century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest clear witness to the devotional turn the timeline now takes: where Justin, Irenaeus, Origen, and Athanasius wield the Name to expel demons, Diadochos gives the intellect the Name to hold — the continual inward invocation “Lord Jesus” as the whole occupation of the praying mind. His hundred Texts are the seed of the Byzantine Jesus Prayer: the Name repeated ceaselessly “in the depths of the heart,” until its remembrance is as natural as a child calling for its father.

Claims & evidence

Give the intellect “nothing but the prayer ‘Lord Jesus’”

When we have blocked all its outlets by means of the remembrance of God, the intellect requires of us imperatively some task which will satisfy its need for activity. For the complete fulfillment of its purpose we should give it nothing but the prayer ‘Lord Jesus’. ‘No one’, it is written, ‘can say ‘Lord Jesus’ except in the Holy Spirit’ (1 Cor. 12:3). Let the intellect continually concentrate on these words within its inner shrine with such intensity that it is not turned aside to any mental images. Those who meditate unceasingly upon this glorious and holy name in the depths of their heart can sometimes see the light of their own intellect.

— On Spiritual Knowledge and Discrimination, Text 59 (The Philokalia, vol. 1, trans. Palmer, Sherrard & Ware), source. This is the doctrine’s pivot into interior prayer. The Name is no longer chiefly a word spoken over a demoniac but a word held within the praying mind — “these words” concentrated “within its inner shrine,” “in the depths of their heart.” Diadochos anchors it in 1 Corinthians 12:3 (only in the Spirit can one say “Lord Jesus”), so the invocation is no bare technique but the Spirit’s own confession made continual.

Grace itself repeats “Lord Jesus” with the soul — as a mother teaches a child

The soul now has grace itself to share its meditation and to repeat with it the words ‘Lord Jesus’, just as a mother teaches her child to repeat with her the word ‘father’, instead of prattling in his usual way, until she has formed in him the habit of calling for his father even in his sleep.

— On Spiritual Knowledge and Discrimination, Text 61 (The Philokalia, vol. 1), source. The image guards the practice against the very charge the earlier fathers pressed against the magicians: the Name is not a formula the operator works, but a word grace teaches the soul to say — until, like the child calling “father” even in sleep, the remembrance of Jesus becomes unceasing and involuntary, the beginning of what the later tradition calls prayer of the heart.

“Calls ceaselessly upon the Lord Jesus” — the Name kept unceasingly in the heart

If, then, a man begins to make progress in keeping the commandments and calls ceaselessly upon the Lord Jesus, the fire of God’s grace spreads even to the heart’s more outward organs of perception, consciously burning up the tares in the field of the soul.

These effects of grace are always present in those who are approaching perfection and have the remembrance of the Lord Jesus unceasingly in their hearts.

— On Spiritual Knowledge and Discrimination, Texts 85 and 88 (The Philokalia, vol. 1), source. The ceaseless calling on “the Lord Jesus” is now the mark of the maturing soul — a “remembrance of the Lord Jesus unceasingly” that fulfils Paul’s “pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17), the same text that would later set the pilgrim of nineteenth-century Russia on the road. The power once seen in the routing of demons is here interior and transforming: the grace carried in the Name “burns up the tares” of the soul.

Notes

  • Diadochos is seated here as the earliest father on the power of the Name timeline to give the Name a sustained devotional use — the hinge between the exorcistic power argued by Justin, Irenaeus, Origen, and Athanasius and the Byzantine Jesus Prayer that follows him (John Climacus, the Sinai fathers, Gregory Palamas). His hundred Texts are quoted in every later stratum of the tradition and stand at the head of The Philokalia’s teaching on inward prayer.
  • Diadochos does not yet give the full later formula (“Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me”); his invocation is the shorter “Lord Jesus.” The lengthening of the phrase and its binding to the breath belong to the Sinai tradition (John Climacus, Hesychios) and after.
  • The later controversy over whether the continual invocation of the Name is a distinct spirituality of the Name — and, in its twentieth-century Athonite form, whether the Name of God is itself divine (the onomatodoxy / imiaslavie dispute) — is weighed among this doctrine’s arguments; Diadochos’s inward “Lord Jesus” is the practice those disputes would later be about.
