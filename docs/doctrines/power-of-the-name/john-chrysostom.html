John Chrysostom on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 400 (the Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles, preached at Constantinople c. 400–401, with the earlier Baptismal Instructions given at Antioch c. 388–390; Chrysostom c. 349–407) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching on the sons of Sceva, Chrysostom explains their rout in one line — “then not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith” — and lays the failure on the men, not the Name: the demon is made to confess Christ first, “that there may not seem to be any slight put upon the Name of Jesus.” The same principle runs through his catechetical preaching, where he warns that even the demons uttered the name of God and were still demons, and refuses to excuse the enchantress who “utters nothing else than the name of God.” He tells the convert to go nowhere without the renunciation of Satan and the sign of the cross on his forehead — armour with which “even the devil himself” will be unable to hurt him — and sets the cross against the amulets and charms his hearers wore.

Claims & evidence

“Then not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith” — the sons of Sceva

“Then certain of the vagabond Jews, exorcists, took upon them to call over them which had evil spirits the name of the Lord Jesus, saying, We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth.” So entirely did they do all by way of trade! Observe: vagabond, or, itinerant, Jewish exorcists. And to believe indeed, they had no mind; but by that Name they wished to cast out the demons… They did it in secret: then their impotence is publicly exposed. Then not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith… For, that there may not seem to be any slight put upon the Name of Jesus, (the demon) first confesses (Him), and then has permission given him. For, to show that it was not any weakness of the Name, but all owing to the imposture of those men, why did not the same take place in the case of Paul?

— Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles, Homily XLI (on Acts 19:13–20), source.1 In Chrysostom’s account the power belongs to the Name, but the Name does nothing unless it is spoken with faith. He reads the exorcists as men “of trade” — they “had no mind” to believe but wished to work the demons “by that Name” — and compares them to Simon Magus, who “for the sake of merchandise sought the grace of the Spirit”2 — joining the attempted purchase of Acts 8 to the traded adjuration of Acts 19. He insists that the scene put no “slight… upon the Name”: the demon is compelled to own Christ (“Jesus I know, and Paul I know”) before it is given leave to overpower the men, so that, he argues, the rout is owing to “the imposture of those men,” not to “any weakness of the Name” — and the demon spared Paul, with whom, on Chrysostom’s reading, the Name was named in faith.

“Even the demons uttered the name of God, but still they were demons” — the enchantress who uses the name of God

Chrysostom makes the same point in his catechetical preaching, against converts who excused the village enchantress on the ground that she “utters nothing else than the name of God”:

When we deliver these exhortations, and lead them away, thinking that they defend themselves, they say, that the woman is a Christian who makes these incantations, and utters nothing else than the name of God. On this account I especially hate and turn away from her, because she makes use of the name of God, with a view to ribaldry. For even the demons uttered the name of God, but still they were demons, and thus they used to say to Christ, “We know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God,” and notwithstanding, he rebuked them, and drave them away.

— Instructions to Catechumens, Second Instruction, §5, source.3 Chrysostom applies to daily life the principle he draws from the sons of Sceva: on his argument, uttering the divine Name is no guarantee of its power, for the demons themselves confessed the “Holy One of God” and were driven off all the same. To use the Name for “ribaldry” — as an incantation — is, for him, to profane it.

“The sign of the cross on thy forehead… even the devil himself will be unable to hurt you” — the renunciation and the cross as everyday armour

Against those same charms Chrysostom sets the Christian’s own weapons — the renunciation of Satan spoken at the threshold and the sign of the cross traced on the brow — and promises them a real, apotropaic force:

Keep hold of this word as a staff… when thou art about to pass over the threshold of the gateway, say this word first: I leave thy ranks, Satan, and thy pomp, and thy service, and I join the ranks of Christ. And never go forth without this word. This shall be a staff to thee, this thine armor, this an impregnable fortress, and accompany this word with the sign of the cross on thy forehead. For thus not only a man who meets you, but even the devil himself, will be unable to hurt you at all, when he sees thee everywhere appearing with these weapons.

— Instructions to Catechumens, Second Instruction, §5, source.4 The renunciation formula (“I leave thy ranks, Satan”) and the sealing of the forehead are the same baptismal gestures Cyril of Jerusalem expounds for the rite of initiation, but Chrysostom carries them out of the baptistery into the street: the convert is to make the sign and say the word every time he crosses his own gateway, and the enemy who sees him “everywhere appearing with these weapons” cannot touch him. In the same instruction he sets the sign of the cross against the talismans of the day: those who “use charms and amulets, and encircle their heads and feet with golden coins of Alexander of Macedon” have forgotten “what great result the cross has achieved” — it “has abolished death, has extinguished sin, has made Hades useless, has undone the power of the devil.” The Christian’s armour is the cross of Christ, not “an image of a Greek king.”5

Notes

  • Chrysostom is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 400, after Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) and before Augustine of Hippo (c. 415). The Homilies on Acts were preached at Constantinople c. 400–401; the Baptismal Instructions quoted here belong to his Antioch years (c. 388–390). The timeline uses c. 400.
  • The sentence on the sons of Sceva. Chrysostom puts the distinction in one sentence — “not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith” — and adds that the rout is “not any weakness of the Name, but all owing to the imposture of those men.”
  • Magic in pastoral practice. In the catechetical instructions the enchantress who “utters… the name of God” is still, for Chrysostom, working incantations (the demons said as much and were cast out), and the amulet and the “golden coins of Alexander” are set against the cross and the renunciation.
  • The sign of the cross in daily life. Chrysostom carries the forehead-sealing and the renunciation from the baptismal rite into the convert’s daily comings and goings.
  • Chrysostom appears on this wiki also on other timelines; this page is confined to his teaching on the power of the Name.
