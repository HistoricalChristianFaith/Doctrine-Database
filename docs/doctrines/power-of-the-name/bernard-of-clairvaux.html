Bernard of Clairvaux on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1140 (Sermon 15 of the Sermons on the Song of Songs, the great series preached and revised at Clairvaux c. 1135–1153; Bernard c. 1090–1153) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bernard is the fountainhead of the medieval Western devotion to the Holy Name of Jesus. Where the earlier witnesses argue the Name’s power in exorcism and the Byzantine tradition keeps it as the continual invocation of the praying heart, Bernard sounds a third note — the sweetness of the Name. Preaching on the Song of Songs’ “thy name is as ointment poured forth” (1:3), he reads the Name of the Bridegroom as oil with a threefold property: “it gives light, it nourishes, it anoints… When preached it gives light, when meditated it nourishes, when invoked it relieves and soothes” — light, food, and medicine. He gives the devotion its most quoted line — “Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart” — and presses the Name as a medicine for every ill of the soul: the sad, the despairing, the tempted have only to invoke it. This is the strain the Franciscan Bernardine of Siena would carry into the streets three centuries later, and the lyric heart of the hymn Jesu dulcis memoria long ascribed to Bernard.

Claims & evidence

“It gives light, it nourishes, it anoints” — the Name as oil poured out: light, food, medicine

Bernard takes his text from the bride’s praise of the Bridegroom, “thy name is as ointment poured forth” (Song of Songs 1:3), and asks why the Holy Spirit should liken a name to oil. His answer is the governing image of the whole sermon — and of the Western devotion it founds:

Unless you can persuade me otherwise, I hold that the likeness is to be found in the threefold property of oil: it gives light, it nourishes, it anoints. It feeds the flame, it nourishes the body, it relieves pain: it is light, food, medicine. And is not this true too of the Bridegroom’s name? When preached it gives light, when meditated it nourishes, when invoked it relieves and soothes. Let us consider each point.

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15 (“The Name of Jesus”), §5, source.1 The whole sermon turns on the outpouring of the Name — “O blessed name, oil poured out without limit!” — poured “from heaven… on Judea and from there over all the earth,” so that “all beings in the heavens, on earth and in the underworld should bend the knee in the name of Jesus” (Philippians 2:10). Bernard is not arguing the Name’s power against magicians, as the fathers did, but expounding its savour to monks who already possess it: the Name that the earlier witnesses wield against demons he draws inward as the soul’s light, food, and healing.

“Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart” — the Name as food

Working through the three properties, Bernard comes to the Name as nourishment — and gives the devotion the single line by which it is best remembered:

But the name of Jesus is more than light, it is also food. Do you not feel increase of strength as often as you remember it?… Every food of the mind is dry if it is not dipped in that oil; it is tasteless if not seasoned by that salt. Write what you will, I shall not relish it unless it tells of Jesus. Talk or argue about what you will, I shall not relish it if you exclude the name of Jesus. Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart.

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15, §6, source.2 This is the sweetness-of-the-Name strain in its purest form. It is the same continual keeping of the Name in the heart that Diadochos of Photike and the Byzantine hesychasts taught — the remembrance of Jesus that never leaves the mind — but sung in a Western, affective register: not the warfare of the praying heart but its delight.

“For all the ills and disorders to which flesh is heir, this name is medicine”

The third property is the Name as remedy, and here Bernard is at his most pastoral, running through the soul’s afflictions one by one:

Again, it is a medicine. Does one of us feel sad? Let the name of Jesus come into his heart, from there let it spring to his mouth, so that shining like the dawn it may dispel all darkness and make a cloudless sky. Does someone fall into sin? Does his despair even urge him to suicide? Let him but invoke this life-giving name and his will to live will be at once renewed… In short, for all the ills and disorders to which flesh is heir, this name is medicine. For proof we have no less than his own promise: “Call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me.”

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15, §6, source.3 The Name curbs anger and pride, “cures the wound of envy… and quenches the flame of lust”; Bernard tells the soul to keep it “hidden as in a vase… always close to your heart, always in your hand.” The apotropaic power the fathers claimed over demons is here interiorized as a therapy of the passions — the Name invoked not to expel a spirit from a body but to heal the disordered heart. It is the same power under a new description, and it is the note the later Holy Name devotion would amplify.

Notes

  • Bernard is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 1140, after John Climacus and the Sinai tradition (c. 630) and before Gregory Palamas (c. 1340). The chronology places him inside the long Byzantine hesychast arc, but his devotion is the Western parallel to it: the same continual love of the Name, sounded in a Latin, affective key rather than as the disciplined breath-prayer of the Christian East.
  • The fountainhead of the Western Holy Name devotion. Where the fathers argue the Name’s power in exorcism and apology, and the Byzantines keep it as the Jesus Prayer, Bernard opens the medieval Western strain: the sweetness (dulcedo) of the Name of Jesus, meditated for its savour. This is the devotion the Franciscan Bernardine of Siena made civic and public three centuries later, raising the IHS tablet for veneration — his Sermon on the Holy Name inherits Bernard’s theme directly, and the wider devotion issues in the Feast of the Most Holy Name and the Holy Name Society.
  • The hymn Jesu dulcis memoria. The medieval hymn Jesu dulcis memoria (“Jesu, the very thought of thee / With sweetness fills my breast,” in Edward Caswall’s 1849 rendering) is the lyric heart of this same sweetness-of-the-Name devotion and was long ascribed to Bernard. The attribution is now doubted: the manuscript tradition points instead to an anonymous English Cistercian of the late twelfth century, and Bernard’s name attaches only from the late 1200s. The hymn is a fit witness to the devotion Bernard founded even if not from his own hand.4
  • The mechanism, not the boundary. Bernard does not press the anti-magic boundary the rest of the timeline polices — he writes for monks who already hold the Name in faith, not against sorcerers who would work it as a spell. But his teaching sits squarely on the wiki’s finding that the Name’s power is the power of the Person invoked (sound): the Name is “light, food, medicine” because “he himself is poured out” — the efficacy is Christ’s, laid hold of by the soul that names him in love, never a property of the syllables.
  • Bernard appears on this wiki also on other timelines; this page is confined to his teaching on the power and sweetness of the Name.
