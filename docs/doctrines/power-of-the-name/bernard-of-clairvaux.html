Bernard of Clairvaux on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1140 (Sermon 15 of the Sermons on the Song of Songs, the great series preached and revised at Clairvaux c. 1135–1153; Bernard c. 1090–1153) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where the earlier witnesses on this timeline argue the Name’s power in exorcism and the Byzantine writers keep it as the continual invocation of the praying heart, Bernard writes of the sweetness of the Name. Preaching on the Song of Songs’ “thy name is as ointment poured forth” (1:3), he reads the Name of the Bridegroom as oil with a threefold property: “it gives light, it nourishes, it anoints… When preached it gives light, when meditated it nourishes, when invoked it relieves and soothes” — light, food, and medicine. He writes “Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart,” and commends the Name as a medicine for every ill of the soul: the sad, the despairing, the tempted have only to invoke it. The hymn Jesu dulcis memoria was long ascribed to him.

Claims & evidence

“It gives light, it nourishes, it anoints” — the Name as oil poured out: light, food, medicine

Bernard takes his text from the bride’s praise of the Bridegroom, “thy name is as ointment poured forth” (Song of Songs 1:3), and asks why the Holy Spirit should liken a name to oil. His answer is the governing image of the sermon:

Unless you can persuade me otherwise, I hold that the likeness is to be found in the threefold property of oil: it gives light, it nourishes, it anoints. It feeds the flame, it nourishes the body, it relieves pain: it is light, food, medicine. And is not this true too of the Bridegroom’s name? When preached it gives light, when meditated it nourishes, when invoked it relieves and soothes. Let us consider each point.

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15 (“The Name of Jesus”), §5, source.1 The whole sermon turns on the outpouring of the Name — “O blessed name, oil poured out without limit!” — poured “from heaven… on Judea and from there over all the earth,” so that “all beings in the heavens, on earth and in the underworld should bend the knee in the name of Jesus” (Philippians 2:10). Bernard addresses monks, expounding the Name’s savour as the soul’s light, food, and healing.

“Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart” — the Name as food

Working through the three properties, Bernard comes to the Name as nourishment:

But the name of Jesus is more than light, it is also food. Do you not feel increase of strength as often as you remember it?… Every food of the mind is dry if it is not dipped in that oil; it is tasteless if not seasoned by that salt. Write what you will, I shall not relish it unless it tells of Jesus. Talk or argue about what you will, I shall not relish it if you exclude the name of Jesus. Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart.

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15, §6, source.2 Bernard’s emphasis here is the delight of the Name, which gives “increase of strength as often as you remember it”; compare the continual remembrance of Jesus taught by Diadochos of Photike and the Byzantine hesychasts.

“For all the ills and disorders to which flesh is heir, this name is medicine”

The third property is the Name as remedy, and Bernard runs through the soul’s afflictions one by one:

Again, it is a medicine. Does one of us feel sad? Let the name of Jesus come into his heart, from there let it spring to his mouth, so that shining like the dawn it may dispel all darkness and make a cloudless sky. Does someone fall into sin? Does his despair even urge him to suicide? Let him but invoke this life-giving name and his will to live will be at once renewed… In short, for all the ills and disorders to which flesh is heir, this name is medicine. For proof we have no less than his own promise: “Call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me.”

— Sermons on the Song of Songs, Sermon 15, §6, source.3 The Name curbs anger and pride, “cures the wound of envy… and quenches the flame of lust”; Bernard tells the soul to keep it “hidden as in a vase… always close to your heart, always in your hand.” In this sermon the Name is invoked against the passions rather than over a demoniac.

Notes

  • Bernard is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 1140, after John Climacus and the Sinai tradition (c. 630) and before Gregory Palamas (c. 1340). His Latin, affective treatment of the Name stands beside the Byzantine breath-prayer of the same centuries.
  • Later Western Holy Name devotion. Bernard writes of the sweetness (dulcedo) of the Name of Jesus, meditated for its savour. The Franciscan Bernardine of Siena (c. 1420) preached the Holy Name in public and raised the IHS tablet for veneration; the Feast of the Most Holy Name and the Holy Name Society are recorded on their own page.
  • The hymn Jesu dulcis memoria. The medieval hymn Jesu dulcis memoria (“Jesu, the very thought of thee / With sweetness fills my breast,” in Edward Caswall’s 1849 rendering), a hymn on the sweetness of the Name, was long ascribed to Bernard. The Hymnology Archive, following Dom André Wilmart, assigns it to an anonymous English Cistercian of the late twelfth century, with Bernard’s name attaching to it only from the late 1200s.4
  • The Name and the Person. Bernard writes for monks rather than against sorcerers, and the sermon does not take up the question of magic. In it the Name of the Bridegroom is poured out “since he himself is poured out” (§4), and the Name is “light, food, medicine” (§5).1
  • Bernard appears on this wiki also on other timelines; this page is confined to his teaching on the power and sweetness of the Name.
