John Climacus and the Sinai tradition on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 630 (John Climacus, abbot of Sinai, c. 579–649; his Ladder of Divine Ascent) · Wikipedia
Summary: At Sinai the inward “Lord Jesus” of Diadochos is bound to the body’s own rhythm: John Climacus makes the remembrance of Jesus a thing of every breath, and calls the Name the strongest weapon in heaven or earth for the soul’s warfare. His younger contemporary Hesychios of Sinai carries the same teaching — the name of Jesus made to “adhere to your breath” — so that the two mark the moment the Jesus Prayer becomes a discipline of breath and heart, midway between Diadochos and Gregory Palamas.

Claims & evidence

“Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with your every breath”

Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with your every breath. Then indeed you will appreciate the value of stillness.

— The Ladder of Divine Ascent, Step 27 (on stillness), trans. Colm Luibheid & Norman Russell (Classics of Western Spirituality), source. The older Holy Transfiguration / Lazarus Moore rendering reads: “Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with each breath, and then you will know the value of solitude” (Step 27:61), source. This is the line the whole later tradition quotes for uniting the Name to the breath: the invocation is not an occasional exercise but a continual thing, as constant and as involuntary as breathing itself — the discipline of hesychia (stillness) that gives the Byzantine practice its other name, hesychasm.

“Flog your enemies with the name of Jesus” — the Name as the soul’s weapon

As you go on your way, arm yourself with prayer. When you reach the place, stretch out your hands. Flog your enemies with the name of Jesus, for there is no stronger weapon in heaven or earth.

— The Ladder of Divine Ascent, Step 21 (on cowardice), trans. Archimandrite Lazarus Moore, source. Here the old exorcistic power of the Name is drawn inward to the ascetic’s warfare against fear and the demons that provoke it. The Name still routs spirits — the note Justin, Irenaeus, and Athanasius sounded — but the battlefield is now the praying heart, and the weapon is the invoked Name itself: “there is no stronger weapon in heaven or earth.”

Notes

  • Hesychios of Sinai (a later abbot of the same Sinai monastery, dated by the Philokalia translators to the eighth–ninth century) carries John’s teaching forward in On Watchfulness and Holiness: the watchful heart “breathes and invokes, endlessly and without ceasing, only Jesus Christ who is the Son of God and Himself God” (Text 5), and — echoing Climacus — “lash the enemy with the name of Jesus and, as a certain wise man has said, let the name of Jesus adhere to your breath, and then you will know the blessings of stillness” (Text 100). Source (The Philokalia, vol. 1, Palmer/Sherrard/Ware). The “certain wise man” Hesychios cites is Climacus himself — the Sinai tradition quoting its own abbot.
  • John Climacus is seated here on the power of the Name timeline as the Sinai stage of the Jesus Prayer’s development: after Diadochos’s inward “Lord Jesus” and before Gregory Palamas’s fourteenth-century theological defense. The binding of the Name to the breath, first clear here, becomes the hesychast method Palamas would have to defend against Barlaam.
  • Like Diadochos, Climacus gives the shorter invocation (“the name of Jesus,” “the remembrance of Jesus”), not yet the fixed later formula “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me,” which is stabilized in the hesychast Method quoted by Palamas and in The Way of a Pilgrim.
