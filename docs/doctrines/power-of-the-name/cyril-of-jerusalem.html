Cyril of Jerusalem on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 350 (the Catechetical Lectures, delivered to the candidates for baptism at Jerusalem during Lent, with the Mystagogical Catecheses expounding the rite in Easter week; Cyril c. 313–386) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril gives the doctrine its liturgical face. Where Justin, Tertullian, Origen, and Athanasius argue the Name’s power in public exorcism and apology, Cyril shows it enacted in the church’s own rite of initiation: the pre-baptismal exorcisms of the catechumenate that put “the hostile demon” to flight, the renunciation of Satan (“I renounce thee, Satan”) spoken facing the West, and baptism “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.” His governing statement of the mechanism is a plain one — “the invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch and drive out evil spirits” — so that the same power the earlier witnesses claim is here the power the whole rite turns on: the Name is named over the convert, and the enemy is driven off.

Claims & evidence

“Receive with earnestness the exorcisms… the hostile demon flees away” — the exorcisms of the catechumenate

Let thy feet hasten to the catechisings; receive with earnestness the exorcisms: whether thou be breathed upon or exorcised, the act is to thee salvation… Even so without exorcisms the soul cannot be purified; and these exorcisms are divine, having been collected out of the divine Scriptures… even so when the exorcists inspire terror by the Spirit of God, and set the soul, as it were, on fire in the crucible of the body, the hostile demon flees away, and there abide salvation and the hope of eternal life, and the soul henceforth is cleansed from its sins and hath salvation.

— Procatechesis (Prologue to the Catechetical Lectures), §9, source.1 Before the convert is baptized he is exorcised, repeatedly, through the weeks of Lent. Cyril grounds the power not in the exorcist but in “the Spirit of God” and in the Scriptures the exorcisms are “collected out of”: the exorcist merely “inspire[s] terror,” and the demon — not the man — is the one who acts, fleeing. This is the doctrine’s recurring boundary kept in the rite itself: the power is real and it drives out spirits, yet it is the Spirit’s and not the operator’s (see the argument Is the Name’s power intrinsic to the words, or the power of the Person invoked?, sound).

“I renounce thee, Satan” — the renunciation, and the Lamb’s blood as “the charm to scare evil spirits”

On the night of baptism the candidate first entered the vestibule of the baptistery, turned to face the West — “the region of sensible darkness” where the “dark and gloomy potentate” has his dominion — and, with arm outstretched as though Satan stood present, renounced him aloud:

First ye entered into the vestibule of the Baptistery, and there facing towards the West ye listened to the command to stretch forth your hand, and as in the presence of Satan ye renounced him… But nevertheless thou art bidden to say, with arm outstretched towards him as though he were present, “I renounce thee, Satan.”

— Catechetical Lecture XIX (First Lecture on the Mysteries), §§2 and 4, source.2 Cyril reads the whole scene through the Exodus: as the blood of the paschal lamb on the doorposts made “the destroyer flee from the houses,” so now “the blood of the Lamb without blemish Jesus Christ is made the charm to scare evil spirits,” and “the daring and shameless spirit, the author of evil,” pursuing the candidate “even to the very streams of salvation,” “disappears in the water of salvation.”3 The renunciation is thus itself an act of the Name’s power: the enemy is not merely disowned but routed. Having renounced Satan “and all his works… and all his pomp… and all his service” — and Cyril lists the “service of the devil” as the very magic the doctrine condemns: “the watching of birds, divination, omens, or amulets, or charms written on leaves, sorceries, or other evil arts” — the candidate then turned from West to East and made his confession.

“I believe in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost” — the confession that answers the renunciation

When therefore thou renouncest Satan, utterly breaking all thy covenant with him, that ancient league with hell, there is opened to thee the paradise of God… and a symbol of this was thy turning from West to East, the place of lights. Then thou weft told to say, “I believe in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost, and in one Baptism of repentance.”

— Catechetical Lecture XIX, §9, source.4 The naming of the Three is the counterweight to the renunciation of the one: the enemy is put off, and the Name is put on. Cyril closes the lecture with 1 Peter, the reading appointed for the day — “our adversary the devil… as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour” — “but… at the holy Layer of regeneration God has wiped away every tear.” The Name confessed over the water is the answer to the lion.

“The invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch and drive out evil spirits” — the mechanism stated, and baptism in the Name

The exorcised oil therefore was a symbol of the participation of the fatness of Christ, being a charm to drive away every trace of hostile influence. For as the breathing of the saints, and the invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch and drive out evil spirits, so also this exorcised oil receives such virtue by the invocation of God and by prayer, as not only to burn and cleanse away the traces of sins, but also to chase away all the invisible powers of the evil one.

— Catechetical Lecture XX (Second Lecture on the Mysteries: Of Baptism), §3, source.5 This is Cyril’s plainest statement of the whole doctrine: “the invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch[es] and drive[s] out evil spirits.” The Name is not one instrument among several; it is the fire before which the spirits cannot stand. And the naming reaches its climax at the font itself, where the candidate was “asked, whether he believed in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost,” made “that saving confession, and descended three times into the water.”6 So the Name that routs the demon in the exorcism is the same Name confessed and named over the convert in the water — the corporate, ritual invocation, over against the private brandishing the doctrine elsewhere polices.

Notes

  • Cyril is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 350, after Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 318) and before Diadochos of Photike (c. 486). The Catechetical Lectures were delivered to the baptismal candidates at Jerusalem, most likely c. 348–350; the timeline uses c. 350.
  • The liturgical face of the doctrine. Cyril’s witness is not an argument about the Name’s power but a description of it at work in the church’s rite. The exorcisms, the renunciation, and the baptismal confession are the doctrine translated from apologetic claim into corporate practice — the Name named over the convert by the church, not wielded as a private formula. This is the thread the leads flag as the doctrine’s liturgical dimension; the narrower sub-claim about the efficacy of the baptismal “in the name of” formula is weighed on its own among this doctrine’s arguments.
  • The boundary against magic, kept in the rite. Cyril draws the same line every earlier witness draws, and draws it inside the liturgy: the power that drives out the demon is “the Spirit of God” and “the invocation of the Name of God… and… prayer,” not a technique the exorcist owns; and among the works the candidate must renounce as “the service of the devil” Cyril names divination, amulets, “charms written on leaves, sorceries, [and] other evil arts.” The Name’s power and the condemnation of name-magic stand together, as they do from Irenaeus on.
  • A note on the Mystagogical Catecheses. Lectures XIX–XXIII (the Mystagogical Catecheses, which supply the quotations above on the renunciation and baptism) are transmitted under Cyril’s name; a minority of scholars have assigned them to his successor John of Jerusalem (d. 417), which would date them a generation later. The manuscript tradition and most editors keep them Cyril’s, and even on the later attribution they remain a Jerusalem witness of the same era; the substance — the Name named over convert and enemy in the rite — is unaffected either way.
  • Cyril appears on this wiki also on other timelines (e.g. the eucharistic real presence, which his Lecture XXIII expounds); this page is confined to his teaching on the power of the Name.
