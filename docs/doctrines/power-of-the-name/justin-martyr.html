Justin Martyr on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 160 (Justin Martyr, c. 100–165; the Dialogue with Trypho and Second Apology) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest father to make the exorcistic power of the Name a public, repeatable argument. Writing around 160, Justin appeals four times to a fact he says his hearers can verify with their own eyes: demons are “overcome and subdued” when Christians exorcise them “in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate,” where the professional exorcists, incantations, and drugs fail. And he draws Scripture’s own line for us: this one Name works where the names of “kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs” do not — the boundary between the power of the Name and mere adjuration that the whole timeline follows.

Claims & evidence

“The power of whose name even the demons do fear” — exorcism in the name of Jesus crucified

For we call Him Helper and Redeemer, the power of whose name even the demons do fear; and at this day, when they are exorcised in the name of Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate, governor of Judaea, they are overcome. And thus it is manifest to all, that His Father has given Him so great power, by virtue of which demons are subdued to His name, and to the dispensation of His suffering.

— Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter XXX, source. Justin’s first statement of the theme is compact but complete: the power is in the Name (“the power of whose name even the demons do fear”), it is a present, ongoing fact (“at this day”), and it is grounded not in the exorcist but in what the Father has given the Son — a power tied to “the dispensation of His suffering,” the Name of Jesus crucified. The exorcistic power of the Name is not a bare incantation but the naming of the crucified Lord.

“When we exorcise all demons and evil spirits, have them subjected to us”

And now we, who believe on our Lord Jesus, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, when we exorcise all demons and evil spirits, have them subjected to us.

— Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter LXXVI, source. In the same chapter Justin has just quoted the Lord’s own warning — “Many shall say to Me in that day, Lord, Lord… cast out demons in Thy name? And I will say to them, Depart from Me” (Matthew 7:22–23) — and the promise “I give unto you power to tread on serpents… and on all the might of the enemy” (Luke 10:19). So the boundary text and the power text stand side by side: the Name subdues the spirits, yet is no guarantee for the one the Lord does not know. The exorcistic power is real and it is not at anyone’s disposal — the exact tension the summary timeline works out.

“Overcome and subdued” — this Name and no other name of kings or patriarchs

For every demon, when exorcised in the name of this very Son of God—who is the First-born of every creature, who became man by the Virgin, who suffered, and was crucified under Pontius Pilate by your nation, who died, who rose from the dead, and ascended into heaven—is overcome and subdued. But though you exorcise any demon in the name of any of those who were amongst you—either kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs—it will not be subject to you. But if any of you exorcise it in [the name of] the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, it will perhaps be subject to you. Now assuredly your exorcists, I have said, make use of craft when they exorcise, even as the Gentiles do, and employ fumigations and incantations.

— Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter LXXXV, source. This is Justin’s sharpest drawing of the line. The Name that subdues demons is not any powerful name but this Name, defined by the whole story of the incarnate, crucified, risen Lord — and Justin sets it against a graded ladder of rivals: the names of Israel’s own kings, righteous men, prophets, and patriarchs do not subdue the demon; the covenant name “the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob” “perhaps” will; but the Jewish exorcists who trade on “craft,” “fumigations and incantations” like the Gentiles are working sorcery, not wielding the Name. The efficacy is bound to who Jesus is, not to the technique of the operator — the boundary-vs-magic thread of this doctrine, stated by its earliest theorist.

“Have healed and do heal” where all the other exorcists failed

For numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city, many of our Christian men exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, have healed and do heal, rendering helpless and driving the possessing devils out of the men, though they could not be cured by all the other exorcists, and those who used incantations and drugs.

— Second Apology, Chapter VI, source. Addressed not to a Jewish interlocutor but to the Roman authorities, this is Justin’s public, empirical claim: the cures are “throughout the whole world, and in your city,” done by ordinary “Christian men,” and they succeed precisely where “all the other exorcists, and those who used incantations and drugs” fail. Justin stakes the truth of the Name on something his readers could check — the contrast between the Name of Jesus and the crowded market of professional exorcism, the same contrast Origen would press a century later against Celsus.

Notes

  • Justin is here seated as the earliest father on the power of the Name timeline — the first to argue the exorcistic efficacy of the Name of Jesus as a public, repeatable fact and to draw the line between it and the incantations of the professional exorcist. The motif is picked up and pressed harder by Origen (Against Celsus I.24–25, VII.4), later on the same timeline.
  • Justin’s distinction in Dialogue 85 — the Name of Jesus crucified subdues the demon where the names of kings, prophets, and patriarchs do not — is the second-century root of the boundary-question this doctrine keeps returning to: where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins. That the same Name is no guarantee for the false worker (Matthew 7:22–23, which Justin quotes in Dialogue 76) is the New-Testament side of the same line.
  • Justin appears across this wiki as a witness on several timelines — the sons of God and the Nephilim, the divine council, aliens, UFOs, and the demonic, the Eucharist, and more — where the Second Apology’s account of the fallen angels and their demon offspring supplies the same cast of hostile spirits that the Name here subdues.
