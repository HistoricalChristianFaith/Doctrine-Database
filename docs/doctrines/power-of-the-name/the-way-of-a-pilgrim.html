The Way of a Pilgrim on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1860 (The Way of a Pilgrim / Otkrovennye rasskazy strannika, anonymous Russian narrative, composed mid-nineteenth century, first printed at Kazan 1884) · Wikipedia
Summary: An anonymous Russian narrative of the Jesus Prayer. Its wanderer-narrator, arrested by St Paul’s command to “pray without ceasing,” is taught by a starets the continual invocation of the name of Jesus — the practice described earlier on this timeline by Diadochos, John Climacus, and Gregory Palamas — and told to say it three thousand, then six, then twelve thousand times a day, until the Prayer says itself in his heart.

Claims & evidence

“Pray without ceasing” — the question that begins the search

On the 24th Sunday after Pentecost I went to church to say my prayers there during the Liturgy. The First Epistle of St. Paul to the Thessalonians was being read, and among other words I heard these—“Pray without ceasing.” It was this text, more than any other, which forced itself upon my mind, and I began to think how it was possible to pray without ceasing, since a man has to concern himself with other things also in order to make a living.

— The Way of a Pilgrim, opening (trans. R. M. French), source.1 The pilgrim’s search begins from 1 Thessalonians 5:17.

The Jesus Prayer defined — “a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus”

The continuous interior Prayer of Jesus is a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart; while forming a mental picture of his constant presence, and imploring His grace, during every occupation, at all times, in all places, even during sleep. The appeal is couched in these terms, ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’

— The Way of a Pilgrim (the starets instructing the pilgrim), trans. French, source.1 The starets defines the Prayer as a calling upon “the divine Name of Jesus” “with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart,” “at all times… even during sleep”; compare Diadochos’s image of the child calling for its father even in sleep.

“Three thousand times a day” — the Prayer counted until it says itself

Take it, and to start with say the Prayer three thousand times a day. Whether you are standing or sitting, walking or lying down, continually repeat ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’ Say it quietly and without hurry, but without fail exactly three thousand times a day without deliberately increasing or diminishing the number.

…make up your mind by God’s help from to-day to say the Prayer of Jesus twelve thousand times a day. Remain in your solitude, get up early, go to bed late, and come and ask advice of me every fortnight.

— The Way of a Pilgrim, trans. French, source.2 In the narrative the counted repetitions — three thousand, then six, then twelve thousand a day — are the method by which the spoken Prayer becomes self-acting in the pilgrim’s heart.

Notes

  • The Way of a Pilgrim is seated at c. 1860, the latest witness on the power of the Name timeline, after the Holy Name confraternity and feast (c. 1564). It reached a Western readership in translation (R. M. French’s English, 1930), is quoted at length in J. D. Salinger’s Franny and Zooey, and is associated with the twentieth-century Anglo-American revival of the practice (⚠ unverified). It describes in narrative form the practice set down by Diadochos, John Climacus, and Gregory Palamas.
  • This is a witness to a work, not a datable named author: the pilgrim-narrator is anonymous, the author is unknown, and the date is approximate (a mid-nineteenth-century Russian composition, first printed at Kazan in 1884). The timeline anchor (c. 1860) is the work, not a person.
  • The book teaches the fixed formula “Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me” (some pages give the fuller “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me”) — the same phrase Gregory Palamas quotes from the earlier hesychast Method.
