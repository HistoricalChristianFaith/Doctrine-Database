The Way of a Pilgrim on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1860 (The Way of a Pilgrim / Otkrovennye rasskazy strannika, anonymous Russian narrative, composed mid-nineteenth century, first printed at Kazan 1884) · Wikipedia
Summary: The popular vehicle that carried the Jesus Prayer out of the monasteries to a wide readership, East and West. An anonymous Russian wanderer, arrested by St Paul’s command to “pray without ceasing,” is taught by a starets the continual invocation of the name of Jesus — the very practice Diadochos, Climacus, and Palamas transmitted — and told to say it three thousand, then six, then twelve thousand times a day, until the Prayer says itself in his heart. It is the same “single-phrased Jesus Prayer,” now a lay pilgrimage.

Claims & evidence

“Pray without ceasing” — the question that begins the search

On the 24th Sunday after Pentecost I went to church to say my prayers there during the Liturgy. The First Epistle of St. Paul to the Thessalonians was being read, and among other words I heard these—“Pray without ceasing.” It was this text, more than any other, which forced itself upon my mind, and I began to think how it was possible to pray without ceasing, since a man has to concern himself with other things also in order to make a living.

— The Way of a Pilgrim, opening (trans. R. M. French), source. The pilgrim’s whole journey is set going by the same text — 1 Thessalonians 5:17 — that the Byzantine fathers had made the charter of ceaseless invocation. What Diadochos called the “remembrance of the Lord Jesus unceasingly in the heart” the pilgrim now sets out to find as a way of life.

The Jesus Prayer defined — “a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus”

The continuous interior Prayer of Jesus is a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart; while forming a mental picture of his constant presence, and imploring His grace, during every occupation, at all times, in all places, even during sleep. The appeal is couched in these terms, ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’

— The Way of a Pilgrim (the starets instructing the pilgrim), trans. French, source. The definition gathers the whole tradition into a sentence: the Name called “with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart” (Diadochos’s inward “Lord Jesus”), “at all times… even during sleep” (his image of the child calling for its father even in sleep), “a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name.” The invocation is of “the divine Name of Jesus” itself — the phrasing the twentieth-century onomatodoxy dispute would fasten on.

“Three thousand times a day” — the Prayer counted until it says itself

Take it, and to start with say the Prayer three thousand times a day. Whether you are standing or sitting, walking or lying down, continually repeat ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’ Say it quietly and without hurry, but without fail exactly three thousand times a day without deliberately increasing or diminishing the number.

…make up your mind by God’s help from to-day to say the Prayer of Jesus twelve thousand times a day. Remain in your solitude, get up early, go to bed late, and come and ask advice of me every fortnight.

— The Way of a Pilgrim, trans. French, source. The counted repetitions — three thousand, then six, then twelve thousand a day — are the concrete method by which the spoken Prayer becomes the self-acting “prayer of the heart” the whole tradition aims at: the point Diadochos described, where the soul repeats the Name as naturally as breathing, and Climacus bound to the breath itself.

Notes

  • The Way of a Pilgrim is seated here as the latest classic witness on the power of the Name timeline — the work, more than any other, that carried the Jesus Prayer to a lay and Western readership (it lies behind the twentieth-century Anglo-American revival of the practice and is quoted at length in J. D. Salinger’s Franny and Zooey). It transmits, in narrative form, exactly the practice Diadochos, John Climacus, and Gregory Palamas set down.
  • This is a witness to a work, not a datable named author: the pilgrim-narrator is anonymous and the text’s authorship and exact date remain debated (a mid-nineteenth-century Russian composition, first printed at Kazan in 1884). The timeline anchor (c. 1860) is the work, not a person.
  • The book teaches the fixed formula “Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me” (some pages give the fuller “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me”) — the same phrase Gregory Palamas quotes from the earlier hesychast Method. Whether the continual invocation of the “divine Name of Jesus” is a distinct spirituality of the Name, and whether the Name is itself divine (the Athonite onomatodoxy / imiaslavie dispute of 1913), are weighed among this doctrine’s arguments.
