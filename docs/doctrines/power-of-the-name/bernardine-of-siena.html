Bernardine of Siena on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 1420 (Bernardino degli Albizzeschi, Franciscan Observant preacher, 1380–1444; his Holy Name missions and the golden IHS tablet were at their height across the 1410s–1420s, and the controversy they provoked came to a head in 1427) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Latin West’s counterpart to the Byzantine Jesus Prayer. Where the Eastern tradition internalized the Name in the praying heart, Bernardine made it a public, civic devotion — preaching the Holy Name of Jesus and raising for veneration a tablet blazoned with the monogram IHS in a sunburst of rays. The devotion drew the very charge this whole timeline turns on: that reverence for the Name had tipped over into idolatry and superstition. He was tried on the charge, defended by John of Capistrano, and vindicated — a textbook instance of the church policing the boundary between honouring the Name and treating it as a talisman.

Claims & evidence

The Name preached — “the glory of preachers”

The name of Jesus is the glory of preachers, because the shining splendor of that name causes his word to be proclaimed and heard. And how do you think such an immense, sudden and dazzling light of faith came into the world, if not because Jesus was preached? Was it not through the brilliance and sweet savor of this name that God ‘called us into his marvelous light’? When we have been enlightened, and in that same light behold the light of heaven, rightly may the apostle Paul say to us: ‘Once you were darkness, but now you are light in the Lord; walk as children of light.’

— Bernardine of Siena, Sermon on the glorious Name of Jesus Christ (De glorioso Nomine Iesu Christi, ch. 2), source (the reading appointed for his feast, from his collected works, Opera omnia 4:505–506). Bernardine’s whole preaching turned on the Name: to make Jesus “proclaimed and heard” is, for him, to release into the world the “dazzling light of faith” that the Name itself carries. This is the same conviction the fathers argued in exorcism — that the Name is effectual, not decorative — now bent to the pulpit and the public square.

The IHS tablet — the monogram raised for veneration

He used to hold a board in front of him while preaching, with the sacred monogram painted on it in the midst of rays and afterwards expose it for veneration.

Everywhere Bernardine persuaded the cities to take down the arms of their warring factions from the church and palace walls and to inscribe there, instead, the initials I. H. S.

— Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “St. Bernardine of Siena,” source. The IHS trigram (the first letters of ΙΗΣΌΥΣ, IHSOUS, “Jesus,” in Greek capitals) painted in a blaze of gilded rays is Bernardine’s enduring emblem — the sunburst still carved over Italian doorways and, later, adopted as the badge of the Society of Jesus. In his hands it was not ornament but a focus of worship: the Name made visible and set up, literally, for the crowd to venerate in place of the heraldry of blood-feud.

Charged with idolatry — and vindicated

He was accused of heresy, the tablets he had used to promote devotion to the Holy Name being made the basis of a clever attack by the adherents of the Dominican, Manfred of Vercelli… The saint was charged with having introduced a profane, new devotion which exposed the people to the danger of idolatry.

The latter took place at St. Peter’s in presence of the pope, 8 June, St. John Capistran having charge of the saint’s defence… The malice and futility of the charges against Bernardine were so completely demonstrated that the pope not only justified and commended the saint’s teaching, but urged him to preach in Rome.

— Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), source, drawing on the older Franciscan tradition. The episode itself is well attested. Bernardine was summoned to Rome to answer the charge (the summons is placed in 1426), examined before Pope Martin V — who at first received him coldly and forbade him to preach or show the tablets pending inquiry — and then fully acquitted, the pope not only clearing him but pressing him to preach in the city. (the Order of Friars Minor’s account) The future saint John of Capistrano is remembered as conducting the defence, and Iris Origo gives the affair a whole chapter (“The charge of heresy”) in The World of San Bernardino (1962), as does Franco Mormando’s The Preacher’s Demons (1999). The attack was renewed under Eugene IV and finally quashed by a surviving papal act: the Bull of Eugene IV of 7 January 1432 reserves the case to the pope, annuls the proceedings taken against Bernardine, and imposes perpetual silence on his accuser (edited in the Bullarium Franciscanum, Nova Series I, ed. U. Hüntemann, Quaracchi 1929, pp. 27–28, no. 40). Two details are looser than the hagiographic tradition makes them, and are flagged here for honesty rather than left to stand: the precise “8 June 1427” dating is the encyclopedia’s alone — the summons is usually put in 1426 and the year given variously, so it is best read as 1426–1427; and while the encyclopedia blames “the adherents of the Dominican, Manfred of Vercelli,” the one surviving papal document names a different accuser (a Prague parish priest), so the Manfred attribution belongs to the tradition, not to the documentary record.(near-contemporary Life by Leonardo Benvoglienti, written 1446 at John of Capistrano’s instance, ed. Van Ortroy) What is not in doubt is the shape of the episode, and it is exactly the doctrine’s recurring tension made concrete: the church affirmed the veneration of the Name while entertaining, and then rejecting, the fear that it had become a charm. The line Justin and Irenaeus drew against the magicians, the West here draws against its own preacher — and clears him.

Notes

  • The deeper root — Bernard of Clairvaux. Bernardine’s devotion inherits a long medieval love of the Name whose fountainhead is Bernard of Clairvaux’s Sermon 15 on the Song of Songs (“The Name of Jesus”), where Bernard reads the Name as oil that “gives light… nourishes… and soothes” and coins the devotion’s most quoted line, “Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart” — the sweetness-of-the-Name strain (given its own block on the timeline at c. 1140) that the Eastern tradition kept in the praying heart and Bernardine carried into the streets.
  • The hymn Jesu dulcis memoria. The medieval hymn Jesu dulcis memoria (“Jesu, the very thought of thee / With sweetness fills my breast” in Edward Caswall’s 1849 rendering) is the lyric heart of this same devotion. It was long ascribed to Bernard, but the manuscript tradition points instead to an anonymous English Cistercian of the late twelfth century; Bernard’s name attaches only from the late 1200s. (on the text and its authorship)
  • The devotion’s afterlife. The Holy Name devotion was institutionalized long after Bernardine: the Feast of the Most Holy Name of Jesus was granted to the Franciscans in 1530 and extended to the universal church in 1721, and the Dominican Holy Name Society — whose object is “to beget due love and reverence for the Holy Name of God and Jesus Christ” and to suppress blasphemy — traces its charge to Gregory X’s letter to the Order of Preachers in 1274.(Catholic Encyclopedia, “Society of the Holy Name”)
  • Bernardine is seated here as the Western counterpart to the Byzantine witnesses above (Diadochos, John Climacus, Gregory Palamas): both traditions hold that the Name of Jesus carries a real power and both must answer the charge that reverence for it shades into magic. On the boundary question the whole power of the Name timeline follows — where honouring the Name ends and treating it as a talisman begins — his acquittal is the West’s formal answer that the veneration itself is licit.
