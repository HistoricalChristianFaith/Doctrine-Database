Cyprian of Carthage on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 250 (Cyprian of Carthage, c. 200/210–258, bishop of Carthage from 248/249; the works cited here span c. 247–252 — On the Vanity of Idols, the exorcism datum, c. 247; To Demetrianus and On the Lord’s Prayer c. 251–252) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is the Latin voice beside Origen at mid-century. He presses the same datum Justin and Tertullian had made public — that demons “adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed” — and turns it, like Tertullian, into an apologetic dare, first to the pagan idolater (On the Vanity of Idols), then to the persecutor Demetrianus. He keeps the doctrine’s hard boundary against magic by inverting the charge: the very demons the pagans venerate as gods are the ones who cower and confess at the Christian’s voice, and it was the Jews who slandered Christ’s own power as sorcery. And in On the Lord’s Prayer he gives the earliest Latin exposition of “Hallowed be Thy name” — the Name reverenced not for God’s sake but that it “may be hallowed in us.” He slots between Origen (c. 248) and Athanasius (c. 318) on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

“Adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess” — the demons driven out

These, however, when adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed. You may see them at our voice, and by the operation of the hidden majesty, smitten with stripes, burnt with fire, stretched out with the increase of a growing punishment, howling, groaning, entreating, confessing whence they came and when they depart, even in the hearing of those very persons who worship them, and either springing forth at once or vanishing gradually, even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects.

— On the Vanity of Idols, ch. 7, source. This is the same fact Justin offered the Roman authorities and Tertullian dared the magistrates to test: the possessing spirit, put under Christian adjuration, confesses what it is and leaves the body it holds. Cyprian sharpens the polemical edge peculiar to his tract: the demons that flee are precisely the “impure and wandering spirits” who “lurk under the statues and consecrated images,” who animate the oracles and afflict men into worshipping them — so the exorcism is an argument against idolatry itself, the pagan gods unmasked as the very spirits the Name expels. And the mechanism is not automatic: the demon goes out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects” — the power turns on faith and grace, not on a formula, the line the whole doctrine draws.

“Tortured with spiritual scourges… they confess the judgment to come” — the dare to the persecutor

Oh, would you but hear and see them when they are adjured by us, and tortured with spiritual scourges, and are ejected from the possessed bodies with tortures of words, when howling and groaning at the voice of man and the power of God, feeling the stripes and blows, they confess the judgment to come!… You will see that we are entreated by those whom you entreat, that we are feared by those whom you fear, whom you adore. You will see that under our hands they stand bound, and tremble as captives, whom you took up to and venerate as lords.

— To Demetrianus, ch. 15, source. Turning the same power apologetically against the persecutor, Cyprian makes it “the voice of man and the power of God” together — the operator speaks, but God acts. As in Tertullian’s Apology, the demoniac becomes a public, checkable proof: Demetrianus is invited to come and hear his own gods, “at once upon our interrogation betraying what they are.” The rhetorical hinge is the inversion — the beings the pagan “entreat” and “venerate as lords” are the beings that stand “bound” and trembling under a Christian’s word. The Name does not merely heal; it exposes the whole cult as the worship of conquered spirits.

“Regarded Him as a sorcerer for the authority of His power” — the not-magic boundary inverted

Therefore when Christ Jesus, in accordance with what had been previously foretold by the prophets, drove out from men the demons by His word… the Jews, who had believed Him man only from the humility of His flesh and body, regarded Him as a sorcerer for the authority of His power.

— On the Vanity of Idols, ch. 13, source. Cyprian keeps the doctrine’s recurring boundary against magic, but from the far side: it is the unbelievers who mistake divine power for sorcery. Christ “drove out… the demons by His word,” and only those blind to who he was could call it magic — just as Tertullian headed off “Do you say that it is done by magic?” before it could be raised. Cyprian names the true source of magic plainly: the pagan gods are demons through whom “the Magi have a power either for mischief or for mockery” (ch. 6), spirits that “give efficiency to oracles” (ch. 7). So the sorcerer serves the very spirits the Name commands; to call the Name’s power “magic” is to confuse the two.

“Hallowed be Thy name” — the Name reverenced in prayer

After this we say, “Hallowed be Thy name;” not that we wish for God that He may be hallowed by our prayers, but that we beseech of Him that His name may be hallowed in us. But by whom is God sanctified, since He Himself sanctifies?… we ask and entreat, that we who were sanctified in baptism may continue in that which we have begun to be.

— On the Lord’s Prayer, ch. 12, source. The earliest Latin exposition of the first petition turns the reverence of the Name inward. God’s Name is not made holy by our prayers — it is already holy; we ask that it “may be hallowed in us,” that the sanctification received in baptism endure. Cyprian anchors that sanctification in the Name itself, quoting Paul: “ye are washed… ye are justified… ye are sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and by the Spirit of our God” (1 Corinthians 6:11). Here the power of the Name is not exorcistic but devotional and baptismal — the Name named over the believer and reverenced in prayer — the thread that, centuries on, the Byzantine Jesus Prayer and the Western Holy Name devotion would carry forward.

Notes

  • Cyprian is seated on the power of the Name timeline between Origen of Alexandria (c. 248) and Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 318), the Latin counterpart to Origen’s Greek. Where Origen theorized why a name has power, Cyprian simply presses the practice — demons adjured, confessing, and cast out — and, like Tertullian before him, makes it a public apologetic proof, first against the idolater, then against the persecutor.
  • His witness bears on two of this doctrine’s arguments. On whether the Name’s power is intrinsic to the words or the power of the Person invoked, Cyprian is for the Person and the relation: the demon goes out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects” and by “the voice of man and the power of God” together — not by a syllable working of itself. And on the sons of Sceva (warrant or rebuke?), his inversion of the magic charge — that the Name’s power was slandered as sorcery only by those blind to the One named — sits alongside the same line Luke drew at Ephesus.
  • Cyprian appears across this wiki on other timelines — notably Roman primacy, where his On the Unity of the Church and the rebaptism controversy are treated. The demonology behind his exorcism argument — the fallen spirits that lurk beneath the idols and taught the arts of divination — is the same the fathers trace to the Watchers of Genesis 6 (see astrology and the Nephilim), the demons the Name overturns.
