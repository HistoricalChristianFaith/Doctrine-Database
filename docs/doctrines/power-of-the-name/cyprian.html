Cyprian of Carthage on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 250 (Cyprian of Carthage, c. 200/210–258, bishop of Carthage from 248/249; the works cited here span c. 247–252 — On the Vanity of Idols, the exorcism datum, c. 247; To Demetrianus and On the Lord’s Prayer c. 251–252) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is a Latin witness contemporary with Origen at mid-century. He repeats the claim Justin and Tertullian had made public — that demons “adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed” — and turns it, like Tertullian, into an apologetic dare, first to the pagan idolater (On the Vanity of Idols), then to the persecutor Demetrianus. He answers the charge of magic by turning it round: the demons the pagans venerate as gods are, he says, the ones who cower and confess at the Christian’s voice, and he writes that the Jews of Christ’s day “regarded Him as a sorcerer.” In On the Lord’s Prayer he expounds “Hallowed be Thy name” — the Name reverenced not for God’s sake but that it “may be hallowed in us.” He is placed between Origen (c. 248) and Athanasius (c. 318) on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

“Adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess” — the demons driven out

These, however, when adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed. You may see them at our voice, and by the operation of the hidden majesty, smitten with stripes, burnt with fire, stretched out with the increase of a growing punishment, howling, groaning, entreating, confessing whence they came and when they depart, even in the hearing of those very persons who worship them, and either springing forth at once or vanishing gradually, even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects.

— On the Vanity of Idols, ch. 7, source.1 Cyprian makes the claim Justin offered the Roman authorities and Tertullian put to the magistrates: the possessing spirit, put under Christian adjuration, confesses what it is and leaves the body it holds. In this tract the demons that flee are, he says, the “impure and wandering spirits” who “lurk under the statues and consecrated images,” who animate the oracles and afflict men into worshipping them — so he uses the exorcism as an argument against idolatry, identifying the pagan gods with the spirits that are expelled. In his account the demon goes out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects” — on faith and grace rather than on a formula.

“Tortured with spiritual scourges… they confess the judgment to come” — the dare to the persecutor

Oh, would you but hear and see them when they are adjured by us, and tortured with spiritual scourges, and are ejected from the possessed bodies with tortures of words, when howling and groaning at the voice of man and the power of God, feeling the stripes and blows, they confess the judgment to come!… You will see that we are entreated by those whom you entreat, that we are feared by those whom you fear, whom you adore. You will see that under our hands they stand bound, and tremble as captives, whom you took up to and venerate as lords.

— To Demetrianus, ch. 15, source.2 Addressing the persecutor, Cyprian describes the exorcism as “the voice of man and the power of God” together — the operator speaks, God acts. As Tertullian had in the Apology, Cyprian offers the demoniac as a public, checkable proof: Demetrianus is invited to come and hear his own gods, “at once upon our interrogation betraying what they are.” The argument turns on an inversion — the beings the pagans “entreat” and “venerate as lords” are, he says, the beings that stand “bound” and trembling under a Christian’s word, so that he presents the cult as the worship of conquered spirits.

“Regarded Him as a sorcerer for the authority of His power” — Cyprian on the charge of sorcery

Therefore when Christ Jesus, in accordance with what had been previously foretold by the prophets, drove out from men the demons by His word… the Jews, who had believed Him man only from the humility of His flesh and body, regarded Him as a sorcerer for the authority of His power.

— On the Vanity of Idols, ch. 13, source.3 Cyprian addresses the charge of magic from the other side: on his account it is those who believed Christ “man only” who called his power sorcery. Christ “drove out… the demons by His word”; Tertullian had answered “Do you say that it is done by magic?” in his Apology. Cyprian names what he takes to be the source of magic: the pagan gods are demons through whom “the Magi have a power either for mischief or for mockery” (ch. 6), spirits that “give efficiency to oracles” (ch. 7). On his account the sorcerer serves the spirits the Name commands.

“Hallowed be Thy name” — the Name reverenced in prayer

After this we say, “Hallowed be Thy name;” not that we wish for God that He may be hallowed by our prayers, but that we beseech of Him that His name may be hallowed in us. But by whom is God sanctified, since He Himself sanctifies?… we ask and entreat, that we who were sanctified in baptism may continue in that which we have begun to be.

— On the Lord’s Prayer, ch. 12, source.4 Cyprian’s exposition of the first petition turns the reverence of the Name inward, as Tertullian’s On Prayer (ch. 3) had done (“that it may be hallowed in us who are in Him”). On Cyprian’s reading God’s Name is not made holy by our prayers; we ask that it “may be hallowed in us,” that the sanctification received in baptism endure. Cyprian anchors that sanctification in the Name itself, quoting Paul: “ye are washed… ye are justified… ye are sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and by the Spirit of our God” (1 Corinthians 6:11). Here the Name appears not in exorcism but in devotion and baptism — the Name named over the believer and reverenced in prayer.

Placement on the timeline

  • Cyprian is placed on the power of the Name timeline between Origen of Alexandria (c. 248) and Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 318), a Latin writer contemporary with Origen. Where Origen gave an account of why a name has power, Cyprian describes the practice — demons adjured, confessing, and cast out — and, as Tertullian had, offers it as a public apologetic proof, first against the idolater, then against the persecutor.
  • On whether the Name’s power lies in the words or in the Person invoked, Cyprian ties it to the Person and to faith: the demon goes out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects” and by “the voice of man and the power of God” together.
  • Cyprian appears across this wiki on other timelines — notably Roman primacy, where his On the Unity of the Church and the rebaptism controversy are treated. The demonology behind his exorcism argument — the fallen spirits that lurk beneath the idols and give efficiency to oracles — resembles the account Tertullian traces to the Watchers of Genesis 6 (see astrology and the Nephilim).
