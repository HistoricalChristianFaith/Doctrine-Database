Irenaeus of Lyons on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 180 (Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 130–202; Against Heresies, composed at Lyons) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus is a two-sided witness who states the doctrine’s central tension in its earliest sharp form. On one side he claims a real, present, freely-given power for the Name: the church, “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ,” casts out demons, heals the sick, and (he says) has even raised the dead — done for the benefit of others, without fee, and without “any wicked curious art.” On the other he attacks the Marcosian and Valentinian “redemption” rite, with its secret Hebrew-sounding invocations and magical formulae, as sorcery masquerading as sacrament. The same writer who claims the Name’s power draws the hardest line against name-magic.

Claims & evidence

“In His name perform miracles… the dead even have been raised up” — the power freely given

Wherefore, also, those who are in truth His disciples, receiving grace from Him, do in His name perform [miracles], so as to promote the welfare of other men, according to the gift which each one has received from Him. For some do certainly and truly drive out devils, so that those who have thus been cleansed from evil spirits frequently both believe [in Christ], and join themselves to the Church. Others have foreknowledge of things to come: they see visions, and utter prophetic expressions. Others still, heal the sick by laying their hands upon them, and they are made whole. Yea, moreover, as I have said, the dead even have been raised up, and remained among us for many years. And what shall I more say? It is not possible to name the number of the gifts which the Church, [scattered] throughout the whole world, has received from God, in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, and which she exerts day by day for the benefit of the Gentiles, neither practising deception upon any, nor taking any reward from them… For as she has received freely from God, freely also does she minister [to others].

— Against Heresies, Book II, Chapter XXXII.4, source. Irenaeus grounds the Name’s efficacy exactly where Justin did — in the crucified Lord (“in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate”) — and adds two marks the timeline keeps returning to: the power is exercised for the benefit of others, and it is free (“freely… freely”, echoing Matthew 10:8). Whether one credits the reported raisings or not, the doctrinal claim is plain: the Name of Jesus is the church’s effectual instrument in exorcism and healing, over against paid technique.

“Nor… by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art” — the Name against magic

Nor does she perform anything by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art; but, directing her prayers to the Lord, who made all things, in a pure, sincere, and straightforward spirit, and calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, she has been accustomed to work miracles for the advantage of mankind, and not to lead them into error. If, therefore, the name of our Lord Jesus Christ even now confers benefits [upon men], and cures thoroughly and effectively all who anywhere believe on Him, but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates, or of any other man whatever, it is manifest that… He did all things truly through the power of God.

— Against Heresies, Book II, Chapter XXXII.5, source. Here Irenaeus draws the line himself. The Name works “not… by… incantations, or by any other wicked curious art” but by prayer and the plain invocation of Jesus Christ — and it is this Name, not “that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates, or of any other man whatever,” that heals. The efficacy is bound to the One named, not to a formula the operator commands: the same boundary Justin drew between the Name and the sorcerer’s craft, now framed against the church’s Gnostic rivals by name.

“By means of prayer… with much fasting and prayer” — the church raises the dead, the magician cannot

And so far are they from being able to raise the dead, as the Lord raised them, and the apostles did by means of prayer, and as has been frequently done in the brotherhood on account of some necessity—the entire Church in that particular locality entreating [the boon] with much fasting and prayer, the spirit of the dead man has returned, and he has been bestowed in answer to the prayers of the saints… but in the Church, sympathy, and compassion, and stedfastness, and truth, for the aid and encouragement of mankind, are not only displayed without fee or reward, but we ourselves lay out for the benefit of others our own means.

— Against Heresies, Book II, Chapters XXXI.2 and XXXI.3, source. The contrast that sets up the passage above: Simon and Carpocrates, “who are said to perform miracles,” work “by means of magical deceptions” and “for the sake of destroying and misleading mankind” — they can neither heal nor raise the dead. The church’s works, by contrast, are done “by means of prayer” and “without fee or reward.” The distinguishing marks are not technique but prayer to the Lord, the benefit of others, and gratuity — the anti-magic criteria of the whole doctrine.

“A perfect adept in magical impostures” — Marcus and the misuse of the name

But there is another among these heretics, Marcus by name, who boasts himself as having improved upon his master. He is a perfect adept in magical impostures… For, joining the buffooneries of Anaxilaus to the craftiness of the magi, as they are called, he is regarded by his senseless and cracked-brain followers as working miracles by these means.

— Against Heresies, Book I, Chapter XIII.1, source. Marcus “pretending to consecrate cups mixed with wine, and protracting to great length the word of invocation,” and engaging “in certain invocations, so as to astound his deluded victim,” is Irenaeus’s type-case of the wonder-worker whose power is technique — the drawn-out “invocation,” the conjured sign, the demon “familiar spirit” — not the Name. This is the negative image against which the Book II claim for the church’s Name is measured.

“Others still repeat certain Hebrew words” — the secret-name formulae of the “redemption” rite

Others still repeat certain Hebrew words, in order the more thoroughly to bewilder those who are being initiated, as follows: “Basema, Chamosse, Baoenaora, Mistadia, Ruada, Kousta, Babaphor, Kalachthei.” The interpretation of these terms runs thus: “I invoke that which is above every power of the Father, which is called light, and good Spirit, and life, because Thou hast reigned in the body.”… The name of restitution stands thus: Messia, Uphareg, Namempsoeman, Chaldoeaur, Mosomedoea, Acphranoe, Psaua, Jesus Nazaria.

— Against Heresies, Book I, Chapter XXI.3, source. Irenaeus’s exposure of the Valentinian/Marcosian “redemption” is the sharpest early attack on name-magic dressed as sacrament: strings of untranslatable Hebrew-sounding voces mysticae, invoked “to bewilder those who are being initiated,” a rite Irenaeus says was “instigated by Satan to a denial of that baptism which is regeneration to God.” The very form — efficacy located in secret sounds recited over the initiate — is what the church’s plain invocation of “the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” is set against. It is the second-century root of the boundary this doctrine polices: where invocation ends and incantation begins.

Notes

  • Irenaeus is seated on the power of the Name timeline just after Justin Martyr (c. 160) and before Origen (c. 248). Justin argued the Name’s exorcistic power as a public fact and drew the line against “fumigations and incantations”; Irenaeus makes both moves within one work — affirming the church’s free, prayer-borne power in the Name (Book II) and dissecting the Gnostic misuse of secret names (Book I) — so the “power vs. magic” tension is fully joined a generation before Origen presses it against Celsus.
  • The two Book I passages feed the doctrine’s boundary-question directly: they are the earliest detailed Christian description of name-magic (drawn-out invocations, voces mysticae, a “familiar spirit”) being condemned as magic by a churchman who in the same breath claims real power for the one Name. This is the raw material for the planned argument on where the line between the power of the Name and magic falls, and it parallels the magic-adjacency question weighed on “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?”
  • Irenaeus appears across this wiki as a witness on several timelines — the Nephilim and the sons of God, the divine council, the serpent of Eden, and the Eucharist — where the same demonology (the fallen powers and their arts) supplies the hostile spirits the Name here subdues, and the Marcosian counterfeit Eucharist of Book I.13 stands behind his account of the true one.
