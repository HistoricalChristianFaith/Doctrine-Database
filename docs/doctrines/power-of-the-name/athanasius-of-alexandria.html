Athanasius of Alexandria on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 318 (On the Incarnation of the Word, a youthful apologetic work; Athanasius c. 296–373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Athanasius takes the Name’s power that Justin, Irenaeus, and Origen had claimed and turns it into an apologetic proof — of the Resurrection and of the Godhead of Christ. Because “actions, and power over men, belong to the living only,” the plain fact that “where Christ is named… all idolatry is deposed” and every spirit flies “even on barely hearing” the Name shows that the One named is alive and is God. The shift in use is the point: for the earlier witnesses the Name is a power wielded in exorcism; for Athanasius the same power is marshalled as public, checkable evidence — “let him come who would test by experience.”

Claims & evidence

“Where Christ is named… all idolatry is deposed” — the Name proves the Risen One lives

For where Christ is named, and His faith, there all idolatry is deposed and all imposture of evil spirits is exposed, and any spirit is unable to endure even the name, nay even on barely hearing it flies and disappears. But this work is not that of one dead, but of one that lives—and especially of God. In particular, it would be ridiculous to say that while the spirits cast out by Him and the idols brought to nought are alive, He who chases them away, and by His power prevents their even appearing, yea, and is being confessed by them all to be Son of God, is dead.

— On the Incarnation of the Word, §30.6–7, source.1 This is the whole move that distinguishes Athanasius from the witnesses before him. He has just laid down the rule that “on a man’s decease he can put forth no power… actions, and power over men, belong to the living only.” From this he reasons backwards: the Name of Jesus visibly works — demons flee, idols are deserted — therefore the One named is not dead but living, “and especially… God.” The power of the Name, an assumed datum in Justin and Origen, becomes here a premise in an argument for the Resurrection and the divinity of Christ.

“By the sign of the Cross all magic is stopped” — the impotence of the idols against the constraining Name

… whereas by the sign of the Cross all magic is stopped, and all witchcraft brought to nought, and all the idols are being deserted and left, and every unruly pleasure is checked, and every one is looking up from earth to heaven: Whom is one to pronounce dead? Christ, that is doing so many works? But to work is not proper to one dead.

— On the Incarnation of the Word, §31.2, source.2 Athanasius sets the “constraining power of Christ and of the sign of the Cross” against the “impotence of idols, for good or evil.” If power is the sign of life, then the gods and spirits that can do nothing at the presence of Christ — that “shew themselves dead” — are the dead ones, and Christ, whose Name and sign put their whole apparatus to flight, is alive. The Cross and the Name work together throughout: the invoked Name and the traced sign are, for Athanasius, one instrument of the same power.

“If they be manifestly expelled by the naming of His name” — the demons themselves testify

For it is plain that if Christ be dead, He could not be expelling demons and spoiling idols; for a dead man the spirits would not have obeyed. But if they be manifestly expelled by the naming of His name, it must be evident that He is not dead; especially as spirits, seeing even what is unseen by men, could tell if Christ were dead and refuse Him any obedience at all. But as it is, what irreligious men believe not, the spirits see—that He is God,—and hence they fly and fall at His feet…

— On the Incarnation of the Word, §32.4–5, source.3 The demons’ own obedience is pressed into the proof: they would not obey a dead man, and they — who “see even what is unseen by men” — confess Him “the Holy One of God.” What the unbeliever refuses to grant, the evil spirits know and act on at the naming of the Name. The exorcistic fact is, once more, evidence: God is invisible and “known from His works,” and here the works “cry aloud.”

“Since Christ has begun to be preached everywhere… there is none among them to divine any more” — the oracles fall silent

And whereas formerly every place was full of the deceit of the oracles, and the oracles at Delphi and Dodona, and in Boeotia and Lycia and Libya and Egypt and those of the Cabiri, and the Pythoness, were held in repute by men’s imagination, now, since Christ has begun to be preached everywhere, their madness also has ceased and there is none among them to divine any more… now, after the divine visitation of the Word, their deception has ceased. For by the Sign of the Cross, though a man but use it, he drives out their deceits… And what is one to say of the magic esteemed among them? that before the Word sojourned among us this was strong and active among Egyptians, and Chaldees, and Indians, and inspired awe in those who saw it; but that by the presence of the Truth, and the Appearing of the Word, it also has been thoroughly confuted, and brought wholly to nought.

— On the Incarnation of the Word, §47.1–4, source.4 Here the argument becomes historical and cumulative: the whole ancient machinery of divination and demon-cult — oracles at named shrines, springs and rivers and stones haunted by spirits, the magic of Egypt and Chaldea — was “strong and active” before Christ and has since fallen silent where the Name is preached. Athanasius offers this as a public, dateable fact about the world his readers live in, not a private claim of Christian power.

“Let him use the Sign of that Cross… and he shall see how by its means demons fly” — the challenge to test it

Now these arguments of ours do not amount merely to words, but have in actual experience a witness to their truth… And let him come who would test by experience what we have now said, and in the very presence of the deceit of demons and the imposture of oracles and the marvels of magic, let him use the Sign of that Cross which is laughed at among them, and he shall see how by its means demons fly, oracles cease, all magic and witchcraft is brought to nought. Who, then, and how great is this Christ, Who by His own Name and Presence casts into the shade and brings to nought all things on every side…? … if His Cross has won the victory over absolutely all magic, and over the very name of it, it must be plain that the Saviour is not a magician, seeing that even those demons who are invoked by the other magicians fly from Him as their Master.

— On the Incarnation of the Word, §48.1–6, source.5 Athanasius stakes the claim on experiment: the reader is invited to try the sign and Name and watch the result — the empirical note Justin had struck (“numberless demoniacs… in your city”) now made a standing dare. And he draws the boundary his predecessors drew, in his own idiom: Christ is not a magician, precisely because “by a magician all magic is destroyed, instead of being confirmed” — the very demons the magicians invoke “fly from Him as their Master.” The Name that undoes all magic cannot itself be magic; it is the power against which magic is powerless.

Antony routs demons “by prayer and speaking the name of Christ” — the same doctrine narrated as a life

What On the Incarnation argues, Athanasius’s Life of Antony (c. 357) narrates. Antony teaches the frightened to “sign yourselves therefore with the cross, and depart boldly”; the demons “are nothing and quickly disappear, especially if a man fortify himself… with faith and the sign of the cross.”6 A towering demon who claimed to be “the power of God” vanished when Antony “spoke the name of Christ”; a possessed youth on shipboard was freed when “the demon being rebuked in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ departed.”7 Confronting the pagan philosophers, Antony makes the same apologetic Athanasius made in his own voice: “by the mention of Christ crucified [we] put all demons to flight, whom you fear as if they were gods. Where the sign of the Cross is, magic is weak and witchcraft has no strength” — and then, cleansing the demoniacs before them with the sign of the Cross and the calling on Christ, he insists, “We are not the doers of these things, but it is Christ who worketh them.”8 Antony “healed not by commanding, but by prayer and speaking the name of Christ”9 — the boundary against technique kept even in the miracle-story.

Notes

  • Athanasius is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 318, after Justin Martyr (c. 160), Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180), and Origen of Alexandria (c. 248). The dating of On the Incarnation is debated (some place it a decade or two later), but it is universally taken as an early work of Athanasius, the companion to Against the Heathen; the timeline uses the traditional c. 318.
  • The shift in use. This is the doctrine’s turn from technique to testimony. Justin, Irenaeus, and Origen all claimed the Name casts out demons; Athanasius keeps the claim intact but changes what it is for. The Name’s power is no longer chiefly a thing the exorcist does but a fact the apologist points to — evidence, offered to unbelievers as checkable in the open, that the crucified One is risen and is God. “Let him come who would test by experience” is the signature of the move.
  • The boundary against magic, kept. Athanasius holds the same line every earlier witness drew, and argues it freshly: Christ cannot be a magician because he abolishes magic rather than confirming it, and the demons magicians conjure flee from him. The Name that undoes all sorcery is by that very fact set over against sorcery — the doctrine’s recurring boundary (see the argument “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?”, contested), here answered not by theory but by the character of the effect.
  • Athanasius appears on this wiki also as a witness on other timelines (e.g. the Trinity and perpetual virginity); this page is confined to his teaching on the power of the Name.
