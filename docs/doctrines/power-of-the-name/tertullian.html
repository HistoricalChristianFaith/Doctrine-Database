Tertullian on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 197 (Tertullian of Carthage, c. 155–c. 240; the Apology, written at Carthage c. 197) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian presents the exorcistic power of the Name as a public, courtroom-style challenge. Writing to the Roman magistrates, he dares them to bring any demoniac before the bench: at the mere questioning of a Christian the possessing spirit will confess itself a demon, and — charged with the name of Christ and the coming judgment — will leave the body it holds. The whole authority Christians wield over the demons, he says, comes “from our naming the name of Christ.” He adds that the deliverances are worked “without reward or hire,” and denies that they are magic, which he elsewhere condemns as a “curious art” taught by the fallen angels. He is placed between Irenaeus (c. 180) and Origen (c. 248) on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

“All the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ” — the demons compelled in court

Let a person be brought before your tribunals, who is plainly under demoniacal possession. The wicked spirit, bidden to speak by a follower of Christ, will as readily make the truthful confession that he is a demon, as elsewhere he has falsely asserted that he is a god… Do you say that it is done by magic, or some trick of that sort? You will not say anything of the sort, if you have been allowed the use of your ears and eyes.

Why, all the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ, and recalling to their memory the woes with which God threatens them at the hands of Christ as Judge, and which they expect one day to overtake them. Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ, they become subject to the servants of God and Christ. So at our touch and breathing, overwhelmed by the thought and realization of those judgment fires, they leave at our command the bodies they have entered, unwilling, and distressed, and before your very eyes put to an open shame.

— Apology, ch. XXIII, source.1 Where Justin reported exorcisms and Irenaeus made a claim for the church at large, Tertullian puts a challenge to the magistrates to test the claim on the spot. In his account the power lies not in a formula the operator commands but in the Person named and feared: the demon submits because it dreads “Christ as Judge,” “Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ.” He also answers in advance the charge that it is “done by magic.”

“Without reward or hire we exorcise” — the anti-magic mark of gratuity

Who would save you, I mean, from the attacks of those spirits of evil, which without reward or hire we exorcise? This alone would be revenge enough for us, that you were henceforth left free to the possession of unclean spirits.

— Apology, ch. XXXVII, source.2 Tertullian uses the criterion Irenaeus had used to distinguish the Name from magic — the church exorcises “without reward or hire.” He presents freely-given deliverance, offered even to those who persecute Christians, as a public benefit and as charity rather than a technique for sale.

“Delivered from devils… the little boy of a third” — exorcism in the emperor’s own household

The clerk of one of them who was liable to be thrown upon the ground by an evil spirit, was set free from his affliction; as was also the relative of another, and the little boy of a third. How many men of rank (to say nothing of common people) have been delivered from devils, and healed of diseases! Even Severus himself, the father of Antonine, was graciously mindful of the Christians; for he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Torpacion, the steward of Euhodias, and in gratitude for his having once cured him by anointing, he kept him in his palace till the day of his death.

— To Scapula, ch. IV, source.3 Writing to the proconsul of Africa (c. 212), Tertullian moves from the courtroom challenge of the Apology to named cases, reaching into the imperial establishment itself: the clerks and kin of advocates freed from evil spirits, men of rank “delivered from devils,” and the emperor Septimius Severus himself cured by the Christian Proculus and kept in the palace in gratitude. Tertullian offers these cases as matters of public record his addressee could check, as Justin and Irenaeus had also appealed to observable cures.

“Magic… the discoverers of this curious art” — Tertullian on magic

One proposition I lay down: that those angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women, were likewise the discoverers of this curious art, on that account also condemned by God… For if magic is punished, of which astrology is a species, of course the species is condemned in the genus.

— On Idolatry, ch. IX, source.4 Here Tertullian condemns “magic” and its species astrology as a “curious art” and grounds the condemnation in the primeval rebellion: the fallen “angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women” (identified with the Watchers of Genesis 6 / 1 Enoch) were, he says, its discoverers. On his account the craft is the demons’ own gift. As in Irenaeus, the claim of power in the Name and the condemnation of magic appear in the one writer.

Placement on the timeline

  • Tertullian is placed on the power of the Name timeline between Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180) and Origen of Alexandria (c. 248), by the date of the Apology (c. 197). Where Irenaeus claimed the church’s power in the Name and described Gnostic use of secret names, Tertullian puts the demons’ submission to the Roman magistrates as a public test. Athanasius (c. 318), later on the timeline, argues from the demons’ flight to the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ.
  • On whether the Name’s power lies in the words or in the Person invoked, Tertullian locates it in the Person: the demon submits, he says, because it fears “Christ as Judge” and the coming “judgment fires.” His “Do you say that it is done by magic?… You will not say anything of the sort” and his “without reward or hire” set the Name against magic and against paid spell-craft.
  • Tertullian appears across this wiki as a witness on many timelines. Nearest to this one is astrology, where the same On Idolatry IX condemnation of the “curious art” is treated in full — and its demonology reaches back to the Nephilim / sons of God and the book of Enoch he defended, whose fallen “angels… lovers of women” he names as the discoverers of the magical arts.
