Tertullian on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 197 (Tertullian of Carthage, c. 155–c. 240; the Apology, written at Carthage c. 197) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian turns the exorcistic power of the Name into a public, courtroom-style challenge. Writing to the Roman magistrates, he dares them to bring any demoniac before the bench: at the mere questioning of a Christian the possessing spirit will confess itself a demon, and — charged with the name of Christ and the coming judgment — will leave the body it holds. The whole authority Christians wield over the demons, he says, comes “from our naming the name of Christ.” And he draws the doctrine’s recurring boundary himself: the deliverances are worked “without reward or hire,” not by magic — the very magic he elsewhere condemns as a “curious art” taught by the fallen angels. He slots between Irenaeus (c. 180) and Origen (c. 248) on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

“All the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ” — the demons compelled in court

Let a person be brought before your tribunals, who is plainly under demoniacal possession. The wicked spirit, bidden to speak by a follower of Christ, will as readily make the truthful confession that he is a demon, as elsewhere he has falsely asserted that he is a god… Do you say that it is done by magic, or some trick of that sort? You will not say anything of the sort, if you have been allowed the use of your ears and eyes.

Why, all the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ, and recalling to their memory the woes with which God threatens them at the hands of Christ as Judge, and which they expect one day to overtake them. Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ, they become subject to the servants of God and Christ. So at our touch and breathing, overwhelmed by the thought and realization of those judgment fires, they leave at our command the bodies they have entered, unwilling, and distressed, and before your very eyes put to an open shame.

— Apology, ch. XXIII, source. This is the doctrine’s boldest public form yet: not a report of exorcisms elsewhere (Justin) or a claim for the church at large (Irenaeus), but a standing dare to the magistrates to test it on the spot. The mechanism Tertullian names is exactly the one the whole timeline works out — the power is not in a formula the operator commands but in the Person named and feared: the demon submits because it dreads “Christ as Judge,” “Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ.” And he heads off the obvious pagan rejoinder — “Do you say that it is done by magic?” — before it can be raised, the same line against sorcery every witness draws.

“Without reward or hire we exorcise” — the anti-magic mark of gratuity

Who would save you, I mean, from the attacks of those spirits of evil, which without reward or hire we exorcise? This alone would be revenge enough for us, that you were henceforth left free to the possession of unclean spirits.

— Apology, ch. XXXVII, source. Tertullian reaches for the same criterion Irenaeus used to sever the Name from magic — the church exorcises “without reward or hire,” where the sorcerer sells his craft. Freely-given deliverance is offered even to Rome’s persecutors as a public benefit, an argument that the Name’s power is charity, not technique for sale.

“Delivered from devils… the little boy of a third” — exorcism in the emperor’s own household

The clerk of one of them who was liable to be thrown upon the ground by an evil spirit, was set free from his affliction; as was also the relative of another, and the little boy of a third. How many men of rank (to say nothing of common people) have been delivered from devils, and healed of diseases! Even Severus himself, the father of Antonine, was graciously mindful of the Christians; for he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Torpacion, the steward of Euhodias, and in gratitude for his having once cured him by anointing, he kept him in his palace till the day of his death.

— To Scapula, ch. IV, source. Writing to the proconsul of Africa (c. 212), Tertullian moves from the courtroom challenge of the Apology to named cases, reaching into the imperial establishment itself: the clerks and kin of advocates freed from evil spirits, men of rank “delivered from devils,” and the emperor Septimius Severus himself cured by the Christian Proculus and kept in the palace in gratitude. The Name’s power is offered as a matter of public record its accusers can check, not private testimony — the empirical footing Justin and Irenaeus had also claimed.

“Magic… the discoverers of this curious art” — the boundary against name-craft

One proposition I lay down: that those angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women, were likewise the discoverers of this curious art, on that account also condemned by God… For if magic is punished, of which astrology is a species, of course the species is condemned in the genus.

— On Idolatry, ch. IX, source. Here Tertullian draws the far side of the boundary the doctrine polices. The power to command demons in the name of Christ stands over against “magic” and its species astrology as a “curious art” — and he grounds its condemnation in the primeval rebellion: the fallen “angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women” (the Watchers of Genesis 6 / 1 Enoch) were its first teachers. So the very craft the Name overturns is the demons’ own gift; the Christian who names Christ commands the spirits, while the magician who trades in their arts serves them. The two halves of the doctrine — real power in the Name, hard condemnation of name-magic — sit in the one writer, as they had in Irenaeus.

Notes

  • Tertullian is seated on the power of the Name timeline between Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180) and Origen of Alexandria (c. 248), anchored by the Apology (c. 197). He carries the doctrine one step past Irenaeus: where Irenaeus claimed the church’s power in the Name and dissected the Gnostic misuse of secret names, Tertullian makes the demons’ forced submission a public, testable proof put to the Roman magistrates — the apologetic use Athanasius would later develop into an argument for the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ.
  • His witness bears directly on two of this doctrine’s arguments. On whether the Name’s power is intrinsic to the words or the power of the Person invoked, Tertullian is squarely for the Person: the demon submits not to a syllable but because it fears “Christ as Judge” and the coming “judgment fires” — the authority is relational and eschatological, not phonemic. And on the sons of Sceva (warrant or rebuke?), his “Do you say that it is done by magic?… You will not say anything of the sort” and his “without reward or hire” both press the same line Luke drew at Ephesus — the Name is effectual, but not a spell in any mouth for sale.
  • Tertullian appears across this wiki as a witness on many timelines. Nearest to this one is astrology, where the same On Idolatry IX condemnation of the “curious art” is treated in full — and its demonology reaches back to the Nephilim / sons of God and the book of Enoch he defended, whose fallen “angels… lovers of women” supply both the arts the Name overturns and the hostile spirits it subdues.
