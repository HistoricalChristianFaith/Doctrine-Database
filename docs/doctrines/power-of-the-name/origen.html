Origen of Alexandria on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 248 (Against Celsus, at Caesarea; Origen c. 185–253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen gives the fullest patristic theory of why a name has power — the account the rest of this timeline is measured against. Against Celsus’s gibe that it makes no difference whether God be called Zeus or Sabaoth, Origen argues that names are “not conventional but natural”: a power is lodged in the very sounds, so that the Hebrew divine names — Sabaoth, Adonai, “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, of Jacob” — vanquish demons in their native form and go dead when translated. Above all the name of Jesus “has expelled myriads of evil spirits,” and works even on unworthy lips. Yet he draws the doctrine’s hard boundary himself: sorcery is forbidden by the law and demonic in its power — the reality of names is not, for Origen, a license for magic.

Claims & evidence

Names are natural, not conventional — a power lodged in the very sounds

… this involves a deep and mysterious subject—that, viz., respecting the nature of names: it being a question whether, as Aristotle thinks, names were bestowed by arrangement, or, as the Stoics hold, by nature… or whether, as Epicurus teaches (differing in this from the Stoics), names were given by nature… If, then, we shall be able to establish, in reference to the preceding statement, the nature of powerful names… and shall be able to make out that the so-called magic is not, as the followers of Epicurus and Aristotle suppose, an altogether uncertain thing, but is, as those skilled in it prove, a consistent system, having words which are known to exceedingly few; then we say that the name Sabaoth, and Adonai, and the other names treated with so much reverence among the Hebrews, are not applicable to any ordinary created things, but belong to a secret theology which refers to the Framer of all things. These names, accordingly, when pronounced with that attendant train of circumstances which is appropriate to their nature, are possessed of great power.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXIV, source.1 The mechanism is stated exactly in the next chapter: the power is in the word, not in what the word means. “It is not the things signified, but the qualities and peculiarities of words, which possess a certain power for this or that purpose” — which is why, he says, “the utterance of the same incantation in its proper language can accomplish what the spell professes to do; but when translated into any other tongue, it is observed to become inefficacious and feeble.”2 This name-realism is the theoretical root the whole doctrine of the power of the Name rests on — and it is the same teaching weighed, on the question of the primeval tongue, on Origen’s first-language page and the argument was the primordial language a divine gift?

The Hebrew divine names vanquish demons — and lose the power when translated

… something is transferred from the name Abraham when translated into Greek, and something is signified by that of Isaac, and also by that of Jacob; and accordingly, if any one, either in an invocation or in swearing an oath, were to use the expression, “the God of Abraham,” and “the God of Isaac,” and “the God of Jacob,” he would produce certain effects, either owing to the nature of these names or to their powers, since even demons are vanquished and become submissive to him who pronounces these names; whereas if we say, “the god of the chosen father of the echo, and the god of laughter, and the god of him who strikes with the heel,” the mention of the name is attended with no result, as is the case with other names possessed of no power… And we may say the same also of the pronunciation of “Sabaoth,” a word which is frequently employed in incantations; for if we translate the term into “Lord of hosts,” or “Lord of armies,” or “Almighty”… we shall accomplish nothing; whereas if we retain the original pronunciation, we shall, as those who are skilled in such matters maintain, produce some effect. And the same observation holds good of Adonai.

— Against Celsus, Book V, ch. XLV, source.3 This is the reason Origen gives, against Celsus’s “it makes no difference,” for the Christians’ refusal to swap the divine names for Greek equivalents: the untranslated Hebrew names really bind the spirits, and a translation is a dead letter. The same names, and their untranslated survival in the biblical text, are traced on his Divine Name page; that the doctrine sits close enough to theurgy to draw a charge of teaching magic is weighed on “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?” (assessment: contested).

“Whose name has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits” — the name of Jesus

And a similar philosophy of names applies also to our Jesus, whose name has already been seen, in an unmistakeable manner, to have expelled myriads of evil spirits from the souls and bodies (of men), so great was the power which it exerted upon those from whom the spirits were driven out.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXV, source.4 The name of Jesus is thus, for Origen, the supreme case of a natural name of power: it is set alongside Sabaoth and Adonai and the angelic names as effectual by its very nature. Earlier, answering Celsus’s charge that Christians work by “the names of certain demons… and… incantations,” Origen fixes the difference: it is “not by incantations that Christians seem to prevail… but by the name of Jesus, accompanied by the announcement of the narratives which relate to Him” — and this Name is so bound to the One named, not to the worthiness of the speaker, that “there have been instances where it was effectual, when it was pronounced even by bad men,” exactly as Jesus foretold: “Many shall say to Me in that day, In Thy name we have cast out devils…”5 The Name avails apart from the wielder’s standing — the very tension the New-Testament foundation of this timeline opens (the strange exorcist and the sons of Sceva).

Angelic names, too, are fitted to their offices

And when one is able to philosophize about the mystery of names, he will find much to say respecting the titles of the angels of God, of whom one is called Michael, and another Gabriel, and another Raphael, appropriately to the duties which they discharge in the world, according to the will of the God of all things.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXV, source.4 The angelic names complete the picture: from the divine names, through the angelic names, to the name of Jesus, a whole graded order of names carries power “appropriately to” the reality each names. It is this systematic account — names as naturally, not conventionally, potent, arranged by rank — that makes Origen the hub of the doctrine and the most developed theory of it in the fathers.

The boundary: the reality of names is not a license for magic

… let him tell… how sorcery can exist among those who have accepted the Mosaic law, and read the injunction, “Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them.”

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXVI (quoting Deuteronomy 18:11), source.6 The same books that theorize the power of names condemn the working of magic. Where Origen grants that magic “exists,” he immediately fixes its source in the demonic — Celsus’s conjurers are “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit”7 — and his paradigm case is the Magi, who were “on familiar terms with evil spirits” and whose art collapsed at the Nativity, “the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken”8 (told on the Magi timeline). So the reality of an art and its licitness are, for Origen, two different questions: the divine names vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts, and the sorcerer’s own practice is forbidden and defiling. The power of the Name is not, in his hands, an endorsement of magic but a weapon against it.

Notes

  • Origen is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 248, after Justin Martyr (c. 160) and Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180). Justin argued the Name’s exorcistic power as a public fact and Irenaeus set the church’s prayer-borne power against Gnostic name-magic; Origen supplies the theory both assumed — why a name has power at all — and so is the doctrine’s centrepiece.
  • One doctrine, four homes. Origen’s name-material is drawn on across this wiki, and this page is its hub for the power of the Name. The same passages are read for the untranslated Tetragrammaton in the biblical text on his Divine Name page; for name-realism versus Gregory of Nyssa’s conventionalism on his first-language page and the argument was the primordial language a divine gift? (plausible); and for the charge that this doctrine amounts to teaching magic on “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?” (contested). This page consolidates the exorcistic/invocational thread — the Name of Jesus and the divine names as effectual in casting out demons — and leaves those pages their own questions.
  • The honest caveat. Origen defends the reality of names and of magic-as-a-system, but condemns sorcery as demonic and forbidden (Deut 18:11; the Magi’s art broken at Bethlehem). The standing charge-lists that read him as teaching “magic is not an evil” misname an epistemological and philological claim as a moral one — the two-sided verdict weighed on the magic-and-divine-names argument, which this page does not re-litigate.
