Origen of Alexandria on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 248 (Against Celsus, at Caesarea; Origen c. 185–253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen sets out, in Against Celsus, an account of why a name has power. Against Celsus’s gibe that it makes no difference whether God be called Zeus or Sabaoth, Origen argues that names are “not conventional but natural”: a power is lodged in the very sounds, so that the Hebrew divine names — Sabaoth, Adonai, “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, of Jacob” — vanquish demons in their native form and go dead when translated. Above all the name of Jesus “has expelled myriads of evil spirits,” and, he says, works even on unworthy lips. He also holds that sorcery is forbidden by the law and demonic in its power; for Origen the reality of names is not a license for magic.

Claims & evidence

Names are natural, not conventional — a power lodged in the very sounds

… this involves a deep and mysterious subject—that, viz., respecting the nature of names: it being a question whether, as Aristotle thinks, names were bestowed by arrangement, or, as the Stoics hold, by nature… or whether, as Epicurus teaches (differing in this from the Stoics), names were given by nature… If, then, we shall be able to establish, in reference to the preceding statement, the nature of powerful names… and shall be able to make out that the so-called magic is not, as the followers of Epicurus and Aristotle suppose, an altogether uncertain thing, but is, as those skilled in it prove, a consistent system, having words which are known to exceedingly few; then we say that the name Sabaoth, and Adonai, and the other names treated with so much reverence among the Hebrews, are not applicable to any ordinary created things, but belong to a secret theology which refers to the Framer of all things. These names, accordingly, when pronounced with that attendant train of circumstances which is appropriate to their nature, are possessed of great power.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXIV, source.1 Origen states the mechanism in the next chapter: the power is in the word, not in what the word means. “It is not the things signified, but the qualities and peculiarities of words, which possess a certain power for this or that purpose” — which is why, he says, “the utterance of the same incantation in its proper language can accomplish what the spell professes to do; but when translated into any other tongue, it is observed to become inefficacious and feeble.”2 The same name-realism is set out, on the question of the primeval tongue, on Origen’s first-language page.

The Hebrew divine names vanquish demons — and lose the power when translated

… something is transferred from the name Abraham when translated into Greek, and something is signified by that of Isaac, and also by that of Jacob; and accordingly, if any one, either in an invocation or in swearing an oath, were to use the expression, “the God of Abraham,” and “the God of Isaac,” and “the God of Jacob,” he would produce certain effects, either owing to the nature of these names or to their powers, since even demons are vanquished and become submissive to him who pronounces these names; whereas if we say, “the god of the chosen father of the echo, and the god of laughter, and the god of him who strikes with the heel,” the mention of the name is attended with no result, as is the case with other names possessed of no power… And we may say the same also of the pronunciation of “Sabaoth,” a word which is frequently employed in incantations; for if we translate the term into “Lord of hosts,” or “Lord of armies,” or “Almighty”… we shall accomplish nothing; whereas if we retain the original pronunciation, we shall, as those who are skilled in such matters maintain, produce some effect. And the same observation holds good of Adonai.

— Against Celsus, Book V, ch. XLV, source.3 This is the reason Origen gives, against Celsus’s “it makes no difference,” for the Christians’ refusal to swap the divine names for Greek equivalents: on his account the untranslated Hebrew names bind the spirits, and a translation has no effect. The same names, and their untranslated survival in the biblical text, are traced on his Divine Name page; the later charge that Origen taught magic is set out on “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?”

“Whose name has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits” — the name of Jesus

And a similar philosophy of names applies also to our Jesus, whose name has already been seen, in an unmistakeable manner, to have expelled myriads of evil spirits from the souls and bodies (of men), so great was the power which it exerted upon those from whom the spirits were driven out.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXV, source.4 Origen sets the name of Jesus alongside Sabaoth and Adonai and the angelic names as a natural name of power, effectual by its nature. Earlier, answering Celsus’s charge that Christians work by “the names of certain demons… and… incantations,” Origen fixes the difference: it is “not by incantations that Christians seem to prevail… but by the name of Jesus, accompanied by the announcement of the narratives which relate to Him” — and he adds that “there have been instances where it was effectual, when it was pronounced even by bad men,” as he reads Jesus’ words: “Many shall say to Me in that day, In Thy name we have cast out devils…”5 On this account the Name avails apart from the speaker’s standing; the New Testament passages on the strange exorcist (Mark 9:38–39) and the sons of Sceva (Acts 19:13–17) are set out at the head of the summary timeline.

Angelic names, too, are fitted to their offices

And when one is able to philosophize about the mystery of names, he will find much to say respecting the titles of the angels of God, of whom one is called Michael, and another Gabriel, and another Raphael, appropriately to the duties which they discharge in the world, according to the will of the God of all things.

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXV, source.4 With the angelic names, Origen’s account runs from the divine names, through the angelic names, to the name of Jesus, each named “appropriately to” the reality it names: names as naturally, not conventionally, potent.

Origen on sorcery: the reality of names and the licitness of magic

… let him tell… how sorcery can exist among those who have accepted the Mosaic law, and read the injunction, “Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them.”

— Against Celsus, Book I, ch. XXVI (quoting Deuteronomy 18:11), source.6 The same books that theorize the power of names condemn the working of magic. Where Origen grants that magic “exists,” he locates its source in the demonic — Celsus’s conjurers are “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit”7 — and his paradigm case is the Magi, who were “on familiar terms with evil spirits” and whose art collapsed at the Nativity, “the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken”8 (told on the Magi timeline). For Origen the reality of an art and its licitness are two different questions: the divine names, he says, vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts, and the sorcerer’s own practice is forbidden and defiling.

Placement on the timeline

  • Origen is placed on the power of the Name timeline at c. 248, after Justin Martyr (c. 160) and Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180). Justin presented the Name’s exorcistic power as a public fact and Irenaeus set the church’s prayer-borne power against Gnostic practice; Origen gives an account of why a name has power at all.
  • Other pages. Origen’s name-material appears on several pages of this wiki; this page collects it for the power of the Name. The same passages are read for the untranslated Tetragrammaton in the biblical text on his Divine Name page; for name-realism versus Gregory of Nyssa’s conventionalism on his first-language page; and for the charge that this doctrine amounts to teaching magic on “Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power?” This page collects the exorcistic and invocational thread — the Name of Jesus and the divine names as effectual in casting out demons.
  • The charge of teaching magic. Origen defends the reality of names and of magic as a system, and condemns sorcery as demonic and forbidden (Deut 18:11; the Magi’s art broken at Bethlehem). Later charge-lists read him as teaching that “magic is not an evil”; how that charge and his text have been read is set out on the magic-and-divine-names page.
