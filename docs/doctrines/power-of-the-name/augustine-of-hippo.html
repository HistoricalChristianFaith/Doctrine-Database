Augustine of Hippo on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 354–430 (bishop of Hippo Regius from 396; the block is seated at c. 415 by The City of God, whose books on the demons and the idols were written c. 413–417 and whose catalogue of contemporary deliverances “in the name of Christ” closes the work c. 426; On Christian Doctrine Book II dates to c. 397, the Tractates on the Gospel of John to c. 406–421) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine is the great Latin hinge of this doctrine, speaking to both its poles in their sharpest Western form. On the one side he presses, like Athanasius, the apologetic argument that where Christ is named the idols fall and the demons are put to flight — the church casts them out “by exorcising… not by propitiating,” and he catalogues the deliverances wrought in his own day “in the name of Christ.” On the other he draws the anti-magic boundary in the classic patristic anatomy of the licit and illicit sign: amulets, characters, and incantations are “leagues with devils,” a herb drunk as medicine is lawful where the same herb hung round the neck as a charm is condemned. And he names the exact point of danger this whole timeline polices — the sorcerers who “mingle the name of Christ with their incantations” — while allowing that a man may lawfully lay the Gospel at his aching head, “not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets.”

Claims & evidence

“The Christian religion is overthrowing all lying figments” — where Christ is named, the idols fall

Augustine takes up the apologetic proof Athanasius had pressed a century before — that the plain, public collapse of idolatry where Christ is preached is itself evidence of his power. Reading the Hermetic lament that the Egyptian gods would one day be driven from their temples, he turns it into a witness against its own author:

Hermes then follows out at great length the statements of this passage, in which he seems to predict the present time, in which the Christian religion is overthrowing all lying figments with a vehemence and liberty proportioned to its superior truth and holiness, in order that the grace of the true Saviour may deliver men from those gods which man has made, and subject them to that God by whom man was made.

— The City of God, Book VIII, ch. 23, source.1 The pagan sage foresaw the overthrow of his gods and mourned it; Augustine reads the same fact the other way, as the promised deliverance of men from “those gods which man has made.” This is the historical form of the argument the whole timeline records: the demon-cult that was strong before Christ falls silent where his name is preached — the datum Athanasius had made a proof of the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ.

“Conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity” — the church exorcises the demons, it does not propitiate them

Against the Platonist theurgists (Porphyry above all), who sought to purify the soul by rites that placated the spirits of the air, Augustine sets the church’s wholly opposite practice: it drives the demons out by the name of Christ, praying not to them but against them.

It is by true piety that men of God cast out the hostile power of the air which opposes godliness; it is by exorcising it, not by propitiating it; and they overcome all the temptations of the adversary by praying, not to him, but to their own God against him. For the devil cannot conquer or subdue any but those who are in league with sin; and therefore he is conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity, and that without sin, that Himself being both Priest and Sacrifice, He might bring about the remission of sins.

— The City of God, Book X, ch. 22, source.2 The verb is exact: the demon is “conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity.” And the contrast with theurgy carries the doctrine’s central line — the Name is not a rite that manages the spirits on their own terms (that would be to join them), but the invocation of the sinless Mediator before whom they cannot stand. The power is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith, the reading weighed on the intrinsic-power-or-the-Person argument (sound).

“Even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ” — the deliverances of Augustine’s own day

In the last book of The City of God Augustine answers the sceptic’s taunt — why are the apostolic miracles no longer done? — by insisting they are, and cataloguing cures and exorcisms he has himself witnessed or verified:

For even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ, whether by His sacraments or by the prayers or relics of His saints…

Hesperius… finding that his family, his cattle, and his servants were suffering from the malice of evil spirits, he asked our presbyters, during my absence, that one of them would go with him and banish the spirits by his prayers. One went, offered there the sacrifice of the body of Christ, praying with all his might that that vexation might cease. It did cease forthwith, through God’s mercy… I know that a young woman of Hippo was immediately dispossessed of a devil, on anointing herself with oil, mixed with the tears of the presbyter who had been praying for her. I know also that a bishop once prayed for a demoniac young man whom he never saw, and that he was cured on the spot.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 8, with ch. 9, source.3 The deliverances are done “in the name of Christ” by prayer, the eucharistic sacrifice, and the relics of the martyrs — and Augustine is careful, in the following chapters, to keep them clear of the demons’ own marvels: the martyrs work these signs “that God may be worshipped,” the demons theirs “that they themselves may be supposed to be God,” so that the two are opposites, not competitors — the same distinction between the power of the Name and the presumption of magic that the whole timeline draws.

“Amulets… incantations… marks which they call characters” — the anatomy of the illicit sign

The counter-pole is drawn in its sharpest Latin form in On Christian Doctrine, where Augustine sorts the arts of the heathen into the lawful and the superstitious and files amulet-magic squarely with the latter:

In this class we must place also all amulets and cures which the medical art condemns, whether these consist in Incantations, or in marks which they call characters, or in hanging or tying on or even dancing in a fashion certain articles, not with reference to the condition of the body, but to certain signs hidden or manifest; and these remedies they call by the less offensive name of physica, so as to appear not to be engaged in superstitious observances, but to be taking advantage of the forces of nature.

— On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 20, source.4 All such arts, he concludes, “are either nullities, or are part of a guilty superstition, springing out of a baleful fellowship between men and devils, and are to be utterly repudiated and avoided by the Christian as the covenants of a false and treacherous friendship.”5 The category is the one Cyril had listed among the renounced “service of the devil” (“amulets, or charms written on leaves, sorceries”) and Chrysostom set the cross against (“charms and amulets… golden coins of Alexander”) — here given its theory: what makes a thing sorcery is not its outward form but that it is a sign transacted with demons.

“The gospel is preferred to amulets” — where the line actually runs

Augustine also shows how fine the line is, and that it runs through the disposition, not the object. The same herb, he observes, is lawful as medicine and forbidden as a charm:

For it is one thing to say: If you bruise down this herb and drink it, it will remove the pain from your stomach; and another to say: If you hang this herb round your neck, it will remove the pain from your stomach. In the former case the wholesome mixture is approved of, in the latter the superstitious charm is condemned; although indeed, where incantations and invocations and marks are not used, it is frequently doubtful whether the thing that is tied or fixed in any way to the body to cure it, acts by a natural virtue, in which case it may be freely used; or acts by a sort of charm, in which case it becomes the Christian to avoid it the more carefully, the more efficacious it may seem to be.

— On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 29, source.6 Preaching on John, he names the precise abuse this doctrine most fears — the Name itself folded into a spell: the seducers “by means of amulets, by incantations… mingle the name of Christ with their incantations… they add some honey, that by means of the sweet the bitter may be concealed.” Yet in the same sermon he blesses a use of the sacred text that stops just short of the charm:

When thy head aches, we praise thee if thou placest the gospel at thy head, instead of having recourse to an amulet. For so far has human weakness proceeded… that we rejoice when we see a man who is upon his bed, and tossed about with fevers and pains, placing his hope on nothing else than that the gospel lies at his head; not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets.

— Tractates on the Gospel of John, Tractate VII, §§6 and 12, source.7 The Gospel at the head is licit not because the parchment works a cure — that would be an amulet — but because a man in pain who trusts the Gospel rather than a charm has his hope in the right place. This is the exact crux the argument on apotropaic devotion and the talisman (sound) turns on: the line between licit devotion and magic runs through the disposition of the heart, not the outward object — and even the most anti-magic of the fathers could bless a scrap of Scripture at the neck while condemning the charm.

Notes

  • Augustine is seated on the power of the Name timeline at c. 415, after John Chrysostom (c. 400) and before Diadochos of Photike (c. 486). He is the great Latin voice the timeline had been missing between the Greek fathers’ exorcistic argument and the medieval Western devotion.
  • Both poles, in their Western form. Augustine restates the doctrine’s two halves that the earlier witnesses hold together — the real power of the Name against the demons, and the hard line against wielding a name as a spell — but sharpens each: the first as an anti-theurgy argument (the church exorcises, it does not propitiate), the second as the classic anatomy of the licit and illicit sign (a thing used by nature versus a sign transacted with demons).
  • The line runs through the disposition, not the object. His herb-drunk-versus-herb-hung and Gospel-at-the-head passages are the tradition’s clearest statement that the boundary against magic cannot be read off the artifact: the same act may be medicine or charm, devotion or talisman, according to whether the trust is in a Person or in a technique. This is why he is cited on the apotropaic-devotion argument.
  • Augustine appears on this wiki also on many other timelines; this page is confined to his teaching on the power of the Name.
