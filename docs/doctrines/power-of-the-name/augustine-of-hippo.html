Augustine of Hippo on the power of the Name

Dates: c. 354–430 (bishop of Hippo Regius from 396; the block is seated at c. 415 by The City of God, whose books on the demons and the idols were written c. 413–417 and whose catalogue of contemporary deliverances “in the name of Christ” closes the work c. 426; On Christian Doctrine Book II dates to c. 397, the Tractates on the Gospel of John to c. 406–421) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine writes on both sides of the question. On the one side he makes, like Athanasius, the apologetic argument that where Christ is named the idols fall and the demons are put to flight — the church casts them out “by exorcising… not by propitiating,” and he catalogues the deliverances wrought in his own day “in the name of Christ.” On the other he sorts licit from illicit signs in On Christian Doctrine: amulets, characters, and incantations are “leagues with devils,” a herb drunk as medicine is lawful where the same herb hung round the neck as a charm is condemned. He also names the sorcerers who “mingle the name of Christ with their incantations” — while praising a man who lays the Gospel at his aching head, “not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets.”

Claims & evidence

“The Christian religion is overthrowing all lying figments” — where Christ is named, the idols fall

Augustine makes an apologetic argument like the one Athanasius had made a century before — that the public collapse of idolatry where Christ is preached is evidence of his power. Reading the Hermetic lament that the Egyptian gods would one day be driven from their temples, he reads it against its author:

Hermes then follows out at great length the statements of this passage, in which he seems to predict the present time, in which the Christian religion is overthrowing all lying figments with a vehemence and liberty proportioned to its superior truth and holiness, in order that the grace of the true Saviour may deliver men from those gods which man has made, and subject them to that God by whom man was made.

— The City of God, Book VIII, ch. 23, source.1 Where the Hermetic text mourns the overthrow of its gods, Augustine reads the same event as the deliverance of men from “those gods which man has made.” Athanasius had used the falling-silent of the oracles in an argument for the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ.

“Conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity” — the church exorcises the demons, it does not propitiate them

Against the Platonist theurgists (Porphyry above all), who on his account sought to purify the soul by rites that placated the spirits of the air, Augustine sets the church’s practice: it drives the demons out by the name of Christ, praying not to them but against them.

It is by true piety that men of God cast out the hostile power of the air which opposes godliness; it is by exorcising it, not by propitiating it; and they overcome all the temptations of the adversary by praying, not to him, but to their own God against him. For the devil cannot conquer or subdue any but those who are in league with sin; and therefore he is conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity, and that without sin, that Himself being both Priest and Sacrifice, He might bring about the remission of sins.

— The City of God, Book X, ch. 22, source.2 The demon, Augustine writes, is “conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity.” In his contrast with theurgy the Name is not a rite that manages the spirits on their own terms but the invocation of the sinless Mediator, “both Priest and Sacrifice.”

“Even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ” — the deliverances of Augustine’s own day

In the last book of The City of God Augustine answers the sceptic’s taunt — why are the apostolic miracles no longer done? — by saying that they are, and cataloguing cures and exorcisms he reports having witnessed or verified:

For even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ, whether by His sacraments or by the prayers or relics of His saints…

Hesperius… finding that his family, his cattle, and his servants were suffering from the malice of evil spirits, he asked our presbyters, during my absence, that one of them would go with him and banish the spirits by his prayers. One went, offered there the sacrifice of the body of Christ, praying with all his might that that vexation might cease. It did cease forthwith, through God’s mercy… I know that a young woman of Hippo was immediately dispossessed of a devil, on anointing herself with oil, mixed with the tears of the presbyter who had been praying for her. I know also that a bishop once prayed for a demoniac young man whom he never saw, and that he was cured on the spot.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 8, with ch. 9, source.3 The deliverances are done “in the name of Christ” by prayer, the eucharistic sacrifice, and the relics of the martyrs — and in the following chapters Augustine distinguishes them from the demons’ own marvels: the martyrs work these signs “that the true God may be worshipped,” the demons theirs “that they themselves may be supposed to be God.”

“Amulets… incantations… marks which they call characters” — the anatomy of the illicit sign

In On Christian Doctrine Augustine sorts the arts of the heathen into the lawful and the superstitious and places amulet-magic with the latter:

In this class we must place also all amulets and cures which the medical art condemns, whether these consist in Incantations, or in marks which they call characters, or in hanging or tying on or even dancing in a fashion certain articles, not with reference to the condition of the body, but to certain signs hidden or manifest; and these remedies they call by the less offensive name of physica, so as to appear not to be engaged in superstitious observances, but to be taking advantage of the forces of nature.

— On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 20, source.4 All such arts, he concludes, “are either nullities, or are part of a guilty superstition, springing out of a baleful fellowship between men and devils, and are to be utterly repudiated and avoided by the Christian as the covenants of a false and treacherous friendship.”5 The category is the one Cyril had listed among the renounced “service of the devil” (“amulets, or charms written on leaves, sorceries”) and Chrysostom set the cross against (“charms and amulets… golden coins of Alexander”) — and Augustine gives an account of it: on his view what makes a thing sorcery is not its outward form but that it is a sign transacted with demons.

“The gospel is preferred to amulets” — Augustine on cases near the line

Augustine also treats cases close to the line. The same herb, he observes, is lawful as medicine and forbidden as a charm:

For it is one thing to say: If you bruise down this herb and drink it, it will remove the pain from your stomach; and another to say: If you hang this herb round your neck, it will remove the pain from your stomach. In the former case the wholesome mixture is approved of, in the latter the superstitious charm is condemned; although indeed, where incantations and invocations and marks are not used, it is frequently doubtful whether the thing that is tied or fixed in any way to the body to cure it, acts by a natural virtue, in which case it may be freely used; or acts by a sort of charm, in which case it becomes the Christian to avoid it the more carefully, the more efficacious it may seem to be.

— On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 29, source.6 Preaching on John, he names the use of the Name itself within a spell: the seducers “by means of amulets, by incantations… mingle the name of Christ with their incantations… they add some honey, that by means of the sweet the bitter may be concealed.” In the same sermon he praises a use of the sacred text that he distinguishes from the charm:

When thy head aches, we praise thee if thou placest the gospel at thy head, instead of having recourse to an amulet. For so far has human weakness proceeded… that we rejoice when we see a man who is upon his bed, and tossed about with fevers and pains, placing his hope on nothing else than that the gospel lies at his head; not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets.

— Tractates on the Gospel of John, Tractate VII, §§6 and 12, source.7 Augustine praises the Gospel at the head “not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets”: on his account what he commends is the sick man’s hope in the Gospel rather than in a charm.

Placement on the timeline

  • Augustine is placed on the power of the Name timeline at c. 415, after John Chrysostom (c. 400) and before Diadochos of Photike (c. 486), by the books of The City of God cited here.
  • Two sides in one writer. Like earlier witnesses on the timeline, Augustine both claims power for the Name against the demons and condemns using a name as a spell. He frames the first against theurgy (the church exorcises, it does not propitiate) and the second as a distinction between licit and illicit signs (a thing used by nature versus a sign transacted with demons).
  • Disposition and object. In the herb-drunk-versus-herb-hung and Gospel-at-the-head passages Augustine treats the same act as medicine or charm according to how it is used and what it is trusted for.
  • Augustine appears on this wiki also on many other timelines; this page is confined to his teaching on the power of the Name.
