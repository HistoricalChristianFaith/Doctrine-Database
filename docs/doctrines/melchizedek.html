Who Was Melchizedek?

Melchizedek appears in a single scene of three verses — a king of Salem and “priest of the most high God” who meets Abram returning from battle, brings out bread and wine, blesses him, and receives a tithe of the spoils (Genesis 14:18–20) — and then leaves the narrative, with no genealogy, birth, or death recorded. Two later texts return to him. A royal psalm swears Israel’s king into an eternal priesthood “after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), and the Epistle to the Hebrews makes that order central to its argument that Christ’s priesthood surpasses Aaron’s, describing Melchizedek as “without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life” and “made like unto the Son of God” (Hebrews 7:3). Later readers asked who he was: a Canaanite priest-king of Salem, an angel or heavenly being, the Holy Spirit, a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ, the patriarch Shem under another name, or a man whose obscurity the Epistle reads as a type. They also asked what the bread and wine he brought out signified, a gesture Cyprian and later Fathers read as a figure of the Eucharist.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Melchizedek, but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest extant source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis scene and the oath of Psalm 110, the Epistle to the Hebrews, the heavenly redeemer-judge of the Qumran scroll 11QMelchizedek, Philo’s priest who figures reason, Josephus’s founder of Jerusalem, the birth legend of 2 Enoch, the rabbinic identification with Shem, the sect called the Melchizedekians, the Fathers from Cyprian to Theodoret, and a survey of modern scholarship. One question on which these sources divide, whether Melchizedek was Shem, has its own page of readings.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and the Epistle to the Hebrews)

The later tradition works from one brief encounter and two texts that reach back to it. In Genesis 14:18–20, as Abram returns from rescuing Lot and defeating the eastern kings, “Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God” (El Elyon); he blesses Abram in the name of “the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth,” and Abram “gave him tithes of all.” The scene records no lineage, birth, or death. A royal enthronement psalm swears Israel’s king into that same priest-king office — “The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4) — a priesthood defined not by descent from Aaron but by a divine oath.1 The Epistle to the Hebrews makes this the pivot of its argument. Quoting Psalm 110:4 repeatedly (Hebrews 5:6, 10; 6:20), it describes Melchizedek in the terms the Genesis record leaves blank: he is “first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace; without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually” (Hebrews 7:1–3). Because Abraham — and so the unborn Levi “in the loins of his father” — paid tithes to Melchizedek and received his blessing, the argument runs, this priesthood is greater than the Levitical, and Christ, a priest “after the order of Melchizedek” rather than of Aaron, holds an eternal and unchanging office (Hebrews 7:4–28). Many of the Christian witnesses below read Genesis 14 through Psalm 110 and this chapter of Hebrews.2

The heavenly redeemer-judge of Qumran (11QMelchizedek, c. 100 BC)

The earliest extant interpretation of Melchizedek outside the Bible presents him as a heavenly figure. In a fragmentary eschatological midrash from Qumran Cave 11 — 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), composed around 100 BC — Melchizedek is a heavenly, quasi-divine deliverer: an elohim who presides over the final Day of Atonement and the tenth Jubilee, proclaims “liberty” to the captives (fusing the release of Leviticus 25 with the “liberty to the captives” of Isaiah 61:1), and executes God’s judgment on Belial and the spirits of his lot.3 The scroll quotes Psalm 82:1 — “Elohim will [st]and in the assem[bly of God,] in the midst of the gods he judges” — and Joseph Fitzmyer reads the presiding elohim not as YHWH but as Melchizedek himself, seated among the divine beings as God’s eschatological judge; Rick Van de Water reopens the identification of the figure in “Michael or YHWH? Toward Identifying Melchizedek in 11Q13.” A separate anointed herald, tied to the “anointed prince” of Daniel 9:25, announces his day of salvation. The surviving text does not cite Genesis 14 or Psalm 110:4. The scroll’s use of Psalm 82 also appears on the divine council timeline.4

The priest who figures reason in Philo (Legum Allegoriae III.79–82, early first century AD)

The earliest extant allegorical reading of the Genesis scene itself comes from the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – AD 50). In his Allegorical Interpretation III.79–82 he parses the names as Hebrews 7:2 also does — “Melchizedek” as “righteous king,” “Salem” as “peace” — and turns the priest-king into a figure of the mind’s ruling faculty: the “kingly mind” that governs by persuasion, “directed in its course by a good governor and pilot; and this good pilot is right reason” (orthos logos), over against the “tyrannical mind” that rules by force. The bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 become the “food full of cheerfulness and joy” that reason supplies to the soul — a “divine intoxication, more sober than sobriety itself.” (The Eucharistic reading of the bread and wine is traced on the real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.)5 The pivotal line is his gloss on “priest of the most high God”: “reason is a priest, having, as its inheritance the true God.” Whether logos here denotes the divine Word or only the soul’s rational principle is the question of a recent study, “Did Philo of Alexandria Consider Melchizedek to Be the Logos of God in Legum allegoriae III, 79–82?” Philo and Hebrews both attest the same first-century etymology and put it to different uses: for Philo, Melchizedek is an allegory of reason within the soul; for Hebrews, a figure “made like unto the Son of God.”6

The Canaanite founder-priest of Josephus (Antiquities I.180; Jewish War VI.438, c. AD 75–94)

The historian Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) presents Melchizedek as a historical man. Retelling Genesis 14 in Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2, he glosses the name as the others do — “Melchisedec, king of the city Salem… that name signifies, the righteous king” — and states: “such he was, without dispute, insomuch that, on this account he was made the priest of God: however, they afterward called Salem Jerusalem.” Melchizedek feasts Abram’s returning army “in an hospitable manner,” blesses him, and accepts “the tenth part of his prey”; in this retelling the bread and wine are provisions for the army.7 In the Jewish War VI.10.1, pausing at Jerusalem’s fall to reflect on its history, he names its founder as this same figure: “he who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King… on which account he was the first priest of God, and first built a temple, and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.” William Horbury treats Josephus as the principal early witness to the equation Salem = Jerusalem and to the reading of Melchizedek as a historical Canaanite priest-king, and compares him with the heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13.8

The birth of Melchizedek in 2 Enoch (2 Enoch 71–72; first century AD or later)

Another more-than-human Melchizedek appears in a birth legend. The closing chapters of the longer recension of the Slavonic 2 Enoch (the Book of the Secrets of Enoch) tell how, in the generation before the Flood, Melchizedek is born miraculously from the corpse of Sopanima, the barren and aged wife of Noah’s brother Nir the priest — who “did not sleep with her, nor knew her.” She dies at Nir’s rebuke, and “then came an infant from the dead Sopanima… the child was complete in its body, like one of three years old; and spake with its lips, and blessed the Lord.” On his chest is “the seal of the priesthood”; Noah and Nir clothe him in priestly raiment, give him “the blessed bread,” and name him Melchizedek — the priesthood inborn rather than conferred.9 To save him from the coming deluge the archangel Michael carries the child “on his wings” into “the paradise of Eden,” so that he outlives the Flood without entering the ark and the priesthood continues to a later, earthly Melchizedek, “the first king in the town of Salem.” The legend supplies a birth and a rescue where Genesis is silent, the same silences that Hebrews 7:3 describes — “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days, nor end of life.” Its date, and whether it is Jewish or Christian in origin, are disputed (F. I. Andersen), and the Melchizedek section may be a separate unit; it is placed here as a reception-stratum rather than a fixed first-century witness.10

The rabbinic identification with Shem son of Noah (the classical rabbinic corpus, c. AD 200–600)

The rabbinic sources identify Melchizedek with Shem, the eldest son of Noah, still alive in Abram’s day on the long lifespans of Genesis 11. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan glosses Malka Zadika as “Shem bar Noah”: “Malka Zadika, who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem, came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine” (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18). On this identification the priest who blesses Abram belongs to Abram’s own ancestry. Jerome reported “the opinion of the Hebrews,” with its chronological calculation (“Shem… survived Abraham by thirty-five years”), to a Latin correspondent in Letter 73 (c. 398).11 The Babylonian Talmud adds that Melchizedek forfeits the priesthood to Abram for blessing Abram before he blessed God — “Once Melchizedek… placed the blessing of Abraham before the blessing of the Omnipresent, He had the priesthood emerge from Abraham” — and Psalm 110:4 (al divrati malki tzedek) is read homiletically as “due to the improper words of Melchizedek, the offspring of Abraham shall be priests of God forever” (b. Nedarim 32b). The Epistle to the Hebrews reads the same verse of an eternal priesthood after the order of Melchizedek; the Gemara reads it of the priesthood passing from Melchizedek to the line of Abraham. The sources are set out on the rabbinic-tradition page, and the identification is the subject of the disputed reading Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?12

The Melchizedekians: Melchizedek made a power above Christ (Theodotus’s sect, reported from c. AD 235)

Around AD 200, Hippolytus and Pseudo-Tertullian report, a Roman group they call the Melchizedekians — founded by Theodotus “the banker” (the money-changer), who split from the adoptionist school of Theodotus the Cobbler — made Melchizedek not a type of Christ but a heavenly Power ranked above him. Their earliest reporter, Hippolytus of Rome (Refutation of All Heresies VII, c. 235), states the core doctrine: Theodotus “attempted to establish… that a certain Melchisedec constitutes the greatest power, and that this one is greater than Christ. And they allege that Christ happens to be according to the likeness (of this Melchisedec).” Pseudo-Tertullian preserves the mediatorial form — Melchizedek “a heavenly Virtue of pre-eminent grace” who intercedes for the angels as Christ does for men, and so “better than Christ.”13 The fullest ancient account is Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 55, c. 377), who reports their proof-text — a literal reading of “a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), which makes “Christ… younger than Melchizedek” — and answers it by insisting on the figure’s humanity: Melchizedek “was not of the heavenly beings, nor did he come down from heaven,” but a just man, king and priest of Salem, born of human parents. The sect is catalogued afterward by Theodoret (Compendium of Heretical Fables 2.6) and Augustine (De haeresibus 34). A separate identification of Melchizedek with the Holy Spirit is reported of Hieracas of Leontopolis (see Epiphanius).14

The bread and wine as Eucharist in Cyprian of Carthage (Epistle 62/63, c. AD 253)

The earliest Latin witness on this timeline, Cyprian of Carthage (c. 200/210–258), reads the scene through its offering. In Epistle 62 (to Caecilius; = Ep. 63 in the Oxford numbering), c. 253, he makes Melchizedek’s bread and wine a figure of the Eucharist: “in the priest Melchizedek we see prefigured the sacrament of the sacrifice of the Lord… that Melchizedek bore a type of Christ, the Holy Spirit declares in the Psalms.” For Cyprian the “order of Melchizedek” is defined by the sacrifice — Christ “offered that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered, that is, bread and wine, to wit, his body and blood.” (The Eucharistic reading of the bread and wine is traced on the real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.)15

The non-Levitical priest of the nations: Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica 5.3, c. AD 318)

The earliest of the Greek exegetes on this timeline, Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260/265–339), treats Melchizedek at length in an apologetic work. In the Demonstratio Evangelica (Proof of the Gospel) 5.3 he reads the figure as the type of a priesthood defined by everything the Aaronic order was not: Melchizedek “had no characteristics shared by the Aaronic priesthood… he was not chosen by men, he was not anointed with prepared oil, he was not of the tribe… and strangest of all, he was not even circumcised in his flesh, and yet he blesses Abraham, as if he were far better than he.” For Eusebius he is a real priest — not an angel — standing outside Israel, before the Law and circumcision, and a type of the priesthood of Christ, which is likewise not by descent and reaches the nations. Eusebius links the bread and wine to the Church’s “spiritual sacrifice” offered “among all nations… with wine and bread darkly express[ing] the mysteries of His Body and saving Blood.” Jerome names Eusebius among the writers who held Melchizedek “a Canaanite man” (Letter 73, c. 398).16

“This Melchizedek is Shem”: Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Genesis XI, c. AD 370)

Among the Fathers on this timeline, Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373) is the one who holds the identification of Melchizedek with Shem son of Noah as his own reading. Where Eusebius and Ambrose held to a human Canaanite priest-king, and Jerome reports the Shem equation as “the opinion of the Hebrews,” Ephrem states it in his Commentary on Genesis XI: “This Melchizedek is Shem, who became a king due to his greatness; he was the head of fourteen nations. In addition, he was a priest. He received this from Noah, his father, through the rights of succession.” He grounds the equation in Shem’s longevity — “because of the length of Melchizedek’s life extended to the time of Jacob and Esau… he was Shem” — and in the deference of the patriarchs: Rebekah would not have gone to consult a stranger, nor Abraham have tithed to a mere Canaanite, unless this priest were the greatest man then living. Ephrem read the Syriac Peshitta, whose Genesis 11 carries the shorter chronology on which Shem’s six hundred years overlap the patriarchs. He is the earliest extant Christian holder of the identification on this timeline; see the disputed reading Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?17

“A holy man, not an angel”: Ambrose of Milan (De fide III; On the Sacraments, c. AD 380)

Ambrose of Milan (c. 339–397) states the identity directly. Ambrose insists the Church holds him to be “not… an angel (as some Jewish triflers do), but a holy man and priest of God, who, prefiguring our Lord, is described as ‘without father or mother…’” (Exposition of the Christian Faith III.88). For Ambrose the silences of Hebrews 7:3 make him a type, not a divine being: he is “received as a priest of God made upon the model of Christ… the one we regard as the type, the other as the original.” In his baptismal catechesis he reads the bread and wine as a Eucharistic figure, calling Melchizedek “the author of the sacraments.”18

“From its not being expressed”: John Chrysostom (Homilies on Genesis 35; on Hebrews 12, c. AD 388–403)

An extended exposition comes from John Chrysostom (c. 349–407). Preaching through Hebrews 7 in Homily 12, he reads Hebrews 7:3 this way: Melchizedek is called “without father, without mother… having neither beginning of days nor end of life” not because he literally lacked parents or was deathless — “he both died and had parents” — but “from its not being expressed” in Scripture. For Chrysostom the record’s silence is what makes him a type: we know Melchizedek’s beginning and end “not… because they have not been written; so we know them not in the case of Jesus, not because they have not been written, but because they do not exist. For that indeed is a type… but this is the reality.” On this reading Melchizedek is neither deathless nor a heavenly being. In his Homilies on Genesis he describes him as a priest “possibly self-appointed… like Noah, like Abel, like Abraham,” who “in a particular manner… was to prove a type of Christ.”19

The survey of the opinions: Jerome (Letter 73, Ad Evangelum, c. AD 398)

Jerome (c. 347–420) sets out the earliest extant survey of the opinions. Asked about an anonymous tract arguing that Melchizedek was the Holy Spirit or “of a more divine nature,” he searches the earlier writers and reports two camps: Origen and Didymus made him an angel, while “Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, and Emisenus, Apollinaris also, and… Eustathius” all held “that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem, which was first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem.” He then relays “the opinion of the Hebrews” that Melchizedek was Shem son of Noah, with its chronological reckoning (set out on the rabbinic-tradition page and on the disputed reading Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?), and closes by leaving the judgement to his correspondent: “It was my responsibility to cite (or repeat) witnesses; it is your responsibility to judge the credibility of the witness.” He refuses only the anonymous author’s claim that “Melchizedek had the Holy Spirit.”20

“First appeared the sacrifice”: Augustine (City of God 16.22, c. AD 419)

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) places his weight on the offering and the order. In The City of God 16.22 Melchizedek’s bread and wine are the moment when “first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world”; of Melchizedek “many and great things are written” in Hebrews (which, he notes, “most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this”), and for Augustine the oath “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” means that the Aaronic order was to be “taken away.” At 17.17 he points to the state of things in his own day: “there is nowhere a priesthood and sacrifice after the order of Aaron, and everywhere men offer under Christ as the Priest, which Melchizedek showed.” Augustine treats Melchizedek as a human priest and does not take up the identity question.21

“The image has not all the properties of the archetype”: Theodoret of Cyrus (Eranistes; on Hebrews 7, c. AD 447)

The latest of the Fathers on this timeline, Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 393–458/466), argues from image and archetype. In the Eranistes Melchizedek is called “without father, without mother” only “according to the dispensation of the divine Scripture” — because the record omits his lineage — for “if he had really been without father and without mother, he would not have been an image, but a reality.” What is said of Melchizedek by Scripture’s silence belongs, for Theodoret, to Christ “by nature and in reality,” and he states the direction of the likeness: “he likened not Christ the Lord to Melchizedek, but Melchizedek to Christ the Lord.” Theodoret also catalogues the Melchizedekians, who had made the priest-king a divine Power, some even the Holy Spirit and above Christ; on his argument, a figure fatherless only by the record’s silence, and an image that never holds every property of its archetype, is no divine being. For Theodoret Melchizedek is a man — a Canaanite priest-king, as in Josephus — made a type of Christ.22

Modern scholarship (a survey)

Four lines of modern study recur. First, the Canaanite background: Frank Moore Cross reads El Elyon, “God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth” (Genesis 14:19), as old West-Semitic religious language with close Phoenician and Ugaritic parallels; J. A. Emerton identifies Salem with Jerusalem; and on H. H. Rowley’s hypothesis Melchizedek is a priest-king of pre-Israelite (Jebusite) Jerusalem, his “-zedek” name of a piece with the later Jebusite Adoni-zedek (Joshua 10:1), whose priesthood David inherited with the city and grounded in Abraham through Genesis 14. Second, the dating of the texts: whether Genesis 14 is archaic or a late “erratic block,” whether the Melchizedek scene is a still-later insertion, and whether Psalm 110 is a pre-exilic royal psalm whose “order of Melchizedek” legitimated a non-Levitical Davidic priest-kingship (Emerton, von Rad, Westermann). Third, the heavenly Melchizedek of 11QMelchizedek and the miraculous birth of 2 Enoch: Fred Horton’s survey concluded, against his own initial expectation, that the Epistle to the Hebrews did not draw on that heavenly tradition, while Paul Kobelski holds that Hebrews presupposes a heavenly Melchizedek. Fourth, on Hebrews 7:3, F. F. Bruce and David deSilva read the “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days nor end of life” as a rhetorical argument from silence — the Genesis record’s omissions read as testimony — and Harold Attridge grounds the device in the rabbinic maxim “what is not in the Torah is not in the world,” while allowing that Hebrews may also know a tradition of a heavenly Melchizedek; Chrysostom and Theodoret had read the verse from the record’s silence in antiquity.23 Full detail »
