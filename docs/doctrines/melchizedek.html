Who Was Melchizedek?

No figure of the Hebrew Bible is at once so slight and so weighty as Melchizedek. He appears in a single scene of three verses — a king of Salem and “priest of the most high God” who meets Abram returning from battle, brings out bread and wine, blesses him, and receives a tithe of the spoils (Genesis 14:18–20) — and then vanishes from the narrative entirely, without genealogy, birth, or death. Yet on that silence a whole theology was built. A royal psalm swears Israel’s king into an eternal priesthood “after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), and the Epistle to the Hebrews makes that order the hinge of its argument that Christ’s priesthood surpasses Aaron’s — reading the very gaps in the Genesis record (“without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life”) as a portrait “made like unto the Son of God” (Hebrews 7:3). Who, then, was he? A Canaanite priest-king of Jebusite Salem? An angel or heavenly being? The Holy Spirit? A pre-incarnate appearance of Christ himself? The patriarch Shem living on under another name, as the rabbis held? Or a real man whose very obscurity was, in the Epistle’s hands, made to preach? And what of the bread and wine he carried out — the gesture the Fathers read as the first shadow of the Eucharist?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Melchizedek — the sources for that are too thin — but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis scene and the oath of Psalm 110, the Epistle to the Hebrews, the heavenly redeemer-judge of the Qumran scroll 11QMelchizedek, Philo’s Logos-priest, Josephus’s founder of Jerusalem, the marvelous-birth legend of 2 Enoch, the rabbinic identification with Shem, and the long line of the Fathers — down to the modern scholarship that weighs it all. The genuinely contested questions — what kind of being was he? is the bread and wine a Eucharistic type? who were the Melchizedekians who made him a power above Christ? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and the Epistle to the Hebrews)

The whole later tradition grows from one brief encounter and two texts that reach back to it. In Genesis 14:18–20, as Abram returns from rescuing Lot and defeating the eastern kings, “Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God” (El Elyon); he blesses Abram in the name of “the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth,” and Abram “gave him tithes of all.” The scene ends as abruptly as it began: no lineage, no birth, no death, nothing before and nothing after. Centuries later a royal enthronement psalm swears Israel’s king into that same priest-king office — “The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4) — a priesthood defined not by descent from Aaron but by a divine oath.1 The Epistle to the Hebrews then makes this the pivot of its whole argument. Quoting Psalm 110:4 repeatedly (Hebrews 5:6, 10; 6:20), it turns the silences of the Genesis record into positive testimony: Melchizedek is “first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace; without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually” (Hebrews 7:1–3). Because Abraham — and so the unborn Levi “in the loins of his father” — paid tithes to Melchizedek and received his blessing, the argument runs, this priesthood is greater than the Levitical, and Christ, a priest “after the order of Melchizedek” rather than of Aaron, holds an eternal and unchanging office (Hebrews 7:4–28). It is this reading — the obscure priest-king whose very lack of a recorded genealogy prefigures the Son of God — that every later witness on this timeline inherits and reworks.2

The heavenly redeemer-judge of Qumran (11QMelchizedek, c. 100 BC)

The earliest interpretation of Melchizedek outside the Bible does not read him as a mortal priest-king at all. In a fragmentary eschatological midrash from Qumran Cave 11 — 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), composed around 100 BC — Melchizedek is a heavenly, quasi-divine deliverer: an elohim who presides over the final Day of Atonement and the tenth Jubilee, proclaims “liberty” to the captives (fusing the release of Leviticus 25 with the “liberty to the captives” of Isaiah 61:1), and executes God’s judgment on Belial and the spirits of his lot.3 Most strikingly, the scroll quotes Psalm 82:1 — “Elohim will [st]and in the assem[bly of God,] in the midst of the gods he judges” — and reads the presiding elohim not as YHWH but, on the majority reading of its editors, as Melchizedek himself, seated among the divine beings as God’s eschatological judge (a line-level identification that is genuinely contested). A separate anointed herald, tied to the “anointed prince” of Daniel 9:25, announces his day of salvation. Notably the text never cites Genesis 14 or Psalm 110:4 — so this exalted Melchizedek is a parallel development, not one drawn from the priest-king scene; it is the wellspring of the later “Melchizedek = angel / heavenly being” reading. This witness also anchors the divine-council reading of Psalm 82 developed elsewhere on this wiki.4

The Logos-priest of Philo (Legum Allegoriae III.79–82, early first century AD)

The first interpreter to read the Genesis scene itself allegorically is the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – AD 50). In his Allegorical Interpretation III.79–82 he parses the names exactly as Hebrews 7:2 later will — “Melchizedek” as “righteous king,” “Salem” as “peace” — and turns the priest-king into a figure of the mind’s ruling faculty: the “kingly mind” that governs by persuasion, “directed in its course by a good governor and pilot; and this good pilot is right reason” (orthos logos), over against the “tyrannical mind” that rules by force. The bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 become the “food full of cheerfulness and joy” that reason supplies to the soul — a “divine intoxication, more sober than sobriety itself” — not a Eucharistic type (that is a distinct, later Christian reading, weighed on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines).5 The pivotal line is his gloss on “priest of the most high God”: “reason is a priest, having, as its inheritance the true God.” On the standard (though contested) reading this identifies Melchizedek with the divine Logos — God’s Word — making Philo the bridge from the obscure priest-king to the Logos-christology the Fathers would draw out of Hebrews. Philo and Hebrews independently attest the same first-century etymology, but part ways on its point: for Philo, Melchizedek is an allegory of reason within the soul; for Hebrews, a type “made like unto the Son of God.”6

The Canaanite founder-priest of Josephus (Antiquities I.180; Jewish War VI.438, c. AD 75–94)

The historian Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) gives the tradition’s opposite pole: not a heavenly elohim and not an allegory of the soul, but a real man. Retelling Genesis 14 in Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2, he glosses the name as the others do — “Melchisedec, king of the city Salem… that name signifies, the righteous king” — but draws a plain historical judgment: “such he was, without dispute, insomuch that, on this account he was made the priest of God: however, they afterward called Salem Jerusalem.” Melchizedek feasts Abram’s returning army “in an hospitable manner,” blesses him, and accepts “the tenth part of his prey” — the bread and wine reduced to abundant provisions, with nothing sacramental in view.7 In the Jewish War VI.10.1, pausing at Jerusalem’s fall to reflect on its history, he names its founder as this same figure: “he who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King… on which account he was the first priest of God, and first built a temple, and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.” Josephus is thus the chief early source for two moves the later tradition inherited — the equation Salem = Jerusalem and the reading of Melchizedek as a historical Canaanite (Jebusite) priest-king — the exact opposite of the exalted heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13.8

The marvelous birth of Melchizedek in 2 Enoch (2 Enoch 71–72; first century AD or later)

A third route to a more-than-human Melchizedek runs not through a heavenly courtroom but through a birth-legend. The closing chapters of the longer recension of the Slavonic 2 Enoch (the Book of the Secrets of Enoch) tell how, in the generation before the Flood, Melchizedek is born miraculously from the corpse of Sopanima, the barren and aged wife of Noah’s brother Nir the priest — who “did not sleep with her, nor knew her.” She dies at Nir’s rebuke, and “then came an infant from the dead Sopanima… the child was complete in its body, like one of three years old; and spake with its lips, and blessed the Lord.” On his chest is “the seal of the priesthood”; Noah and Nir clothe him in priestly raiment, give him “the blessed bread,” and name him Melchizedek — the priesthood inborn rather than conferred.9 To save him from the coming deluge the archangel Michael carries the child “on his wings” into “the paradise of Eden,” so that he outlives the Flood without entering the ark and the priesthood continues to a later, earthly Melchizedek, “the first king in the town of Salem.” The legend literalizes the very silences that Hebrews 7:3 reads out of Genesis — “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days, nor end of life” — and is a development parallel to, but independent of, the heavenly elohim of 11Q13. Its date and even its Jewish-versus-Christian origin are disputed (the Melchizedek section may be a separate unit), so it sits here as a reception-stratum, not a firm first-century anchor.10

The rabbinic identification with Shem son of Noah (the classical rabbinic corpus, c. AD 200–600)

Against the exalted Melchizedek of Qumran and 2 Enoch, the ancient synagogue took the opposite tack: it made the priest-king an insider. The dominant rabbinic reading identifies Melchizedek with Shem, the eldest son of Noah — still alive in Abram’s day on the long antediluvian lifespans. The Palestinian Targum states it outright: “Malka Zadika, who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem, came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine” (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18). The move removes the scandal of an uncircumcised gentile priest greater than the patriarch and keeps the priesthood, the blessing, and the tithe inside the chosen line; Jerome would carry “the opinion of the Hebrews,” with its chronological calculation (“Shem… survived Abraham by thirty-five years”), into Latin Christianity.11 More pointed still is what the Babylonian Talmud does with the identification: Melchizedek forfeits the priesthood to Abram for blessing Abram before he blessed God — “Once Melchizedek… placed the blessing of Abraham before the blessing of the Omnipresent, He had the priesthood emerge from Abraham” — and Psalm 110:4 (al divrati malki tzedek) is read homiletically as “due to the improper words of Melchizedek, the offspring of Abraham shall be priests of God forever” (b. Nedarim 32b). This is the Jewish counter-tradition to the christological reading: the very verse the Epistle to the Hebrews makes the ground of an eternal Melchizedek priesthood becomes, in the Gemara, the etiology of that priesthood leaving Melchizedek for the line of Abraham. Whether the identification itself holds is weighed on the rabbinic-tradition witness page and rated on the argument Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?12

The Melchizedekians: Melchizedek made a power above Christ (Theodotus’s sect, reported from c. AD 235)

Around AD 200 the exalted Melchizedek reached its most extreme, and heretical, conclusion. A Roman sect the heresiologists call the Melchizedekians — founded, they report, by Theodotus “the banker” (the money-changer), a group that split from the adoptionist school of Theodotus the Cobbler — made Melchizedek not a type of Christ but a heavenly Power ranked above him. Their earliest reporter, Hippolytus of Rome (Refutation of All Heresies VII, c. 235), states the core doctrine: Theodotus “attempted to establish… that a certain Melchisedec constitutes the greatest power, and that this one is greater than Christ. And they allege that Christ happens to be according to the likeness (of this Melchisedec).” Pseudo-Tertullian preserves the mediatorial form — Melchizedek “a heavenly Virtue of pre-eminent grace” who intercedes for the angels as Christ does for men, and so “better than Christ.” It is the “dark endpoint” of the heavenly-Melchizedek trajectory that begins with 11QMelchizedek and 2 Enoch, and the reading the church took Hebrews 7:3’s “made like unto the Son of God” to guard against: the likeness is the copy, not the archetype.13 The fullest ancient account is Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 55, c. 377), who reports their proof-text — a literal reading of “a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), which makes “Christ… younger than Melchizedek” — and answers it by insisting on the figure’s humanity: Melchizedek “was not of the heavenly beings, nor did he come down from heaven,” but a just man, king and priest of Salem, born of human parents. The sect is catalogued afterward by Theodoret (Compendium of Heretical Fables 2.6) and Augustine (De haeresibus 34). Whether its central claim holds — that Melchizedek was a heavenly power greater than Christ — is weighed on the argument Was Melchizedek a heavenly power greater than Christ? (rated weak); it is distinct from the separate identification of Melchizedek with the Holy Spirit taught by Hieracas of Leontopolis.14

The bread and wine as Eucharist in Cyprian of Carthage (Epistle 62/63, c. AD 253)

With the Latin Fathers the scene is read above all through its offering. The earliest is Cyprian of Carthage (c. 200/210–258), who in Epistle 62 (to Caecilius; = Ep. 63 in the Oxford numbering), c. 253, makes Melchizedek’s bread and wine the first figure of the Eucharist: “in the priest Melchizedek we see prefigured the sacrament of the sacrifice of the Lord… that Melchizedek bore a type of Christ, the Holy Spirit declares in the Psalms.” The “order of Melchizedek” is defined by the sacrifice — Christ “offered that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered, that is, bread and wine, to wit, his body and blood” — so the identity question is answered typologically: Melchizedek is a shadow of the coming Christ, not a being to be classified. (Whether the bread and wine are rightly read as a Eucharistic type is weighed on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.)15

The non-Levitical priest of the nations: Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica 5.3, c. AD 318)

The earliest of the Greek exegetes on this timeline, Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260/265–339), gives Melchizedek his first sustained apologetic role. In the Demonstratio Evangelica (Proof of the Gospel) 5.3 he reads the figure as the type of a priesthood defined by everything the Aaronic order was not: Melchizedek “had no characteristics shared by the Aaronic priesthood… he was not chosen by men, he was not anointed with prepared oil, he was not of the tribe… and strangest of all, he was not even circumcised in his flesh, and yet he blesses Abraham, as if he were far better than he.” A real priest — not an angel — but one standing outside Israel, before the Law and circumcision, he prefigures the priesthood of Christ, which is likewise not by descent and reaches the nations. His bread and wine Eusebius already draws forward to the Church’s “spiritual sacrifice” offered “among all nations… with wine and bread darkly express[ing] the mysteries of His Body and saving Blood.” This is the human, priest-of-the-Gentiles pole that Jerome would later cite by name for the “Canaanite man” consensus.16

“A holy man, not an angel”: Ambrose of Milan (De fide III; On the Sacraments, c. AD 380)

Ambrose of Milan (c. 339–397) makes explicit the identity that Cyprian left implicit. Against the reading — traced on this timeline from 11Q13 and reported from Origen by Jerome — that Melchizedek was “of a more divine nature,” Ambrose insists the Church holds him to be “not… an angel (as some Jewish triflers do), but a holy man and priest of God, who, prefiguring our Lord, is described as ‘without father or mother…’” (Exposition of the Christian Faith III.88). The silences of Hebrews 7:3 make him a type, not a divine being: he is “received as a priest of God made upon the model of Christ… the one we regard as the type, the other as the original.” In his baptismal catechesis he presses the bread and wine into full Eucharistic figure, calling Melchizedek “the author of the sacraments.”17

“From its not being expressed”: John Chrysostom (Homilies on Genesis 35; on Hebrews 12, c. AD 388–403)

The fullest patristic exposition comes from John Chrysostom (c. 349–407). Preaching through Hebrews 7 in Homily 12, he settles the crux of Hebrews 7:3: Melchizedek is called “without father, without mother… having neither beginning of days nor end of life” not because he literally lacked parents or was deathless — “he both died and had parents” — but “from its not being expressed” in Scripture. The record’s silence is what makes him a type: we know Melchizedek’s beginning and end “not… because they have not been written; so we know them not in the case of Jesus, not because they have not been written, but because they do not exist. For that indeed is a type… but this is the reality.” The discipline — grounding the type in what the text omits, not in an allegory imposed on it — excludes at a stroke both the over-literal reading (a deathless Melchizedek) and the exalted ones (an angel or divine being): in his Homilies on Genesis he fixes the human footing first, a priest “possibly self-appointed… like Noah, like Abel, like Abraham,” who “in a particular manner… was to prove a type of Christ.”18

The survey of the opinions: Jerome (Letter 73, Ad Evangelum, c. AD 398)

Jerome (c. 347–420) gives the tradition its first scholarly doxography. Asked to judge an anonymous tract arguing that Melchizedek was the Holy Spirit or “of a more divine nature,” he searches the earlier writers and reports two camps: Origen and Didymus made him an angel, while “Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, and Emisenus, Apollinaris also, and… Eustathius” all held “that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem, which was first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem.” Jerome sides with the second — a real, human, Canaanite priest-king, as in Josephus — and rejects the divinizing readings. He also relays, without adopting it as his own verdict, “the opinion of the Hebrews” that Melchizedek was Shem son of Noah, with its chronological reckoning — the material weighed on Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? and set out on the rabbinic-tradition page.19

The first appearance of the Christian sacrifice: Augustine (City of God 16.22, c. AD 419)

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) closes the Latin run by putting the whole weight of the figure on the offering and the order. In The City of God 16.22 Melchizedek’s bread and wine are the moment when “first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world”; of Melchizedek “many and great things are written” in Hebrews (which, he notes, “most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this”), and the oath “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” proves the Aaronic order was to be “taken away.” At 17.17 he makes the present state of things the proof: “there is nowhere a priesthood and sacrifice after the order of Aaron, and everywhere men offer under Christ as the Priest, which Melchizedek showed.” Augustine assumes the human Melchizedek of the Latin consensus and leaves the identity question settled.20

“The image has not all the properties of the archetype”: Theodoret of Cyrus (Eranistes; on Hebrews 7, c. AD 447)

The last of the Fathers on this timeline, Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 393–458/466), states the orthodox reading with dialectical precision. In the Eranistes he presses the logic of image and archetype: Melchizedek is called “without father, without mother” only “according to the dispensation of the divine Scripture” — because the record omits his lineage — for “if he had really been without father and without mother, he would not have been an image, but a reality.” What is said of Melchizedek by Scripture’s silence belongs to Christ “by nature and in reality,” and Theodoret is exact about the direction of the likeness: “he likened not Christ the Lord to Melchizedek, but Melchizedek to Christ the Lord.” This same exegesis is aimed at a live heresy — the Melchizedekians, who had made the priest-king a divine Power, some even the Holy Spirit and above Christ: if the figure is fatherless only by the record’s silence, and an image never holds every property of its archetype, he cannot be a divine being. Melchizedek is a man — a Canaanite priest-king, with Josephus and the majority — made a type of Christ.21

Arguments

The genuinely contested questions about Melchizedek — above all what kind of being was he? — are weighed on their own pages rather than settled on the timeline.

Related arguments — the identity of Melchizedek

“What was Melchizedek?” is the single most-debated question the figure raises, and the tradition answered it in five ways — ranged here from the most human to the most divine. Each is an independently advocated reading, steelmanned and rated on its own page; the mere defeaters (the agenealogētos of Hebrews 7:3, the “made like” of the same verse) are folded into whichever reading they bear on. The first is the baseline the other four depart from.

Was Melchizedek a Canaanite priest-king of El Elyon? (assessment: sound)

Was Melchizedek a Canaanite priest-king of El Elyon? — the “plain historical” reading, and the strongest: Genesis presents him as a real, human priest-king of (Jeru)Salem who served El Elyon, “God Most High” — the reading of Josephus, the patristic majority, and the modern scholarly mainstream alike (Speiser, Sarna, Wenham, von Rad, Westermann, Cross on the Canaanite El background, Emerton on Salem = Jerusalem). What is genuinely contested is a further step — the historicity of the four-kings campaign and the date the passage entered Genesis — not the identification of the figure, on which otherwise-opposed camps converge. The baseline from which every other reading is a departure.22

Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? (assessment: weak)

Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? — the dominant rabbinic identification. Chronologically possible only on the shorter Masoretic lifespans (and impossible on the Septuagint long chronology this wiki rates arguably stronger), resting on no datum of the Genesis text, and colliding with the one New Testament passage that theologizes the figure — the “without father, without mother, without descent” of Hebrews 7:3, since Shem is the most fully-pedigreed man of the primeval line. Not unsupported (it has a coherent motive and the weight of Jewish tradition), but weak as a historical identification.23

Was Melchizedek an angel or heavenly being? (assessment: weak)

Was Melchizedek an angel or heavenly being? — the oldest strand, with the deepest Second-Temple roots (the heavenly elohim-judge of 11QMelchizedek; the marvelous birth of 2 Enoch; Origen and Didymus in the church). A careful distinction carries the verdict: the tradition of a heavenly Melchizedek is real and is genuine background for Hebrews, but the identification of the Genesis-14 priest-king as an angel is weak — the Qumran figure never cites Genesis 14 or Psalm 110 and is a parallel development (Fitzmyer’s own point), Hebrews calls him “this man” and only “made like” the Son, the modern revivers argue for a background tradition rather than the identification, and the Fathers rejected it.24

Was Melchizedek the Holy Spirit? (assessment: weak)

Was Melchizedek the Holy Spirit? — an ancient minority identification, the peculiar teaching of Hieracas of Leontopolis (reported by Epiphanius) and of the anonymous tract Jerome was asked to refute. It presses the more-than-human ring of Hebrews 7:3, but that verse likens Melchizedek to the Son, not the Spirit; a priest who offers bread and wine and receives tithes cannot coherently be the Spirit of God; and it was rejected by every ancient authority that noticed it. The most fragile of the identity readings.25

Was Melchizedek a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ? (assessment: weak)

Was Melchizedek a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ (a Christophany)? — popular in modern conservative circles (Henry Morris and a wide stream). It fastens on a real feature — Hebrews does assimilate Melchizedek toward the eternal — but over-reads likeness into identity: Hebrews says he was “made like unto the Son of God,” calls him “this man,” and makes Christ a priest “after the order of Melchizedek” — which on the identity reading would make Christ a priest after his own order. Weak, on a text-internal ground the whole patristic tradition already pressed.26

Was Melchizedek a heavenly power greater than Christ? (assessment: weak)

Was Melchizedek a heavenly power greater than Christ? — the defining doctrine of the ancient Melchizedekians (the sect of Theodotus the banker), who made the priest-king a heavenly Power, a mediator for the angels, ranked above Christ. Kept off the identity set above because it is not another answer to “what kind of being was he?” so much as a christological ranking — and a heresy the church defined itself against. It presses the more-than-human ring of Hebrews 7:3 and the priority implied by “after the order of Melchizedek,” but it inverts the Epistle it quotes: Hebrews makes Melchizedek “made like unto the Son of God” (the copy, not the archetype) and argues throughout for Christ’s supremacy, the superiority in Hebrews 7:7 is to Levi and not to Christ, and the heavenly ontology rests only on the record’s silences read as predicates. Rejected by every ancient authority that noticed it.27

Is the bread and wine of Genesis 14 a type of the Eucharist? (assessment: contested)

Is the bread and wine of Genesis 14 a type of the Eucharist? — the reading, from Cyprian onward and near-universal in the Fathers, that the bread and wine Melchizedek “brought forth” (Genesis 14:18) are the first shadow of the Eucharist — and, because he did so as priest, a type of the Church’s sacrifice, a load-bearing proof-text for the Mass (enshrined in the Roman Canon and defined at Trent). As typology it is coherent and well-grounded — bread and wine are the very elements Christ used, and the Melchizedek→Christ priesthood typology is the New Testament’s own (Psalm 110:4 in Hebrews 7). But the stronger sacrificial reading strains the text: the Hebrew hôṣî’ means “brought out,” not “offered” (the plainest sense is refreshment for Abram’s men — Calvin’s objection), and Hebrews, which mines every other detail of the scene, conspicuously omits the bread and wine. Strong by the lights of patristic and liturgical tradition, weak by those of grammatical-historical exegesis: a genuinely contested reading, cross-linked to this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.28

Does Hebrews 7:3 claim Melchizedek had no parents, birth, or death? (assessment: weak)

Does Hebrews 7:3 claim Melchizedek literally had no parents, birth, or death? — the hermeneutical question beneath the divine-being identifications. On the reading rated here, “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but… abideth a priest continually” (Hebrews 7:3) states literal facts about Melchizedek — the warrant the ancient Melchizedekians, Hieracas, and modern Christophany writers all drew on, and the reading Attridge leans toward for Hebrews itself. It reads a real feature (the deliberately large language, the present-tense “abideth”), but the whole force of the passage is the strange silence of a book that elsewhere records every genealogy and death: “without descent” renders agenealogētos, a word about the record, and “made like” makes Melchizedek the copy, not the original. The Fathers who expounded the verse are unanimous — it is called this “from its not being expressed” (Chrysostom; “if he had really been without father… he would not have been an image, but a reality,” Theodoret) — and the modern critical majority (Bruce, Lane, O’Brien, Koester) reads it as a typological argument from silence. The literal-predicate reading is weak; the argument-from-silence reading is sound.29
