Who Was Melchizedek?

No figure of the Hebrew Bible is at once so slight and so weighty as Melchizedek. He appears in a single scene of three verses — a king of Salem and “priest of the most high God” who meets Abram returning from battle, brings out bread and wine, blesses him, and receives a tithe of the spoils (Genesis 14:18–20) — and then vanishes from the narrative entirely, without genealogy, birth, or death. Yet on that silence a whole theology was built. A royal psalm swears Israel’s king into an eternal priesthood “after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), and the Epistle to the Hebrews makes that order the hinge of its argument that Christ’s priesthood surpasses Aaron’s — reading the very gaps in the Genesis record (“without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life”) as a portrait “made like unto the Son of God” (Hebrews 7:3). Who, then, was he? A Canaanite priest-king of Jebusite Salem? An angel or heavenly being? The Holy Spirit? A pre-incarnate appearance of Christ himself? The patriarch Shem living on under another name, as the rabbis held? Or a real man whose very obscurity was, in the Epistle’s hands, made to preach? And what of the bread and wine he carried out — the gesture the Fathers read as the first shadow of the Eucharist?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Melchizedek — the sources for that are too thin — but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis scene and the oath of Psalm 110, the Epistle to the Hebrews, the heavenly redeemer-judge of the Qumran scroll 11QMelchizedek, Philo’s Logos-priest, Josephus’s founder of Jerusalem, the marvelous-birth legend of 2 Enoch, the rabbinic identification with Shem, and the long line of the Fathers — down to the modern scholarship that weighs it all. The genuinely contested questions — what kind of being was he? is the bread and wine a Eucharistic type? who were the Melchizedekians who made him a power above Christ? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and the Epistle to the Hebrews)

The whole later tradition grows from one brief encounter and two texts that reach back to it. In Genesis 14:18–20, as Abram returns from rescuing Lot and defeating the eastern kings, “Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God” (El Elyon); he blesses Abram in the name of “the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth,” and Abram “gave him tithes of all.” The scene ends as abruptly as it began: no lineage, no birth, no death, nothing before and nothing after. Centuries later a royal enthronement psalm swears Israel’s king into that same priest-king office — “The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4) — a priesthood defined not by descent from Aaron but by a divine oath.1 The Epistle to the Hebrews then makes this the pivot of its whole argument. Quoting Psalm 110:4 repeatedly (Hebrews 5:6, 10; 6:20), it turns the silences of the Genesis record into positive testimony: Melchizedek is “first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace; without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually” (Hebrews 7:1–3). Because Abraham — and so the unborn Levi “in the loins of his father” — paid tithes to Melchizedek and received his blessing, the argument runs, this priesthood is greater than the Levitical, and Christ, a priest “after the order of Melchizedek” rather than of Aaron, holds an eternal and unchanging office (Hebrews 7:4–28). It is this reading — the obscure priest-king whose very lack of a recorded genealogy prefigures the Son of God — that every later witness on this timeline inherits and reworks.2

The heavenly redeemer-judge of Qumran (11QMelchizedek, c. 100 BC)

The earliest interpretation of Melchizedek outside the Bible does not read him as a mortal priest-king at all. In a fragmentary eschatological midrash from Qumran Cave 11 — 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), composed around 100 BC — Melchizedek is a heavenly, quasi-divine deliverer: an elohim who presides over the final Day of Atonement and the tenth Jubilee, proclaims “liberty” to the captives (fusing the release of Leviticus 25 with the “liberty to the captives” of Isaiah 61:1), and executes God’s judgment on Belial and the spirits of his lot.3 Most strikingly, the scroll quotes Psalm 82:1 — “Elohim will [st]and in the assem[bly of God,] in the midst of the gods he judges” — and reads the presiding elohim not as YHWH but, on the majority reading of its editors, as Melchizedek himself, seated among the divine beings as God’s eschatological judge (a line-level identification that is genuinely contested). A separate anointed herald, tied to the “anointed prince” of Daniel 9:25, announces his day of salvation. Notably the text never cites Genesis 14 or Psalm 110:4 — so this exalted Melchizedek is a parallel development, not one drawn from the priest-king scene; it is the wellspring of the later “Melchizedek = angel / heavenly being” reading. This witness also anchors the divine-council reading of Psalm 82 developed elsewhere on this wiki.4

The Logos-priest of Philo (Legum Allegoriae III.79–82, early first century AD)

The first interpreter to read the Genesis scene itself allegorically is the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – AD 50). In his Allegorical Interpretation III.79–82 he parses the names exactly as Hebrews 7:2 later will — “Melchizedek” as “righteous king,” “Salem” as “peace” — and turns the priest-king into a figure of the mind’s ruling faculty: the “kingly mind” that governs by persuasion, “directed in its course by a good governor and pilot; and this good pilot is right reason” (orthos logos), over against the “tyrannical mind” that rules by force. The bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 become the “food full of cheerfulness and joy” that reason supplies to the soul — a “divine intoxication, more sober than sobriety itself” — not a Eucharistic type (that is a distinct, later Christian reading, weighed on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines).5 The pivotal line is his gloss on “priest of the most high God”: “reason is a priest, having, as its inheritance the true God.” On the standard (though contested) reading this identifies Melchizedek with the divine Logos — God’s Word — making Philo the bridge from the obscure priest-king to the Logos-christology the Fathers would draw out of Hebrews. Philo and Hebrews independently attest the same first-century etymology, but part ways on its point: for Philo, Melchizedek is an allegory of reason within the soul; for Hebrews, a type “made like unto the Son of God.”6

The Canaanite founder-priest of Josephus (Antiquities I.180; Jewish War VI.438, c. AD 75–94)

The historian Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) gives the tradition’s opposite pole: not a heavenly elohim and not an allegory of the soul, but a real man. Retelling Genesis 14 in Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2, he glosses the name as the others do — “Melchisedec, king of the city Salem… that name signifies, the righteous king” — but draws a plain historical judgment: “such he was, without dispute, insomuch that, on this account he was made the priest of God: however, they afterward called Salem Jerusalem.” Melchizedek feasts Abram’s returning army “in an hospitable manner,” blesses him, and accepts “the tenth part of his prey” — the bread and wine reduced to abundant provisions, with nothing sacramental in view.7 In the Jewish War VI.10.1, pausing at Jerusalem’s fall to reflect on its history, he names its founder as this same figure: “he who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King… on which account he was the first priest of God, and first built a temple, and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.” Josephus is thus the chief early source for two moves the later tradition inherited — the equation Salem = Jerusalem and the reading of Melchizedek as a historical Canaanite (Jebusite) priest-king — the exact opposite of the exalted heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13.8
