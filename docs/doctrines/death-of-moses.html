The Death and Assumption of Moses

Deuteronomy records that Moses climbed Nebo, saw the land he would not enter, and “died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD”; it adds that “he buried him in a valley… but no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day” (Deuteronomy 34:5–6). Later writers commented on the solitary death and the unknown grave. The New Testament letter of Jude refers to a scene the Torah does not narrate: “Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses” (Jude 9). Clement of Alexandria and Origen connected the verse with a lost Jewish book on the “assumption” or “ascension” of Moses; the same letter quotes, five verses later, a prophecy found in 1 Enoch. A book titled for his “assumption,” a cloud in Josephus, and his appearance at the Transfiguration have raised the question, asked also of Enoch and Elijah, whether Moses died or was taken up.

This page is a tradition-timeline: not a life of Moses, but a record of what was said and believed about the manner of his passing, set out earliest extant source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on it: the Torah’s death-notice and Jude’s dispute; the lost book linked to Jude 9; Josephus; the Fathers who named the book, who wrote on the death and the hidden grave, and who retold the dispute over the body; and the modern scholars who edited the surviving text and reconstructed its lost ending. What source lies behind Jude 9, and whether Jude’s use of it bears on the canon, are set out under Disputed readings; whether Moses died or was taken up is answered differently by the sources below.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Deuteronomy 34, Jude 9, and the Transfiguration)

The later sources comment on a death-notice and on a New Testament verse. Deuteronomy records the death three times over: “So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD. And he buried him in a valley in the land of Moab… but no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day. And Moses was an hundred and twenty years old when he died: his eye was not dim, nor his natural force abated” (Deuteronomy 34:5–7). The verse does not name the subject of “he buried him,” and it says that no one knows the place of the grave.1 The New Testament adds a scene the Torah does not tell: “Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee” (Jude 9). Jude uses the scene as an example of restraint in speech; Michael’s words echo the rebuke of Satan in Zechariah 3:2. The Gospels record that at the Transfiguration “there appeared unto them Moses and Elias talking with him” (Matthew 17:3). A recorded death and burial, a dispute over the body, and an appearance on the mountain are the passages the sources below take up.2

The Assumption (or Testament) of Moses: the lost book linked to Jude 9 (early 1st c. AD)

The book that Clement, Origen and modern editors connect with Jude 9 is a Jewish pseudepigraphon, dated by Charles to the early first century AD, cast as Moses’ farewell testament to Joshua. It survives in a single mutilated sixth-century Latin palimpsest, discovered and published by Antonio Ceriani in 1861, which breaks off before the end: it contains no death, no burial, and no Michael-and-devil dispute. Bauckham places the scene Jude alludes to in the lost conclusion, reconstructed from patristic quotations.3 The surviving text is cast as a deathbed speech and contains no taking-up, and the ancient book-lists carry both titles. Bauckham’s reconstruction of the ending tells of Moses’ death and burial by the archangel, with Satan slandering him as a murderer (he had killed the Egyptian) to deny him the grave, and Michael answering only “The Lord rebuke thee”; Ryan Stokes (2017) reconstructs the dispute instead as concerning Moses’ bodily ascent (see the modern scholarship entry below). The same letter of Jude quotes, at Jude 14–15, a prophecy found in the Book of Enoch.

Josephus: a cloud, a disappearance, and Moses’ written record of his death (c. AD 93)

Josephus’ Antiquities narrates that as Moses took leave of Eleazar and Joshua, “a cloud stood over him on the sudden, and he disappeared in a certain valley”; in the same sentence Josephus adds that Moses “wrote in the holy books that he died, which was done out of fear, lest they should venture to say that, because of his extraordinary virtue, he went to God.” The Loeb edition’s note compares the scene with the Greco-Roman accounts of figures who vanished in a cloud (Romulus, Aeneas). On Josephus’ telling, Moses recorded his own death to forestall the claim that he had gone to God.4

Clement of Alexandria: Jude 9 “confirms the assumption of Moses” (c. AD 200)

In his brief notes on the epistle, Clement of Alexandria comments on “Michael, the archangel, disputing with the devil… about the body of Moses”: “Here he confirms the assumption of Moses.” Charles prints the remark among the patristic witnesses to the lost book. Clement also comments on the Enoch prophecy of Jude 14–15.5

Origen names the book — The Ascension of Moses — and reads the “two Moseses” (c. AD 230)

Explaining how the powers of evil work, Origen cites “the work entitled The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle),” in which “the archangel Michael, when disputing with the devil regarding the body of Moses,” traces the Fall to the devil-inspired serpent. In his Homilies on Joshua, preaching on “Moses my servant is dead” (Joshua 1:2), he draws on a “little book” which, he says, “is not held in the canon,” for its image of “two Moseses… one alive in the spirit, and the other dead in the body,” read as the letter and the spirit of the Law. Charles and Bauckham identify the little book with the same work.6

The Fathers on the death and the hidden grave (c. 340–440)

Fourth- and fifth-century Fathers commenting on Deuteronomy 34 read the hidden sepulchre in several ways. Aphrahat reads the secret grave as a mercy: lest enemies desecrate the bones, and lest a people who “accounted” Moses “as God” make his tomb a place of worship. Gregory of Nyssa calls the death “more sublime than his life,” a passing that left “neither trace nor memorial”; Paulinus of Nola makes the secret burial a singular honour, with God “alone as witness of his grave”; and Peter Chrysologus writes that “the angels were present at the death of Moses, and God himself took care of his burial.” Ambrose writes that Moses “died by the word of God,” and reads “No man hath known of his sepulture” to mean “that he was taken up into heaven rather than buried,” one “translated rather than left here.”7

The Fathers retell the dispute: Michael, the devil, and the charge of murder (c. 386–1090)

The Greek and Latin commentators on Jude read the scene in two registers. The moral reading takes Michael’s restraint as the point: the archangel “did not dare to bring a judgment of blasphemy” but left judgment to God, and so must Christians (Jerome, Rufinus; Rufinus sets “May the Lord rebuke you” beside Zechariah 3:2). The narrative reading tells the quarrel: “Michael the archangel provided ministry in burying Moses: the Devil could not bear this but brought an accusation because of the killing of the Egyptian… and therefore did not allow him to obtain an honorable burial” (Oecumenius); Theophylact says that this is told “in the apocryphal writings,” and Andreas of Caesarea reads the quarrel over the corpse as a figure of the contest over every departing soul. Bede writes that “we have no certainty” where Jude found the scene, offers the Zechariah parallel, and reports those who take “the body of Moses” to mean the people of Israel. Oecumenius, Theophylact and Andreas each speak of Michael’s part in Moses’ burial.8

Modern scholarship edits the text and reconstructs the lost ending (1861–2017)

Ceriani’s 1861 publication of the sole manuscript recovered the surviving portion of the book, incomplete and without the scene Jude refers to. R. H. Charles (1897) gathered the patristic fragments of the lost ending and dated the work to about AD 7–30. Richard Bauckham (1983) reconstructed the ending (Michael sent to bury Moses, Satan slandering him as a murderer, the “Lord rebuke thee” from Zechariah 3, the burial completed “so that no one saw” it) and separated this murder-and-burial tradition, which he identifies as Jude’s source, from a later, anti-Gnostic “master of matter” version. Johannes Tromp’s critical edition (1993), following Ceriani, keeps the title Assumption of Moses and identifies the manuscript with it. Ryan Stokes (2017) reconstructs the dispute as a quarrel over Moses’ bodily ascent rather than his burial.9

Disputed readings

How the source of Jude 9, and Jude’s use of it, have been read.

  • What source lies behind Jude 9, and does Jude’s use of it bear on the canon? — read as the lost ending of the Assumption or Testament of Moses (Clement, Origen, Charles, Bauckham), as a true tradition cited without making its book Scripture (Origen, Bede, Bauckham), as a ground for doubting the letter (opponents reported by Didymus and Bede), as of uncertain source and read through Zechariah 3 (Bede), and as a dispute over Moses’ ascent (Stokes).10
