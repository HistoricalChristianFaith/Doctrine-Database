Are all sins equal? (the gravity of sins)

Cicero reports the Stoic thesis that all wrongdoing is equal — that a man who needlessly kills a cock commits no lesser crime than one who strangles his father. The Christian writers surveyed here who take up the thesis reject it, and they read it alongside a verse that Augustine, Bede, Theophylact and Bonaventure expound: “whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, he is guilty of all” (James 2:10). Two texts carry most of the discussion. Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Cyprian and Bede read 1 John 5:16–17, with its “sin unto death” and “sin not unto death,” as teaching degrees of sin; Augustine’s Letter 167, Bede, Theophylact and Bonaventure expound James 2:10 in ways that do not make sins equal. Jerome’s Against Jovinianus (393) and Aquinas’s article on whether all sins are equal address the parity thesis directly. The timeline runs from Cicero and 4 Maccabees to Aquinas, earliest extant attestation first.

Several of the witnesses write in disputes over penance and reconciliation: Tertullian against a bishop’s edict reconciling the sexually lapsed, Cyprian to a bishop drawn toward Novatian’s party on the reception of the lapsed, and Ambrose against the Novatianists. Jerome writes against Jovinian’s teaching on merit and reward. Cyprian, Augustine and Gregory the Great are also witnesses on the purgatory timeline.

Timeline

Cicero (c. 46 BC)

Cicero reports the Stoic thesis omnia peccata paria twice. In the speech Pro Murena he lists among the maxims of Zeno’s followers “that all offenses are equal; that every sin is an unpardonable crime; and that he does not commit a less crime who kills a cock if there was no need to do so, than the man who strangles his father.”1 In the Paradoxa Stoicorum he sets out the thesis under the heading “that all misdeeds are in themselves equal,” arguing that “crimes are not to be measured by the issue of events, but from the bad intentions of men.”2 The cock-and-father pair recurs in Ambrose (“a common fowl”) and, with a chicken, in Bede.

4 Maccabees (c. AD 40)

4 Maccabees, a Greek Jewish martyrdom-homily, gives the principle to the priest Eleazar, refusing to eat pork: “to transgress the law in matters either small or great is of equal seriousness, for in either case the law is equally despised.”3 The ground Eleazar gives is the contempt shown to the law.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement is the earliest Christian witness surveyed here to say that Scripture teaches degrees of sin: “John, too, manifestly teaches the differences of sins, in his larger Epistle,” quoting the sin unto death and the sin not unto death of 1 John 5:16–17.4 He argues from the two classes and their different remedies, without naming the Stoics.

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian, in On Modesty, divides sins by the same text into two classes: the sin not unto death “will be remissible,” the sin unto death “will be irremissible.”5 Writing against a bishop’s edict of reconciliation, he holds that since John withholds even prayer from the second class, the church may withhold peace, and he cites the prohibition laid on Jeremiah’s intercession.6

Cyprian (252)

Cyprian is the earliest Christian writer surveyed here to name the parity thesis as a philosophers’ doctrine. Writing to Antonianus, a bishop leaning toward Novatian’s party, about how to receive the lapsed: “The principle of the philosophers and stoics is different, dearest brother, who say that all sins are equal … But there is a wide difference between Christians and philosophers” — and he applies to it the “philosophy and vain deceit” of Colossians 2:8.7 His argument is a physician’s: the lapsed are wounded, to be reckoned “lying half alive,” not dead.8

Ambrose of Milan (c. 384)

Ambrose charges the Novatianists with reasoning “after the fashion of the Stoics,” holding that “he who has stolen a common fowl, as they say, no less than he who has smothered his father, should be for ever excluded from the divine mysteries,” and asks how, on that principle, they can single out those guilty of one offence for perpetual exclusion.9 The fowl and the smothered father are the pair in Cicero’s Pro Murena.

Jerome (393)

In Book II of Against Jovinianus (II.18–34), Jerome answers with scriptural arguments for degrees of merit, guilt and reward, from the thirtyfold and hundredfold of the sower to Christ’s word that another had “the greater sin” than Pilate.10 He charges that Jovinian “in the distribution of rewards and punishments … all at once becomes a Stoic. He exchanges Jerusalem for Citium, Judaea for Cyprus, Christ for Zeno.”11 The fourth of the four propositions Jerome lists at the start of the work is “that there is one reward in the kingdom of heaven for all who have kept their baptismal vow”;12 whether Jovinian also taught the parity of sins is disputed (see Disputed readings). In 416, asked by Augustine to expound James 2:10, Jerome praised Augustine’s letters and did not answer them: “a most trying time has come upon us, in which I have found it better for me to hold my peace than to speak.”13

Augustine of Hippo (415)

Augustine wrote a treatise-length letter to Jerome on James 2:10 alone. He calls the equality of sins a tenet “the Stoics alone dared to maintain in opposition to the unanimous sentiments of mankind”14 and argues that, granting for the sake of argument that the virtues are inseparable, “it by no means follows that among bad actions one cannot be worse than another.”15 Against the Stoic image of a man equally drowned an inch or a mile below the surface he sets a man walking out of a cave into gradually increasing light, so that “no one lives here wholly without sin — one sins more, another less, and the best is the man who sins least.”16 On the verse, one becomes guilty of all “because he does what is contrary to the love on which hangs the whole law,” while “the transgression of the law of love is greater in him who commits a more grievous sin.”17 In his homilies on 1 John he reads the sin unto death as hatred of the brotherhood, adding in the Retractations that no one is to be despaired of while he lives.18

Gregory the Dialogist (c. 591)

Gregory, expounding Job 29:14, describes righteousness as a garment: “he that is just in some deeds and unjust in others, it is as if he covered over this side, and exposed that one naked; nor are those henceforth good deeds, which are defiled by other evil deeds springing up.”19

Bede (c. 716)

Bede treats both texts in his Commentary on the Catholic Epistles. On James 2:10 he gives Augustine’s solution and Augustine’s answer to the parity objection: guilty of all “because the fullness of the law is love … upon which commandments of love the whole law and the prophets depend,” yet sins are unequal because “he acts more against charity who sins more gravely, and less who sins lightly.”20 On 1 John 5:17 he calls the Stoic parity an “inept argument … against all human sense” and distinguishes small sins “without which this life cannot be lived” from sins which “unless they are corrected, lead their perpetrator to eternal punishment.”21 On 5:16 he assigns light sins to confession to a brother and graver ones to the elders of the church.22

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1100)

Theophylact reads James 2:10 by love: “whoever sins in one thing becomes guilty of all, because he does not have perfect love, for love is the head of all that is good, and when the head is absent, the entire remaining body amounts to nothing.” If it were otherwise, he writes, “no one among men would survive, because no one keeps all the commandments.”23 He explains James’s two examples by the same principle: “whoever loves his neighbor will neither commit adultery nor murder.”24 Substantially the same exposition stands in the commentary circulating under the name of Oecumenius.25

Bonaventure (c. 1267)

Bonaventure answers James 2:10 by distinguishing two kinds of punishment: the man who offends in one point is “not … punished with as much punishment of sense as for the transgression of all; but he will be punished with as much punishment of loss, yet not as severely for one as for all.”26 On this distinction “guilty of all” holds of the pain of loss and not of the pain of sense.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Aquinas gives the question a formal article and names the thesis’s source: “The opinion of the Stoics, which Cicero adopts in the book on Paradoxes (Paradox. iii), was that all sins are equal: from which opinion arose the error of certain heretics, who not only hold all sins to be equal, but also maintain that all the pains of hell are equal.”27 He holds that the Stoics treated sin as a pure privation, like death or darkness, which admits of no more or less, whereas sin is like sickness, which “retains something of the opposite habit” — a fault that destroyed the order of reason entirely would destroy the act along with it.28 Gravity, he holds, rises with the object, so that murder is graver than theft and sins directly against God graver still.29 He compares venial and mortal sin in a later question of the Summa.30
