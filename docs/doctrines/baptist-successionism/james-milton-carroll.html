James Milton Carroll on Baptist Successionism

Dates: c. 1931 (1852–1931) · Wikipedia
Summary: James Milton Carroll, a Texas Southern Baptist minister, wrote the booklet most associated with Baptist successionism. The Trail of Blood — five lectures gathered into a tract, completed before Carroll's death in January 1931 and published posthumously that year — charts an unbroken line of "true" churches running, on Carroll's account, from Christ and the apostles, through a chain of persecuted medieval dissenting groups, to the modern Baptists, outside both Rome and the Reformation. With its fold-out color chart it is the work the popular phrase "the Trail of Blood" names; Rodney A. Harrison (2007) counts 2,280,000 copies in sixty-six editions by 2002.1

Claims & evidence

Not "apostolic succession" but an unbroken succession of churches from Christ

Carroll distinguishes his thesis from apostolic succession: the apostolic office ended with the apostles, but the apostolic church, he holds, has existed continuously ever since.

Baptists do not believe in Apostolic Succession. The Apostolic office ceased with the death of the Apostles. It is to His churches that He promised a continual existence from the time He organized the first one during His earthly ministry until He comes again. He promised—

"I will build my church and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it." (Matt. 16:18)

— The Trail of Blood (1931), Lecture I, subtitled "Following the Christians Down Through the Centuries or The History of Baptist Churches From the Time of Christ, Their Founder, to the Present Day," full text at archive.org (scan and catalog page at archive.org/details/TheTrailOfBlood).2

The chain of dissenting groups carrying the "true church"

The accompanying chart lines up the persecuted groups Carroll identifies as Baptists-under-other-names — nicknames, he says, given them by their enemies, the line beginning with "Christians" (Acts 11:26) and running underground through the "dark ages":

All the other names in that column were given in the same manner— Montanists, Novationists, Donatists, Paulicians, Albigenses, Waldenses, etc., and Ana-Baptists. All of these will again and again be referred to as the lectures progress.

Elsewhere he adds Paterines, Cathari, Petro-Brussians, Arnoldists, and Henricians to the roll.3 The naming, on his account, settles only late: the "Ana-Baptist" label was applied to these earlier groups and then, "Very early in the sixteenth century, even prior to the origin of the Lutheran Church, the first of all the Protestant Churches," shortened to "Baptists" — so that Baptists, having never been in Rome, are on this reading neither Catholic nor Protestant.4

Reception and debate

  • Carroll's booklet stands after nineteenth-century Landmarkism (J. R. Graves) and Charles Spurgeon's 1861 pulpit statement of the same idea; it supplies a chart and a named lineage. The successionist writer R. L. Vaughn calls it “neither adademic [sic] nor comprehensive,” “a book for the Baptist people,” and holds that “even if Carroll may have misidentified some, he got others right.”5
  • James Edward McGoldrick (1994), confining his study largely to the sects Carroll cites, examines them one by one: he finds Montanism “much more akin to Pentecostalism than to any historic expression of the Baptist faith” and the Novatians thinking of themselves as “purely Catholic,” and, as Douglas Douma summarizes him, the Paulicians adoptionist, the Bogomils dualist, the Cathars neo-Manichaean and the early Waldensians essentially Catholic. Rodney A. Harrison, who studied Carroll's manuscripts and correspondence in the archives of the A. Webb Roberts Library, Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, reports that the references Carroll added to his second manuscript “were often misleading,” giving as an example a reference to John T. Christian's History of the Baptists, vol. 1, p. 31, and that quotations attributed to Stanislaus Hosius and Isaac Newton in the introduction added to the second and later editions “cannot be substantiated.”1,6 Whether the medieval Waldenses rejected infant baptism is set out at Did the medieval Waldenses reject infant baptism?, and the readings of the wider question at Where did the Baptist churches come from: an unbroken succession of churches from Christ, a beginning among English Separatists, or kinship with earlier dissenters and the Anabaptists?
  • Publication note: the work appeared posthumously in 1931 with an introduction by Clarence Walker (pastor, Ashland Avenue Baptist Church, Lexington, KY). Vaughn, citing Harrison, reports that letters show Carroll had sought to have the Baptist Sunday School Committee print it several years earlier, and that J. W. Porter brought it out after his death.5 The author's name is James Milton Carroll.
