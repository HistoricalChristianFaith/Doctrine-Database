J. R. Graves on Baptist Successionism

Dates: c. 1855–1880 (1820–1893) · Wikipedia
Summary: James Robinson Graves, the Southern Baptist editor of the Tennessee Baptist (Nashville, later Memphis) and a leader of the Landmark movement, set out Baptist successionism as a doctrine. From the Cotton Grove Resolutions of 1851 through his introduction to Orchard's Baptist history (1855) and his mature treatise Old Landmarkism: What Is It? (1880), he taught that Christ's kingdom has stood in "unbroken continuity" since "the days of John the Baptist," carried by a succession of true (Baptist) churches — and, correspondingly, that the Catholic and Protestant bodies are late human societies, not churches of Christ.

Claims & evidence

Christ's kingdom has had "an unbroken continuity until now"

Landmark Baptists very generally believe that for the Word of the Living God to stand, and for the veracity of Jesus Christ to vindicate itself, the kingdom which He set up "in the days of John the Baptist," has had an unbroken continuity until now.

— Old Landmarkism: What Is It? (Memphis, 1880), ch. "The Seventh Mark," p. 121, full text at archive.org (chapter transcription at reformedreader.org). Graves holds that the kingdom "has never yet been 'broken in pieces'… never for a day 'been moved,' nor ceased from the earth," so that to deny its unbroken existence "is to impeach His veracity."

Baptist churches alone have "preserved the doctrine of the gospel in all ages"

To establish the fact in the minds of all, who will give me an impartial hearing, that Baptist churches are the churches of Christ, and that they alone hold, and have alone ever held, and preserved the doctrine of the gospel in all ages since the ascension of Christ.

— Old Landmarkism: What Is It? (1880), Introductory, p. 25, archive.org. Graves states this as the aim of the whole book. Graves draws from it the Landmark position that, because the Catholic and Protestant bodies "were originated and set up by men many ages after the ascension of Christ," they are "not divine — but human institutions," and their "societies are not churches of Christ" that can validly baptize.

"A succession of Baptist churches… from the days of Paul until now"

…yet it can be shown upon the most unquestionable authorities, that there has been a succession of Baptist churches in England and Wales, from the days of Paul until now, and it is an established fact that a majority of the churches planted in America, from the year 1645-1730, were organized by Welsh Baptists, and constituted upon articles of faith, brought over with them from the mother churches.

— Graves's Introductory Essay to G. H. Orchard, A Concise History of the Baptists (American ed., Nashville, 1855), p. xxi, transcription at baptisthistoryhomepage.com. It was Graves who commissioned and prefaced the American edition of Orchard's succession-history, endorsing its claim to prove "a succession of them from the earliest ages down to the present time."

Reception and debate

  • Graves's statements of the succession appeared in print in 1855 (the Orchard introduction) and 1880 (Old Landmarkism). Spurgeon's address followed in 1861 and J. M. Carroll's The Trail of Blood in 1931. The successionist writer R. L. Vaughn notes that the succession view has been held by Baptists who were not Landmarkers, naming Spurgeon and R. B. C. Howell.1
  • James Edward McGoldrick (1994) dates Landmarkism to the nineteenth century, names Graves and J. M. Pendleton as “its two most influential early leaders,” and dates the “first formal statement of Landmarkism” to 1851; he concludes that “surviving primary documents render the successionist view untenable.”2 The successionist R. L. Vaughn, who grants that the trail “is not historically demonstrable at all times,” answers that “As a doctrine, church perpetuity stands or falls on the infallible word of God rather than the fallible record of human historians.”3 The readings of the question are set out at Where did the Baptist churches come from: an unbroken succession of churches from Christ, a beginning among English Separatists, or kinship with earlier dissenters and the Anabaptists?
  • Placement on the timeline: Graves is anchored at c. 1855 (the Great Iron Wheel and the Orchard introduction), with Old Landmarkism (1880) as his later statement. He is the earliest witness on this timeline.
