William Heth Whitsitt on Baptist Successionism

Dates: c. 1896 (1841–1911) · SBTS Archives
Summary: William Heth Whitsitt, professor of church history and third president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, is placed by Philip Bryan (1966) among the historians of the "English Separatist descent" account of Baptist origins. In his 1896 book A Question in Baptist History he argued from the documents that the immersion of adult believers was not practiced in England before 1641: the earlier Anabaptists sprinkled or poured, as their Dutch Mennonite kin did, so believers'-baptism-by-immersion was introduced in the mid-seventeenth century. The claim, which he first published in 1880, led to the "Whitsitt Controversy" and to his resignation of the seminary presidency.

Claims & evidence

Whitsitt's question: adult immersion in England before 1641

Whitsitt frames the dispute as a datable, documentary question: whether believers' baptism by immersion can be traced in England before the 1640s:

The issue before us is far different, namely: Whether the immersion of adult believers was practiced in England by the Anabaptists before the year 1641?

— A Question in Baptist History (Louisville: C. T. Dearing, 1896), full text at archive.org. He notes he had carried the thesis for sixteen years by then:

In September, 1880, I moved up the figures just eight years, announcing, and proving, in the New York Independent for September 2 and 9, that immersion was introduced into England, not in the year 1633, but in the year 1641.

— ibid., archive.org.1

Whitsitt on the mode of baptism among the earlier Anabaptists

Whitsitt concludes that neither the English Anabaptists of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries nor John Smyth's Amsterdam congregation immersed; their mode was the affusion of their Dutch Mennonite neighbours, and immersion was on his account introduced afterwards:

In conclusion, the general result may be stated that few Anabaptists of any country were immersionists, and that none of the Anabaptists of England in the sixteenth and first half of the seventeenth centuries were immersionists.

Another important item is that the baptism of Smyth and his followers was the same as that of the Mennonites, namely, sprinkling or pouring.

— A Question in Baptist History (1896), archive.org. He read the London congregation's own record as conceding the point: by 1640 adult immersion "had become so far unknown" that the Jessey Church Records could state without reservation "that 'none had then so practiced in England to professed believers.'"2

Reception and debate

  • Whitsitt's thesis bears on the succession that Graves, Spurgeon and Carroll assert: the Landmark Baptists held that an unbroken succession of immersions was necessary to the existence of true churches. His positive account, that the Baptists grew out of English Separatism, is the “English Separatist descent theory” in Philip Bryan's 1966 survey of Baptist historiography, which credits him as “the first modern Baptist historian in America to incorporate these findings in a theory of Baptist origins.”3 The readings of the question are set out at Where did the Baptist churches come from: an unbroken succession of churches from Christ, a beginning among English Separatists, or kinship with earlier dissenters and the Anabaptists?
  • His book carried an appendix arguing that Roger Williams's Providence congregation (1639) did not at first baptize by immersion either.
  • The controversy. Whitsitt first published the thesis anonymously, as editorials in the New York Independent (1880), and later over his own name in an encyclopedia article; after his 1895 election to the seminary presidency the Landmark Baptists, for whom an unbroken succession of immersions was the basis of a true church, opposed him. Under threats of schism and withheld gifts and with enrollment falling, Whitsitt tendered his resignation on 13 July 1898, effective in 1899, and finished his career as professor of philosophy at Richmond College (⚠ unverified).4
  • A successionist reply. John T. Christian, then pastor of East Baptist Church, Louisville, answered the book the same year in Did They Dip? (1896), arguing from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century sources that the English Anabaptists and Baptists practised dipping before 1641: “We have enough proof, however, to show that they practiced dipping.” He cites, among others, Thomas Fuller's account of the Anabaptists of 1538 as “Donatists new dipped,” William Turner's Preservative (1551), which “repeatedly calls them Catabaptists,” and Bishop Hall's Apologie against the Brownists (1610), with a passage from Hall quoted by A. R. in 1642, which together he reads as “undoubted contemporaneous evidence in 1610 that the Baptists were immersionists.” T. T. Eaton's introduction sets the 1641 date beside the end of the Court of High Commission and the Star Chamber in that year, after which, he writes, “could Baptists come from their hiding places and preach openly.”5
