William Heth Whitsitt on Baptist Successionism

Dates: c. 1896 (1841–1911) · SBTS Archives
Summary: William Heth Whitsitt, professor of church history and third president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, is the era's leading counter-witness to Baptist successionism — the scholar who set out the "English Separatist" account against which the successionist chain is measured. In his 1896 book A Question in Baptist History he argued from the documents that the immersion of adult believers was not practiced in England before 1641: the earlier Anabaptists sprinkled or poured, as their Dutch Mennonite kin did, so believers'-baptism-by-immersion was recovered in the mid-seventeenth century, not carried down unbroken from the apostles. The claim, which he first published in 1880, cost him the seminary presidency — the "Whitsitt Controversy" — but the account it founded is now the mainstream one.

Claims & evidence

The question is whether adult immersion was practiced in England before 1641

Whitsitt sharpens the dispute to a single datable, documentary question — not whether some "Baptist principle" recurred through the ages, but whether the specific ordinance the successionist chain requires (believers' baptism by immersion) can be traced in England before the 1640s:

The issue before us is far different, namely: Whether the immersion of adult believers was practiced in England by the Anabaptists before the year 1641?

— A Question in Baptist History (Louisville: C. T. Dearing, 1896), full text at archive.org. He notes he had carried the thesis for sixteen years by then:

In September, 1880, I moved up the figures just eight years, announcing, and proving, in the New York Independent for September 2 and 9, that immersion was introduced into England, not in the year 1633, but in the year 1641.

— ibid., archive.org.1

The earlier Anabaptists were not immersionists — they sprinkled or poured

Whitsitt's conclusion is that neither the English Anabaptists of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries nor John Smyth's Amsterdam congregation immersed at all; their mode was the affusion of their Dutch Mennonite neighbours. If so, there is no continuous line of immersing "true churches" to inherit — the practice had to be restored:

In conclusion, the general result may be stated that few Anabaptists of any country were immersionists, and that none of the Anabaptists of England in the sixteenth and first half of the seventeenth centuries were immersionists.

Another important item is that the baptism of Smyth and his followers was the same as that of the Mennonites, namely, sprinkling or pouring.

— A Question in Baptist History (1896), archive.org. He read the London congregation's own record as conceding the point: by 1640 adult immersion "had become so far unknown" that the Jessey Church Records could state without reservation "that 'none had then so practiced in England to professed believers.'"2

Notes

  • Whitsitt is the timeline's counter-witness: where Graves, Spurgeon, and Carroll assert an unbroken succession, Whitsitt supplies the documentary discontinuity that breaks it. His narrower finding — that immersion was reintroduced in 1641 — is fatal to the hard "Trail of Blood" chain, which needs an unbroken line of immersing churches: a recovery is not a succession. His positive account, that the Baptists grew out of English Separatism (Puritan → Separatist → Baptist), is the "English Separatist theory" now standard among historians and weighed here as sound on Did modern Baptists originate in early-seventeenth-century English Separatism?; the succession it displaces is weighed as weak on Is there an unbroken Baptist succession from the apostles?
  • His book carried an appendix arguing that Roger Williams's Providence congregation (1639) did not at first baptize by immersion either — removing one more supposed early immersionist anchor on the American side.
  • The cost of the claim. Whitsitt had first published the thesis anonymously; when it was traced to him after his 1895 election to the seminary presidency, the Landmark Baptists — heirs of Graves, for whom an unbroken succession of immersions was the very basis of a true church — forced a crisis. Under threats of schism and withheld gifts and with enrollment falling, Whitsitt resigned in 1899 and finished his career as professor of philosophy at Richmond College. Later scholarship has largely vindicated him, which is why he stands here not as a fringe skeptic but as the founder of the mainstream account.3
  • A limit worth stating plainly: Whitsitt's own thesis is about the mode and date of English immersion, not a point-by-point refutation of every group in the medieval chain (the Montanists, Paulicians, Waldenses, and the rest). That fuller demolition — showing the enlisted groups held doctrines Baptists reject and that the documentary links do not exist — is the work of later historians such as James Edward McGoldrick, Baptist Successionism (1994). Whitsitt supplies the decisive wedge (no unbroken immersion), not the whole case.
