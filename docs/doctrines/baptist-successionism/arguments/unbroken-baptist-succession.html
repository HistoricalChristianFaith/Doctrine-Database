Where did the Baptist churches come from: an unbroken succession of churches from Christ, a beginning among English Separatists, or kinship with earlier dissenters and the Anabaptists?

Disputed reading · on Baptist Successionism · witnesses: Charles Spurgeon, J. R. Graves, W. H. Whitsitt, J. M. Carroll, John MacArthur

The passage

We believe that the Baptists are the original Christians. We did not commence our existence at the Reformation, we were Reformers before Luther or Calvin were born; we never came from the Church of Rome, for we were never in it. We have an unbroken line up to the Apostles themselves! We have always existed from the very days of Christ, and our principles, sometimes veiled and forgotten like a river which may travel underground for a little season, have always had honest and holy adherents.

— Charles Spurgeon, “Public Meeting of Our London Baptist Brethren” (2 April 1861), The New Park Street Pulpit, vol. VII, ccel.org.1

Although free church groups in ancient and medieval times sometimes promoted doctrines and practices agreeable to modern Baptists, when judged by standards now acknowledged as baptistic, not one of them merits recognition as a Baptist church. Baptists arose in the 17th century in Holland and England. They are Protestants, heirs of the reformers.

— James Edward McGoldrick, Baptist Successionism: A Crucial Question in Baptist History (1994), pp. 1–2.2

The question

Baptist historiography has long sorted the accounts of Baptist origins into three: an unbroken succession of Baptist churches from Christ, a “spiritual kinship” with earlier dissenters and the Continental Anabaptists, and descent from English Separatists of the early seventeenth century (the classification Philip Bryan, 1966, takes from Robert G. Torbet).3 The question is which account describes where the Baptist churches came from; for Landmark Baptists it also bears on what makes a church and a baptism valid, and for every side on how Baptists stand toward Rome and the Reformation. For the succession reading, no academic treatment later than John T. Christian's (1922) was located in this survey; for the Catholic account of Baptist origins, no modern Catholic scholarly treatment was located, beyond the Catholic Encyclopedia (1907) and popular writers.4 Whether the medieval Waldenses, often placed in the succession, rejected infant baptism is set out separately at Did the medieval Waldenses reject infant baptism?

An unbroken succession of Baptist churches from Christ

On this reading Christ founded churches of Baptist faith and order and has kept them in existence, under many names and through persecution, from his own ministry to the present, outside the Roman communion.

Held by: J. R. Graves (Southern Baptist, Landmark; introduction to Orchard, 1855; Old Landmarkism, 1880); Charles Spurgeon (English Baptist, address of 1861); John T. Christian (Baptist; professor of Christian history, Baptist Bible Institute, New Orleans; A History of the Baptists, 1922); J. M. Carroll (Southern Baptist, The Trail of Blood, 1931).5

The reading starts from Christ's promise. Carroll distinguishes it from apostolic succession: “Baptists do not believe in Apostolic Succession”; the promise of “a continual existence” was made to Christ's churches (Matt. 16:18).6 Graves holds that “for the veracity of Jesus Christ to vindicate itself,” the kingdom set up “in the days of John the Baptist” has had “an unbroken continuity until now,” and that Baptist churches “alone hold, and have alone ever held” the doctrine of the gospel since the ascension; in 1855 he wrote that “a succession of Baptist churches in England and Wales, from the days of Paul until now” could be shown “upon the most unquestionable authorities.”7 The churches of the line are traced under the names their opponents gave them: Carroll's chart runs through Montanists, Novatians, Donatists, Paulicians, Albigenses, Waldenses and Anabaptists, and Christian's history devotes chapters to the same families.6 Christian explains the gaps in the record: the Baptists' history was often told “by enemies who did not scruple … to blacken character,” documents were destroyed, and the persecuted were “far more interested in hiding than they were in giving an account of themselves.” He grants that the ancient parties were not in accord with modern Baptists on every doctrine, but finds through all of them “the vital necessity of a regenerated life; a church pure and separate from the ungodly; believers’ baptism; a simple form of church government; the right of free speech and soul liberty; and the permanent and paramount authority of the New Testament.” His conclusion: “I have no question in my own mind that there has been a historical succession of Baptists from the days of Christ to the present time.”8 Answering Whitsitt in Did They Dip? (1896), Christian argued from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century sources, among them Fuller's account of the Anabaptists of 1538 as “Donatists new dipped,” that the English Anabaptists and Baptists immersed before 1641: “We have enough proof, however, to show that they practiced dipping.”9 A present-day holder, R. L. Vaughn, states the reading's ground directly: “As a doctrine, church perpetuity stands or falls on the infallible word of God rather than the fallible record of human historians.”10 On this reading the Baptist churches of today stand in the line of the churches Christ founded, kept by his promise through every age.

A beginning among English Separatists in the early seventeenth century

On this reading the Baptist churches began when English Separatists, in Amsterdam about 1609 and in London in the 1630s, came to hold that baptism belongs to professed believers and gathered churches on that basis.

Held by: W. H. Whitsitt (Southern Baptist; president, Southern Baptist Theological Seminary; A Question in Baptist History, 1896); Nicholas Weber (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); James Edward McGoldrick (Protestant historian, formerly a successionist; Baptist Successionism, 1994); Tom Nettles (Southern Baptist, Southern Baptist Theological Seminary; paper of 2009, ⚠ unverified).11

The reading rests on dated documents. A Separatist congregation from Gainsborough under John Smyth, a former Church of England clergyman, took refuge in Amsterdam; there Smyth argued in The Character of the Beast (1609) that baptism belongs to believers, baptized himself, and baptized Thomas Helwys, John Murton and the rest.12 Helwys issued A Declaration of Faith of English People Remaining at Amsterdam (1611) and about 1612 planted a congregation at Spitalfields, London, publishing A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity the same year.13 The Particular Baptists arose separately from the Jacob–Lathrop–Jessey congregation in London, where John Spilsbury's church is dated to about 1638.14 Whitsitt argued that “none of the Anabaptists of England in the sixteenth and first half of the seventeenth centuries were immersionists” and that immersion “was introduced into England … in the year 1641.”15 Nettles, as reported, presents Spilsbury holding that believers' immersion could be recovered from Scripture although it “had not been in place in unbroken succession since the time of the apostles.”16 McGoldrick reads the founders the same way: “neither Smyth nor Helwys believed that an unbroken line of true churches was essential for the maintenance of a proper ministry.” He holds that “Baptists arose in the 17th century in Holland and England” as “heirs of the reformers,” and he dates successionism as a scheme of church history, and Landmarkism with it, to the nineteenth century.2,17 Weber's Catholic account gives the same beginnings: “The first Baptist congregations were organized in the beginning of the seventeenth century,” General and Particular, from Smyth's Gainsborough church and a London “Separatist Church.”18 On this reading the Baptists are Protestants, a branch of English Separatism that came to baptize professed believers in the first decades of the seventeenth century.

Kinship with earlier dissenters and the Anabaptists, without organic succession

On this reading the Baptists are one with earlier believers'-baptism dissenters through shared principles (Armitage), or the first English Baptists drew substantially on the Continental Anabaptists (Stassen, Estep, Patterson), without any traceable chain of congregations or baptisms.

Held by: Thomas Armitage (American Baptist, A History of the Baptists, 1887); Glen H. Stassen (Baptist; Mennonite Quarterly Review, 1962, ⚠ unverified); William R. Estep (Southern Baptist, Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary; The Anabaptist Story, 1963, ⚠ unverified); Paige Patterson (Southern Baptist, Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary; paper of 2009, ⚠ unverified).19

Bryan describes the reading as tracing “a spiritual relationship of Baptists through the long line of Anabaptist sects”: these minority groups practised believers' baptism, and the kinship requires no organic continuity of baptisms or ordinations.3 Armitage asks whether “true lineage from the Apostolic Churches does not rest in present conformity to the apostolic pattern,” and answers that “Pure doctrine, as it is found uncorrupted in the word of God, is the only unbroken line of succession which can be traced in Christianity.” His history sets out “to follow certain truths through the ages … down to their chief conservators of this time, the Baptists.”20 For the seventeenth-century beginnings, the reading points to the Anabaptists around the English exiles. Smyth's church was formed in Amsterdam beside the Waterlander Mennonites, with whom Smyth later sought union;12 Estep, as reported, stressed the influence of Dutch Mennonites on the English Baptists and wrote that Smyth “adopted other Mennonite teachings as well.”21 Stassen, as quoted by Bryan, traced the Particular Baptists' London Confession of 1644 to non-separatist Congregational Calvinism read with Menno Simons' Foundation-Book, which “provides a straightforward explanation for the origin of every detail of the Baptist innovations.”22 Patterson, as reported, holds that “On the most critically important doctrines, Baptists [in 17th-century England] were virtually indistinguishable from their Anabaptist cousins”: the sole authority of Scripture, the necessity of adult faith, baptism of the regenerate only, and a regenerate, disciplined church free from other ecclesiastical authority.16 On this reading the Baptists' continuity lies in shared convictions, with believers of every age (Armitage) or with the Continental Anabaptists (Stassen, Estep, Patterson), rather than in a chain of congregations.
