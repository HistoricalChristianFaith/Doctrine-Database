Pius XII on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: 20 November 1947 (the encyclical Mediator Dei, on the sacred liturgy; timeline anchor) · pope 1939–1958 · Wikipedia
Summary: Mediator Dei is the first papal encyclical devoted to the liturgy, and it gives the adage its best-known modern twist. Pius XII first rejects a reading of “Lex orandi, lex credendi” in which the liturgy is a testing ground for doctrine. He then affirms Prosper’s sentence in its proper sense: the liturgy professes the faith and has often supplied arguments when the Church defined a doctrine. But the liturgy “does not decide or determine independently and of itself what is of Catholic faith,” and taken “in absolute and general terms” the relation runs the other way: “let the rule of belief determine the rule of prayer.” The same encyclical reserves the regulation of the liturgy to the pope and warns against restoring ancient rites simply because they are ancient.

Claims & evidence

The reading he rejects (§46)

On this subject We judge it Our duty to rectify an attitude with which you are doubtless familiar, Venerable Brethren. We refer to the error and fallacious reasoning of those who have claimed that the sacred liturgy is a kind of proving ground for the truths to be held of faith, meaning by this that the Church is obliged to declare such a doctrine sound when it is found to have produced fruits of piety and sanctity through the sacred rites of the liturgy, and to reject it otherwise. Hence the epigram, “Lex orandi, lex credendi” — the law for prayer is the law for faith.

— Mediator Dei (1947), §46, source.1 The target is a pragmatic test of doctrine: a teaching is true if the worship built on it bears fruit. The encyclical names no one. Later commentators have read the paragraph as aimed partly at the Modernist reading of the adage, for which George Tyrrell’s Lex Orandi (1903) is the standard example.2

The liturgy professes the faith (§47)

But this is not what the Church teaches and enjoins. The worship she offers to God, all good and great, is a continuous profession of Catholic faith and a continuous exercise of hope and charity, as Augustine puts it tersely. “God is to be worshipped,” he says, “by faith, hope and charity.” … The entire liturgy, therefore, has the Catholic faith for its content, inasmuch as it bears public witness to the faith of the Church.

— Mediator Dei, §47, source.3 The Augustine is Enchiridion 3. The liturgy is a profession of a faith already held: the creed is sung in it, Scripture is read in it, and the mysteries are celebrated.

Prosper’s sentence, and its reversal (§48)

For this reason, whenever there was question of defining a truth revealed by God, the Sovereign Pontiff and the Councils in their recourse to the “theological sources,” as they are called, have not seldom drawn many an argument from this sacred science of the liturgy. For an example in point, Our predecessor of immortal memory, Pius IX, so argued when he proclaimed the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin Mary. Similarly during the discussion of a doubtful or controversial truth, the Church and the Holy Fathers have not failed to look to the age-old and age-honored sacred rites for enlightenment. Hence the well-known and venerable maxim, “Legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi” — let the rule for prayer determine the rule of belief. The sacred liturgy, consequently, does not decide or determine independently and of itself what is of Catholic faith. More properly, since the liturgy is also a profession of eternal truths, and subject, as such, to the supreme teaching authority of the Church, it can supply proofs and testimony, quite clearly, of no little value, towards the determination of a particular point of Christian doctrine. But if one desires to differentiate and describe the relationship between faith and the sacred liturgy in absolute and general terms, it is perfectly correct to say, “Lex credendi legem statuat supplicandi” — let the rule of belief determine the rule of prayer.

— Mediator Dei, §48, source.4 The Latin of the key sentences is “Sacra igitur Liturgia catholicam fidem absolute suaque vi non designat neque constituit … Quodsi volumus eas, quae inter fidem sacramque Liturgiam intercedunt, rationes absoluto generalique modo internoscere ac determinare, iure meritoque dici potest: «Lex credendi legem statuat supplicandi».” The encyclical cites Prosper’s sentence as from the Indiculus, without naming Celestine.

The paragraph does two things. It keeps Prosper’s direction for one case: when a disputed doctrine is being settled, the rites supply “proofs and testimony.” And it adds a second, general rule in the opposite direction: the liturgy is a profession of faith subject to the magisterium, so faith governs prayer. The reversal is qualified (“in absolute and general terms”), and it does not withdraw the first rule. The wording of the inversion, lex credendi legem statuat supplicandi, is the one that the bishop of Orléans had proposed a century earlier against Guéranger, who rejected it.

The pope alone regulates the liturgy (§58)

It follows from this that the Sovereign Pontiff alone enjoys the right to recognize and establish any practice touching the worship of God, to introduce and approve new rites, as also to modify those he judges to require modification. Bishops, for their part, have the right and duty carefully to watch over the exact observance of the prescriptions of the sacred canons respecting divine worship. Private individuals, therefore, even though they be clerics, may not be left to decide for themselves in these holy and venerable matters …

— Mediator Dei, §58, source.5 This follows from the reversal. If the liturgy is a profession of faith subject to the teaching authority, the authority that guards the faith also orders the rites. The next paragraph adds that the Church “grows, matures, develops, adapts and accommodates herself” in her liturgy, “provided only that the integrity of her doctrine be safeguarded.”

Antiquity is not enough (§61)

The same reasoning holds in the case of some persons who are bent on the restoration of all the ancient rites and ceremonies indiscriminately. The liturgy of the early ages is most certainly worthy of all veneration. But ancient usage must not be esteemed more suitable and proper, either in its own right or in its significance for later times and new situations, on the simple ground that it carries the savor and aroma of antiquity. The more recent liturgical rites likewise deserve reverence and respect. They, too, owe their inspiration to the Holy Spirit, who assists the Church in every age even to the consummation of the world.

— Mediator Dei, §61, source.6 Pius was writing against liturgical “antiquarianism”: to wish the altar “restored to its primitive tableform” is, in his words, to stray from the straight path (§62). The principle cuts both ways. In 1947 it told against reformers who wanted the earliest forms back. In the dispute after 2021 it is cited against those who treat the older Roman Missal’s age as decisive.

Notes

  • Repeated in 1950. Defining the Assumption three years later, Pius XII gave the liturgy a section of the bull and quoted Mediator Dei. He also wrote that “the liturgy of the Church does not engender the Catholic faith, but rather springs from it.”7 Whether the feasts nonetheless drove the definitions is weighed at Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption?
  • The reversal’s afterlife. Later Roman documents keep the double rule. John Paul II in 1988 calls fidelity to the rites “a requirement of the Lex orandi, which must always be in conformity with the Lex credendi,” and the Catechism (1992) quotes Prosper and adds that “the Church believes as she prays.” Both are on the timeline. Which way the adage runs, Prosper’s direction or Pius’s, is still disputed among liturgists and theologians. On the timeline, Alexander Schmemann and Aidan Kavanagh hold Prosper’s direction; Kavanagh calls the reversal “a shambles of the dialectic of revelation.” Geoffrey Wainwright reads the encyclical as stating a “two-way relation between worship and doctrine.” Pius’s reversal is weighed at Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer? (contested), beside its two rivals under which way the adage runs.
