Gregory of Nazianzus on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 380–381 (the Constantinople orations; timeline anchor) · Gregory c. 329–390, bishop of Constantinople 380–381 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Constantinople Gregory met opponents who argued from worship against the Spirit’s deity: “who in ancient or modern times ever worshipped the Spirit? Who ever prayed to Him?” He answered that Christians worship and pray in the Spirit, and that worship of One is worship of the Three. His main argument ran from baptism. The Spirit deifies the baptized, so He must be worshipped, and what is worshipped must be God: “the one is linked to the other, a truly golden and saving chain.” To the candidates for baptism he said the same. If he “worshipped a creature, or were baptized into a creature,” he would not be made divine. Unlike Basil, Gregory drew the conclusion in plain words: the Spirit is God.

Claims & evidence

“Who ever prayed to Him?” The argument from silence in prayer (Oration 31.12)

But, he says, who in ancient or modern times ever worshipped the Spirit? Who ever prayed to Him? Where is it written that we ought to worship Him, or to pray to Him, and whence have you derived this tenet of yours? We will give the more perfect reason hereafter, when we discuss the question of the unwritten; for the present it will suffice to say that it is the Spirit in Whom we worship, and in Whom we pray. … Therefore to adore or to pray to the Spirit seems to me to be simply Himself offering prayer or adoration to Himself. And what godly or learned man would disapprove of this, because in fact the adoration of One is the adoration of the Three, because of the equality of honour and Deity between the Three?

— Oration 31.12 (the Fifth Theological Oration, “On the Holy Spirit”), trans. Browne & Swallow, NPNF² vol. 7, source.1 The objection is itself an argument from the Church’s prayer. The opponents took the lack of worship and prayer addressed to the Spirit as evidence that He was not God. Gregory does not deny the fact. He reinterprets it. Prayer “in the Spirit” (John 4:24; Rom 8:26) is the Spirit’s own work in the one who prays, and honour paid to one Person of the Trinity is paid to all three. The same oration admits that the Scriptures speak of the Spirit’s deity less openly than of the Son’s, and explains it as a revelation given by stages (31.26–27; see the argument page on the Spirit’s deity).

“How can He deify me by Baptism?” (Oration 31.28)

This, then, is my position with regard to these things, and I hope it may be always my position, and that of whosoever is dear to me; to worship God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost, Three Persons, One Godhead, undivided in honour and glory and substance and kingdom, as one of our own inspired philosophers not long departed showed. … For if He is not to be worshipped, how can He deify me by Baptism? But if He is to be worshipped, surely He is an Object of adoration, and if an Object of adoration He must be God; the one is linked to the other, a truly golden and saving chain. And indeed from the Spirit comes our New Birth, and from the New Birth our new creation, and from the new creation our deeper knowledge of the dignity of Him from Whom it is derived.

— Oration 31.28, source.2 The chain runs from the rite to doctrine. Baptism deifies, and only God deifies, so the Spirit who acts in baptism is to be worshipped and is God. The last sentence makes the order explicit. The experience of rebirth comes first, and “deeper knowledge” of the Spirit’s dignity follows from it. Basil, who had died in 379, never wrote in his own prose that the Spirit is God (see Basil on the Trinity). Gregory says it outright.

“Baptized into a creature” (Oration 40.42–43, On Holy Baptism)

If I yet pleased men I should not be the servant of Christ, says the Apostle; and if I yet worshipped a creature, or were baptized into a creature, I should not be made divine, nor have changed my first birth. What shall I say to those who worship Astarte or Chemosh, the abomination of the Sidonians, or the likeness of a star, a god a little above them to these idolaters, but yet a creature and a piece of workmanship, when I myself either do not worship Two of Those into Whose united Name I am baptized, or else worship my fellow-servants, for they are fellow-servants, even if a little higher in the scale; for differences must exist among fellow-servants.

— Oration 40.42, preached at Constantinople on 6 January 381, source.3 This sermon was preached to people preparing for baptism, the day after the feast of the Lights (Epiphany). Gregory puts the dilemma in the terms of the rite they are about to receive. Either the Son and the Spirit, into whose “united Name” Christians are baptized, are not worshipped, or creatures are worshipped, which is what the pagans do. In the next chapter he declines to rank the Son above the Spirit for the same reason: “I would honour the Son as Son before the Spirit, but Baptism consecrating me through the Spirit does not allow of this” (40.43).4 The confession he gives the candidates in the chapter before is quoted on Gregory’s trinity page (40.41).

Notes

  • Which way does it run? Gregory argues from baptism to worship, and from worship to deity. That is an argument from the rite to the faith. But he does not argue from the Church’s set prayers. The opponents did that, pointing out that no one prayed to the Spirit, and Gregory had to explain the prayers rather than cite them. So the liturgy was evidence on both sides of this dispute, as it had been in Cyprian’s (see Cyprian). What settled it for Gregory was what baptism does, not the wording of a prayer.
  • Dating. The five Theological Orations (27–31) were preached at the Anastasia chapel in Constantinople. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates them to 380; the NPNF editors allow 379–381.5 A few months after Oration 40, the Council of Constantinople (381), at which Gregory presided for a time, confessed the Spirit as one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.” The creed puts the Spirit’s deity in liturgical terms, as the object of the Church’s worship.
