Martin Luther on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1523–1526 (Formula Missae, 1523; Deutsche Messe, 1526; timeline anchor) · Luther 1483–1546 · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther never cites the adage, but his reform of the Mass is the clearest case of it running in reverse: a changed belief rewrote the prayer. Because he held that the Mass is “neither a sacrifice nor a good work” but Christ’s testament, received by faith, he cut the offertory and “the entire canon,” where “almost everything smacks and savors of sacrifice,” and kept only the Words of Institution, now sung aloud to the people. He said he meant to purify the old order, not abolish it, and kept what he judged harmless (the elevation, for a time) for the sake of the weak. In the Deutsche Messe he refused to make even his own order a law: an order that hurts faith is to be “straightway abolished.” For Luther the rule of faith, the Gospel, judges the rite.

Claims & evidence

Purify the service, do not abolish it (Formula Missae, 1523)

We therefore first assert: It is not now nor ever has been our intention to abolish the liturgical service of God completely, but rather to purify the one that is now in use from the wretched accretions which corrupt it and to point out an evangelical use.

— An Order of Mass and Communion for the Church at Wittenberg (Formula Missae et Communionis, 1523), LW 53:20, source.1 The order was written in Latin for Nicholas Hausmann of Zwickau, and Luther explains why he had waited so long to publish it: he had used “neither authority nor pressure” and had been “hesitant and fearful, partly because of the weak in faith, who cannot suddenly exchange an old and accustomed order of worship for a new and unusual one.” His standard is the oldest form. The Mass was “instituted by Christ himself” and kept by the apostles “quite simply and evangelically without any additions.” The early additions (psalms before the blessing, the Kyrie, the readings, the introit, Gloria, Creed, Sanctus and Agnus Dei) “are unobjectionable,” and the Sunday propers “testify to ancient purity, the canon excepted.”

The Canon: “The mass became a sacrifice”

Then it was that wicked King Ahaz removed the brazen altar and erected another copied from one in Damascus. What I am speaking of is the canon, that abominable concoction drawn from everyone’s sewer and cesspool. The mass became a sacrifice. Offertories and mercenary collects were added. … All these have been tolerated and – with the gospel revealing so many abominations – they can be tolerated until they can be completely removed. In the meanwhile we shall prove all things and hold fast what is good [1 Thess. 5:21]. But in this book we are not going to prove again that the mass is neither a sacrifice nor a good work – we have amply demonstrated that elsewhere. We do accept it as a sacrament, a testament, the blessing (as in Latin), the eucharist (as in Greek), the Table of the Lord, the Lord’s Supper, the Lord’s Memorial, communion, or by whatever evangelical name you please, so long as it is not polluted by the name of sacrifice or work.

— Formula Missae, LW 53:21–22, source.2 The Latin is “loquor autem de Canone illo lacero et abominabili, ex multorum lacunis ceu sentina collecto: ibi cepit missa fieri sacrificium.” The order of argument is the point. The doctrine comes first and is not reargued here; Luther had set it out in The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520) and The Misuse of the Mass (1521). The rite is then measured by it, and the gospel is what reveals the “abominations.”

“Repudiate everything that smacks of sacrifice, together with the entire canon”

Eighth, that utter abomination follows which forces all that precedes in the mass into its service and is, therefore, called the offertory. From here on almost everything smacks and savors of sacrifice. And the words of life and salvation [the Words of Institution] are imbedded in the midst of it all, just as the ark of the Lord once stood in the idol’s temple next to Dagon. … Let us, therefore, repudiate everything that smacks of sacrifice, together with the entire canon and retain only that which is pure and holy, and so order our mass.

— Formula Missae, LW 53:26, source.3 The older Philadelphia Edition renders the same sentences “nearly everything sounds and reeks of oblation … Therefore repudiating all those things which smack of sacrifice and of the Offertory, together with the entire Canon, let us retain those things which are pure and holy.” In place of the offertory and the whole Roman Canon, Luther’s order runs straight from the preface to the Words of Institution, then the Sanctus and Benedictus, the Lord’s Prayer, the Pax and the Agnus Dei. The prayer “Deliver us” is omitted with the signs made over the host. He replaced the final collect “because it sounds almost like a sacrifice.”

The Words of Institution aloud; the elevation kept for the weak

I wish these words of Christ – with a brief pause after the preface – to be recited in the same tone in which the Lord’s Prayer is chanted elsewhere in the canon so that those who are present may be able to hear them, although the evangelically minded should be free about all these things and may recite these words either silently or audibly.

— Formula Missae, LW 53:28, source.4 In the Roman rite the priest said the Canon, Words of Institution included, in a low voice. For Luther these words are the testament itself, Christ’s promise to the communicant, so they must be heard. In the same section he keeps the elevation “according to the customary rite for the benefit of the weak in faith who might be offended if such an obvious change in this rite of mass were suddenly made.” Patience with the weak had a limit. On communion in both kinds he writes that “now that the gospel has been instilled among us these two whole years, we have humored the weak in faith long enough.”

The Deutsche Messe: no order is a law (1526)

For this is the damnable thing in the papal services, that they have been changed into laws, works and merits to the utter destruction of faith.

— The German Mass and Order of Service (Deutsche Messe und Ordnung Gottesdiensts, 1526), Preface, source (Philadelphia Edition).5 The German reads “Denn darümb seind die Bebstlichen Gottes dienste so verdamlich / das sie gesetze / werck / vnd verdienst drauß gemacht / vnnd damit den glauben verdruckt haben.” So Luther asks that his own order be used not “as a law” but “in Christian freedom,” and he lists three kinds of service: the Latin Formula Missae, which he does not “abrogate or change,” the German Mass, which is “a public provocation to faith and to Christianity,” and a third, for “those who mean to be real Christians,” that he does not yet set up. The Words of Institution are again the center, and the exhortation before communion asks the people to “look upon the Testament of Christ in true faith.”6

An order that hurts faith is to be abolished

… this or any other Order shall be so used that whenever it becomes an abuse, it shall be straightway abolished and replaced by another, even as King Hezekiah put away and destroyed the brazen serpent … The Orders must serve for the improvement of faith and love and not cause any injury to faith.

— Deutsche Messe, conclusion, source (Philadelphia Edition).7 This is the principle in its plainest form. The rite has no authority of its own. It serves faith and is judged by whether it does. The elevation shows how the judgement worked: in 1526 Luther keeps it “because it goes well with the German Sanctus and signifies that Christ has commanded us to remember Him.”

The canon prayer by prayer: The Abomination of the Secret Mass (1525)

In Vom Greuel der Stillmesse (1525) Luther printed the Roman Canon in German and commented on it prayer by prayer. According to Bryan Spinks’s summary, he says of the Te igitur, which offers “these gifts, these offerings,” that it is equivalent to saying to God before the whole world “we have to help Christendom with bread and wine; it is a barefaced lie when you say that the blood of your son alone is sufficient.” This survey has not consulted the treatise itself.8

Notes

  • Belief governs prayer. Luther’s liturgical scholars describe his reform in exactly these terms. Bryan Spinks (1973) wrote that “it was not a pruning-knife with which the reformer worked, but the Gospel,” and that “the reason for the new Canon is to be found in Luther’s doctrine of Justification by faith.”9 Frank Senn (1973) tied the rejection of the eucharistic sacrifice to “the whole ‘justification-atonement axis of Reformation theology.’”10 Darius Petkūnas (2025), a Lutheran liturgical historian, states the principle outright: “The church’s lex orandi flows out of the lex credendi.” He adds that the Canon had “references to sacrifice rendering it incompatible with the doctrine of justification.”11 Geoffrey Wainwright’s Doxology (1980) generalizes the point: Protestantism typically gives doctrine priority as a norm for worship, “so that what must be believed governs what may and should be prayed” (as reported by Plaatjies-van Huffel; the book itself has not been consulted here).12
  • But not against the old rite as such. Luther keeps most of the Mass and calls it a witness to “ancient purity.” He never says that tradition counts for nothing. His claim is that the Canon and offertory are late accretions, and that the gospel judges them. Catholic writers answered that the Canon was ancient and was the Church’s own witness to the sacrifice. The Council of Trent declared in 1562 that it was “so pure from every error” that nothing in it does not savour of holiness, and anathematized those who say it “contains errors, and is therefore to be abrogated.”
  • Elsewhere on this wiki. Luther’s doctrine of the presence, which he did not give up when he cut the Canon, is set out on his real-presence page. In the same decade Thomas Cranmer did in England, by stages, what Luther did at Wittenberg.
