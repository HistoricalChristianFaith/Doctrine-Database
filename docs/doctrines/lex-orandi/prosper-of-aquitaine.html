Prosper of Aquitaine on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 435–442 (the Capitula, on Cappuyns’s dating) · Prosper c. 390–c. 455 · Wikipedia
Summary: Prosper of Aquitaine, Augustine’s lay champion against the monks of Marseilles, wrote the sentence from which the adage was later cut: ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, “so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” It stands in chapter 8 of his Capitula, a dossier of papal rulings on grace compiled in Rome. His argument runs this way. The Church everywhere obeys the apostle’s command to pray for all people, and it asks God to give unbelievers faith. So the beginning of faith is God’s gift and not man’s first step. The same argument, without the formula, fills chapter 12 of the first book of his The Call of All Nations (c. 450). There the “law” is plainly the precept of 1 Timothy 2:1.

Claims & evidence

The setting: the Massilians would accept only what Rome had taught

Because some, who glory in the name of Catholic, linger in the condemned view of heretics whether through perverseness or through ignorance, and presume to oppose the very pious disputers, and, although they do not hesitate to anathematize Pelagius and also Caelestius, nevertheless contradict our teachers, as if they overstepped the necessary limit, and profess to follow and approve only those [doctrines] which the most sacred See of the Blessed Apostle PETER has sanctioned and taught against the enemies of the grace of God through the office of its leaders, it has become necessary to inquire diligently as to what the rulers of the Roman Church judged concerning the heresy which had arisen in their times …

— Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei (the Capitula or Indiculus), preface, trans. R. J. Deferrari, source.1 The “very pious disputers” and “our teachers” are Augustine and his defenders. Their opponents, the monks of southern Gaul later called semi-Pelagians, had condemned Pelagius but said Augustine went too far. They would follow Rome and nothing else. Prosper takes them at their word. Chapters 1–6 excerpt Popes Innocent I and Zosimus, and chapter 7 gives the canons of the Council of Carthage (418) that Rome had approved. Then he turns from what the popes decreed to what the Church prays.2

Chapter 8: the law of supplicating establishes the law of believing

But besides these hallowed ordinances of the most blessed and Apostolic See, in accordance with which the most pious Fathers, after casting aside the pride of pernicious novelty, have taught us to refer to Christ’s grace both the beginnings of good will, and the advances in commendable devotions and the perseverance in these unto the end, let us be mindful also of the sacraments of priestly public prayer, which handed down by the Apostles are uniformly celebrated in the whole world and in every Catholic Church, in order that the law of supplication may support the law of believing.

For when the leaders of the holy nations perform the office of ambassador entrusted to them, they plead the cause of the human race before divine Clemency, and while the whole Church laments with them, they ask and pray that the faith may be granted to infidels; that idolaters may be delivered from the errors of their impiety; that the veil of their hearts may be removed and the light of truth be visible to the Jews; that heretics may come to their senses through a comprehension of the Catholic faith; that schismatics may receive the spirit of renewed charity; that the remedy of repentance may be bestowed upon the lapsed; that finally after the catechumens have been led to the sacraments of regeneration, the royal court of heavenly mercy may be opened to them.

— Capitula ch. 8, trans. R. J. Deferrari, source; Latin in the Dionysian collection of papal decretals, source.3 This is the locus classicus of the adage. Three features of the Latin matter. First, Prosper says lex supplicandi, the law of supplicating, not lex orandi. Second, his verb statuat means to fix or establish. Deferrari’s “may support” is too weak, as is the French of Denzinger–Hünermann, “constitue,” in Paul De Clerck’s judgement. Prosper is making an argument meant to end a dispute, so the prayer settles the belief.4 Third, the prayers in view are particular ones. They are not the liturgy in general but the intercessions for those outside the Church: unbelievers, idolaters, Jews, heretics, schismatics, the lapsed and catechumens. Prosper stresses that they are apostolic (ab apostolis tradita) and universal (in toto mundo atque in omni catholica Ecclesia uniformiter). Both points ground the argument in the whole Church and not in one local rite.

The answered prayer shows that conversion is God’s work

Moreover, the effect of these prayers shows that these are not sought from the Lord perfunctorily and uselessly, since indeed God deigns to attract from every kind of error very many whom, torn from the power of darkness, He transfers into the kingdom of the Son of his love [Col. 1:13], and from vessels of wrath He makes vessels of mercy [Rom. 9:22 f.]. This is felt to be so completely a divine work that the action of the graces and the acknowledgement of praise on account of the illumination or correction of such [persons] should always be referred to God who effects these things.

— Capitula ch. 8 (end), trans. R. J. Deferrari, source.5 This is the hinge of the argument against the Massilians. If the first step to faith were man’s own, it would make no sense for the Church to ask God to take it for him, or to thank God when a heretic or pagan is converted. Both the petition and the thanksgiving assume that God gives the beginning of faith. On the doctrinal question itself, see the initium fidei argument on the predestination timeline. The argument itself is Augustine’s. About 427 he had pressed it on Vitalis of Carthage: the priest at the altar prays that God convert unbelievers, and “God does what He commands that He be asked to do.” In The Gift of Perseverance, addressed to Prosper himself, he wrote that the Church which has always prayed this way has always believed it.

Chapter 9: the baptismal exorcisms make the same point

That also, which the holy Church uniformly does in the whole world with regard to those to be baptized, we do not observe with indifferent respect. Since whether children or youths come to the sacrament of regeneration, they do not approach the fountain of life, before the unclean spirit is driven away from them by the exorcisms and the breathings upon them of the priests; so that then it is truly manifest how the prince of this world is sent forth [John 12:31], and how the strong [man] is first bound [Matt. 12:29], and thereafter his vessels are plundered [Mark 3:27], having been transferred to the possession of the victor, who leads captivity captive [Eph. 4:8] and gives gifts to man [Ps. 67:19].

— Capitula ch. 9, trans. R. J. Deferrari, source.6 The second liturgical argument comes from the rite of baptism rather than the intercessions. Every candidate, infant or adult, is exorcized and breathed upon before the font. The rite assumes that each one comes to baptism under the power of the devil and must be freed by Christ. Augustine had argued from the same rite against the Pelagians from 412 until his death: the Church would not exorcise infants if they were not under the devil’s power through original sin. Whether this chapter belongs to Prosper’s lex is disputed: O. T. I. Wright argues that a reading which confines the lex to 1 Timothy 2:1 wrongly leaves it out.7

The conclusion: God is the author of every good movement, from the beginning of faith

Therefore, in accordance with the ecclesiastical rules and documents taken on divine authority, we are so strengthened by our Lord’s aid that we confess openly that God [is] the author of all good dispositions of mind, and also of works, and of all zeal, and of all virtues by which from the beginning of faith we tend towards God; and we do not doubt that all the merits of man are preceded by His grace … Assuredly free choice is not taken away by this aid and gift of God, but it is set at liberty …

— Capitula, conclusion, trans. R. J. Deferrari, source.8 The “ecclesiastical rules” (ecclesiasticis regulis) are the two liturgical arguments just made. They stand beside the papal and conciliar “documents.” The final chapter then sets the deeper questions aside, among them predestination, which Augustine’s defenders had argued at length. The dossier binds its readers only to what it has shown: that grace comes first.

The Call of All Nations I.12: the apostle’s precept is a “law of supplication”

The Apostle commands—rather, the Lord speaking through the Apostle commands through him that supplications and intercessions, thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and for all that are in high station. All priests and all the faithful, adhere unanimously to this norm of supplication in their devotions. There is no part of the world in which Christian peoples do not offer up these prayers. The Church, then, pleads before God everywhere, not only for the saints and those regenerated in Christ, but also for all infidels and all enemies of the Cross of Christ, for all worshippers of idols, for all who persecute Christ in His members, for the Jews whose blindness does not see the light of the gospel, for heretics and schismatics who are alien to the unity of faith and charity.

But what does she beg for them if not that they leave their errors and be converted to God, that they accept the faith, accept charity, that they be freed from the shadows of ignorance and come to the knowledge of the truth? They cannot do this by themselves …

— De vocatione omnium gentium I.12, trans. P. De Letter, source.9 De Letter’s “norm of supplication” renders legem supplicationis. This passage shows what “law” meant to Prosper better than the compressed sentence in the Capitula. Just before it he calls 1 Timothy 2:1–6 a “rule of the apostolic teaching” (doctrinae apostolicae regula) that the whole Church has received. To understand it rightly, he says, we should ask how the universal Church understands it, because “there can be no doubt about what is enjoined, if the efforts made by all those who obey are the same.” The law, then, is first of all the apostle’s precept. The Church’s worldwide obedience to it is what makes it a rule of faith. Karl Federer and Paul De Clerck built their reading of the adage on this passage.10 The book is aimed at the Massilians’ favourite text, “God will have all men to be saved” (1 Tim 2:4). Prosper answers it by reading the verse together with the command to pray that comes just before it.

The limit of the argument: not every prayer is answered

We indeed see it happen that the grace of the Saviour passes by some men and that the prayers of the Church in their favour are not heard. This must be ascribed to the secret judgments of divine justice. We must acknowledge that we cannot understand this profound mystery in this life.

— De vocatione omnium gentium I.13, trans. P. De Letter, source.11 Prosper does not claim that the Church’s prayer for all proves that all will be saved. The intercessions show that conversion, where it happens, is God’s work. Why some are passed over he leaves to God’s hidden judgement. The argument from prayer settles the beginning of faith, not the scope of salvation.

Notes

  • Dating, and which text came first. Marc Cappuyns dated the Capitula to 435–442, after Prosper had moved to Rome. Alexander Hwang has argued for 450–455.12 De Letter, following Cappuyns, puts The Call of All Nations about 450, in the lull between the two phases of the controversy.13 On Cappuyns’s chronology, then, the Capitula came first, and The Call of All Nations spells out at length what the formula had compressed. On Hwang’s, the two are close in time. Neither dating makes The Call of All Nations the earlier work.
  • Authorship. Both works circulated without Prosper’s name. The Capitula travelled in Dionysius Exiguus’s collection of decretals (c. 500), appended to Pope Celestine I’s letter of 431 under the heading “Decreta Coelestini papae.” That is why later writers cited the adage as Celestine’s. The Call of All Nations was printed among the works of Ambrose and of Leo the Great. Cappuyns’s studies restored both to Prosper, and that attribution is now generally accepted.14 The case against Celestine’s authorship, and whether the dossier carried papal authority anyway, is weighed on a separate argument page.
  • An older “ecclesiastical rule.” About half a century before Prosper, the commentator known as Ambrosiaster had already described 1 Timothy 2:1 in similar terms: “This is the ecclesiastical rule, handed down by the master of the Gentiles, which our priests use to supplicate for all.” De Clerck cites it in support of the reading that Prosper’s lex is the apostolic precept.15
  • The prayers Prosper had in mind. De Letter notes that the list of those prayed for in The Call of All Nations I.12 closely resembles the solemn prayers chanted after the Passion on Good Friday in the Roman rite. He takes those prayers to be a remnant of the ancient “prayer of the faithful” once said at every Mass after the homily.16
  • For Prosper’s earlier writings in the Massilian controversy (429–434), see Prosper of Aquitaine on predestination, grace & free will. For how the adage was later used, see the lex orandi timeline.
