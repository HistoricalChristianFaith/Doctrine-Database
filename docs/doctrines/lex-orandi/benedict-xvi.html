Benedict XVI on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: 7 July 2007 (the motu proprio Summorum Pontificum and its letter to the bishops; timeline anchor) · earlier statements as Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, 1997–1998 · pope 2005–2013 · Wikipedia
Summary: Summorum Pontificum turned the adage into a legal category. It declared Paul VI’s Missal “the ordinary expression” and the 1962 Missal “an extraordinary expression of the same lex orandi,” and it said the two would not divide the Church’s lex credendi, “for they are two usages of the one Roman rite.” The 1962 Missal, Benedict wrote, had been “never abrogated.” His reason was the continuity of the Church’s prayer: “What earlier generations held as sacred, remains sacred and great for us too, and it cannot be all of a sudden entirely forbidden or even considered harmful.” As a cardinal he had called the prohibition of the old Missal after 1970 a breach in the history of the liturgy.

Claims & evidence

Two expressions of one lex orandi (art. 1)

Art 1. The Roman Missal promulgated by Pope Paul VI is the ordinary expression of the lex orandi (rule of prayer) of the Catholic Church of the Latin rite. The Roman Missal promulgated by Saint Pius V and revised by Blessed John XXIII is nonetheless to be considered an extraordinary expression of the same lex orandi of the Church and duly honoured for its venerable and ancient usage. These two expressions of the Church’s lex orandi will in no way lead to a division in the Church’s lex credendi (rule of faith); for they are two usages of the one Roman rite.

It is therefore permitted to celebrate the Sacrifice of the Mass following the typical edition of the Roman Missal, which was promulgated by Blessed John XXIII in 1962 and never abrogated, as an extraordinary form of the Church’s Liturgy.

— Summorum Pontificum (7 July 2007), art. 1, source.1 The Latin is “ordinaria expressio ‘Legis orandi’ … extraordinaria expressio eiusdem ‘Legis orandi’ … sunt enim duo usus unici ritus romani.” Prosper’s “law of praying” was the Church’s universal practice of intercession. Here the lex orandi is something a missal “expresses,” and a pope can say which missals do. The preamble grounds the act in the adage, quoting the General Instruction of the Roman Missal (2002) §397: usages received from tradition are kept “so that the faith may be handed on in its integrity, since the Church’s rule of prayer (lex orandi) corresponds to her rule of faith (lex credendi).”

“Never juridically abrogated” (letter to the bishops)

It is not appropriate to speak of these two versions of the Roman Missal as if they were “two Rites”. Rather, it is a matter of a twofold use of one and the same rite.

As for the use of the 1962 Missal as a Forma extraordinaria of the liturgy of the Mass, I would like to draw attention to the fact that this Missal was never juridically abrogated and, consequently, in principle, was always permitted. At the time of the introduction of the new Missal, it did not seem necessary to issue specific norms for the possible use of the earlier Missal.

— Benedict XVI, Letter to the Bishops on the occasion of the publication of the Apostolic Letter “motu proprio data” Summorum Pontificum (7 July 2007), source.2 This is a legal judgement. Between 1970 and 2007 the older Missal had been used under special permissions: a 1971 concession for elderly or infirm priests, the 1984 indult Quattuor abhinc annos, and John Paul II’s Ecclesia Dei (1988). Benedict’s position was that these permissions regulated a Missal that had never been withdrawn. Whether that judgement is right is weighed on its own page.

“Growth and progress, but no rupture”

There is no contradiction between the two editions of the Roman Missal. In the history of the liturgy there is growth and progress, but no rupture. What earlier generations held as sacred, remains sacred and great for us too, and it cannot be all of a sudden entirely forbidden or even considered harmful. It behooves all of us to preserve the riches which have developed in the Church’s faith and prayer, and to give them their proper place.

— Letter to the Bishops (2007), source.3 This is the lex orandi argument in Benedict’s form. The Church’s prayer carries its faith, so a form of prayer that carried the faith for centuries cannot become harmful without implying that the faith it carried was wrong. The same paragraph applies the rule the other way: priests attached to the older use “cannot, as a matter of principle, exclude celebrating according to the new books,” since “the total exclusion of the new rite would not in fact be consistent with the recognition of its value and holiness.” The letter also hoped that “the two Forms of the usage of the Roman Rite can be mutually enriching.”

As cardinal: the Church “never forbids” rites (1997–1998)

The authority of The Church can define and limit the use of rites in different historical situations. She never forbids them purely and simply! The Council, therefore, ordered a reform of the liturgical books but it never forbade the previous books.

— Joseph Ratzinger, address on the tenth anniversary of Ecclesia Dei, Rome, 24 October 1998 (English translation from the French), source.4 A year earlier, in his memoir Milestones, he had written of the new Missal’s introduction that “the prohibition of the missal that was now decreed, a missal that had known continuous growth over the centuries … introduced a breach into the history of the liturgy whose consequences could only be tragic.”5 The two statements fit together. In 1997 he described what had happened in practice, a prohibition. In 1998 he denied that it rested on any act of the Council, and in 2007, as pope, he denied that it had any legal basis.

Notes

  • Reversed in 2021. Fourteen years later Francis’s Traditionis Custodes declared the post-conciliar books “the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite” and abrogated the earlier norms. The two positions are weighed as a pair of related arguments.
  • A criticism from both sides. Critics on both sides of the later dispute rejected the “two forms of one rite” construction. The liturgist Andrea Grillo, who defends Traditionis Custodes, called it a “sophism”; Peter Kwasniewski, who opposes it, argued that the 1970 Missal is a different rite from the 1962. Their reasons are set out on the argument pages.6
