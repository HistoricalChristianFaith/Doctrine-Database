Hincmar of Reims on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 859–860 (De praedestinatione Dei et libero arbitrio; timeline anchor) · Hincmar 806–882, archbishop of Reims from 845 · Wikipedia
Summary: Hincmar is the first writer found in this survey who makes Prosper’s clause a working rule and applies it again and again. In his long treatise against the predestination teaching of Gottschalk of Orbais, written for Charles the Bald in 859–860, he quotes “let the law of supplicating establish the law of believing” eight times, always as a decree of Pope Celestine. He turns it on a new question. Prosper had argued from the Church’s intercessions that faith is God’s gift. Hincmar argues from the same intercessions, which he identifies with the Good Friday prayers for every class of person, that God wills all to be saved. He then widens the “law of supplicating” beyond the intercessions, to Roman Mass prayers that call Christ’s sacrifice the ransom of “the whole world” and to the Easter blessing of the font.

Claims & evidence

The setting: a treatise for the king against Gottschalk (859–860)

Gottschalk of Orbais taught a “twofold” predestination, of the elect to life and of the reprobate to death, and held that Christ did not die for the reprobate, even the baptized. Hincmar, his metropolitan, had him condemned and imprisoned in 849, and drafted the four chapters of Quiersy (853), which confessed that God wills all to be saved and that Christ suffered for all. A rival synod at Valence (855) rejected them (see Gottschalk of Orbais). In June 859 Charles the Bald handed Hincmar a set of chapters from Remigius of Lyon and asked for his judgement. The answer, finished late in 859 or early in 860, is the long De praedestinatione Dei et libero arbitrio. An earlier version of 856 is lost.1

The law of supplicating is the Good Friday prayer for all (ch. 24)

Quae sit autem lex supplicandi, et ipse in eodem capitulo explicat, et Ecclesia quasi specialiter in die crucifixionis Domini nostri Jesu Christi, ostendens pro quibus sit crucifixus, quosque vult salvos fieri, supplicando, et congemiscendo demonstrat, orans pro omnibus hominibus, et singillatim atque distincte pro omnibus ordinibus, pro omnibus gradibus, pro omnibus dignitatibus, pro schismaticis et haereticis, pro Judaeis atque paganis, ut nemo credatur exceptus, agens apud divinam clementiam totius humani generis causam.

— De praedestinatione ch. 24 (PL 125:213D), source.2 In English (the wiki’s translation): “What the law of supplicating is, he himself explains in the same chapter, and the Church shows it, especially on the day of the crucifixion of our Lord Jesus Christ. Showing for whom He was crucified and whom He wills to be saved, she supplicates and groans, praying for all men, and one by one and distinctly for all orders, all ranks, all dignities, for schismatics and heretics, for Jews and pagans, so that no one may be thought excepted, and pleads the cause of the whole human race before the divine mercy.”

Hincmar has just quoted the opening of chapter 8 of the Capitula as Pope Celestine’s, from his “decretal letter to Venerius and the other Gallic bishops,” and as the rule for “what we ought to believe, hold and preach as catholics.” Prosper’s list of intercessions (for unbelievers, idolaters, Jews, heretics and schismatics, the lapsed, catechumens) is here matched to the Church’s prayer on the day of the crucifixion, which, as Hincmar lists it, prays for every order, rank and dignity, for schismatics and heretics, and for Jews and pagans. Then he draws the conclusion:

Legem ergo credendi, ut sanctus Coelestinus jubet, lex nobis statuat supplicandi: ut quoniam ab omni Ecclesia catholica Deo supplicatur pro omnibus, credamus Deum omnes homines velle salvos fieri, licet non omnes salventur, et quod quidam salvantur, salvantis sit donum; quod autem quidam pereunt, sit pereuntium meritum.

— De praedestinatione ch. 24 (PL 125:214B).3 “Let the law of supplicating, then, as holy Celestine commands, establish for us the law of believing: so that, since supplication is made to God by the whole catholic Church for all, we believe that God wills all men to be saved, though not all are saved; and that some are saved is the gift of the One who saves, while that some perish is the desert of those who perish.” The last clause is Prosper’s own formula, which Hincmar quotes elsewhere in the treatise.

“See how holy Augustine agrees” (ch. 25)

In the next chapter Hincmar gathers the “orthodox doctors.” After quoting Augustine’s Gift of Perseverance 23.63 (“When has prayer not been made in the Church for unbelievers and its enemies, that they might believe?”), he comments: “Ecce quomodo sanctus Augustinus consentit et confirmat quod sanctus dixerat Coelestinus: Legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi” (“See how holy Augustine agrees with and confirms what holy Celestine had said”).4 The order is the reverse of the real one. The Capitula restate Augustine’s argument (see Augustine of Hippo), but Hincmar, reading them as a papal decree, has Augustine confirming the pope. Later in the chapter he returns to the clause as “the sentence holy Celestine promulgated,” glosses it again (“because the catholic Church supplicates for all, let us believe that God wills all men to be saved”), and adds the Capitula’s closing sentence as Celestine’s own confirmation: “So great is God’s goodness toward all men that He wills His gifts to be our merits.”5

The Mass prayers: Christ’s blood is the price of the whole world (ch. 34)

Hanc ipsa sancta sedes apostolica in arcanis coelestium mysteriorum ex apostolorum traditione retinet dicens: «Haec hostia quaesumus, Domine, mundet nostra delicta, quae in ara crucis immolata totius mundi tulit offensam.» Et item: «Annue nobis, Domine, ut animae famuli tui Leonis haec prosit oblatio, quam immolando totius mundi tribuisti relaxari delicta.» Et: «Respice, omnipotens et misericors Deus, super hanc familiam tuam, pro qua Dominus noster Jesus Christus non dubitavit manibus tradi nocentium, et crucis subire tormentum.» Unde legem credendi, ut beatus Coelestinus scribit, lex statuat supplicandi.

— De praedestinatione ch. 34 (PL 125:362A); repeated almost word for word in the epilogue, ch. 6 (PL 125:473A–B).6 “This the holy apostolic see itself keeps, from the tradition of the apostles, in the secrets of the heavenly mysteries, saying: ‘May this victim, we pray, O Lord, cleanse our sins, which, offered on the altar of the cross, took away the offence of the whole world.’ And again: ‘Grant us, O Lord, that this offering may profit the soul of your servant Leo, by whose immolation you have granted the sins of the whole world to be loosed.’ And: ‘Look down, almighty and merciful God, upon this your family, for which our Lord Jesus Christ did not hesitate to be delivered into the hands of the guilty and to undergo the torment of the cross.’ Hence, as blessed Celestine writes, let the law of supplicating establish the law of believing.”

The question here is no longer the beginning of faith but the extent of the redemption: whether Christ’s blood was shed for all or only for the elect. The “law of supplicating” is no longer the intercessions of 1 Timothy 2:1 but the wording of particular Mass prayers, one of them for the feast of Pope Leo. Hincmar stresses that they are the prayers of “the holy apostolic see” and of apostolic tradition, as Prosper had stressed that the intercessions were apostolic and universal.

The blessing of the font (ch. 35)

Against the claim that Christ did not die for the baptized reprobate, Hincmar quotes the Easter blessing of the font: “May the power of your Spirit descend into this fullness of the font … that every man who enters this sacrament of regeneration may be reborn into the new infancy of true innocence.” Here, he says, the Church “believes, professes and preaches” the inseparable sacraments of the blood of redemption, the Spirit of sanctification and the water of baptism for all baptized catholics: “Legem ergo, ut sanctus Coelestinus dicit, credendi lex statuat supplicandi.”7

The epilogue: chapter 8 in full (epilogue ch. 5)

The treatise’s epilogue sums up its theses one by one. Under the heading “That God wills all men to be saved, though not all are saved,” the first authority is chapter 8 of the Capitula, quoted in full from “Obsecrationum sacerdotalium sacramenta respiciamus” through the list of intercessions, and again followed by the closing sentence on God’s goodness “toward all men.” Next come Chrysostom on the priest at the altar who prays “for the whole world,” Pseudo-Dionysius, and Cyprian on the Lord’s Prayer.8

Notes

  • A new target for an old argument. Prosper wrote against those who made the first step of faith man’s own; Hincmar wrote against a strict Augustinian who limited God’s saving will and Christ’s death to the elect. The premise is the same in both: the Church prays for all, and its prayer is a rule of faith. Hincmar draws from it a conclusion Prosper himself had drawn in The Call of All Nations I.12, where Paul’s command to pray for all is the lex supplicationis that shows God’s will to save all (see Prosper of Aquitaine). Hincmar quotes that work too, so his use is closer to Prosper’s than to a later “the liturgy as a whole” reading.
  • But the rule is widening. In chapters 34 and 35 the “law of supplicating” is no longer an apostolic precept obeyed everywhere. It is the wording of particular prayers of the Roman rite, cited as proof-texts. This is the step De Clerck calls a banalization of the adage when modern writers make it. Hincmar takes it in the ninth century, while still under the name of a papal decree.
  • The other side used the Capitula too. The authorities in this dispute were largely shared, and Remigius of Lyon, whose chapters Hincmar was answering, is among the writers who cited the Capitula as Celestine’s.9 Whether God wills all to be saved, and in what sense, is weighed on the predestination timeline, not here.
