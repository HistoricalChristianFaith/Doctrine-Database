Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as a reading of Prosper’s sentence the direction is secure. Lex supplicandi is the subject of statuat, and the argument runs from what the Church asks to what it must believe. The fourth- and fifth-century record also shows universal rites settling disputed doctrines: baptism and the doxology for the Spirit’s deity, the intercessions for faith as God’s gift. As a general principle, that worship is primary theology and belief never founds prayer, it is contested. In the same generation that coined the formula, the African church checked its prayers against the faith. The Arian dispute showed that a shared rite settles nothing until some belief is brought to read it. Whether worship is the first source of doctrine or one witness among others depends on views of revelation and church authority that divide the traditions. · advanced by Prosper Guéranger (1840), George Tyrrell (1903, in a form Pius XII rejected), Alexander Schmemann (1961 onward) and Aidan Kavanagh (1984)

Related arguments: which way the adage runs — see also Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer? · Do prayer and belief shape each other, with neither simply prior?

The claim

There is no doubt that the law of belief does indeed shape and influence the law of worship. But the maxim does not say this, nor does it need to. It says only that the latter constitutes or founds the former. To reverse this is to cancel out the meaning of the maxim in its original formulation. The law of belief does not constitute the law of worship. Thus the creeds and the reasoning which produced them are not the forces which produced baptism. Baptism gave rise to the trinitarian creeds. So too the eucharist produced, but was not produced by, a scriptural text, the eucharistic prayer, or all the various scholarly theories concerning the eucharistic real presence. Influenced by, yes. Constituted or produced by, no.

— Aidan Kavanagh, On Liturgical Theology (1984), p. 92, source.1 The claim weighed here is that the adage runs from prayer to belief. The Church’s worship is the norm of its faith, and doctrine articulates what the worshipping Church already knows. In its strongest form, Kavanagh’s, the assembly’s worship is theologia prima, “primary theology.” Doctrine is secondary reflection on it, and belief may shape worship but never founds it.

Who advances it

Dom Prosper Guéranger opened his Institutions liturgiques (1840) with Prosper’s sentence and called the liturgy “tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity.” When the bishop of Orléans proposed to turn the formula round, he refused.2 Alexander Schmemann made the Church’s worship “the ‘rule of prayer’ dwelling within the Church and determining her ‘rule of faith,’” and wrote that “the lex orandi must be recovered as the lex credendi.”3 Aidan Kavanagh rested the direction on Prosper’s verb, as quoted above, and called its reversal “a shambles of the dialectic of revelation.”4 Geoffrey Wainwright, who holds the reciprocal view, still calls this direction “the more usual” way of construing the tag.5

A different form of the claim belongs to the Modernist George Tyrrell, whose Lex Orandi (1903) set out to show how “the Lex Credendi or rule of faith has been determined and must be interpreted by the Lex Orandi or rule of prayer.” For Tyrrell, “prayer” meant the whole religious life of the universal Church, not its liturgical texts.6 That form is weighed below as a variant.

The evidence for

Prosper’s sentence runs from prayer to belief

In ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi the law of supplicating is the subject of the verb and the law of believing its object: “so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” The verb statuere means to fix or establish, not merely to express.7 The argument around it goes the same way. The bishops pray that God give faith to unbelievers, God answers, and so the beginning of faith is His gift. Pius XII, who reversed the adage, did not dispute the grammar. He quoted Prosper’s sentence as “let the rule for prayer determine the rule of belief” and kept it for the settling of a disputed doctrine, before adding a second rule the other way.8 Wainwright’s point that the tag can be read both ways applies to the two-word slogan, not to Prosper’s sentence, whose verb fixes the direction.

The rites settled real disputes

In the fourth century the argument from the rite to the faith decided the deity of the Son and the Spirit. Athanasius asked the Arians why “a thing made [is] classed with the Maker in the consecration of all of us”; they “do not baptize into Father and Son, but into Creator and creature.”9 Gregory of Nazianzus argued from baptism: “if He is not to be worshipped, how can He deify me by Baptism? But if He is to be worshipped, surely He is an Object of adoration, and if an Object of adoration He must be God.”10 The Council of Constantinople (381) then confessed the Spirit in the language of worship, as the one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.” The creed did not say homoousios of the Spirit. It took over the doxology.11 In the fifth century Augustine and then Prosper settled the beginning of faith from the Church’s intercessions, and the original-sin question from the exorcisms before baptism. Aquinas was still drawing the second argument from the same chapter eight hundred years later.12

Worship came before formulated doctrine

Kavanagh’s claim that “baptism gave rise to the trinitarian creeds” has early support. Christians were baptized with a threefold question and answer long before any creed was defined by a council. Tertullian argued from the triple immersion “at each several mention of Their names” to three persons. Cyprian called “the very interrogation which is put in baptism” “a witness of the truth.”13 Earlier still, prayer and hymns to Jesus came centuries before any doctrine of his divinity was defined. Whether that worship came before belief as well is weighed at Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity? (plausible).

Prayer is the witness everyone can read

And I wish that those who are slow and weak of heart, who cannot, or cannot as yet, understand the Scriptures or the explanations of them, would so hear or not hear our arguments in this question as to consider more carefully their prayers, which the Church has always used and will use, even from its beginnings until this age shall be completed.

— Augustine, On the Gift of Perseverance 23.63 (428/429), source.14 This is the epistemic case for the claim. The Church’s prayer is older than its theologians and more widely shared than its doctrinal texts, and every believer takes part in it. Kavanagh made the same point about who does theology. Its primary agents are “more likely to be charwomen and shopkeepers than pontiffs and professors.”15

Adversarial assessment

The church that coined the formula corrected its prayers by its faith

For the prayers of many are corrected every day on being recited to men of greater learning, and many things are found in them contrary to the Catholic faith.

— Augustine, On Baptism, against the Donatists VI.25.47 (c. 400), source.16 The African church that produced the argument from prayer also checked its prayers against the faith. The Council of Hippo (393) ruled that at the altar “the prayer be directed always to the father,” and that prayers copied from elsewhere were not to be used until they had been discussed “with the better-educated brothers.” A council at Carthage in 407 ordered that only prayers approved in synod be used, and “that others contrary to the faith should not be used by any means.”17 So in Prosper’s own century most prayer was judged by belief, not the other way round. His argument works because he picks out a different kind of prayer, one “handed down by the apostles” and celebrated “uniformly throughout the whole world.” That is what separates his law of supplicating from the local prayers that needed correcting (see the original-sense argument, sound). The authority lies in the practice’s apostolic origin and universality, not in its being prayer. That supports the narrow claim and tells against the general one.

A shared rite did not settle the Arian question

The Arian historian Philostorgius, in Photius’s summary, says that the Arians, “though they differed in their doctrinal statements from those who preached the Catholic faith, were nevertheless accustomed to communicate with them in prayers, hymns, and deliberations, and in everything except the mystic sacrifice.”18 Both sides baptized with the same words. Athanasius therefore had to add that a true baptism needs, “with the Name, … also the right faith.”19 Gregory of Nazianzus met an argument from prayer on the other side: “who in ancient or modern times ever worshipped the Spirit? Who ever prayed to Him?” He had to explain the Church’s prayers rather than cite them.20 The rite counted as evidence in these disputes. It could not settle them until some belief was brought to read it.

Basil puts the doxology after the creed

The classic fourth-century case is Basil’s, and he states the order: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.” The confession of faith is “the origin and parent of the doxology.”21 Kavanagh is right that this chain begins with a rite, baptism. But the rite at its head is the one Christ commanded, with its words given in Scripture (Matt 28:19). The prayer in the narrow sense, the doxology Basil was accused of changing, comes after the creed and is defended by it. On Basil’s account the font founds the faith, and the faith then norms the prayer.

Tyrrell’s form fails

Tyrrell’s variant makes the changing religious experience of an age the judge of doctrine. Pius XII rejected a reading of this kind in 1947. It treats the liturgy as “a kind of proving ground for the truths to be held of faith,” so that a doctrine is sound if it produces “fruits of piety and sanctity.” Later commentators take this as aimed partly at Tyrrell.22 None of the other advocates holds it. Prosper argued from a fixed, apostolic practice, not from devotional fruit. Tyrrell himself denied that the maxim proves “every popular devotion” sound. A test by fruits cannot tell a true doctrine from a false one that people find consoling. This is the weakest form of the claim.

Worship is not the only first-order source

Kavanagh’s critics answer that most believers form their convictions from catechesis, Scripture study and preaching as much as from the rite. In Nicholas Jesson’s words, “The question at issue is not the nature of the experiences, but the source of the ‘structures’ with which they interpret the experiences.”23 Schmemann’s readers draw a related limit. As David Fagerberg puts it, “Schmemann is not proposing that the liturgical tail wags the doctrinal dog.”24 And Kavanagh himself, by the end of his book, calls the two laws “subtly correlative, the first founding the second, the second affecting (although not founding) the first.”25 That retreats from “constituted or produced by, no” toward the reciprocal view. The asymmetry he keeps, that belief affects prayer but never founds it, is hard to square with the record. At Hippo, at Antioch and in Cranmer’s 1552 Book, a belief did more than influence the wording of a prayer. It decided it.

Assessment

contested — strong in its narrow form, disputed as a general principle. Prosper’s sentence runs from prayer to belief, and no one who has studied it disputes that. The fourth- and fifth-century record shows universal rites settling doctrinal questions: baptism and the doxology for the Trinity, the intercessions for grace, the exorcisms for original sin. In each case the practice was older than the definition, and the definition took its language from the practice. That much is sound.

The general thesis, that worship is primary theology and belief may shape prayer but never found it, goes further than the evidence shows. The same African church that coined the argument from prayer corrected its prayers by its faith. Prosper’s argument works only for a practice that is apostolic and universal. The shared rites of the Arian period settled nothing until a belief was brought to read them, and in Basil’s own order the doxology follows the creed. Liturgical theologians weigh this evidence one way and magisterial and Protestant theology another, according to where each locates revelation and authority. There is no clear winner. Tyrrell’s form, which makes devotional fruit the test of doctrine, is the weakest and is not held by the others. The rival readings are weighed at Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer? and Do prayer and belief shape each other, with neither simply prior?
