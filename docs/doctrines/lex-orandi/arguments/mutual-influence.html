Do prayer and belief shape each other, with neither simply prior?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — as an account of how worship and doctrine have actually related. The record shows rites settling doctrines and doctrines revising rites, often within the same dispute: in the fourth century the font shaped the creed, the creed reshaped the doxology, and the doxology supplied the creed of 381 with its words. Every witness here who argues for one direction also concedes the other. Kavanagh grants that belief “does indeed shape and influence” worship. Pius XII keeps Prosper’s direction for disputed doctrines. The Catechism sets “the Church’s faith precedes” beside “the Church believes as she prays.” What the reciprocal view does not settle is which side prevails when the two conflict. Wainwright treats that as a question of criteria, and the traditions answer it differently. That open question is what the two directional readings dispute. · advanced by Geoffrey Wainwright (1980); stated in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992); conceded by Aidan Kavanagh (1984)

Related arguments: which way the adage runs — see also Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief? · Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer?

The claim

The Latin tag lex orandi, lex credendi may be construed in two ways. The more usual way makes the rule of prayer a norm for belief: what is prayed indicates what may and must be believed. But from the grammatical point of view it is equally possible to reverse subject and predicate and so take the tag as meaning that the rule of faith is the norm for prayer: what must be believed governs what may and should be prayed. The linguistic ambiguity of the Latin tag corresponds to a material interplay which in fact takes place between worship and doctrine in Christian practice: worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship.

— Geoffrey Wainwright, Doxology (1980), p. 218, source.1 The claim weighed here is that neither law is simply prior. Worship shapes doctrine and doctrine shapes worship, and the history of the Church’s faith is the history of that exchange. Wainwright built his systematic theology on this view. Its two central chapters are titled with the two halves of the adage, one on each direction.

Who advances it

Geoffrey Wainwright, a Methodist, is its main advocate. He describes the characteristic directions of the confessions: Catholicism argues from liturgy to doctrine and Protestantism from doctrine to liturgy. Then he adds that “Catholicism and Protestantism each know also the reverse relationship between the two.”2 He read Mediator Dei itself as stating a “two-way relation between worship and doctrine.”3 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) puts both directions in one paragraph: “The Church’s faith precedes the faith of the believer who is invited to adhere to it. When the Church celebrates the sacraments, she confesses the faith received from the apostles — whence the ancient saying: lex orandi, lex credendi … The law of prayer is the law of faith: the Church believes as she prays.”4 And Aidan Kavanagh, the firmest advocate of the prayer-first reading, ends his book by calling the two laws “subtly correlative, the first founding the second, the second affecting (although not founding) the first.”5

The evidence for

The oldest statement runs both ways

The earliest argument on the timeline from the Church’s worship to its faith is Irenaeus’s (c. 180), against Gnostics who offered the Eucharist while despising matter: “But our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion.” The Latin has belief agreeing with (consonans) the Eucharist and the Eucharist confirming (confirmat) belief, and the Greek is the same.6 Neither is said to come first. The rite confirms the faith, and the faith agrees with the rite.

The fourth century ran both ways in one dispute

In the Trinitarian controversy the influence ran in both directions within a few decades. Baptism shaped the creed: Basil’s “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief.” The creed shaped the prayer: “As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.”7 At Antioch the doxology was rewritten by party, “Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Ghost” replacing “through the Son in the Holy Ghost.”8 And the creed of 381 then took the doxology’s language, confessing the Spirit as the one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.”9 Each step was a real influence, and the steps run in opposite directions.

Prosper’s own setting had both

Prosper argued from the Church’s intercessions to grace, so prayer established belief. In the same African church, in the same generation, councils required prayers to be checked against the faith. Augustine admitted that prayers were “corrected every day” when found “contrary to the Catholic faith.”10 Both held at once, for different kinds of prayer. A practice handed down by the apostles and celebrated uniformly everywhere could settle a doctrine. A local prayer composed by “unskillful men” had to answer to doctrine. Prosper’s three marks, apostolic, universal and uniform, are what tell the two kinds apart (see the original-sense argument, sound).

The Reformation turned the circle

In England a doctrine rewrote the rite. Cranmer’s 1552 Book changed every point of the 1549 rite on which a Catholic reading had rested. Then the rite became a source of doctrine. The Church of England’s Canon A5 says its doctrine is “in particular … to be found in the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion, The Book of Common Prayer, and the Ordinal.” Any new rite must in turn be “neither contrary to, nor indicative of any departure from” that doctrine.11 Within one tradition belief formed the prayer, the prayer then became a standard of belief, and that belief now judges new prayer. The Marian definitions show the same circle in Catholic form. Earlier popes promoted a feast “so that the law of belief itself might be established by the law of prayer,” and the feast was then cited for the dogma (see the Marian-feasts argument, plausible).12

The advocates of each direction concede the other

The view is not only Wainwright’s. It is where the advocates of each direction end up. Kavanagh: “There is no doubt that the law of belief does indeed shape and influence the law of worship.”13 Pius XII, in the paragraph that reverses the adage, first affirms that “the Church and the Holy Fathers have not failed to look to the age-old and age-honored sacred rites for enlightenment” on doubtful questions, and that the liturgy can supply “proofs and testimony, quite clearly, of no little value.”14 Schmemann’s readers say he “is not proposing that the liturgical tail wags the doctrinal dog.”15 None of them holds that influence runs in one direction only. They disagree about which direction is founding, or prevails in general.

Adversarial assessment

It does not say which prevails

The main objection is that the reciprocal view describes the relation without answering the normative question. When a prayer and a doctrine conflict, which gives way? Kavanagh’s asymmetry (prayer founds, belief only affects) and Pius XII’s (belief governs “in absolute and general terms”) both accept two-way influence and still give opposite answers. Wainwright knew this. His plan for the two chapters asks, “According to what criteria is worship allowed to influence doctrine and vice versa?”16 The objection is fair as a limit, but it does not refute the claim. The claim is that neither law is simply prior, and the persistence of the dispute between the two asymmetries is some evidence for it. As a full answer to “which way does the adage run?” the reciprocal view is incomplete. The traditions supply the missing criterion differently: Scripture for the Reformers, the magisterium for Rome, and for Prosper and De Clerck the practice that is apostolic and universal.

Prosper’s sentence runs one way

Wainwright’s grammatical point holds only for the two-word tag. Prosper’s sentence has a verb, and its subject is the law of supplicating. So the reciprocal view is not what Prosper meant. He stated one direction for one case, the intercessions that settle the beginning of faith. That is a correct point about the text, and it limits the view’s claim on the adage’s name. It does not count against the view as an account of the wider relation, which Prosper did not address. His own church, as above, practised both directions.

Is “interplay” too vague to be tested?

A view that admits every case could be empty. This one is not. It would fail if one direction were missing from the record: if no rite had ever settled a disputed doctrine, or no doctrine had ever corrected a rite. Both are well attested, often in the same century and the same controversy. The evidence that defeats the strong forms of the two directional readings is the evidence for this one.

Assessment

sound — as an account of how worship and doctrine have actually related. From Irenaeus’s two-way sentence through the fourth-century font, creed and doxology, the African church that both argued from its prayers and corrected them, and the Reformation’s rites written to a doctrine and then made a standard of it, influence runs both ways, often within one dispute. Each side concedes the other direction: Kavanagh’s “influenced by, yes,” Pius XII’s retention of Prosper’s rule for disputed points, and the Catechism’s two sentences side by side. Two limits should be kept in view. The reciprocal view does not decide which prevails when prayer and doctrine conflict, and that question is what Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief? and Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer? dispute. Nor is it the meaning of Prosper’s sentence, which runs one way for the case he argued.
