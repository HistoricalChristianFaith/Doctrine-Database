Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the worship of Jesus is very early. An Aramaic invocation of him, prayer and hymns to him, baptism in his name and a meal in his honour are already taken for granted in letters Paul wrote about twenty years after the crucifixion. These practices have no real parallel in Jewish devotion to other exalted figures. The worship came centuries before any doctrine of Christ’s divinity was defined, and later writers argued from it to that doctrine. But “before belief” goes too far. The worship already presupposed convictions about Jesus, above all that God had raised and exalted him. The scholars most cited for the thesis see devotion and conviction arising together, and the worship framed the later doctrinal question without settling it. · advanced by Larry Hurtado, Richard Bauckham

Related arguments: the worship of Jesus — see also Did the first Christians worship God through Jesus, rather than Jesus himself as God?

The claim

… the theological developments that led to the doctrine of the Trinity were to some significant degree prompted and even made unavoidable by the dyadic devotional pattern and the triadic shape of discourse about God …

— Larry W. Hurtado, “Monotheism in the New Testament,” link.1 The claim is that in the beginning the order ran from worship to doctrine. Within the first few years after Jesus’ death, Jewish believers who confessed one God began to pray to Jesus, call on his name, sing hymns to him, and baptize and eat a sacred meal in his name. This pattern of worship, in which Jesus is honoured alongside God, came first. The later doctrine of Christ’s full divinity was the Church’s attempt to account for it, and the worship constrained what that doctrine could be. If that is right, the principle later summed up as lex orandi, lex credendi (the rule of prayer is the rule of belief) was at work at the origin of Christianity itself, long before Prosper of Aquitaine coined the formula. This is a different question from where the language of a divine “Word” came from, which is weighed on the “two powers” argument. It is also a different question from what the earliest worship actually was. That question is weighed on the sibling page.

Who advances it

The thesis is chiefly Larry Hurtado’s. In One God, One Lord (1988) he argued that the worship of Jesus was a “mutation” within Jewish monotheism, not a borrowing from pagan cults. In Lord Jesus Christ (2003) he traced it from the earliest Jewish believers to the second century.2 He long called the pattern “binitarian.” Later he preferred “dyadic,” because readers took the older word to read later Trinitarian doctrine into the New Testament. Richard Bauckham, in Jesus and the God of Israel (2008), reaches a similar result from belief rather than practice. For Bauckham the earliest Christians included Jesus “in the unique identity of this one God,” and their worship of Jesus expressed that inclusion.3 Both wrote against the older picture of Wilhelm Bousset’s Kyrios Christos (1913). Bousset held that the cult of Jesus as “Lord” was a later, Gentile development: in Hurtado’s summary, a “second-stage (but still very early) ‘Hellenization’ of the original faith of the ‘Primitive Palestinian Community.’”4 Bart Ehrman, in How Jesus Became God (2014), accepts that Jesus was thought divine very early, but he puts belief first: “belief in the resurrection … is what initially led the followers of Jesus … to believe that Jesus had been exalted to heaven.”5 The rival reading of the evidence, that the first Christians worshipped God through Jesus rather than Jesus himself, is James D. G. Dunn’s and has its own page.

The evidence for

The worship is very early, and Jewish. Paul ends 1 Corinthians (c. 54) with an untranslated Aramaic phrase: “If any man love not the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be Anathema Maranatha.” Marana tha means “Our Lord, come!” The Didache uses the same cry in its eucharistic prayer. Paul expects his Greek-speaking readers in Corinth to know an Aramaic prayer, so it must come from the first Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea. In Hurtado’s words, “The Lord appealed to is clearly Jesus.” Martin Hengel called it the earliest prayer to Jesus that we know.6 Paul addresses the same letter to “all that in every place call upon the name of Jesus Christ our Lord.” In the Old Testament, calling on the name of the Lord means calling on God. Paul applies Joel’s promise, “whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved,” to calling on Jesus. The hymn in Philippians 2 does the same with Isaiah 45:23, where God says “unto me every knee shall bow”: now every knee bows “at the name of Jesus.”7 And in 1 Corinthians 8:6, “to us there is but one God, the Father … and one Lord Jesus Christ,” Paul seems to divide the words of Israel’s confession of the one God, the Shema, between the Father and Jesus. As Bauckham notes, that reading “has now rightly been recognized quite widely.”8

A pattern of practices without precedent. What Hurtado stresses is not one text but a cluster of practices: singing to and about Jesus, prayer to him, calling on and confessing him, baptism and prophecy in his name, and a meal centred on his death. Together they make “a striking constellation of devotional practices in which the exalted Jesus is central, and for which we have no precedent or parallel in second-temple Jewish evidence.” Jews of the period honoured exalted angels, patriarchs and personified Wisdom, but they did not give them a place like this in worship.9 Dunn, the leading critic of Hurtado’s wider thesis, agrees on this point. He calls the calling on Jesus “very striking,” “entirely unusual and without precedent in the Judaism of the time.”10

Outsiders saw the worship before they saw a doctrine. About 112 the Roman governor Pliny the Younger questioned Christians in Bithynia. They told him they met before dawn on a fixed day and sang “to Christ as to a god” (carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere secum invicem). What Pliny learned was a practice, not a creed.11 The pagan critic Celsus (c. 175) attacked the same practice: “they pay excessive reverence to one who has but lately appeared among men, and they think it no offence against God if they worship also His servant.”12

Later writers argued from the worship to the doctrine. About 230 an anonymous Roman writer answered the followers of Artemon, who claimed that the belief that Christ was only a man had been the original faith of Rome. Among his proofs he cites the Church’s songs: “how many psalms and hymns, written by the faithful brethren from the beginning, celebrate Christ the Word of God, speaking of him as Divine.” This is the argument from prayer to belief, applied to Christ, two centuries before Prosper.13 In the fourth century Athanasius argued against the Arians that “creature does not worship creature,” so the Son, whom Christians worship, is no creature. Gregory of Nazianzus argued the same way for the Spirit.14 On this view the Nicene confession did not invent a divine Christ. It gave a precise account of a Christ the Church had worshipped from the start. Hurtado puts the point strongly: later belief “was forced on them … by the earnest convictions and devotional practice of believers from the earliest observable years.”15

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Three things are well established. The worship of Jesus goes back to the earliest Jewish believers, not to a later Gentile stage, and Bousset’s two-stage picture has not survived the evidence of maranatha and the pre-Pauline hymns. That worship came long before any defined doctrine of Christ’s divinity: by the time of Nicaea (325) Christians had been praying and singing to Christ for nearly three hundred years. And from at least the early third century, defenders of Christ’s divinity argued from that worship. To the extent that the claim is “worship before doctrine,” and that the worship shaped the doctrine, it is well grounded.

Belief came with the worship, not after it. The stronger form of the claim, that worship came before belief and produced it, does not fit the evidence. The earliest texts give reasons for the worship, and the reasons are beliefs about what God had done. The Philippians hymn grounds the bowing of every knee in God’s act: “Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him.” Peter’s speech in Acts declares that “God hath made that same Jesus … both Lord and Christ,” and Paul says Jesus was “declared to be the Son of God with power … by the resurrection from the dead.”16 Hurtado himself does not say that the worship came first. He traces the devotion to a conviction about Jesus that “was formed through an interaction of powerful ‘revelatory’ experiences,” including “prophetic oracles (declaring his exaltation)” and “intensive and creative interpretation of certain biblical texts (e.g., Isaiah 45:22-23; Psalm 110:1).”17 Bauckham’s worship “expressed” a belief about Jesus’ place in the identity of God.18 And Ehrman, as noted above, puts belief in the resurrection first. So the best reading is that devotion and conviction arose together. The devotion is the earliest and clearest evidence for the conviction, but not its cause. What came first was worship before formulated doctrine, not worship before belief.

The worship framed the doctrine but did not settle it. The early pattern was “dyadic”: Jesus was honoured alongside God and “to the glory of God the Father” (Phil 2:11). That pattern was compatible with more than one doctrine of the Son. Origen (c. 233) taught that prayer in the full sense should be offered “not even to Christ himself, but only to the God and Father of All.” Yet against Celsus he insisted that Christians “worship with all [their] power the one God, and His only Son.”19 In the fourth century the Arians also worshipped the Son and baptized in the threefold name. That is why Athanasius had to say that the words of the rite were not enough without “the right faith.”20 The worship set the question: what must Christ be, if Christians may rightly worship him? It took doctrinal argument to answer it with Nicaea’s homoousios rather than with a subordinate “second God.” Hurtado’s own wording is careful on this point: the worship “prompted” and constrained the doctrine. It did not dictate it.

What the worship was is itself disputed. The thesis assumes that what the first Christians gave Jesus was worship in the full sense. Dunn and others read the same evidence as devotion offered to God through Jesus. That reading is weighed on its own page. If it is right, the worship-first thesis still holds in a weaker form: later Christians read the early devotion as worship of Christ as God, and argued from it.

What is overstated in popular statements.

  • “Binitarian” is a label, not a finding. Hurtado dropped it for “dyadic” because it suggested a fixed doctrine of two persons in God.21
  • Calling this “lex orandi at the origin of Christianity” is a modern description. No first-century writer argues from the worship to a doctrine about Christ. The earliest explicit argument of that kind found here is the anti-Artemonite writer of c. 230.
  • “Worship drove belief” turns a correlation into a cause. The sources give beliefs about the resurrection and exaltation as the reasons for the worship.

Assessment

plausible — the worship of Jesus is very early, it comes from Jewish believers, and its pattern has no real precedent. It came long before any defined doctrine of Christ’s divinity and was later used to argue for that doctrine. In that form the claim is well grounded. But the claim as usually stated, that worship came before belief and drove it, overstates the case. The worship rested on convictions that God had raised and exalted Jesus, and the leading scholars of the question see devotion and conviction arising together. The worship also left open which doctrine of the Son would follow. So lex orandi describes the earliest period only in a qualified sense: the Church’s prayer set the question that its later creeds answered.
