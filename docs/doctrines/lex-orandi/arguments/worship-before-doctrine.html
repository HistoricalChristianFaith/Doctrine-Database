Did the first Christians worship Jesus, and did that worship come before and shape the doctrine of his divinity?

Disputed reading · on Lex orandi, lex credendi

The passage

… the theological developments that led to the doctrine of the Trinity were to some significant degree prompted and even made unavoidable by the dyadic devotional pattern and the triadic shape of discourse about God …

— Larry W. Hurtado, “Monotheism in the New Testament,” link.1

… by and large the first Christians did not worship Jesus as such.

— James D. G. Dunn, Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? (2010), 150, link, as quoted by Hurtado.2

The question

Paul’s letters of the 50s already show prayer, invocation and hymns connected with Jesus, and from about 230 Christian writers argued from the Church’s hymns to Christ’s divinity. Scholars differ on when and where devotion to Jesus as divine arose, on whether the earliest devotion was worship of Jesus himself or of God through him, and so on whether the principle later called lex orandi, lex credendi describes the origin of Christian belief about Christ. They also differ on the Jewish antecedents: William Horbury traces the cult of Christ to Jewish honour for the messianic king, and Crispin Fletcher-Louis points to Jewish texts in which Adam or the Enochic Son of Man is honoured, reading those figures as manifesting God.3 Bart Ehrman (University of North Carolina; How Jesus Became God, 2014), who accepts that Jesus was thought divine very early, puts belief in the resurrection first: it “is what initially led the followers of Jesus … to believe that Jesus had been exalted to heaven.”4 No Catholic or Orthodox scholar holding any of the readings below was located for this page. Where the language of a divine “Word” came from is a separate question, treated at the “two powers” page.

Devotion to Jesus as divine arose later, in a Gentile setting

On this reading the cult of Jesus as “Lord” and the confession of him as God belong to a second, Hellenistic stage of the movement, not to its first Jewish believers.

Held by: Wilhelm Bousset (German Lutheran New Testament scholar, Göttingen; Kyrios Christos, 1913), as summarized by Hurtado; P. M. (Maurice) Casey (University of Nottingham; From Jewish Prophet to Gentile God, 1991).5

Bousset held that the cult of Jesus as “Lord” was a later, Gentile development: in Hurtado’s summary, a “second-stage (but still very early) ‘Hellenization’ of the original faith of the ‘Primitive Palestinian Community.’” As Hurtado reports his schema, the “Kyrios-cult,” treating the risen Jesus “as rightful recipient of corporate worship,” was “perhaps the most significant development in early Christianity,” and it arose first “in places such as Antioch and Damascus, where the influence of pagan cults devoted to various gods and divinized heroes was sufficiently strong to prompt believers in such locations to treat the risen Jesus in an equivalent manner”; the first Jerusalem and Judean circles confessed Jesus as “the Son of Man.” Casey posits a slower development within the New Testament itself. He held that Jesus was first openly called God only in the Johannine community, after it had broken with the synagogue, as the church became more Gentile.6 On both accounts the decisive step came with the movement’s passage into the Gentile world.

The first Jewish believers worshipped Jesus, and the worship shaped the later doctrine

On this reading prayer, hymns and invocation addressed to Jesus go back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers, came long before any defined doctrine of his divinity, and prompted and constrained that doctrine.

Held by: Larry W. Hurtado (University of Edinburgh; One God, One Lord, 1988; Lord Jesus Christ, 2003).7

Paul ends 1 Corinthians (c. 54) with an untranslated Aramaic cry, Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!”, which the Didache also uses in its eucharistic prayer; it must come from the first Aramaic-speaking believers, and in Hurtado’s words “The Lord appealed to is clearly Jesus.”8 Believers are those who “call upon the name of Jesus Christ our Lord,” Joel’s promise about calling on the Lord is applied to Jesus, and in Philippians 2 every knee bows “at the name of Jesus,” echoing Isaiah 45:23.9 Hurtado describes “a striking constellation of devotional practices in which the exalted Jesus is central, and for which we have no precedent or parallel in second-temple Jewish evidence,”10 a pattern he came to call “dyadic” rather than “binitarian.”11 Outsiders described the practice: Pliny (c. 112) heard that Christians sang “to Christ as to a god”12 and Celsus (c. 175) that they “worship also His servant.”13 Jesus receives doxologies, and in Revelation the Lamb shares the worship of the one on the throne, while an angel forbids John to worship him.14 Hurtado traces the devotion to “powerful ‘revelatory’ experiences,” including “prophetic oracles (declaring his exaltation).”15

Later writers argued from the practice. About 230 an anonymous Roman writer answered the Artemonites from “how many psalms and hymns, written by the faithful brethren from the beginning, celebrate Christ the Word of God, speaking of him as Divine,”16 and Athanasius argued that “creature does not worship creature.”17 Later belief, in Hurtado’s words, “was forced on them … by the earnest convictions and devotional practice of believers from the earliest observable years.”18 On this reading the fourth-century argument from worship drew on a practice that reached back to the first believers.

The worship of Jesus expressed, from the first, a belief that he shares the identity of the one God

On this reading the worship did not come before the belief: from the earliest Palestinian Jewish community, worship of Jesus expressed the confession that he is included in the unique identity of Israel’s God.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican; Jesus and the God of Israel, 2008).19

In 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul divides the words of the Shema between the Father and Jesus, a reading Bauckham says “has now rightly been recognized quite widely.”20 The Philippians hymn grounds the bowing of every knee in God’s act, “Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him”; Acts declares that “God hath made that same Jesus … both Lord and Christ.”21 Bauckham finds “probably the earliest expression” of this belief in the reading of Jesus’ exaltation through Psalm 110:1: seated on God’s cosmic throne, Jesus “is included in the unique rule of God over all things,” a rule that “cannot be delegated as a mere function to a creature,” so that “the earliest christology was already in nuce the highest christology.” He reads the Philippians hymn’s use of Isaiah 45:23 in the same way, as an allusion to “the most insistently monotheistic passage in Deutero-Isaiah.”22 For Bauckham the worship of Jesus “certainly began in Palestinian Jewish Christianity,” and it “expressed the inclusion of Jesus in the unique identity” of the one Creator and Sovereign.23 On this reading confession and worship of Jesus belong together to the movement’s first stage.

The first Christians worshipped God through Jesus

On this reading the earliest worship was offered to the one God, in and through Jesus, God’s exalted agent, who was not himself the object of worship in the sense that God was.

Held by: James F. McGrath (Butler University; The Only True God, 2009); James D. G. Dunn (Methodist; Durham University; Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?, 2010).24

For Dunn the worship of Jesus “was a way of worshipping God.” The verb for cultic service, latreuō, always has God as its object: “Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve.” McGrath argues that sacrifice was the line Jewish monotheism drew between God and every other being, and that the New Testament never crosses it for Jesus, who is the sacrifice and the high priest.25 Paul’s pattern is “I thank my God through Jesus Christ,” “To God only wise, be glory through Jesus Christ,” and Dunn concludes that prayers “as such” are “always made to God.”26 The Didache thanks the Father for what “Thou madest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant,” closing “through Jesus Christ for ever,”27 and Ignatius’s song is addressed “to the Father through Jesus Christ.”28 The Philippians hymn ends “to the glory of God the Father,” and in the end the Son “shall … be subject unto him … that God may be all in all.”29 Dunn wrote against loose talk of “worshipping Jesus” that could slide into “Jesus-olatry,” “a denial of Christianity’s claim to be a monotheistic religion.”30

Origen’s On Prayer (c. 233) makes the rule explicit: “if we accept prayer in its full meaning, we may not ever pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself, but only to the God and Father of All.”31 He allows thanksgiving to Christ and intercession with him, as Stephen interceded,32 and against Celsus he says Christians “offer our petitions to the God of the universe through His only-begotten Son.”33 On this reading prayer to the Father through the Son, the rule Origen states, is the pattern of the earliest Christian worship.
