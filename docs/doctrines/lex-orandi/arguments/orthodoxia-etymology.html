Does “Orthodoxy” mean “right worship”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as a claim about what the word means. Greek orthodoxia was formed from doxa in its sense of “opinion,” and every early use, with its opposite heterodoxia, means right belief. The “glory” sense of doxa that dominates the Greek Bible and liturgy gives the word a real echo. The Slavonic pravoslavie (“right glory”) translates it that way, but it is a part-by-part calque that sat alongside pravoverie (“right belief”). That Orthodoxy, as the Church understands it, includes right worship is a separate theological claim, and a well-grounded one. · advanced by Kallistos Ware (1963) and much Orthodox catechesis

The claim

The word “Orthodoxy” has the double meaning of “right belief” and “right glory” (or “right worship”).

— Timothy (Kallistos) Ware, The Orthodox Church (1963), introduction.1 The claim is that the name of the Orthodox faith itself means “right worship,” or at least carries that meaning alongside “right belief.” The Greek doxa can mean “glory,” so ortho-doxia is “right glory,” that is, glorifying God rightly. If so, the word is lex orandi in miniature: the faith is named for its worship. The claim comes in two strengths. The strong form says the word literally means “right worship” (or “right teaching or right worship”). The softer form, Ware’s, says it has a double meaning. A third question is kept apart below: whether, whatever the word first meant, “right glory” is a fair theological reading of what Orthodoxy is.

Who advances it

Ware’s introduction, the most widely read English account of the Orthodox Church, puts the double meaning on its opening pages and draws the conclusion: the Orthodox regard their Church as the one that teaches the true belief about God and glorifies Him with right worship.1 Catechetical writing often states the strong form. A missionary leaflet by Bishop Alexander Mileant (Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia) says the word “literally means right teaching or right worship,” from orthos and doxa, “(teaching or worship).” An Orthodox Church in America diocese glosses the word “(‘right belief and right glory’).” An Antiochian parish’s catechism page (2025) gives doxa as “belief, glory, worship” and concludes that orthodoxy “isn’t just about having right opinions about God, it’s about glorifying Him rightly.”2 Fr. Stephen Freeman (2021) argues from the Slavonic. Cyril and Methodius, he writes, rendered orthodoxia as pravoslavie, “right glory,” and “how they rendered that word into Slavonic tells us much about how they heard it in their native Greek.”3 The reading has reached the reference works. The English Wikipedia article on doxa says the word’s meanings come together in the modern term “orthodoxy,” and glosses pravoslavie as “true belief,” “true way of worship.”4

The evidence for

In Christian Greek, doxa means “glory.” The Septuagint used doxa for the Hebrew kabod, the glory of God. In the New Testament it never means a mere opinion: Abbott-Smith’s lexicon notes that there it is “always good opinion,” hence praise, honour and glory, and then the radiant glory of God’s presence.5 The liturgy is full of it: the doxology, Doxa Patri (“Glory to the Father”), the Great Doxology. A Greek Christian of the fourth century or later heard doxa far more often in church as “glory” than as “opinion.” So the echo of “right glory” in orthodoxia was there to be heard.

Byzantine hymns put the two together. The ninth-century Trinitarian canons of Metrophanes of Smyrna, sung on Sundays in the Parakletike, pair the adverb with the verb “glorify.” In the canon of the fourth tone (ode 7) “all we who are born of earth, imitating” the angelic orders, “orthodoxly glorify (orthodoxôs … doxazomen) one Godhead in three hypostases of equal operation.” The same canon’s refrain asks the Trinity to “save those who glorify you in faith.”6

The feast of “Orthodoxy” is a feast of worship. The Sunday of Orthodoxy keeps the restoration of the icons in 843, the Triumph of Orthodoxy. What was restored was a practice of worship. Its Synodikon proclaims Christ and honours His saints “in words, in writings, in thoughts, in sacrifices, in churches, in holy icons,” worshipping Christ and venerating the saints, and ends: “This is the Faith of the Orthodox.”7 On the day the Church names for Orthodoxy, right worship is part of what “Orthodoxy” names.

The Slavonic heard it as glory. The Slavic churches call the faith pravoslavie: pravo-, “right,” and slava, “glory.” Etymological dictionaries agree that it is a calque of the Greek word.8 The largest Orthodox language community therefore names its faith “right glory” every day.

Adversarial assessment

The word was made from doxa as “opinion.” The verb orthodoxein is pre-Christian. Aristotle uses it in the Nicomachean Ethics: neither in mathematics nor in conduct does argument teach the first principles, “but virtue, natural or habituated, [teaches] right opinion (tou orthodoxein) about the first principle.” Liddell–Scott–Jones gives the verb as “have a right opinion” and the noun orthodoxia as “right opinion,” citing Pollux and the Neoplatonist commentators. It gives orthodoxos as “orthodox in religion” only from imperial law and Christian inscriptions.9 Behind these stands the philosophers’ orthê doxa, “right opinion,” which Plato set between ignorance and knowledge, and Aristotle’s “rightness of opinion is truth.”10 The compound was formed in that world, from the sense of doxa that belongs to the verb dokein, “to think, to seem.” The word appears neither in the Septuagint nor in the New Testament, where the “glory” sense rules.11

Every early Christian use means belief.

  • The opposite term came first. About 110, Ignatius warns the Magnesians: “Be not deceived with strange doctrines (heterodoxiais), nor with old fables.” He also writes of “those who are of a different opinion” (heterodoxountas) about the grace of Christ (Smyrnaeans 6.2). Heterodoxia can only mean “other opinion.” “Other glory” makes no sense, and the pair ortho-/hetero- only works with a single sense of doxa.12
  • Clement of Alexandria has the first Christian use of the root (c. 200), in a passage defending dialectic: “And do not those who are called orthodox (orthodoxastai) apply themselves to good works, knowing not what they do?” His contrast echoes Plato’s: these are people of right opinion who lack knowledge.13
  • Eusebius (c. 300–325) uses the noun far more than any earlier writer, and it always concerns doctrine. He trusts Irenaeus and Clement as men “who have maintained the orthodoxy of the Church.” He rejects apocryphal gospels as “out of accord with true orthodoxy,” and he speaks of Origen’s “orthodoxy in the faith,” of Pinytus’s, and of an “orthodoxy of the ecclesiastical faith.”14
  • From the fourth century on the word becomes common, and its company is doctrinal: “the orthodox faith,” “the doctrine of the orthodox,” “right doctrines” (Athanasius). Cyril of Alexandria asks Nestorius to keep “the right opinion” (orthên … doxan) by calling Mary Mother of God.15

So “right glory” is not what the word meant to those who made it and first used it. Nothing turned up here shows a Greek writer explaining orthodoxia as “right glorification.” The gloss “right worship” goes a step further still. The lexica consulted here give doxa as glory, honour, praise and splendour, not as worship. Mileant’s “doxa (teaching or worship)” matches neither of the word’s real senses exactly.

The steelman, weighed. The softer claim is Ware’s “double meaning,” and it deserves a fair hearing. It does not say the coiners meant “glory.” It says Greek-speaking Christians, for whom doxa mostly meant glory, could not help hearing both. That is a fair point about how a word sounds. A word can gather associations its parts suggest, and preachers are entitled to use them. But it is a point about overtone, not meaning. The two pieces of Greek evidence for it show less than they seem to:

  • Metrophanes’s “orthodoxly glorify” joins the two words because they mean different things. The adverb says how the hymn glorifies: as right belief requires, one Godhead in three hypostases. The University of Athens commentator reads it the same way. Glorifying must be done “orthodoxly,” for “heretical lips cannot hymn God as is fitting.”6 If orthodoxôs already meant “with right glory,” the line would say “we glorify with right glory.”
  • The Synodikon makes right worship part of the content of the Orthodox faith. It does not make it the meaning of the word. Its refrain is “This is the Faith of the Orthodox,” and the acclamation runs “thus we declare, thus we assert, thus we proclaim.” It confesses a faith that includes how to worship.

The Slavonic calque. Pravoslavie is real, but it proves less than Freeman asks of it.

  • Church Slavonic translators built learned words part by part from Greek ones. Slavonic Scripture renders biblical doxa by slava (“Slava vŭ vyšĭnichŭ Bogu,” Luke 2:14), so a part-by-part rendering of ortho-doxia would give pravo-slavie whatever the translator thought the compound meant. A calque shows how the parts were matched, not how the whole was understood.
  • The other calque was common too. Hilarion of Kiev’s Sermon on Law and Grace (1037–1050), with the Confession of faith copied with it, uses pravověr- (“right-believing”) three times: Vladimir built his church “on a right-believing foundation,” Hilarion prays that “right belief” (pravověrie) be glorified in the land, and he confesses the seven councils “of the right-believing holy fathers.” Pravoslavĭn- appears once, of the apostles who taught the nations “the orthodox faith.” The text survives in a copy of the later fifteenth century, so the exact spellings may not be Hilarion’s. Its modern Russian translation turns every one of these into pravoslavnyi, which shows how completely the “glory” calque later took over.16 The Slavs who first received the word therefore called the faith both “right belief” and “right glory.”
  • No evidence was offered, and none was found here, that Cyril and Methodius coined pravoslavie. Even if they did, a ninth-century translation tells us how ninth-century Greeks matched the word’s parts. It cannot tell us what the compound meant to Eusebius.

The objection is not partisan. The plainest rejection of the strong form comes from an Orthodox priest. Archimandrite Sergius of Sofia, writing for the Sunday of Orthodoxy in 1998, grants that the Slavonic word “does not convey precisely the meaning of the Greek word.” Since “the primary meaning of doxa is thought or opinion,” orthodoxia “is properly translated as right thinking or right opinion, not true glory, as the Slavonic would suggest.”17 A Reformed critic, the Orthodox Presbyterian Church’s question-and-answer page (2005), reaches the same result from the standard English dictionaries. It then uses the result for a polemical point of its own, that worship depends on doctrine, which the lexical evidence does not settle.18

What is overstated in the advocates’ statements.

  • “Literally means right worship” (Mileant) is not correct. The word literally means right opinion. “Right glory” is a later overtone, and “worship” is not a sense of doxa at all.
  • Ware’s “double meaning” is the defensible form, read as a claim about how the word sounds to Christian ears. Read as a claim about what the word means, it puts a pun on a level with the meaning.
  • Freeman’s inference from the Slavonic to how Greeks “heard” the word runs backwards. The Greek usage is directly attested and means belief. The Slavonic calque is indirect and mechanical, and it competed with “right belief.”

A separate question: is “right worship” a fair reading of Orthodoxy?

That the word does not mean “right worship” does not make the theology wrong. For the Church that bears the name, right belief has always been bound up with right worship, and this is the claim the etymology is usually made to carry.

The early sources tie the two together. Eusebius’s first proof of Origen’s “orthodoxy in the faith” is about prayer: living in the same house as the heretic Paul of Antioch, the boy “could never be induced to join with him in prayer,” for he “held … the rule of the Church.”14 The fourth-century fights over the Trinity were fought over the doxology and the baptismal formula, as the timeline’s Basil and Athanasius entries show. The feast called “Orthodoxy” celebrates the restoration of a form of worship. Metrophanes’s canon says that only right belief glorifies God rightly. So the double sense is a fair theological summary: Orthodox belief includes right worship, and Orthodox worship is meant to express right belief. On this wiki that relation is weighed on its own pages, under which way the adage runs.

But the word cannot be evidence for that theology. The argument “the very name means right worship, so the faith is worship-first” borrows its premise from an overtone. The historical record runs the other way. Orthodoxia began as a word for right opinion, and the Church filled it with a faith that included worship. The theology can stand on that record, and it does not need the etymology.

Assessment

weak — the claim that “Orthodoxy” means “right worship” does not hold on its best case. The word was formed from doxa as “opinion.” Its pre-Christian uses, its Christian opposite heterodoxia, and its uses in Clement, Eusebius and the fourth-century Fathers all mean right belief. Nothing found here shows a Greek writer glossing it as right glorification. What the claim has going for it is real but secondary. In Christian Greek doxa mostly means glory, so the echo was audible. Byzantine hymns set “orthodoxly” beside “glorify.” The Slavic churches use a calque meaning “right glory,” though early Rus’ also said “right belief.” Ware’s “double meaning” is fair as a description of overtone and homiletic resonance, but not as the meaning of the word. “Literally right worship” is a less probable reading. The separate theological claim, that Orthodoxy as the Church holds it embraces right worship and not only right doctrine, is well grounded in the sources. It does not depend on the etymology and gains nothing from it.
