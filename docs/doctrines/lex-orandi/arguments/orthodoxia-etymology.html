Does the word “Orthodoxy” mean “right glory” or “right worship”?

Disputed reading · on Lex orandi, lex credendi

The passage

The word “Orthodoxy” has the double meaning of “right belief” and “right glory” (or “right worship”).

— Timothy (Kallistos) Ware, The Orthodox Church (1963), introduction.1

The question

The Greek doxa can mean “opinion” or “glory,” so ortho-doxia can be parsed as “right opinion” or “right glory.” Writers differ on whether the name carries “right glory” or “right worship” as part of its meaning, which would make the word lex orandi in miniature, or means right belief, with glory as an overtone. Apart from the lexica and Robert Kraft’s study cited below, the literature on both sides located here is catechetical or pastoral rather than academic.

The word means both right belief and right glory

On this reading the name of the Orthodox faith carries a double sense: teaching the true belief about God and glorifying Him with right worship.

Held by: Timothy (Kallistos) Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, 1963); Alexander Mileant (Orthodox, Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia, leaflet); Stephen Freeman (Orthodox, Glory to God for All Things, 2021).2

Ware draws the conclusion on his opening pages: the Orthodox regard their Church as the one that teaches the true belief about God and glorifies Him with right worship. Mileant states the strong form, that the word “literally means right teaching or right worship,” from orthos and doxa “(teaching or worship).”

In Christian Greek doxa means glory. The Septuagint used it for the Hebrew kabod, and Abbott-Smith’s lexicon notes that in the New Testament it is “always good opinion,” hence praise, honour and glory, and then the radiant glory of God’s presence.3 The liturgy is full of it: the doxology, Doxa Patri, the Great Doxology. Byzantine hymns put the two words together: the ninth-century Trinitarian canon of Metrophanes of Smyrna in the Parakletike has all born of earth “orthodoxly glorify (orthodoxôs … doxazomen) one Godhead in three hypostases of equal operation,” and its refrain asks the Trinity to “save those who glorify you in faith.”4 The Sunday of Orthodoxy keeps the restoration of the icons in 843, the Triumph of Orthodoxy; its Synodikon proclaims Christ and honours His saints “in words, in writings, in thoughts, in sacrifices, in churches, in holy icons,” and ends, “This is the Faith of the Orthodox.”5 Eusebius’s first proof of Origen’s “orthodoxy in the faith” is that, living in the same house as Paul of Antioch, whom Eusebius counts a heretic, the boy “could never be induced to join with him in prayer.”6

The Slavic churches call the faith pravoslavie, from pravo-, “right,” and slava, “glory,” a calque of the Greek word.7 Freeman argues that Cyril and Methodius rendered orthodoxia as pravoslavie, and that “how they rendered that word into Slavonic tells us much about how they heard it in their native Greek.”8 The English Wikipedia article on doxa, citing Ware, glosses pravoslavie as “true belief,” “true way of worship.”9

The word means right opinion or right belief

On this reading orthodoxia was formed from doxa in its sense of “opinion,” and its Greek uses mean right belief.

Held by: Robert A. Kraft (University of Pennsylvania, “The Development of the Concept of ‘Orthodoxy’ in Early Christianity,” 1975); Archimandrite Sergius of Sofia (Orthodox, Orthodox Tradition, 1998); the Orthodox Presbyterian Church’s question-and-answer page (Reformed, 2005).10,11

Kraft: orthodoxia, “which means ‘correct belief’ or ‘right opinion,’” occurs neither in the New Testament nor in the Greek Jewish scriptures and is frequent from the fourth century. The verb is pre-Christian. Aristotle writes that virtue teaches “right opinion (tou orthodoxein) about the first principle,” and Liddell–Scott–Jones gives the verb as “have a right opinion” and the noun as “right opinion,” orthodoxos as “orthodox in religion” only from imperial law and Christian inscriptions.12 Behind these stands the philosophers’ orthê doxa, which Plato set between ignorance and knowledge, from the sense of doxa that belongs to dokein, “to think, to seem.”13

The Christian uses run the same way. About 110 Ignatius warns against “strange doctrines” (heterodoxiais) and those “of a different opinion” (heterodoxountas); the pair ortho-/hetero- takes one sense of doxa.14 Clement of Alexandria (c. 200) writes of “those who are called orthodox (orthodoxastai)” who do good “knowing not what they do,” right opinion set against knowledge.15 Eusebius trusts Irenaeus and Clement as men “who have maintained the orthodoxy of the Church” and rejects apocryphal gospels as “out of accord with true orthodoxy.” Sergius collects the fourth-century company of the word, “the orthodox faith,” “right doctrines,” and Cyril of Alexandria’s “right opinion” (orthên … doxan) to Nestorius.16

Sergius holds that the Slavonic word “does not convey precisely the meaning of the Greek word”: since “the primary meaning of doxa is thought or opinion,” orthodoxia “is properly translated as right thinking or right opinion, not true glory.”17 Early Rus’ also used the other calque. Hilarion of Kiev’s Sermon on Law and Grace (1037–1050), in a fifteenth-century copy, says pravověr- (“right-believing”) three times and pravoslavĭn- once; its modern Russian translation renders all of these as pravoslavnyi.18 The OPC page reaches “right belief” from the standard English dictionaries and concludes that worship depends on doctrine.
