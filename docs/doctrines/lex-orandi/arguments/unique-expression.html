Are the post-conciliar books the only expression of the Roman Rite’s lex orandi?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as law the claim stands on firm ground. Popes have imposed a single Missal before, and Roman teaching since 1947 reserves the ordering of the liturgy to papal authority. As a reading of the adage it is newer and harder. Prosper’s “law of supplication” was the Church’s apostolic and universal practice, not the book currently in force, and the older Missal is still prayed with the Church’s permission. Whether the two Missals carry one faith or two visions of the Church is the centre of the dispute, and the history of the adage does not settle it. · advanced by Pope Francis (2021), Cardinal Arthur Roche and Andrea Grillo

Related arguments: the Roman Rite’s lex orandi — see also Did the older Roman Missal remain an expression of the Church’s lex orandi?

The claim

Art. 1. The liturgical books promulgated by Saint Paul VI and Saint John Paul II, in conformity with the decrees of Vatican Council II, are the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite.

— Francis, Traditionis Custodes (16 July 2021), art. 1, link.1 The claim is that the Roman Rite has one law of prayer, and that the reformed books of 1969–2002 express it. The older Missal of 1962 is not part of that law, though its use may be permitted. The Latin unica expressio means “the only expression”; the official English “unique” is softer. The claim rejects Benedict XVI’s 2007 settlement, under which the two Missals were the ordinary and extraordinary expressions of one lex orandi. That rival position is weighed on its own page.

Who advances it

Pope Francis advanced it in Traditionis Custodes and its accompanying letter (2021), and again in Desiderio desideravi (2022), where he called the question “primarily ecclesiological.” The Congregation for Divine Worship’s Responsa ad dubia (December 2021) repeated it.2 Cardinal Arthur Roche, prefect of the dicastery, defended it in interviews. In 2022 he said that “the Church throughout the centuries, has always regulated the form of liturgy that it has come to believe is more pertinent for a particular age,” and that “there has never before been two versions of the Roman Missal.”3 Among scholars its best-known defender is the liturgical theologian Andrea Grillo of the Pontifical Athenaeum Sant’Anselmo in Rome. The day after the document appeared he wrote that it “radically supplants the bold sophism on which SP rested: namely the ‘parallel co-validity’ of two ritual forms, one of which contradicted the other.” In his view, re-establishing “one single form of the Roman rite in force” is “the only horizon on which peace can be built.”4

The evidence for

Authority over the rite. Since Mediator Dei (1947), Roman teaching has held that the lex credendi governs the lex orandi “in absolute and general terms,” and that “the Sovereign Pontiff alone enjoys the right to recognize and establish any practice touching the worship of God, to introduce and approve new rites, as also to modify those he judges to require modification.” The Second Vatican Council repeated that regulation of the liturgy “depends solely on the authority of the Church,” and the Council of Trent had already claimed the power to change the rites, “their substance being untouched.”5 On this view the lex orandi of a rite is the worship the Church prescribes for it. A pope can say which books those are.

One Missal for the sake of unity. The precedent Francis cited is real. After Trent, Pius V’s Quo primum (1570) imposed one Missal on the Latin Church and abolished rites that could not show two hundred years of use. Paul VI presented his own Missal in 1969 in the same terms, so that “one unique prayer will rise as an acceptable offering,” and in 1976 he said it was promulgated “to take the place of the old.”6 The nineteenth century gives a precedent from the other side of the modern dispute. Prosper Guéranger, often called the father of the liturgical movement, campaigned for decades to have the diocesan liturgies of France replaced by the one Roman rite, and he did so in the name of the lex orandi itself.

The adage cuts for unity. If the law of prayer establishes the law of belief, two forms of prayer used side by side can come to carry two beliefs. That was Francis’s reading of the 2020 consultation of bishops. He found that the older Missal was often used to reject “not only … the liturgical reform, but … the Vatican Council II itself,” and he saw the reformed liturgy as “intimately joined to the vision of Church” of Lumen gentium.7 Benedict had argued that the two forms would “in no way lead to a division in the Church’s lex credendi”; on this view the experience of 2007–2021 showed otherwise. A critic of the reform supports a related premise from the other side. Peter Kwasniewski argues that a liturgy which “has lost 83% of its euchological material is no longer the same rite as its predecessor.”8 If he is right, then “two forms of one rite” was never an accurate description, which is what Grillo also says.

Antiquity is not decisive. Mediator Dei itself warns that “ancient usage must not be esteemed more suitable and proper … on the simple ground that it carries the savor and aroma of antiquity,” and that more recent rites “too, owe their inspiration to the Holy Spirit.”9

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a legal act the claim is well grounded in Catholic terms. The Church’s own documents from Mediator Dei to the Catechism give the pope authority over the Roman liturgy. There is precedent for imposing one Missal, and the older Missal’s critics and defenders agree that the 2007 construction of “one rite in two forms” was unstable. The argument from unity also uses the adage in a way the history supports. From Prosper to Guéranger it was invoked for what the whole Church prays in common.

The adage was about received practice, not books in force. Prosper’s “law of supplication” was the intercession “handed down by the Apostles” and “uniformly celebrated in the whole world and in every Catholic Church.”10 Its force came from being ancient and universal, not from being prescribed. Guéranger called the liturgy “tradition itself at its highest degree of power,” and the Catechism (1992) calls it “a constitutive element of the holy and living Tradition.” To make lex orandi the legal status of whichever books are now prescribed is a new use of the phrase. Kwasniewski’s objection is that the document “fumblingly makes ‘lex orandi’ do duty as a juridical, canonical term,” when “in reality, the lex orandi is the whole complex of historical prayer texts, ceremonies, and music that make up the Roman Rite.”11 The objection has to be scoped, though. It was Summorum Pontificum in 2007, not Traditionis Custodes, that first used the adage as a legal category. In 2021 Francis kept Benedict’s vocabulary and changed its answer.

The Pius V precedent cuts both ways. Quo primum abolished rites of less than two hundred years’ standing, and it explicitly spared the older ones. The Missal restricted in 2021 is the descendant of Pius V’s own, and its Canon is the one Trent declared “so pure from every error.” The precedent shows that a pope may impose one Missal. It does not show that a pope has set aside a Missal of that age before. Guéranger’s campaign is the same: the diocesan books he wanted gone were eighteenth-century compositions, and the rite he restored was the ancient one. Defenders reply that the reformed Missal keeps the Roman Canon and, in Francis’s words, “all the elements of the Roman Rite.”12 On that view the continuity Pius V protected is preserved in the reformed books.

“Only” while still in use. Traditionis Custodes names one Missal as the “only” expression of the Roman Rite’s law of prayer, yet it still lets the other be celebrated, with the bishop’s permission. Joseph Shaw argues that the article is “incomprehensible because it is self-contradictory”: “What, then, can it mean to say that the Roman Rite has only one law of prayer, and that this is the one expressed in a particular Missal, and not in another, in a document which allows both to be used in the Church?” Anthony Ruff’s answer is that the 1962 Missal reflects the Roman Rite’s lex orandi “to the extent that it reflects the Church’s liturgy as found in the 1970 Missal.”13 That reading is coherent. It makes the reformed books the standard by which the older one is measured, which fits Mediator Dei’s reversal of the adage better than Prosper’s original sense.

Many rites, one faith. The Second Vatican Council declared “all lawfully acknowledged rites to be of equal right and dignity,” and the liturgist Paul De Clerck argued that the adage can only be applied responsibly by comparing the liturgical families.14 The Church’s lex orandi has always had many expressions at once. This does not refute the claim, which is about the Roman Rite alone. It does mean that a single expression is not something the adage itself requires. The case for one Roman Missal rests on unity and authority, not on the logic of lex orandi.

Overstatements in the defence. In 2023 Cardinal Roche was reported as saying that “the theology of the Church has changed.”15 Taken literally, that concedes the critics’ charge that the two Missals carry different beliefs, which the official documents do not say. They speak of the Council’s vision of the Church and of unity, and they never call the older Missal erroneous. Roche’s further claim that there had never been two versions of the Roman Missal in use is accurate only for the Missal; the Latin Church had several rites and uses side by side before 1570, and the Ambrosian and other rites still stand beside it. The Catechism also limits the authority the claim relies on: “Even the supreme authority in the Church may not change the liturgy arbitrarily, but only in the obedience of faith and with religious respect for the mystery of the liturgy.”16 Critics read the 2021 restrictions as just that kind of arbitrary change. Defenders point to the consultation and the Council’s mandate. History cannot settle which description is right.

Assessment

contested — Two different claims are combined here. The legal claim is sound by Catholic standards: the pope may prescribe the books of the Roman Rite, and Pius V, Paul VI and Guéranger’s campaign all show the Church preferring one Missal for the sake of unity. The theological claim is the contested one. It says that the law of prayer of the Roman Rite simply is the reformed books, and that a Missal used for four centuries, still celebrated with permission, is not part of it. That fits Mediator Dei’s rule that belief governs prayer. It sits poorly with Prosper’s sense, in which the law of prayer is what has been handed down and prayed everywhere. Whether the two Missals express one faith, as both 2007 and 2021 officially hold, or two visions of the Church, as both Grillo and the most committed traditionalists suspect, is the real question. It divides informed Catholics, and the adage’s history gives each side part of what it needs.
