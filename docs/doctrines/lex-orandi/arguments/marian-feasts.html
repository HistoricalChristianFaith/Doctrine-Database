Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption?

Type: historical-thesis argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — as a claim that the feasts were a real cause of the two definitions, and not just evidence cited after the fact. In both cases the feast came first. In both, the feast carried the belief into the Church’s public prayer for centuries, and both bulls argue from it. For the Immaculate Conception the order is especially clear: the feast was kept before anyone argued for the doctrine, and the doctrine was first argued in the feast’s defence. But neither feast fixed its own meaning. The Conception feast was kept for centuries by people who denied the doctrine. Its object was settled by scholastic theology and by papal decrees, and the standard Catholic reference says the feast became a feast of the Immaculate Conception only as theological argument advanced. The bodily Assumption came into the feast of 15 August from the apocryphal narratives of Mary’s death, and Rome’s oldest Mass for the day does not mention it. Pius XII himself denied that the liturgy “engenders” the faith. The feasts set the question and spread the belief, but they did not decide either doctrine. · advanced by Paul De Clerck; argued from by Pius IX and Pius XII; answered by Bernard of Clairvaux, Thomas Aquinas, and Orthodox and Anglican writers

The claim

On ne s’étonnera donc pas que les deux exemples majeurs de sa force heuristique soient deux définitions dogmatiques des deux derniers siècles, celle de l’Immaculée Conception par le pape Pie IX en 1854, et celle de l’Assomption de la Vierge Marie par le pape Pie XII en 1950.

— Paul De Clerck, “Lex orandi, lex credendi: un principe heuristique” (2000). (“So it is no surprise that the two major examples of [the adage’s] heuristic force are two dogmatic definitions of the last two centuries: the Immaculate Conception, by Pope Pius IX in 1854, and the Assumption of the Virgin Mary, by Pope Pius XII in 1950.”)1 The claim is that the two modern Marian dogmas are lex orandi at work. The feasts of Mary’s Conception (8/9 December) and of her Dormition or Assumption (15 August) spread first, and the doctrine followed the prayer. The question weighed here is whether the liturgy actually drove the definitions, or was only cited for them afterwards.

Who advances it

The definitions themselves argue from the liturgy. Ineffabilis Deus (1854) has a section headed “Liturgical Argument.” It also recalls that the popes allowed the Immaculate Conception to be proclaimed “in the Litany of Loreto, and in the Preface of the Mass, so that the rule of prayer might thus serve to illustrate the rule of belief.” Munificentissimus Deus (1950) gives four paragraphs (§§16–19) to the “solemn liturgical offices” of the feast, East and West, and quotes Mediator Dei: the liturgy “can supply proofs and testimonies of no small value for deciding a particular point of Christian doctrine.”2 De Clerck, the liturgist whose reading of Prosper is set out on the timeline, calls the two definitions the adage’s leading examples. In the Anglican–Roman Catholic dialogue, Catholics answered Anglican doubts about the two definitions by pointing to “the sensus fidelium, the liturgical tradition throughout the local churches, and the active support of the Roman Catholic bishops.” A 2025 doctrinal note of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith puts the general case for Mary: in the first millennium “the lex orandi, which developed into the lex credendi, shaped Eastern Mariology.”3 The historian’s version of the thesis, without the dogmatic claim, is Edmund Bishop’s. He called the Conception feast a case in which the growth of a belief “may be observed and examined under peculiarly, I may indeed say uniquely, favourable circumstances”: “first in the observance of piety in the eleventh century, and in the twelfth with a growing consciousness of the theological implications of the case; then at the end of the thirteenth century speculative theology in a scholastic form deduces a definite doctrine, no part of the communis sensus of the Schools … but eventually penetrating and dominating, so that in our own day the dogma is solemnly proclaimed as of revealed truth.”4

The evidence for

The Immaculate Conception: the feast came first. A feast of Mary’s conception was kept in the monasteries of Palestine by the late seventh century; its oldest witness is the canon of Andrew of Crete. It reached southern England in the early eleventh century, was suppressed after the Norman Conquest, and was revived in the 1120s.5 Only then did anyone argue for the doctrine. Eadmer’s treatise (c. 1125), the first sustained case that Mary was preserved from original sin at her conception, was written in the setting of that revival. When the canons of Lyons took up the feast about 1140, Bernard rebuked them because a feast commits the Church to something. To keep the day was to call the conception holy, and that he denied.6 By Aquinas’s time the argument from the feast was standard enough to appear as an objection: “no feast is celebrated except of some saint. But some keep the feast of the Conception of the Blessed Virgin. Therefore it seems that in her very Conception she was holy.”7 Rome then took up the feast long before it took up the doctrine. Sixtus IV approved a Mass and Office in 1476 and in 1483 forbade either side to call the other heretical, since the question “was not yet decided.” The feast was raised in rank in 1693 and made a holy day of obligation for the whole Church in 1708, 146 years before the definition.8 Ineffabilis Deus puts this “Liturgical Argument” first among its proofs, ahead of the Fathers and Scripture. By keeping the feast, the Church “made it clear indeed that the conception of Mary is to be venerated as something extraordinary, wonderful, eminently holy, and different from the conception of all other human beings — for the Church celebrates only the feast days of the saints.”9

The Assumption: the feast carried the belief. A “Memory of Mary” was kept in Jerusalem on 15 August by the fifth century. It became a feast of her death, and the Emperor Maurice is credited with fixing it on that day across the empire around 600.10 The Greek homilies that state the bodily assumption were preached for this feast. John of Thessalonica (c. 615) wrote his to bring the feast into a church that did not yet keep it.11 At Rome, Sergius I (687–701) gave the day a stational procession. The sacramentary Hadrian I sent to Charlemagne has a prayer for the feast, the Veneranda, which says that the Mother of God “suffered temporal death, but still could not be kept down by the bonds of death.” Leo IV added a vigil and an octave in 847.12 Bernard learned the doctrine from the feast in just the way the thesis describes: “I have received then from the Church that day to be reverenced with the highest veneration, when being taken up from this sinful earth, she made entry into the heavens.”13 So in both cases the order of events is the one the thesis needs: first the feast, then theology, then definition.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The chronology is not in doubt, and the feasts did more than decorate the definitions. They put each question to the Church. Bernard and Aquinas both had to explain why a feast did not mean what it seemed to mean. Over centuries the feasts made each belief a matter of public prayer in every diocese. That mattered when Pius IX (1849) and Pius XII (1946) polled the bishops on what their clergy and people believed. A belief celebrated every year as a holy day is a different thing from a theologians’ opinion. In that sense the feasts were a real cause, and the thesis is right against anyone who treats the liturgical sections of the bulls as decoration.

(1) The Conception feast did not fix its own meaning. The feast was kept for centuries by people who denied the doctrine. Aquinas answered the objection from the feast by saying that “the celebration of this feast does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception”: since no one knows when she was sanctified, “the feast of her Sanctification, rather than the feast of her Conception, is kept on the day of her conception.” The Franciscans adopted the feast for their whole order in 1263, and the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that this “does not mean that they professed at that time the doctrine.” The Dominicans kept it as the “Sanctification” of Mary until Gregory XV abolished that title in 1622.14 The same reference describes the feast’s development the other way round from the thesis: “Originally the Church celebrated only the Feast of the Conception of Mary … not discussing the sinlessness. This feast in the course of centuries became the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, as dogmatical argumentation brought about precise and correct ideas, and as the thesis of the theological schools regarding the preservation of Mary from all stain of original sin gained strength.” It adds that before 1854 the words Immaculata Conceptio are “nowhere found in the liturgical books, except in the invitatorium of the Votive Office of the Conception.” The turning point was Scotus’s answer to the objection from Christ’s universal redemption: “From the time of Scotus not only did the doctrine become the common opinion at the universities, but the feast spread widely to those countries where it had not been previously adopted.” On that account the feast’s spread followed the doctrine’s rise.15 Ineffabilis Deus says the same about its own predecessors. Because “whatever pertains to sacred worship is intimately connected with its object and cannot have either consistency or durability if this object is vague or uncertain,” the popes set out “to enunciate the exact doctrine.” They condemned the view that the Church honoured Mary’s “sanctification” rather than her conception, and Alexander VII (1661) declared that the feast was kept in the sense of her preservation “in the first instant.” Here belief fixed the meaning of prayer. The Preface the bull mentions was added so that prayer would “illustrate” a belief already held.16 Geoffrey Wainwright read the bull’s phrase lex credendi ipsa supplicandi lege statueretur the same way. The pope, he wrote, “gave a new twist to Prosper’s dictum: a new lex orandi is seen by Pius to have been deliberately introduced by his papal predecessors in order to promote (that would seem to be the meaning here of establish) a particular doctrine.”17

(2) The Assumption feast took its content from the apocrypha. The bodily assumption did not come out of the feast. It came into the feast from the Transitus narratives of Mary’s death, which the wiki weighs as a documentary foundation and rates weak. The Catholic Encyclopedia says plainly that the belief “is founded on the apocryphal treatise De Obitu S. Dominae.” It reports Ferdinand Probst’s argument that Rome’s oldest Mass for 15 August, in the Gelasian Sacramentary, “does not mention the corporeal assumption of Mary.” On that argument the Gregorian Mass that replaced it came from Gaul, where the belief, “under the influence of the apocryphal writings, is older … than in Rome.” The Anglican–Roman Catholic commission likewise says the feast “was influenced by legendary narratives of the end of Mary’s life already widely in circulation.”18 The Latin West then kept the feast for centuries without settling the doctrine. The sermon read at the Assumption Office was Paschasius Radbertus’s Cogitis me, circulating as Jerome’s, and it leaves the bodily assumption open: “many of our people doubt whether she was assumed with the body or left the body behind,” and “it is more fitting to hold an opinion with a pious desire than to define unadvisedly what is not known.”19 Pius XII drew the conclusion himself. Right after his liturgical paragraphs he wrote that “the liturgy of the Church does not engender the Catholic faith, but rather springs from it,” so the Fathers “did not draw their teaching from the feast itself as from a primary source, but rather they spoke of this doctrine as something already known and accepted.”20 That is a disclaimer from the definition’s own author. It fits the Western record better than the thesis does.

(3) By De Clerck’s own test, the Conception feast does not carry the doctrine. De Clerck argues that the adage can no longer be applied responsibly without comparative liturgy. A text found “here or there” is not enough; the usage has to be checked across the liturgical families.21 Applied to the Conception, that test tells against the thesis. The East, where the feast began, still keeps 9 December as the “Conception of St Anne” and rejects the Roman dogma. The Catholic Encyclopedia explains why this is consistent: the Greeks “did not think it absurd to celebrate a conception which was not immaculate, as we see from the Feast of the Conception of St. John,” and their object “was less the purity of the conception than the holiness and heavenly mission of the person conceived.” Whether the feast’s hymns nevertheless imply the doctrine is weighed on the all-holy vocabulary argument (contested).22 The Church of England is a second case. From 1561 its calendar, reproduced in the 1662 Book of Common Prayer, kept the “Conception of Mary” while dropping the Assumption, and that church has never taught the Immaculate Conception as doctrine.23 In three traditions, then, the same day was kept with three different beliefs about it. The Assumption does better on this test. East and West both keep 15 August, and the Byzantine texts Pius XII quotes say plainly that God “has kept your body incorrupt in the tomb and has glorified it by his divine act of transferring it from the tomb.” The Eastern name “Dormition” still leaves open what the 1950 definition left open, whether she died first.24

(4) The definitions rest formally on consensus, and the feasts are evidence for it. Neither pope defined from a feast. Pius IX first asked the bishops, in Ubi primum (1849), about “the devotion which animates your clergy and your people” and their own judgement. Pius XII asked the same two questions in 1946 and called the near-unanimous answer, “the universal agreement of the Church’s ordinary teaching authority,” “a certain and firm proof.” The liturgy appears in his text as one of the “testimonies, indications and signs of this common belief.”25 The critic can answer that the consensus was itself shaped by feasts that Rome had promoted for centuries, and Ineffabilis Deus lists that promotion with pride: indulgences, patronages, a holy day, and a Preface. That answer has force, but it changes the thesis. What it shows is papal teaching using the liturgy to spread a belief, which is belief shaping prayer. A further caution comes from ecumenical work on Mary: the Groupe des Dombes held that the adage “should not be applied to popular piety but to the official liturgical prayer of the church.” Both feasts began in monastic and popular devotion, “prompted by popular piety and gradually adopted by local churches.”26

Assessment

Plausible. The feasts were a real cause of the 1854 and 1950 definitions, not merely evidence gathered for them afterwards. That much is sound. In both cases the feast came first. It raised the question and carried the belief into the Church’s public prayer for centuries, and both bulls argue from it. The thesis is strongest for the Immaculate Conception, where the feast was kept before the doctrine was argued and the doctrine was first argued in its defence. It is weaker for the Assumption, whose content came into the feast from the apocryphal Transitus narratives, and whose Latin observance sat for centuries with an open question about the body. But the stronger claim, that the liturgy by itself drove the Church to these doctrines, does not hold. In neither case did the feast settle its own meaning. The Conception feast was kept by those who denied the doctrine, in the East, by the Dominicans, and later in the Church of England. Its object was fixed by Scotist theology and papal decree, and the standard Catholic reference describes the feast changing as the theology advanced. Pius XII expressly denied that the liturgy engenders the faith. The fairest summary is that the feasts set the question and spread the belief, while theology, narrative and papal teaching decided what the feasts meant. That is lex orandi and lex credendi working in both directions, which is how Bernard and Aquinas already argued in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. For the adage’s history see the timeline. For the doctrines see the Immaculate Conception timeline and its late-development argument, and the Assumption timeline and its argument on whether its tradition traces to the apostles or the apocrypha.
