Did the Marian feasts lead to the definitions of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950)?

Disputed reading · on Lex orandi, lex credendi · witnesses: Bernard of Clairvaux, Thomas Aquinas, Pius XII

The passage

On ne s’étonnera donc pas que les deux exemples majeurs de sa force heuristique soient deux définitions dogmatiques des deux derniers siècles, celle de l’Immaculée Conception par le pape Pie IX en 1854, et celle de l’Assomption de la Vierge Marie par le pape Pie XII en 1950.

— Paul De Clerck, “Lex orandi, lex credendi: un principe heuristique” (2000). (“So it is no surprise that the two major examples of [the adage’s] heuristic force are two dogmatic definitions of the last two centuries: the Immaculate Conception, by Pope Pius IX in 1854, and the Assumption of the Virgin Mary, by Pope Pius XII in 1950.”)1

The question

The feasts of Mary’s Conception (8/9 December) and of her Dormition or Assumption (15 August) were kept for centuries before Pius IX defined the Immaculate Conception in 1854 and Pius XII the Assumption in 1950, and both bulls argue from them. The Orthodox keep both feasts; Kallistos Ware writes that Orthodoxy, “while for the most part denying the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception of Mary, firmly believes in her Bodily Assumption,” which it has not proclaimed as a dogma.2 Writers differ on whether the feasts led to the two definitions, on whether theology, narrative and papal teaching settled what the feasts meant, and on whether the feasts carry a faith that is held without a definition. The question bears on how the adage worked in its two best-known modern uses.

The feasts came first and carried the belief to definition

On this reading prayer led belief: each feast spread before its doctrine was argued or defined, and the definitions rest on what the Church had long celebrated.

Held by: Pius IX (Catholic, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854); Edmund Bishop (Catholic liturgical historian, 1886, note of 1918); Paul De Clerck (Catholic, La Maison-Dieu, 2000); the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith (Catholic, Mater Populi fidelis, 2025).3,4,5

Ineffabilis Deus puts its “Liturgical Argument” first among its proofs: by keeping the feast the Church “made it clear indeed that the conception of Mary is to be venerated as something extraordinary, wonderful, eminently holy, and different from the conception of all other human beings — for the Church celebrates only the feast days of the saints.”6 A feast of Mary’s conception was kept in Palestinian monasteries by the late seventh century, its oldest witness the canon of Andrew of Crete, and reached southern England in the early eleventh century.7 Eadmer’s treatise (c. 1125), the earliest extant sustained case that Mary was preserved from original sin at her conception, was written in its revival, and by Aquinas’s time the argument from the feast was a standard objection.8,9 The feast became a holy day for the whole Latin Church in 1708.10 Bishop called it a case in which the growth of a belief “may be observed and examined under peculiarly, I may indeed say uniquely, favourable circumstances”: “first in the observance of piety in the eleventh century,” then theology, “so that in our own day the dogma is solemnly proclaimed as of revealed truth.”11

The Assumption followed the same order. A “Memory of Mary” was kept in Jerusalem on 15 August by the fifth century, and Maurice is credited with fixing the feast across the empire about 600;12 the Greek homilies that state the bodily assumption were preached for it.13 Rome’s Veneranda prayer says the Mother of God “suffered temporal death, but still could not be kept down by the bonds of death.”14 The Dicastery’s note puts the general case: in the first millennium “the lex orandi, which developed into the lex credendi, shaped Eastern Mariology.”15

Theology and papal teaching fixed what the feasts meant

On this reading the feasts raised the questions and spread the beliefs, but their meaning was settled by theology, by the narratives of Mary’s death and by papal decree, and the definitions rest formally on the consent of the bishops.

Held by: Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907–1910); Pius XII (Catholic, Munificentissimus Deus, 1950; he also argues from the liturgy); Geoffrey Wainwright (Methodist, Doxology, 1980); the Anglican–Roman Catholic International Commission (Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ, 2005, on the Assumption).16

The Conception feast was kept by people who denied the doctrine. Aquinas held that “the celebration of this feast does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception,” and the Dominicans kept it as Mary’s “Sanctification” until 1622.17 Holweck describes the feast that “in the course of centuries became the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, as dogmatical argumentation brought about precise and correct ideas.”18 Ineffabilis Deus says that worship “cannot have either consistency or durability if this object is vague or uncertain,” so the popes set out “to enunciate the exact doctrine,” Alexander VII (1661) fixing the feast’s sense.19 Wainwright reads its phrase lex credendi ipsa supplicandi lege statueretur as “a new twist to Prosper’s dictum: a new lex orandi is seen by Pius to have been deliberately introduced by his papal predecessors in order to promote (that would seem to be the meaning here of establish) a particular doctrine.”20

For the Assumption, Holweck writes that the belief “is founded on the apocryphal treatise De Obitu S. Dominae,” and ARCIC that the feast “was influenced by legendary narratives of the end of Mary’s life already widely in circulation.”21 The Latin Office read Paschasius Radbertus’s Cogitis me: “it is more fitting to hold an opinion with a pious desire than to define unadvisedly what is not known.”22 Both popes first polled the bishops; Pius XII called their agreement “a certain and firm proof,” the liturgy among the “testimonies, indications and signs of this common belief,”23 and he stated the order of the two in general terms: “the liturgy of the Church does not engender the Catholic faith, but rather springs from it.”24

The feasts hold the Orthodox faith about the Theotokos without a dogmatic definition

On this reading, put by Orthodox theologians, the hymns of both feasts carry the faith and praise of the Mother of God within the Church’s worship and tradition, without a dogmatic definition, and the feast of Mary’s conception does not carry the 1854 dogma.

Held by: Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, “Panagia,” c. 1949); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, 1963).25

Lossky holds that in teaching about the Mother of God “it is therefore impossible to separate dogmatic data, in the strict sense, from the data of the Church’s cultus”: “devotion should enrich dogma with the Church’s living experience.”26 Ware puts it in the adage’s own words: the inner Tradition “is preserved above all in the Church’s worship. Lex orandi lex credendi: men’s faith is expressed in their prayer.”27 Belief in the Assumption “is clearly and unambiguously affirmed in the hymns sung by the Church on 15 August,” “But Orthodoxy, unlike Rome, has never proclaimed the Assumption as a dogma, nor would it ever wish to do so”: its glorification of Our Lady “belongs to the Church’s inner Tradition.” Ware quotes Lossky: “It is not so much an object of faith as a foundation of our hope, a fruit of faith, ripened in Tradition.”28

For the conception, Lossky sets Mary beside John the Baptist, “whose conception and birth are also feasts of the Church”: she “was born under the law of original sin,” yet “sin never could become actual in her person.”29 Ware writes that the Orthodox Church “has never in fact made any formal and definitive pronouncement on the matter,” but “since 1854 the great majority of Orthodox have rejected the doctrine,” feeling that “at any rate as defined by the Roman Catholic Church, it implies a false understanding of original sin.”30 Lev Gillet notes that Orthodox writers “multiplied expressions such as ‘all holy’, ‘all pure’, ‘immaculate’” “to describe Mary’s incomparable holiness,” and records Patriarch Euthymios of Constantinople (d. 917) preaching “on the conception of St Anne (that is to say, on Mary’s conception by Anne and Joachim).”31 On this reading the feasts keep the Orthodox Church’s praise of the Theotokos, held in its worship and tradition without a definition.
