Who wrote the “Capitula Caelestini,” and under whose authority were they issued?

Disputed reading · on Lex orandi, lex credendi · witness: Prosper of Aquitaine

Related readings: What did “the law of supplicating” mean in the Capitula?

The passage

Hence a kind of intimate relationship between dogma and sacred liturgy, and likewise between Christian worship and the sanctification of the people. Therefore, Celestine I proposed and expressed a canon of faith in the venerated formulas of the Liturgy: “Let the law of supplication establish the law of believing. For when the leaders of holy peoples administer legislation enjoined upon themselves they plead the cause of the human race before divine Clemency, and they beg and pray while the entire Church sighs with them.”

— Pius XI, apostolic constitution Divini cultus (20 December 1928), trans. R. J. Deferrari, link.1

But besides these hallowed ordinances of the most blessed and Apostolic See, in accordance with which the most pious Fathers, after casting aside the pride of pernicious novelty, have taught us to refer to Christ's grace both the beginnings of good will, and the advances in commendable devotions and the perseverance in these unto the end, let us be mindful also of the sacraments of priestly public prayer, which handed down by the Apostles are uniformly celebrated in the whole world and in every Catholic Church, in order that the law of supplication may support the law of believing.

— Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei (the Indiculus or “Capitula Caelestini”), ch. 8, trans. R. J. Deferrari, The Sources of Catholic Dogma, no. 139.2

The question

The dossier in which the clause ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi first appears was copied from about 500 directly after Pope Celestine I’s genuine letter Apostolici verba (431) to the Gallic bishops, and was long cited as his. Writers differ on whether Celestine wrote it, whether it was drafted by another hand and issued under his authority, or whether it is a later compilation by Prosper of Aquitaine; the answer bears on whether the clause was first published as a papal ruling or as a theologian’s argument. Pierre Coustant (1721) lists writers from Johann Wendelstein’s edition of the canons (Mainz, 1525) onward who had departed from the Celestine attribution.3 Modern academic literature defending Celestine’s own authorship was not located; the first reading below is held in the manuscript tradition, by medieval canonists and theologians, and in later papal and reference use.

The dossier is Pope Celestine’s, sent with his letter to the Gallic bishops

On this reading the chapters are part of Celestine’s letter of 431, and the clause on the law of supplicating is a pope’s.

Held by: Dionysius Exiguus (collection of papal decretals, c. 500); the Scythian monks in Rome (letter to Fulgentius, c. 520); Hincmar of Reims (De praedestinatione, 859–860); Pius XI (Catholic, Divini cultus, 1928).4

The text never travelled alone. In every early collection the auctoritates follow Apostolici verba directly, and Coustant could find no copy of either piece edited separately: from the start of the sixth century “the African bishops and then Dionysius Exiguus and countless others” held both to be one writing of Celestine’s.5 The letter bears on the same quarrel. Prosper and Hilary had gone to Rome to complain of the Gallic critics of Augustine, and Celestine rebuked those critics and praised Augustine as one whom “even my predecessors” counted among the best teachers.

The attribution is early. Around 519–520 the Scythian monks (Peter the Deacon and his companions), writing to the African bishops exiled in Sardinia, Fulgentius of Ruspe among them, quoted chapter 8 under the name of “the most blessed Celestine in his letter to the Gauls,” from “when the leaders of the holy peoples” through the list of intercessions, and introduced its conclusion with “the same master closes this very letter thus.”6 Dionysius divided letter and dossier into one run of titles under “the decrees of Pope Celestine,” and the later collections and medieval theologians followed him. Hincmar quotes chapter 8’s clause eight times as “holy Celestine’s,” and Thomas Aquinas cites chapter 9 as “Pope Celestine” (see Hincmar and Aquinas).7

The dossier was received in Rome as authoritative. In 520 Pope Hormisdas told the African bishop Possessor that the Roman Church’s teaching on grace could be learned from Augustine’s works, “but the prominent chapters are contained in the ecclesiastical archives,” which Coustant reports “the learned” took to be the chapters of the Indiculus.8 In the nineteenth century Prosper Guéranger built his liturgical campaign on the axiom “promulgated by the great Pope Saint Celestine,” and Pius XI’s Divini cultus cites the passage as Celestine’s letter in Migne.9

Drafted by Celestine’s deacon Leo and issued with Celestine’s authority

On this reading another hand drafted the chapters, but they went out from Rome in Celestine’s time and under his name, as papal letters regularly did.

Held by: Pasquier Quesnel (edition of Leo’s works, Dissertation 3, 1675), as quoted by Coustant.10

Quesnel held that the future Leo the Great compiled the Indiculus “not after he held the pontificate, but at the time when, serving only as a deacon, he assisted Pope Celestine as a minister of ecclesiastical affairs.” Papal letters were commonly written by others and sent in the pope’s name, so a dossier drafted by a deacon in the papal service and issued with the pope’s letter carries the pope’s authority, and the authorship question in the narrow sense gives way to whether the text left Rome under Celestine’s name and with his approval. Coustant noted that on this view “what would prevent the same Indiculus from being ascribed to Celestine?”11

A later compilation by Prosper of Aquitaine, attached to Celestine’s letter

On this reading Prosper gathered the dossier after Celestine’s death in 432, and it came to be copied as part of the letter it followed.

Held by: Pierre Coustant (Catholic, Maurist, Epistolae Romanorum Pontificum, 1721); Marc Cappuyns (Catholic, Benedictine, Revue bénédictine, 1929); Paul De Clerck (Catholic, La Maison-Dieu, 2000); O. T. I. Wright (Oxford Journal of Law and Religion, 2024).12

Coustant set out the case from the text itself. Popes speak of “my predecessors,” as Celestine does in the very letter to which the dossier is attached; the compiler speaks of the Roman see “as of another’s, not as of his own,” and titles his work “the authorities of the past bishops of the Apostolic See.”13 Apostolici verba says nothing of any appendix, and in every copy of the Dionysian and Hadrianic collections a separate heading opens the dossier with Incipiunt, the word copyists used to mark a new piece.14 Prosper’s Against the Conferencer, written after Celestine died in July 432 (it calls Sixtus “now Pontiff”), reaches back to Innocent and Zosimus to prove that good will begins with God; listing Celestine’s services against the Pelagians, it names no ruling on the beginning of faith.15 For Coustant this showed that the dossier was in no way drawn up “by Celestine’s authority or command,” against Quesnel’s version as well.16

The positive case for Prosper is the dossier’s agreement “in language and thought” with his other writings, its reliance on the same testimonies of Innocent, Zosimus and the African councils that he deploys against Cassian, and its purpose, to defend Augustine with the Apostolic See’s authority, which was the charge Prosper says Rome had given him.17 Cappuyns dated the dossier 435–442 and Alexander Hwang 450–455; Wright reports that the chapters are now “without exception” accepted as Prosper’s, and De Clerck calls Prosper “very probably” their author.18 How the dossier came to be joined to the letter is left open on this reading.19 Later Catholic documents name no author or name Prosper: Mediator Dei (1947) cites the sentence as De gratia Dei “Indiculus,” and the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) gives it to “Prosper of Aquitaine [5th cent.].”20
