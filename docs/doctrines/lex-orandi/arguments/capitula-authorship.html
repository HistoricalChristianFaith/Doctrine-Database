Did Pope Celestine I write the “Capitula Caelestini”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the attribution is ancient, but it rests entirely on the text’s having been copied after Celestine’s letter of 431. The text’s own voice, the letter’s silence about it, and Prosper’s writings after Celestine’s death all tell against it. Scholars had abandoned it long before Cappuyns (1929) settled the authorship on Prosper. · advanced by the manuscript tradition (from c. 500) and by Pius XI, Divini cultus (1928)

The claim

Hence a kind of intimate relationship between dogma and sacred liturgy, and likewise between Christian worship and the sanctification of the people. Therefore, Celestine I proposed and expressed a canon of faith in the venerated formulas of the Liturgy: “Let the law of supplication establish the law of believing. For when the leaders of holy peoples administer legislation enjoined upon themselves they plead the cause of the human race before divine Clemency, and they beg and pray while the entire Church sighs with them.”

— Pius XI, apostolic constitution Divini cultus (20 December 1928), trans. R. J. Deferrari, link.1 The claim is that the dossier in which the adage first appears was written by Pope Celestine I (422–432), and so the sentence ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi is a pope’s. The text is the Praeteritorum Sedis Apostolicae episcoporum auctoritates de gratia Dei, also called the Indiculus or “Capitula Caelestini.” A softer form of the claim has the text drafted by someone else but issued with Celestine’s authority, which would also make it his in the sense that counts. Whether the text carried papal authority whoever wrote it is a separate question, taken up in its own section below.

Who advances it

For about a thousand years the attribution was simply the received one. Dionysius Exiguus’s collection of papal decretals (c. 500) divided Celestine’s letter to the Gallic bishops and the appended auctoritates into one run of titles under “the decrees of Pope Celestine.” Cresconius’s Breviarium, the Spanish collection and Pseudo-Isidore followed. So did a line of Carolingian and later theologians: Florus of Lyon, Hincmar of Reims, Lupus of Ferrières, Remigius of Lyon and Ivo of Chartres.2 In the modern period the attribution survives mainly in reference works and in passing citations. Pius XI’s Divini cultus (1928, quoted above) credits the sentence to Celestine and cites Celestine’s letter in Migne. John Hardon’s Modern Catholic Dictionary defines lex orandi as “the axiom of Pope St. Celestine I (422–32).” The English Wikipedia article on the adage says the passage was “quoted and ratified by Pope Celestine I in epistle 21.”3 No modern scholar has been found who defends Celestine as the author.

The evidence for

The text never travelled alone. In every early collection the auctoritates follow Celestine’s letter Apostolici verba (431) directly. Its eighteenth-century editor, the Maurist Pierre Coustant, could find no copy of either piece edited separately.4 The letter itself is genuine and does bear on the same quarrel. Prosper and Hilary had gone to Rome to complain about the Gallic critics of Augustine. Celestine rebuked those critics and praised Augustine as one whom “even my predecessors” counted among the best teachers.

The attribution is early. Around 519–520 the Scythian monks in Rome (Peter the Deacon and his companions) wrote to the African bishops exiled in Sardinia, Fulgentius of Ruspe among them. They quoted chapter 8 under the name of “the most blessed Celestine in his letter to the Gauls,” from “when the leaders of the holy peoples” through the list of intercessions. They quoted the conclusion too, introducing its last sentence with “the same master closes this very letter thus.” So within a lifetime of its writing, readers in Rome took letter and dossier as one papal text.5 They used it, moreover, as an argument from prayer. In the same chapter they appealed to Basil’s anaphora, “which almost the whole East uses,” as proof that God makes the wicked good.

The steelman: Celestine’s authority, a deacon’s pen. The strongest version of the claim is Pasquier Quesnel’s (1675). He held that the future Leo the Great drafted the Indiculus while still a deacon serving Celestine. As Coustant pointed out, that would make it Celestine’s after all. A secretary’s draft issued by a pope is the pope’s, just as papal letters were written by others and sent in the pope’s name.6 On this version authorship in the narrow sense hardly matters. The only question is whether the text left Rome under Celestine’s name and with his approval.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The attribution is not a modern invention or a careless slip. It goes back to the early sixth century, to Roman readers and to the canonists who built the Church’s law books. Its cause is also plain: a compiler’s appendix, attached to a genuine papal letter on the same subject, was copied as part of that letter.

The text’s own voice. Coustant set out the case against the attribution in 1721. Popes speak of “my predecessors,” as Celestine does in the very letter to which the dossier is attached. The compiler speaks of the Roman see as someone else’s. He calls its popes “the rulers of the Roman Church” and “the most sacred See of the Blessed Apostle Peter,” and he says we had to look into what they judged.7 His title, “the authorities of the past bishops of the Apostolic See,” reads like the work of someone gathering what popes had already decided, not of a pope deciding.

The letter’s silence, and the manuscripts’ seam. When popes attached documents to a letter, they normally said so in the letter. Apostolici verba says nothing of any appendix. The manuscripts also mark the join. In every copy of the Dionysian and Hadrianic collections a separate heading opens the dossier with Incipiunt (“here begin”), the word copyists used to mark the start of a new piece.8

Prosper’s own testimony after Celestine’s death. This is the decisive argument. Prosper’s Against the Conferencer, his reply to John Cassian, was written after Celestine died in July 432: it calls Sixtus “now Pontiff.” To prove that good will begins with God, Prosper reaches back to Innocent and Zosimus. When he lists Celestine’s services against the Pelagians, he names the expulsion of Caelestius, the mission to Britain, the condemnation of Nestorius and the letter praising Augustine. He mentions no ruling on the beginning of faith. He then hopes that what God did through “Innocent, Zosimus, Boniface, Celestine” He will do through Sixtus. And he closes by bearing with Augustine’s critics “until the Lord, through the princes of the Church and the lawful ministers of His judgements,” settles what they have disturbed.9 Had Celestine sent Gaul a set of chapters ending in “we do not deem that to be at all Catholic which appears to be contrary to the views presented above,” his greatest admirer would have cited them, not waited for a future ruling. This argument also defeats Quesnel’s softer version. Coustant’s conclusion was that the text was in no way drawn up “by Celestine’s authority or command.” Cardinal Noris added a weaker supporting point. Gallic bishops such as Faustus of Riez went on teaching against these rules without being treated as defying a papal sentence.10

The positive case for Prosper. The dossier matches Prosper’s other writings in style and thought. It rests on the same testimonies of Innocent, Zosimus and the African councils that he deploys at length against Cassian, as if compressing that book. Its purpose, to defend Augustine with Rome’s authority, was the task Prosper says Rome had given him. It also shares the argument from the Church’s intercessions with his Call of All Nations.11 Marc Cappuyns’s study of 1929 made the case in full. He dated the dossier 435–442, after Celestine’s death. Alexander Hwang has since argued for 450–455, which puts it later still. O. T. I. Wright (2024) reports that it is now “without exception” accepted as Prosper’s. Paul De Clerck (2000), more cautiously, calls Prosper “very probably” its author.12 Both datings rule out Celestine. The case against him does not depend on Cappuyns either, since the internal arguments above were already made in the seventeenth century.

A long-settled question. Doubts about the attribution are not a twentieth-century novelty. Coustant lists those who had already departed from it. They include Johann Wendelstein’s edition of the canons (Mainz, 1525), the council editors Crabbe, Surius, Nicolini and Labbe, Baronius’s Annales (at the year 431), the Jesuit Francisco Suárez, Jacques Sirmond, Gerardus Vossius, Ellies Dupin and Jacques-Hyacinthe Serry, along with the special studies of Quesnel and Noris.13 These are Catholic and Protestant writers alike, and Jesuits as well as Augustinians, so the doubt is not one party’s. By 1908 the Catholic Encyclopedia stated plainly that the “Capitula Coelestini” “are no longer attributed to his authorship.”14

What is overstated or imprecise in the modern statements.

  • Divini cultus (1928) follows the traditional heading and cites the passage as Celestine’s letter (PL 50:535). In 1928 that was already an outdated attribution, but a pope quoting a familiar text under its familiar name is not deciding a question of authorship. Pius XII’s Mediator Dei (1947) cites the same sentence only as De gratia Dei “Indiculus,” with no author. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) gives it to “Prosper of Aquitaine [5th cent.].”15
  • Hardon’s dictionary also misquotes the sentence as “Legem credendi statuit lex orandi.” The indicative statuit (“has established”) and orandi are the later slogan’s wording. Prosper wrote the subjunctive statuat and supplicandi.
  • Wikipedia’s “quoted and ratified by Pope Celestine I in epistle 21” reverses the relation. Celestine’s letter does not quote the dossier. The dossier was attached to the letter. On every modern dating it was also written after Celestine had died, so he could not have ratified it.

A separate question: did the text carry papal authority anyway?

That Celestine did not write the dossier does not settle what authority it had. Two things need to be kept apart.

Its reception as Roman teaching is real. Joseph Pohle, writing in 1912 when the text was thought later than it now is, judged it “certain that about A.D. 500 this work was recognized as the official expression of the views of the Apostolic See.” The Dionysian collection put it among the papal decrees, and the Scythian monks quoted it in Rome as a pope’s.16 In 520 Pope Hormisdas told the African bishop Possessor that the Roman Church’s teaching on grace and free will could be learned from Augustine’s works, “but the prominent chapters are contained in the ecclesiastical archives.” Coustant reported that “the learned” took these to be the chapters of the Indiculus. The identification is likely, but Hormisdas does not name the text.17 Coustant also suggested how the dossier came to be joined to the letter. Prosper may have compiled it to win Sixtus III’s approval and then fused it with Celestine’s letter himself. Hwang has since made the same suggestion, though Wright regards Hwang’s account as “overly simplistic.”18 How the text came to circulate under Celestine’s name is therefore an open question, not an established forgery. The Roman reception, though, is plain. The Catholic documents that later used the sentence went on using it after dropping Celestine’s name, as Mediator Dei and the Catechism do. Its standing in that tradition never depended on the attribution.

But chapter 8 is not a decree. The papal material in the dossier is chapters 1–6, the excerpts from Innocent and Zosimus. Chapter 8 deliberately turns away from them: “besides these hallowed ordinances of the most blessed and Apostolic See … let us be mindful also” of the Church’s prayers. Its force is that of an argument, from prayers that are “handed down by the Apostles” and “uniformly celebrated in the whole world.” It is not the force of a ruling. Making the sentence a pope’s therefore misdescribes it, whoever signed the dossier. In the Capitula, Rome’s authority is in chapters 1–7, and chapter 8 stands on the Church’s universal prayer. That is the sense of “law” weighed on the timeline’s Prosper page, not a papal enactment.

Assessment

weak — Celestine as author, or as the authority under whom the dossier was issued, does not hold even on its best case. All the evidence for it comes from the text’s transmission: a compiler’s appendix copied as part of the genuine letter it followed. The evidence against comes from the text itself, from the letter’s silence, from the scribes’ own marking of a new piece, and above all from Prosper’s writing after Celestine’s death, which knows of no Celestinian ruling on the question. Scholars of every party had dropped the attribution by the early eighteenth century, and Prosper’s authorship is now the settled view. Citing the adage as “Pope Celestine’s” follows a sixth-century heading, not the evidence. The separate claim, that the dossier was received in Rome as authoritative from about 500, is well grounded. But it attaches to the dossier as a whole, and it does not turn chapter 8’s argument from prayer into a papal decree.
