Does the law of belief govern the law of prayer?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as a description of what churches do, it holds. From the African councils of 393 and 407 to the Church of England’s canons, every tradition surveyed here checks its prayers against its faith. Augustine, whose argument the formula compresses, says prayers were “corrected every day” when found contrary to the Catholic faith. As the relation between faith and liturgy “in absolute and general terms,” as Pius XII put it, it is contested. It reverses Prosper’s sentence, and Pius kept Prosper’s direction for disputed points. In the decisive cases the belief that judges the prayer was itself partly learned in worship. Which belief governs is a further dispute: doctrine formed by Scripture for the Reformers, the teaching authority for Pius. · advanced by the bishop of Orléans (1847), Pius XII (1947, 1950) and, in practice, the Reformers

Related arguments: which way the adage runs — see also Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief? · Do prayer and belief shape each other, with neither simply prior?

The claim

The sacred liturgy, consequently, does not decide or determine independently and of itself what is of Catholic faith. More properly, since the liturgy is also a profession of eternal truths, and subject, as such, to the supreme teaching authority of the Church, it can supply proofs and testimony, quite clearly, of no little value, towards the determination of a particular point of Christian doctrine. But if one desires to differentiate and describe the relationship between faith and the sacred liturgy in absolute and general terms, it is perfectly correct to say, “Lex credendi legem statuat supplicandi” — let the rule of belief determine the rule of prayer.

— Pius XII, Mediator Dei (1947), §48, source.1 The claim weighed here is that the adage, taken as a general rule, runs from belief to prayer. The liturgy professes a faith already held. It can witness to that faith, but it does not found it, and where a prayer and the faith disagree, the prayer is corrected. Three years later, defining the Assumption, Pius put it more sharply: “the liturgy of the Church does not engender the Catholic faith, but rather springs from it, in such a way that the practices of the sacred worship proceed from the faith as the fruit comes from the tree.”2

Who advances it

The inverted formula, legem deprecandi statuat lex credendi, was first proposed in 1847 by Jean-Jacques Fayet, bishop of Orléans, against Guéranger, who refused it.3 Pius XII adopted it in Mediator Dei and drew the consequence. The pope alone may “introduce and approve new rites, as also … modify those he judges to require modification.”4 John Paul II repeated it in 1988: the lex orandi “must always be in conformity with the Lex credendi.”5 The Reformers did not use the adage, but they acted on its reversal. Luther purified the Mass by a doctrine he took as proven and cut “everything that smacks of sacrifice, together with the entire canon.”6 Geoffrey Wainwright generalizes: “it was the policy of the Reformers to establish doctrinal control over worship, and the critical primacy of doctrine in relation to liturgy has remained characteristic of Protestantism.” A modern Lutheran writer puts it in the adage’s terms: “The church’s lex orandi flows out of the lex credendi.”7

The evidence for

Prayer was checked against the faith in the formula’s own century

For the prayers of many are corrected every day on being recited to men of greater learning, and many things are found in them contrary to the Catholic faith.

— Augustine, On Baptism, against the Donatists VI.25.47 (c. 400), source.8 The man whose argument Prosper compressed knew that most prayer was answerable to belief. The African councils made it law. Hippo (393) ruled that at the altar “the prayer be directed always to the father,” and that prayers copied from elsewhere be discussed first “with the better-educated brothers.” Carthage (407) allowed only prayers approved in synod, “and that others contrary to the faith should not be used by any means.”9 The principle is older still. Tertullian wrote that “our Lord Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom,” and Cyprian that “custom without truth is the antiquity of error.”10

Rites do not read themselves

In the Arian dispute both sides shared the rite. Philostorgius, the Arian historian, reports that the Arians “were nevertheless accustomed to communicate with [the Catholics] in prayers, hymns, and deliberations, and in everything except the mystic sacrifice.”11 Athanasius had to add that baptism requires, “with the Name, … also the right faith.”12 And the prayer itself was changed to fit the belief. Philostorgius says that Flavian of Antioch was the first to have a band of monks sing aloud “Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Ghost,” where the form “more customarily received” had been “Glory be to the Father through the Son in the Holy Ghost.”13 Theodoret describes Leontius, the Arian bishop of Antioch, facing a congregation split between the two forms: “He himself offered all the doxology in silence, and all that those standing near him could hear was the ‘For ever and ever.’”14 The doxology became a party badge because the parties’ beliefs reshaped it. Basil, accused of innovation over his own doxology, defended it from the faith: “As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.” The confession is “the origin and parent of the doxology.”15

A new feast must first be justified by the faith

When Lyons adopted a feast of Mary’s conception, Bernard called it “a rite which the Church knows nothing of, and which reason does not prove, nor ancient tradition hand down to us.”16 Aquinas held that the feast, which Rome “tolerates” in some churches, “does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception.” The meaning of the feast was fixed by doctrine.17 Even in the bull defining the Assumption, Pius XII denied that the Fathers “draw their teaching from the feast itself as from a primary source.” Whether the Marian feasts nonetheless led to the definitions is weighed at Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption? (plausible).18

Every reform has run this way

In the sixteenth century a revised belief rewrote the rite. Cranmer’s Defence of 1550 named transubstantiation, the real presence and the priestly sacrifice as “the roots of the weeds,” and his 1552 Book changed every point of the 1549 rite on which a Catholic reading had rested (Cranmer).19 The Catholic response ran the same way. Trent first defined the sacrifice from Scripture and tradition and then declared the Roman Canon “pure from every error.”20 The Church of England still requires that any new service be “neither contrary to, nor indicative of any departure from, the doctrine of the Church of England in any essential matter.”21 Guéranger, the strongest modern champion of the opposite direction, described the same mechanism in his theory of heresy. Every sectary who wants a new doctrine must change the books, and Lutheranism, Calvinism and Anglicanism needed only “the substitution of new books and new formulas for the old.”22 Nicholas Jesson sums up: “the notion that theological reflection determines the worship patterns of the community is not difficult to accept.”23

Adversarial assessment

It reverses Prosper, and Pius knew it

Prosper’s sentence makes the law of supplicating the subject of statuat. Pius XII did not claim otherwise. In the same paragraph he cites Prosper’s direction, “let the rule for prayer determine the rule of belief,” for the settling of disputed doctrines, and he gives the reversal as a separate and more general rule. As a reading of the adage, then, the reversal is not Prosper’s. Aidan Kavanagh says that to reverse it “is to cancel out the meaning of the maxim in its original formulation.”24 That does not refute the reversal as a theological claim, which Pius offered on its own terms. It does mean that the claim cannot borrow the authority of the fifth-century text, which says the opposite for the case it addresses.

The belief that governs was partly learned in worship

The cases where doctrine was new are the hardest for the claim. Prayer and hymns to Jesus came long before any defined doctrine of his divinity, and later writers argued from them to it (see Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity?, plausible). Basil’s chain begins not with a creed but with the font: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief.” The Council of Constantinople (381) confessed the Spirit as the one “who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified,” taking the creed’s wording from the doxology.25 And Prosper’s intercessions settled a question on which the bishops of Gaul were divided. Where the belief was already settled, belief judged the prayer. Where it was not, the prayer often helped to settle it. That is a real limit on the claim that belief governs “in absolute and general terms.”

The Marian definitions run in a circle

The Catholic use of the reversal has a further difficulty. Pius IX’s bull on the Immaculate Conception (1854) says that earlier popes promoted the feast so “that the law of belief itself might be established by the law of prayer.” Wainwright calls this “a new twist to Prosper’s dictum”: a new lex orandi deliberately introduced to promote a doctrine.26 Here a teaching authority shapes the prayer, and the prayer is then cited as evidence for the teaching. Jesson calls the argument “circular.” It shows belief governing prayer, but it also shows that the governed prayer is then used to establish belief. The relation is not one-way even in the documents that state the reversal.

Whose belief?

The claim has two forms that disagree with each other. For the Reformers the governing belief was doctrine formed by Scripture, and on that basis they set aside the Roman Canon. For Pius XII it is the faith as taught by the Church’s magisterium, and on that basis Trent defended the Canon. Jesson, after granting that doctrine shapes worship, adds: “The connection of this principle to papal authority is more difficult.”27 In the Church of England the governed prayer then became a standard of doctrine in its own right. Canon A5 names the Book of Common Prayer among the places where the Church’s doctrine “is to be found.”28 So the principle that belief governs prayer does not say which belief, or who decides. And once a rite has been reformed by a doctrine, it tends to become an authority for that doctrine in turn.

Assessment

contested — secure as a description of practice, disputed as the general relation. Every tradition on the timeline checks its prayers against its faith: the African councils of Augustine’s day, Bernard and Aquinas on a new feast, the Reformers and Trent, the Church of England’s canons, Rome since 1947. A rite cannot settle a dispute in which both parties share it, as the Arian controversy showed. To that extent belief does govern prayer, and the claim is sound.

As the relation “in absolute and general terms,” it is contested. It reverses Prosper’s sentence for the very case Prosper addressed, and Pius XII himself kept Prosper’s direction for disputed doctrines. Where a doctrine was new, the worship often came first: the worship of Jesus, the baptismal formula, the doxology that the creed of 381 adopted. The Marian definitions show belief and prayer feeding each other rather than one governing the other. The two forms of the claim also disagree about whose belief governs. Magisterial and Protestant theology hold it in their different forms, and liturgical theology rejects it. The rival readings are weighed at Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief? and Do prayer and belief shape each other, with neither simply prior?
