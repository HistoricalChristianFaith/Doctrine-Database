Did Prosper’s “law of supplicating” mean the apostle’s command to pray for all, not the liturgy at large?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — in its best form. Prosper’s lex is first of all the command of 1 Timothy 2:1 to pray for all people, as the whole Church obeys it in its intercessions. It is not “the liturgy” as a general source of doctrine. The Call of All Nations says so in as many words, and the Capitula echo the verse’s vocabulary. The strict form overstates. Chapter 9 argues the same way from the baptismal exorcisms, which no apostolic precept commands, and the Capitula themselves never quote the verse. What decides the question is the precept, the Church’s universal obedience, and the wording of the prayers taken together, not Scripture in place of the liturgy. · advanced by Karl Federer (1950), Paul De Clerck (1978, 2000) and Geoffrey Wainwright (1980)

The claim

… obsecrationum quoque sacerdotalium sacramenta respiciamus, quae ab apostolis tradita, in toto mundo atque in omni catholica Ecclesia uniformiter celebrantur, ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi.

— Prosper of Aquitaine, Capitula (the Indiculus), ch. 8, source.1 “Let us be mindful also of the sacred rites of the priestly supplications, which, handed down by the apostles, are celebrated uniformly in the whole world and in every catholic Church, so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.” The adage lex orandi, lex credendi was later cut from this clause. Today it is usually taken to mean that the liturgy as a whole is a source of doctrine: what the Church prays in any of its rites tells you what it believes.

The claim weighed here is that this was not Prosper’s meaning. On this reading his “law of supplicating” is a law in the plain sense: the apostle’s command, “I exhort therefore, first of all, that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men” (1 Tim 2:1). The Church obeys that command everywhere, in the intercessions for unbelievers, Jews, heretics and catechumens that chapter 8 lists, and those prayers ask God to give faith. So the beginning of faith is grace. The argument rests on one commanded, universal practice and on what its prayers ask for. It is not an appeal to the liturgy at large. Paul De Clerck, the reading’s chief modern advocate, put it this way:

A la suite de l’étude la plus fine de ce passage, due à Karl Federer, il faut reconnaître que la lex supplicationis ou la lex orandi ne désigne pas d’abord, dans l’esprit de Prosper, une loi liturgique, mais bien le précepte apostolique que l’on trouve en 1 Tm 2, 1. La lex orandi, qui tranche le débat sur la grâce, ce n’est pas d’abord la prière que l’Eglise fait pour que les infidèles viennent à la foi, mais le précepte apostolique, attribué à saint Paul, auquel l’Eglise universelle obéit de manière si unanime que la loi biblique est devenue loi liturgique.

— Paul De Clerck, “Lex orandi, lex credendi: un principe heuristique” (2000), p. 68, source.2 In English: following Federer’s study, “we must recognise that the lex supplicationis or lex orandi does not first of all designate, in Prosper’s mind, a liturgical law, but the apostolic precept found in 1 Tim 2:1.” What settles the dispute is “the apostolic precept … which the universal Church obeys so unanimously that the biblical law has become a liturgical law.”

Who advances it

Karl Federer’s Liturgie und Glaube (Freiburg, Switzerland, 1950) first made the case from the parallel passage in The Call of All Nations. Paul De Clerck set it out in “Lex orandi, lex credendi: sens originel et avatars historiques d’un adage équivoque” (1978; English 1994), the article that gave the debate its terms, and restated it in 2000. Geoffrey Wainwright’s Doxology (1980) takes the same line: “the apostolic injunction [1 Tim 2:1–4] to pray for the whole human race — which the church obeys in its intercessions — proves the obligation to believe” that faith is from start to finish a work of grace. Nicholas Jesson’s survey (2001) follows them, and reports that “many contemporary theologians believe that the common interpretation is incorrect.”3

The broad reading has users rather than exegetes. Dom Prosper Guéranger opened his Institutions liturgiques (1840) with the axiom and glossed it as “the Liturgy is tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity.” Pius XI’s Divini cultus (1928) says that “Celestine I held the canon of faith to be expressed in the venerable formulas of the liturgy.” Aidan Kavanagh (1984) built his “primary theology” of the worshipping assembly on Prosper’s verb. The Catechism (1992) quotes Prosper and adds that “the Church believes as she prays.” Each reads the clause as a general rule about the Church’s worship. None of them argues from Prosper’s text that this is what he meant. They are weighed below under “The broad reading.”4

The evidence for

Prosper’s own gloss calls the apostle’s precept a “law of supplication”

The Apostle commands—rather, the Lord speaking through the Apostle commands through him that supplications and intercessions, thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and for all that are in high station. All priests and all the faithful, adhere unanimously to this norm of supplication in their devotions. There is no part of the world in which Christian peoples do not offer up these prayers.

— Prosper, De vocatione omnium gentium I.12, trans. P. De Letter, source.5 This is the strongest single piece of evidence. De Letter’s “norm of supplication” renders legem supplicationis, and the antecedent of quam legem is the apostle’s command just quoted. A few lines earlier Prosper calls 1 Timothy 2:1–6 a “rule of the apostolic teaching” (doctrinae apostolicae regula) with which the whole Church is imbued. To know what it means, he says, ask how the universal Church understands it, for “there can be no doubt about what is enjoined, if the efforts made by all those who obey are the same.” The law here is the precept, and the Church’s worldwide obedience is what shows its meaning. This is the same author making the same argument against the same opponents within a few years. On Cappuyns’s dating the Capitula (435–442) come first, and on Hwang’s the two works are close in time.

The Capitula echo the verse’s vocabulary

The Latin of 1 Timothy 2:1 in the Vulgate reads Obsecro igitur primum omnium fieri obsecrationes, orationes, postulationes, gratiarum actiones, pro omnibus hominibus. Chapter 8 calls the rites it appeals to obsecrationum sacerdotalium sacramenta, “the rites of the priestly obsecrationes,” using the verse’s first word for prayer. It describes the bishops as performing “the embassy enjoined on them” (mandata sibimet legatione), and lists what they ask for: that faith be given to unbelievers, the veil lifted from the Jews’ hearts, “the light of truth” shown to them, heretics brought to “a comprehension of the Catholic faith.” That is the verse’s “for all men” spelled out, and its purpose clause (1 Tim 2:4), “who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth.” Verse 4 was the Massilians’ favourite text. Prosper answers it from the command that comes just before it.6

Before Prosper, the verse was already read as the Church’s rule of prayer

About half a century earlier the commentator known as Ambrosiaster wrote of 1 Timothy 2:1: “This is the ecclesiastical rule, handed down by the master of the Gentiles, which our priests use to supplicate for all” (haec regula ecclesiastica est tradita a magistro gentium, qua utuntur sacerdotes nostri, ut pro omnibus supplicent). The phrase joins the same three things as Prosper’s chapter 8: a rule, handed down by the apostle, carried out by the priests in supplication for all.7 Augustine, whom Prosper followed closely, had done the same in 414. Asked how Paul’s four words for prayer differ, he mapped them onto the parts of the Eucharist, “what the entire, or almost the entire, church observes.” He then pressed the verse’s “for all men” against anyone who thought the Church need not pray for its persecutors.8 So by Prosper’s time 1 Timothy 2:1 was read as both a command and a description of what the Church did at every Eucharist. That is exactly the fusion of “biblical law” and “liturgical law” the reading proposes.

Prosper’s criteria pick out particular rites, not the liturgy as a whole

Chapter 8 does not say that whatever the Church prays is a rule of faith. It names prayers with three marks: handed down by the apostles (ab apostolis tradita), celebrated in the whole world and in every catholic Church (in toto mundo atque in omni catholica Ecclesia), and celebrated uniformly (uniformiter). Those marks select a practice; they do not cover a local feast, a hymn or a preface. They are the marks an opponent who wanted only what Rome and the apostles had taught would have to accept. The argument then works from what those prayers ask for and from their answer, since God does convert pagans and heretics and the Church thanks Him for it. De Clerck calls this a structure of “three levels”: the command of 1 Timothy 2:1 understood as a law, the Church’s universal obedience to it, and the wording of the prayers. “It is the three coordinated levels that settle the doctrinal debate, and not the third taken in isolation.”9

Adversarial assessment

The Capitula never quote 1 Timothy

The reading draws its clearest evidence from The Call of All Nations, not from the sentence it interprets. Chapter 8 itself does not cite 1 Timothy 2:1. It grounds the prayers in apostolic tradition (ab apostolis tradita) and universal practice, and calls them sacramenta, sacred rites. Read on its own, the sentence makes the rites themselves the law. Nicholas Jesson draws the contrast with Augustine’s letter to Vitalis, whose argument is “a liturgical one, unlike Prosper’s which turns first to I Timothy.” That holds for The Call of All Nations. For the Capitula it is an inference, though a good one, given the echo of obsecrationes, the same author and the same opponents. The fair conclusion is that in chapter 8 the precept and the practice are not set against each other. The practice is the precept carried out, and Prosper did not need to choose between them.10

Chapter 9 argues from a rite that no precept commands

The next chapter runs the same argument from the exorcisms and breathings that every candidate for baptism undergoes, “which the holy Church uniformly does in the whole world.” No apostolic text commands those rites, though Prosper reads them through the Gospel images of the strong man bound and the prince of this world cast out. The conclusion then gathers both arguments as “ecclesiastical rules” (his ergo ecclesiasticis regulis), in the plural, set beside “the documents taken on divine authority.” So for Prosper the argument from universal practice reaches beyond the one commanded intercession. Wainwright noted that “doctrinal appeal is … made not only to the word-content of prayers but also to a complex ritual act.” O. T. I. Wright (2024) faults both Federer and De Clerck for leaving chapter 9 out when they define the adage.11

This defeats a strict form of the reading, that Prosper’s argument from prayer is confined to the intercessions. It does not defeat the best form. De Clerck himself treats chapter 9 as “a similar argument,” and the exorcisms meet the same tests as the intercessions: universal, uniform, received from the beginning. Chapter 9 adds a second rite to the one Prosper argues from. It does not make “the liturgy” as such a rule of faith. It does show that the clause is not about 1 Timothy alone. The criterion is a universal, apostolic practice, of which the commanded intercession is the clearest case.

Was Paul’s command a “law” at all?

De Clerck concedes a shift: the verb in 1 Timothy means “to recommend,” and it is Prosper’s reading that makes it a law. Wright objects from the other side. Paul’s parakalō is a real exhortation, not a recommendation later promoted, and Prosper’s lex carries the weight of Roman law. On his reading Prosper is acting rather like a jurist, giving a customary practice the binding form of law. Wright also notes that supplicandi is not the Vulgate’s word in 1 Timothy 2. Neither objection overturns the reading. Wright’s point strengthens the sense in which the precept is a law, and his legal reading still makes the lex a norm drawn from the Church’s practice rather than a licence to argue from any prayer. The word supplicandi is Prosper’s own, but obsecrationum in the same sentence is the verse’s.12

Augustine’s argument from prayer was meant to work without exegesis

And I wish that those who are slow and weak of heart, who cannot, or cannot as yet, understand the Scriptures or the explanations of them, would so hear or not hear our arguments in this question as to consider more carefully their prayers, which the Church has always used and will use, even from its beginnings until this age shall be completed.

— Augustine, On the Gift of Perseverance 23.63 (428/429), addressed to Prosper and Hilary, source.13 This is the best evidence for the broad side. In the letter Prosper himself received, Augustine sets the Church’s prayers beside the Scriptures as a separate witness, one even the unlearned can read. If Prosper took the argument from him, the force of the prayers would not depend on a proof-text. But the prayers Augustine names are the same ones, “when was not prayer made in the Church for unbelievers and its opponents that they should believe?” His point is that the practice is plain to everyone, not that any prayer whatever counts. And Augustine had read 1 Timothy 2:1 as that practice’s charter fourteen years earlier. The passage shows that the argument from prayer stood on the practice, not on exegesis alone. That is a correction to the strict reading, which makes Scripture rather than the liturgy decide. It is not evidence that Prosper meant the liturgy at large.

The broad reading

The broad reading holds that Prosper already appealed to the Church’s worldwide, uniform worship as an authority for doctrine, so that the later “the liturgy is a source of doctrine” is his principle, not a broadening of it. Its best evidence is the wording of chapter 8 taken alone: the authority it names is rites (sacramenta) handed down and universally celebrated, not a verse. Chapter 9 extends the argument to a second rite, and Augustine set the Church’s prayer beside Scripture as a witness in its own right. All of this is real, and it is enough to defeat the strict narrow reading set out above.

It does not reach the claim that Prosper meant the liturgy at large. His three marks of apostolic origin, universality and uniformity restrict the argument; they do not extend it. Both rites he argues from are ones his opponents could not deny were universal, and he argues from each to one doctrinal point, the priority of grace. Nothing in either text appeals to the prayers of a particular rite, to the Church’s feasts, or to the liturgy as a body of texts. The modern advocates of the broad sense do not argue it from Prosper. Guéranger and Pius XI attributed the text to Celestine and quoted it as an axiom. Kavanagh’s case concerns the direction of the verb, not the scope of the noun. The Catechism cites the clause in support of a general statement about the sacraments. They are using the adage in its later sense, which is a legitimate development with its own history on the timeline. It is not a rival reading of what Prosper meant, so it is weighed here rather than on a page of its own. De Clerck himself objected only to what he called the “banal” use, in which “taking account of the lex orandi” means no more than citing a prayer, a preface or a hymn. He did not object to arguing from the liturgy. His own proposal was to apply the principle across all the rites by comparative liturgy, so that only a practice shared by all of them, or nearly all, counts.14

Which way the adage runs, from prayer to belief or from belief to prayer, is a separate question from what its lex meant. Kavanagh and Pius XII disagree on direction while both use the adage in the broad sense.

Assessment

sound — in its best form, the one De Clerck himself gives. Prosper’s “law of supplicating” is first of all the apostle’s command to pray for all, as the whole Church obeys it in its intercessions. That is what The Call of All Nations says outright, what the Capitula’s vocabulary echoes, and what Ambrosiaster and Augustine had already made of 1 Timothy 2:1. The argument rests on a universal, apostolic practice and on what its prayers ask for. It is not a claim that the liturgy as such is a source of doctrine, and that later sense is a broadening of Prosper’s clause, not its original meaning. Two overstatements should be dropped. Prosper does not confine the argument to the intercessions, because chapter 9 makes the same argument from the baptismal exorcisms. And he does not set Scripture against the liturgy, because in chapter 8 the practice is the precept carried out, and it is the practice that he cites. Read that way, Prosper argues from what the whole Church has always done, under the apostle’s command, and not from any one text or from the liturgy at large.
