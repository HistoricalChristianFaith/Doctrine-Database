What did Prosper’s “law of supplicating” mean in the Capitula?

Disputed reading · on Lex orandi, lex credendi · witness: Prosper of Aquitaine

Related readings: Who wrote the “Capitula Caelestini,” and under whose authority were they issued?

The passage

… obsecrationum quoque sacerdotalium sacramenta respiciamus, quae ab apostolis tradita, in toto mundo atque in omni catholica Ecclesia uniformiter celebrantur, ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi.

— Prosper of Aquitaine, Capitula (the Indiculus), ch. 8, source.1 “Let us be mindful also of the sacred rites of the priestly supplications, which, handed down by the apostles, are celebrated uniformly in the whole world and in every catholic Church, so that the law of supplicating may establish the law of believing.”

The question

The adage lex orandi, lex credendi was later cut from this clause. Writers differ on what Prosper’s lex supplicandi named: the Church’s apostolic and universal worship as such, the apostle’s command to pray “for all men” (1 Tim 2:1) as the Church obeys it, or a customary practice given the binding force of law. The answer bears on whether the later use of the adage, in which the liturgy at large is a source of doctrine, restates Prosper or extends him. Which way the adage runs, from prayer to belief or from belief to prayer, is a separate question, treated on the timeline.

The Church’s apostolic and universal worship as a rule of faith

On this reading Prosper appeals to the rites themselves, because they come from the apostles and are celebrated everywhere, as an authority for doctrine.

Held by: Prosper Guéranger (Catholic, Institutions liturgiques, 1840); Pius XI (Catholic, Divini cultus, 1928); Joris Geldhof (KU Leuven, Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Religion, 2015); Jakob K. Rinderknecht (Pray Tell, 2018).2

The authority chapter 8 names is rites, sacramenta, marked by three things: handed down by the apostles (ab apostolis tradita), celebrated in the whole world and in every catholic Church, and celebrated uniformly. The sentence does not quote a verse. Guéranger glossed the axiom: “the Liturgy is tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity,” and Pius XI wrote that “Celestine I held the canon of faith to be expressed in the venerable formulas of the liturgy.”3 Geldhof states the point of the passage: “the rightness of a theological position was additionally justified by the content of the prayers actually used in liturgy.” For Rinderknecht, “for Prosper, liturgy is a source for reflection because it is apostolic and universal,” two characteristics that make it “an unimpeachable guide for theological reflection.”4

The next chapter runs the same argument from a second rite, the exorcisms and breathings that every candidate for baptism undergoes, “which the holy Church uniformly does in the whole world,” and the conclusion gathers both as “ecclesiastical rules” (his ergo ecclesiasticis regulis), in the plural, beside “the documents taken on divine authority.”5 The argument from prayer was Augustine’s before it was Prosper’s, and in the treatise Prosper received from him Augustine set the Church’s prayers beside the Scriptures as a witness even the unlearned can read:

And I wish that those who are slow and weak of heart, who cannot, or cannot as yet, understand the Scriptures or the explanations of them, would so hear or not hear our arguments in this question as to consider more carefully their prayers, which the Church has always used and will use, even from its beginnings until this age shall be completed.

— Augustine, On the Gift of Perseverance 23.63 (428/429), addressed to Prosper and Hilary, source.6 On this reading the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) states Prosper’s sense when it quotes him and adds, “the Church believes as she prays.”7

The apostle’s command to pray for all, as the whole Church obeys it

On this reading the “law” is first of all the precept of 1 Timothy 2:1, and the argument rests on the Church’s universal obedience to it and on what its intercessions ask God to give.

Held by: Karl Federer (Catholic, Liturgie und Glaube, 1950); Paul De Clerck (Catholic, Questions liturgiques, 1978, and La Maison-Dieu, 2000); Geoffrey Wainwright (Methodist, Doxology, 1980); Nicholas Jesson (Toronto School of Theology, 2001).8

A la suite de l’étude la plus fine de ce passage, due à Karl Federer, il faut reconnaître que la lex supplicationis ou la lex orandi ne désigne pas d’abord, dans l’esprit de Prosper, une loi liturgique, mais bien le précepte apostolique que l’on trouve en 1 Tm 2, 1. La lex orandi, qui tranche le débat sur la grâce, ce n’est pas d’abord la prière que l’Eglise fait pour que les infidèles viennent à la foi, mais le précepte apostolique, attribué à saint Paul, auquel l’Eglise universelle obéit de manière si unanime que la loi biblique est devenue loi liturgique.

— Paul De Clerck, “Lex orandi, lex credendi: un principe heuristique” (2000), p. 68, source.9 In English: “the biblical law has become a liturgical law.” The case rests first on Prosper’s own gloss:

The Apostle commands—rather, the Lord speaking through the Apostle commands through him that supplications and intercessions, thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and for all that are in high station. All priests and all the faithful, adhere unanimously to this norm of supplication in their devotions. There is no part of the world in which Christian peoples do not offer up these prayers.

— Prosper, De vocatione omnium gentium I.12, trans. P. De Letter, source.10 “Norm of supplication” renders legem supplicationis, whose antecedent is the apostle’s command, which Prosper calls “a rule of the apostolic teaching.” Chapter 8 calls its rites obsecrationum sacerdotalium sacramenta, using the first of the Vulgate’s four words for prayer in 1 Timothy 2:1, and its list of petitions spells out the verse’s “for all men” and its purpose clause (2:4), the Massilians’ favourite text.11 Ambrosiaster had called the verse “the ecclesiastical rule,”12 and Augustine in 414 mapped its four words onto the parts of the Eucharist, “what the entire, or almost the entire, church observes.”13 Nicholas Jesson contrasts Augustine’s letter to Vitalis, whose argument is “a liturgical one, unlike Prosper’s which turns first to I Timothy.”14 Wainwright puts it that the apostolic injunction … to pray for the whole human race — which the church obeys in its intercessions — proves the obligation to believe that faith is a work of grace. De Clerck describes “three levels”: the precept understood as a law, the Church’s universal obedience to it, and the wording of the prayers; “it is the three coordinated levels that settle the doctrinal debate, and not the third taken in isolation.”15,16,17

A customary practice given the binding force of law

On this reading Prosper’s lex carries its Roman legal sense: he gives the Church’s universal practices of prayer, the intercessions and the baptismal exorcisms alike, the binding form of law.

Held by: O. T. I. Wright (Oxford, Oxford Journal of Law and Religion, 2024).18

Wright reads the clause against fifth-century Roman law, in which lex and ius had become relatively interchangeable: “Prosper’s ‘lex…supplicandi’ references and appeals to the notion of ius,” the unwritten, customary tradition, and Prosper appears “in the guise of a jurist,” making an established custom binding. Paul’s word in 1 Timothy 2:1 is parakalō, “an exhortation, an urging”: “This is no mere ‘recommendation’ that Prosper then turns into a ‘lex’, as De Clerck suggests.” Supplicandi is not the Vulgate’s word in 1 Timothy 2. On Wright’s reading Prosper appeals to “a universal custom always and everywhere observed” and establishes it as “capable of being legally enshrined and binding”; the formal prayers are “newly activated to prescribe.”19 The rule therefore covers what chapters 8 and 9 both argue from. To define the adage from chapter 8 alone, as Federer and De Clerck do, is in Wright’s words “an unwarranted exclusion of context,” since chapter 9 argues from the exorcisms before baptism, which no apostolic precept commands. On Wright’s reading the conclusion’s “ecclesiastical rules” take in chapters 8 and 9 together: the Church’s universal practices of prayer, which Prosper makes binding as law.20
