Did the older Roman Missal remain an expression of the Church’s lex orandi?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the core is secure. No Roman document has called the 1962 Missal erroneous, it has never ceased to be celebrated with the Church’s permission, and Prosper’s sense of the adage, a law of prayer handed down and prayed everywhere, favours it. The two further claims built on that core are disputed. One is that the Missal was “never juridically abrogated” between 1970 and 2007. The other is that what earlier generations held sacred cannot be forbidden. Both press against the authority over the rites that the same tradition records. · advanced by Benedict XVI (2007), Peter Kwasniewski and Joseph Shaw

Related arguments: the Roman Rite’s lex orandi — see also Are the post-conciliar books the only expression of the Roman Rite’s lex orandi?

The claim

The Roman Missal promulgated by Saint Pius V and revised by Blessed John XXIII is nonetheless to be considered an extraordinary expression of the same lex orandi of the Church and duly honoured for its venerable and ancient usage. These two expressions of the Church’s lex orandi will in no way lead to a division in the Church’s lex credendi (rule of faith); for they are two usages of the one Roman rite.

In the history of the liturgy there is growth and progress, but no rupture. What earlier generations held as sacred, remains sacred and great for us too, and it cannot be all of a sudden entirely forbidden or even considered harmful.

— Benedict XVI, Summorum Pontificum (7 July 2007), art. 1, and the accompanying Letter to the Bishops, link.1 The claim is that the Missal in use before the reform, last edited in 1962, did not stop being an expression of the Church’s law of prayer when Paul VI’s Missal replaced it in 1970. In its strongest form the claim says that it cannot stop being one. A form of prayer that carried the Church’s faith for centuries cannot become foreign to that faith by decree. The rival position, that the reformed books are the Roman Rite’s only lex orandi, is weighed on its own page.

Who advances it

As a cardinal, Joseph Ratzinger wrote in 1997 that the prohibition of the old Missal “introduced a breach into the history of the liturgy,” and said in 1998 that the Church’s authority “can define and limit the use of rites … She never forbids them purely and simply!” As pope he wrote the claim into law in 2007.2 After 2021 its chief advocates were the critics of Traditionis Custodes. The theologian Peter Kwasniewski argued that the older books “too, are the lex orandi and cannot be otherwise,” and that “the lex orandi is the whole complex of historical prayer texts, ceremonies, and music that make up the Roman Rite.” Joseph Shaw, chairman of the Latin Mass Society, wrote that “Pope Benedict was correct.”3 The claim that the Missal had never been abrogated was older than 2007. Cardinal Alfons Stickler said in 1995 that a commission of nine cardinals, appointed by John Paul II in 1986, had answered that “the Mass of Saint Pius V … has never been suppressed.” ⚠ unverified: the report is a secondhand account of his spoken remarks, and the commission’s findings were never published.4

The evidence for

The adage’s own sense. Prosper’s “law of supplication” carried weight because it was “handed down by the Apostles” and “uniformly celebrated in the whole world.” The witness came from antiquity and universality, not from a decree.5 Guéranger made the point a principle: the liturgy is “tradition itself at its highest degree of power,” and heresy spreads by substituting “new books and new formulas for the old.” The Catechism of 1992 calls the liturgy “a constitutive element of the holy and living Tradition.”6 If the law of prayer is what has been handed down, then a Missal that carried the Roman Rite for four centuries, and much of whose content is far older, belongs to it.

The Church’s own verdict on its content. Trent declared the Roman Canon “so pure from every error, that nothing is contained therein which does not in the highest degree savour of a certain holiness and piety,” and anathematized anyone who says it “contains errors, and is therefore to be abrogated.”7 No later Roman document has called the 1962 Missal erroneous. Traditionis Custodes itself still allows it to be celebrated, and the 1984 indult asked those who used it to accept the “doctrinal exactitude” of the 1970 Missal, not to doubt their own.8 A Missal that professes the Catholic faith and is prayed with the Church’s sanction is, in the ordinary sense of the words, an expression of the Church’s law of prayer.

Respect for ancient rites. Even Pius V’s Quo primum, the model for imposing one Missal, spared rites more than two hundred years old.9 The Second Vatican Council declared all lawful rites “of equal right and dignity” and asked that new forms “grow organically from forms already existing.” Ratzinger’s summary was that the Council “ordered a reform of the liturgical books but it never forbade the previous books.”10

The legal record. Paul VI’s constitution Missale Romanum (1969) promulgated the new Missal but did not name the old one as abrogated. Benedict’s judgement in 2007 was that the 1962 Missal “was never juridically abrogated and, consequently, in principle, was always permitted.”11

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core claim holds. The 1962 Missal professes the Catholic faith, no Roman document says otherwise, and it has been celebrated with the Church’s permission without a break since 1970. In Prosper’s sense of the adage, a law of prayer handed down and kept by the Church, it is hard to deny that a Missal of that age expresses the Church’s faith. Even the defenders of 2021 grant much of this. Ruff’s reading of Traditionis Custodes is that the older Missal reflects the Roman Rite’s lex orandi “to the extent that it reflects the Church’s liturgy as found in the 1970 Missal”: on that reading it still expresses the law of prayer, though as measured by the newer books.12

“Never abrogated” is a disputed legal judgement. Paul VI’s 1969 constitution was to be “firm and effective now and in the future, notwithstanding, to the extent necessary, the apostolic constitutions and ordinances issued by Our predecessors.” An instruction of 1971 allowed the old rite only to elderly or infirm priests celebrating without a congregation, with the Ordinary’s permission. In 1976 Paul VI said that the new Order of Mass was promulgated “to take the place of the old.”13 The indults of 1984 and 1988 were framed as permissions, and a permission implies a general rule against. Benedict’s reading is a legitimate one, and as pope he was entitled to give it. It is still a judgement about an unclear record, not a plain fact, and Paul VI’s own words point the other way. The Stickler report cannot carry much weight while the commission’s findings remain unpublished. Since 2021 the question is in any case historical for present practice: Traditionis Custodes art. 8 abrogates the earlier norms, whatever their status before.

“Cannot be forbidden” is not a rule the tradition clearly recognizes. Andrea Grillo answers Benedict’s sentence directly: “one can never assume as a principle that ‘what was sacred for earlier generations must remain so for later ones.’ This is not a theological principle, but a problem of a distorted understanding of tradition.”14 Mediator Dei had said that ancient usage is not to be preferred “on the simple ground that it carries the savor and aroma of antiquity,” and that the pope alone may “modify those [rites] he judges to require modification.” The Church has suppressed rites and uses before: Pius V abolished the younger ones, and the French dioceses gave up their own liturgies in the nineteenth century, with Guéranger’s encouragement. Those precedents concern recent local books, not a rite of the Roman Missal’s age, so they do not settle the case. But they show that the adage was never taken to forbid the Church from withdrawing a form of prayer. Ratzinger’s own formulation, that authority may “define and limit” rites but “never forbids them purely and simply,” describes the 2021 settlement fairly closely: the older Missal was restricted, not banned.

The two-forms construction has few defenders. Benedict’s legal basis was that the two Missals are “two usages of the one Roman rite.” Kwasniewski, one of the claim’s most prolific advocates, rejects that, arguing that the 1970 Missal is a different rite. Grillo rejects it too, for the opposite reason. If Kwasniewski is right, the older Missal can be defended as the Church’s law of prayer in its own right, but not by Benedict’s route, and Traditionis Custodes addresses only “the Roman Rite.”15

Assessment

contested — Three claims need to be kept apart. That the 1962 Missal expresses the Church’s faith, and so in Prosper’s sense belongs to its law of prayer, is well grounded. Rome has never said otherwise, and the adage’s history, which ties the law of prayer to what has been handed down, supports it. That the Missal was never juridically abrogated between 1970 and 2007 is a defensible legal reading, given authoritative force by Benedict, but it is against Paul VI’s stated intention, and in 2021 a later pope ruled the other way. That a form of prayer once held sacred cannot be withdrawn is the claim’s strongest form, and the weakest by the tradition’s own standards. The same documents that honour the received liturgy also give the pope authority to modify it. The question is genuinely divided, and it is divided along the line Mediator Dei drew in 1947, between Prosper’s direction (prayer establishes belief) and its reversal (belief, and the authority that guards it, governs prayer).
