Did the first Christians worship God through Jesus, rather than Jesus himself as God?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — most New Testament prayer is addressed to God through Jesus. Cultic service (latreia) and sacrifice are never offered to Jesus, and the honour he receives is given “to the glory of God the Father.” Those observations are sound, and the reading’s main opponents accept them. Its weak point is its denial. Jesus is also called on in prayer, sung to and given doxologies, and in Revelation the Lamb receives worship that an angel refuses. Whether that honour ranks Jesus as God or honours God’s exalted agent is a judgement on which historians divide. Most now lean the other way, but the reading is coherent and not ruled out. · advanced by James D. G. Dunn, James F. McGrath, Maurice Casey

Related arguments: the worship of Jesus — see also Did the first Christians’ worship of Jesus come before, and shape, belief in his divinity?

The claim

… by and large the first Christians did not worship Jesus as such.

— James D. G. Dunn, Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? (2010), 150, link.1 The claim is that the earliest Christian worship was worship of the one God, offered in and through Jesus. Jesus was revered as the exalted Lord, God’s agent and the one through whom believers came to God. He was not, on this reading, himself the object of worship in the sense that God was. For Dunn, the worship of Jesus “was a way of worshipping God.” The question matters for lex orandi. If the earliest prayer was offered to God through Jesus, then the worship that later writers cited for Christ’s divinity was not, at first, worship of Christ as God. The rival reading, that the earliest Christians did worship Jesus and that the worship shaped the later doctrine, is weighed on the sibling page.

Who advances it

The reading’s fullest statement is Dunn’s 2010 book, written in direct response to Larry Hurtado and Richard Bauckham. Dunn’s answer to his title question is a qualified no. He worried that loose talk of “worshipping Jesus” could slide into “Jesus-olatry,” which he called “a denial of Christianity’s claim to be a monotheistic religion.”2 James F. McGrath, in The Only True God (2009), argued that sacrifice was the line Jewish monotheism drew between God and every other being, and that the New Testament never crosses it for Jesus. Maurice Casey, in From Jewish Prophet to Gentile God (1991), went further. He held that Jesus was first openly called God only in the Johannine community, after it had broken with the synagogue, as the church became more Gentile.3 The three differ. Dunn and McGrath accept a very early and very high place for Jesus in devotion and dispute only what it implied. Casey posits a slower development.

The evidence for

The vocabulary of cultic worship is kept for God. The New Testament verb for cultic service, latreuō, always has God as its object, never Jesus: “Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve.” Sacrifice is likewise offered only to God. Jesus is the sacrifice and the high priest, not the one who receives the offering.4

Prayer runs to God through Jesus. Paul’s regular pattern is “I thank my God through Jesus Christ,” “To God only wise, be glory through Jesus Christ,” “giving thanks to God and the Father by him.” Dunn concludes that prayers “as such” are “always made to God.”5 The earliest Christian liturgy outside the New Testament keeps the same shape. The Didache’s eucharistic prayers thank the Father for what “Thou madest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant,” and close “Thine is the glory and the power through Jesus Christ for ever.”6 Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110) can say that in the Ephesians’ harmony “Jesus Christ is sung,” but the song itself is addressed “to the Father through Jesus Christ.”7

A third-century theologian made the rule explicit. Origen’s treatise On Prayer (c. 233) holds that “if we accept prayer in its full meaning, we may not ever pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself, but only to the God and Father of All to whom our Savior both prayed himself.” Christ, he says, taught his disciples to pray not to himself but to the Father. Those who pray to the Son are “committing a crude error in all simplicity for lack of discrimination and examination.”8 Here a leading Christian thinker of the early third century treats prayer to the Son as a mistake.

The honour given to Jesus serves the glory of God. Even the Philippians hymn, the strongest text for the worship of Jesus, ends with every tongue confessing that Jesus Christ is Lord “to the glory of God the Father.” In the end “shall the Son also himself be subject unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all.” Jesus is never worshipped apart from God or in place of God. Hurtado accepts this point and calls Dunn’s observation “correct.”9

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The reading rightly describes the main line of early Christian prayer. It runs to God, through Jesus, and ends in God’s glory. Cultic language and sacrifice are kept for God. Origen’s rule shows that a third-century theologian could still treat prayer to the Son as improper. And the reading guards against a real anachronism: talk of the first Christians “worshipping Jesus” can easily import the fully developed Trinitarian worship of the fourth century into the first.

But Jesus is also addressed directly. The earliest prayer we can recover, the Aramaic marana tha, is addressed to Jesus. Believers are defined as those who “call upon the name of Jesus Christ our Lord,” with Joel’s words about calling on God applied to him (see the sibling page). Paul tells of praying to the Lord three times about his “thorn in the flesh,” and the answer he receives speaks of “the power of Christ.” The dying Stephen prays, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit.” And Paul prays to God and Jesus together: “Now God himself and our Father, and our Lord Jesus Christ, direct our way unto you.”10 Dunn grants that calling on Jesus in this way is “very striking,” “entirely unusual and without precedent in the Judaism of the time.”11

Jesus receives doxologies and worship alongside God. Later New Testament writings give Jesus the doxology that Jewish prayer gave God: “to whom be glory for ever and ever. Amen.” In Revelation the Lamb shares the worship of the one on the throne: “Blessing, and honour, and glory, and power, be unto him that sitteth upon the throne, and unto the Lamb for ever and ever.” Yet when John falls at the feet of an angel, the angel forbids it: “See thou do it not … worship God.”12 The same book that refuses worship to the highest created messenger gives it to the Lamb. Dunn can answer that these texts are late. But Revelation is still a first-century book, and outsiders soon described the practice in the same terms. Pliny (c. 112) heard that Christians sang “to Christ as to a god”, and Celsus complained that they “worship also His servant.”

Origen cuts both ways. Origen forbade prayer to the Son because Christians were praying to him: “Are we not indeed divided if we pray some to the Father, others to the Son?” Even he allowed thanksgiving to Christ and intercession with him, as Stephen interceded.13 His rule did not prevail. The fourth-century Church prayed to Christ and sang doxologies to him, and his restriction came to be read as part of his subordination of the Son.

The real dispute is about what the worship meant. Both sides largely agree on the data. They disagree on its meaning. For Bauckham, “worship in the Jewish tradition was precisely recognition of this unique identity of the one God,” so worship of Jesus includes him in that identity.14 For Hurtado, the devotional pattern makes Jesus “a recipient of worship with God.” Hurtado also suspected that Dunn’s concern about “Jesus-olatry” shaped his reading of the evidence.15 For McGrath, sacrifice remained the line between God and his agent, and it was never crossed. The Jewish parallels do not settle it. William Horbury traces the cult of Christ to Jewish honour for the messianic king. Crispin Fletcher-Louis points to Jewish texts in which Adam or the Enochic Son of Man is honoured, though he reads those figures as manifesting God, in support of an early high Christology.16 Whether giving a figure prayer, hymns and doxologies counts as worshipping him as God depends on how the categories of ancient worship are drawn. Historical method alone does not fix that line.

What is overstated.

  • “Through Jesus rather than Jesus” is a false choice for the texts. They show both: most prayer goes to God through Jesus, and some goes to Jesus directly. A reading that accounts for only one pattern is incomplete.
  • “Never offered sacrifice” proves less than it seems. Christians set up no sacrificial cult of their own after the death of Christ, whose death they took to be the one sacrifice. So the absence of sacrifice to Jesus is not a clear sign of his lower rank.

Assessment

plausible — the reading is right that the main line of early Christian prayer runs to God through Jesus, that cultic language is kept for God, and that Jesus is never worshipped apart from or instead of God. Origen’s rule shows that this pattern could still be insisted on in the third century. But in its strong form, that the first Christians did not worship Jesus as such, it has to explain away direct invocation, hymns, doxologies and the shared worship of Revelation 5, all attested in the first century. Whether that devotion amounts to worship of Jesus as God or the highest honour given to God’s agent depends on contested judgements about ancient worship. Most scholars now side with Hurtado and Bauckham. Dunn’s reading remains a coherent minority position that the evidence does not rule out.
