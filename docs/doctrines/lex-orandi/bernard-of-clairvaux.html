Bernard of Clairvaux on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1140 (Letter 174, to the canons of Lyons; timeline anchor) · Bernard 1090–1153, abbot of Clairvaux from 1115 · Wikipedia
Summary: When the cathedral of Lyons began to keep a feast of Mary’s conception, Bernard wrote to rebuke its canons. His letter argues from the Church’s worship in both directions. The feasts the whole Church had received taught him what to believe: from the feast of Mary’s birth he learned that she was holy before she was born, as the feast of John the Baptist’s birth showed of John. But a new feast could not teach. It had no warrant in reason, in ancient tradition or in the Apostolic See, and the belief it implied, a holy conception, he judged false. For Bernard a received feast is a rule of faith; a feast still on trial has to be justified by the faith first. He did not cite Prosper’s clause, but his letter set the terms in which the scholastics argued over the feast.

Claims & evidence

The Church’s feasts teach what to hold (Letter 174.2)

These things the Church sings concerning her, and has taught me to repeat the same things in her praise, and what I have learnt from the Church I both hold securely myself and teach to others; what I have not received from the Church I confess I should with great difficulty admit. I have received then from the Church that day to be reverenced with the highest veneration, when being taken up from this sinful earth, she made entry into the heavens; a festival of most honoured joy. With no less clearness have I learned in the Church to celebrate the birth of the Virgin, and from the Church undoubtedly to hold it to have been holy and joyful; holding most firmly with the Church, that she received in the womb that she should come into the world holy.

— Letter 174.2 (c. 1140), trans. S. J. Eales, source.1 Bernard’s source of doctrine here is what the Church sings and celebrates. The feast of the Assumption teaches Mary’s entry into heaven. The feast of her Nativity teaches that her birth was holy, and so that she was sanctified in the womb. He makes the same argument from the feast of John the Baptist’s birth: the Church, “which regards and declares, not the nativity, but only the death of other saints as precious, makes a singular exception for him,” and so John too was holy before birth. Of Mary he concludes that “holy Church” is “not at all in error in accounting the day of her nativity holy, and celebrating it each year.”2

A new feast cannot teach: no reason, no tradition, no Rome (Letter 174.1, 8)

Wherefore I cannot but wonder that there should have been among you at this time some who wished to sully this splendid fame of your Church by introducing a new Festival, a rite which the Church knows nothing of, and which reason does not prove, nor ancient tradition hand down to us. Have we the pretension to be more learned or more devoted than the Fathers? It is a dangerous presumption to establish in such a matter what their prudence left unestablished.

— Letter 174.1.3 The feast’s defenders had argued that the conception should be honoured because without it there would have been no holy birth, and they produced a writing “given, as they say, by revelation from on high.” Bernard answers that by the same logic Mary’s parents and grandparents would need feasts too, and that he is “not to be influenced by such writings, which are supported neither by reason nor by any certain authority.” Then the doctrinal point: holiness requires existence, and the conception itself involved concupiscence, so Mary was sanctified after conception, which “made her birth holy, but not her conception.” (The theology is set out on Bernard’s Immaculate Conception page.) So the feast fails:

And as this is so, what ground can there be for a Festival of the Conception of the Virgin? On what principle, I say, is either a conception asserted to be holy which is not by the Holy Ghost, not to say that it is by sin, or a festival be established which is in no wise holy? … And, besides, she will by no means be pleased by a presumptuous novelty against the custom of the Church, a novelty which is the mother of rashness, the sister of superstition, the daughter of levity. For if such a festival seemed advisable, the authority of the Apostolic See ought first to have been consulted, and the simplicity of inexperienced persons ought not to have been followed so thoughtlessly and precipitately.

— Letter 174.8.4 He ends by submitting the whole matter “to the authority and decision of the See of Rome,” ready to change his opinion if he thinks otherwise than that see.

Notes

  • Both directions at once. Bernard’s letter can be read as running the adage in reverse, belief judging prayer. That is half of it. The other half argues from prayer to belief: the received feasts of the Assumption, of Mary’s birth and of John’s birth are his evidence for doctrine. What separates the two is reception. A feast kept by the whole Church, handed down from the Fathers, teaches. A feast newly adopted by one church, from popular devotion and a private revelation, has to be tested by reason, tradition and Rome before it can teach anything. This is close to Prosper’s own condition, that the prayers be handed down from the apostles and kept “uniformly throughout the whole world” (see Prosper of Aquitaine).
  • The feast won. The feast spread anyway. Sixtus IV approved it for Rome in 1476 without defining the doctrine (see Sixtus IV), and Pius IX appealed to the Church’s feasts when he defined it in 1854. Whether the feast’s spread is a case of the law of prayer establishing the law of belief is a separate question about the two Marian definitions, weighed at Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption? (plausible).
  • “Against the custom of the Church.” Bernard’s complaint is partly liturgical discipline. He praises Lyons because “in the Offices of the Church” it “has always shown itself opposed to attempts at sudden innovation.”5 A novelty in worship is suspect before its content is examined.
