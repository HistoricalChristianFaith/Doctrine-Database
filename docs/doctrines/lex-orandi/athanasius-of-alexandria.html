Athanasius of Alexandria on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 359–361 (Letters to Serapion; timeline anchor) · the Orations against the Arians are earlier, their date debated (c. 340 or the later 350s) · Athanasius c. 296–373, bishop of Alexandria from 328 · Wikipedia
Summary: Athanasius argued from baptism to the deity of the Son and then of the Spirit. Christians are baptized into the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. If the Son were a creature, a creature would be “classed with the Maker” in the rite, and the Arians would be baptizing “into Creator and creature.” He applied the same reasoning to those who granted the Son but made the Spirit a creature. Their “rite of initiation” would not be “entirely into the Godhead.” He also argued from worship: a creature does not worship a creature, so the Son, whom angels and Christians worship, is not one. But Athanasius did not treat the rite as a source on its own. The baptismal formula is Christ’s command in Matthew 28:19, and a baptism given with the right words but the wrong faith is, he says, “empty and unprofitable.”

Claims & evidence

Why is the Son named with the Father in baptism? (Against the Arians II.41)

Since, were it not so, wherefore through Him does the Father create, and in Him reveal Himself to whom He will, and illuminate them? or why too in the baptismal consecration is the Son named together with the Father? For if they say that the Father is not all-sufficient, then their answer is irreligious, but if He be, for this it is right to say, what is the need of the Son for framing the worlds, or for the holy laver? For what fellowship is there between creature and Creator? or why is a thing made classed with the Maker in the consecration of all of us? or why, as you hold, is faith in one Creator and in one creature delivered to us?

— Four Discourses Against the Arians II.41, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source.1 The rite is the premise. Every Christian is baptized in the Son’s name as well as the Father’s. On the Arian view that puts a creature beside God in the act that makes us Christians. Athanasius answers with his own account: “where the Father is or is named, there plainly is the Son also,” as the radiance is with the light, so “whom the Father baptizes, him the Son baptizes; and whom the Son baptizes, he is consecrated in the Holy Ghost.”

The Arians baptize “into Creator and creature” (Against the Arians II.42)

And these too hazard the fulness of the mystery, I mean Baptism; for if the consecration is given to us into the Name of Father and Son, and they do not confess a true Father, because they deny what is from Him and like His Essence, and deny also the true Son, and name another of their own framing as created out of nothing, is not the rite administered by them altogether empty and unprofitable, making a show, but in reality being no help towards religion? For the Arians do not baptize into Father and Son, but into Creator and creature, and into Maker and work. And as a creature is other than the Son, so the Baptism, which is supposed to be given by them, is other than the truth, though they pretend to name the Name of the Father and the Son, because of the words of Scripture, For not he who simply says, ‘O Lord,’ gives Baptism; but he who with the Name has also the right faith. On this account therefore our Saviour also did not simply command to baptize, but first says, ‘Teach;’ then thus: ‘Baptize into the Name of Father, and Son, and Holy Ghost;’ that the right faith might follow upon learning, and together with faith might come the consecration of Baptism.

— Four Discourses Against the Arians II.42, source.2 Here the argument turns round. The Arians used the same words as the Church, so the words alone could not settle the question. Athanasius says a baptism needs “the right faith” along with “the Name,” and that Christ put teaching before baptizing. Belief decides whether the rite is what it claims to be. His language is guarded all the same: Arian baptism is “hazarded,” and he does not say outright that it is void.3

“Creature does not worship creature” (Against the Arians II.23–24; I.8)

For if He was worshipped as excelling them in glory, each of things subservient ought to worship what excels itself. But this is not the case; for creature does not worship creature, but servant Lord, and creature God. … But He had not been thus worshipped, nor been thus spoken of, were He a creature merely. But now since He is not a creature, but the proper offspring of the Essence of that God who is worshipped, and His Son by nature, therefore He is worshipped and is believed to be God.

— Four Discourses Against the Arians II.23–24, source.4 The worship in this passage is mostly the worship Scripture records: Peter refuses Cornelius’s homage, the angel refuses John’s, and “all the Angels of God” are told to worship the Son (Heb 1:6). But the argument also bears on the Church’s own worship of Christ. Earlier in the work Athanasius asks who ever taught the Arians, “Abandon the worship of the creation, and then draw near and worship a creature and a work?”5 A convert renounced the worship of creatures and was then taught to worship Christ. On the Arian view the second step undoes the first.

The faith delivered in baptism is the Church’s foundation (Letters to Serapion I.28)

But, beyond these sayings, let us look at the very tradition, teaching, and faith of the Catholic Church from the beginning, which the Lord gave, the Apostles preached, and the Fathers kept. Upon this the Church is founded, and he who should fall away from it would not be a Christian, and should no longer be so called. … And that they may know this to be the faith of the Church, let them learn how the Lord, when sending forth the Apostles, ordered them to lay this foundation for the Church, saying: ‘Go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.’ The Apostles went, and thus they taught; and this is the preaching that extends to the whole Church which is under heaven.

— Letters to Serapion I.28, trans. C. R. B. Shapland (1951), pp. 133–136, source.6 Serapion, bishop of Thmuis, had reported a group (Athanasius calls them “Tropici”) who confessed the Son but called the Spirit a creature. Between the two parts of this passage Athanasius sets out the Triad, “all creative,” with nothing created mixed into it (quoted on the argument page on the Spirit’s deity). His translator notes what the “tradition” here is. It is not a source apart from Scripture. It is “the faith as delivered, expounded, and confessed in baptism,” and the “foundation” is “nothing other than the threefold Name as invoked in baptism.”7

A rite “not entirely into the Godhead” (Letters to Serapion I.29–30)

If you divide and alienate the Spirit from the Godhead, you have not that which is ‘in all’; and, if you think like this, the rite of initiation which you reckon to perform is not entirely into the Godhead. For with the Godhead there is mixed a creature; and, like the Arians and the heathen, you too confess creation to be divine together with God who made it through his own Word.

For as the faith in the Triad, which has been delivered to us, joins us to God; and as he who takes anything away from the Triad, and is baptized in the name of the Father alone, or in the name of the Son alone, or in the Father and the Son without the Holy Spirit, receives nothing, but remains ineffective and uninitiated, both himself and he who is supposed to initiate him (for the rite of initiation is in the Triad); so he who divides the Son from the Father, or who reduces the Spirit to the level of the creatures, has neither the Son nor the Father, but is without God, worse than an unbeliever, and anything rather than a Christian.

— Letters to Serapion I.29 and I.30, trans. Shapland, pp. 136–140, source.8 This is the argument of Against the Arians II.42 carried over to the Spirit, as Shapland notes. Baptism names the Spirit with the Father and the Son. If the Spirit were a creature, the rite would join the baptized to a creature. Athanasius argues here from the Spirit’s place in the baptismal invocation, not from prayers addressed to the Spirit. Shapland points out that the Spirit was not yet called “God” in so many words, and that the later Pneumatomachi refused to worship Him with the Father.9

Notes

  • Which way does it run? For Athanasius the rite and the faith confirm each other, and both go back to Christ’s command. The baptismal formula is evidence of what the Church believes, so a doctrine that cannot be squared with it is false. But the formula is also Scripture (Matt 28:19), and a heretic who uses the right words with the wrong belief does not, on his account, really baptize. So the rite works as evidence only when it is used with the faith it expresses. This is close to the balance in Tertullian and Cyprian. Shapland draws one contrast with Cyprian: Cyprian rejected heretical baptism because it was given outside the Church, Athanasius because the faith in which it was given was defective.10 The three answers to the direction question are weighed under which way the adage runs.
  • Dating. The Letters to Serapion belong to Athanasius’s third exile (356–362); Shapland puts the first letter late in it, after the second half of 358, and the trinity timeline uses c. 359–361. The Orations against the Arians were once placed in the same exile. Shapland reports stronger reasons for an earlier date, perhaps as early as 338.11
  • Related pages. Athanasius on the Trinity (the Son’s deity and homoousios); is the Spirit’s deity unscriptural?; Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus, who carried the baptismal argument further; the lex orandi timeline.
