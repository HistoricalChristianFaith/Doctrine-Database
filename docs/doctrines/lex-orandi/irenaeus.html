Irenaeus of Lyons on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, Books IV–V, written at Lyons) · Irenaeus c. 130–202 · Wikipedia
Summary: Two and a half centuries before Prosper’s formula, Irenaeus argued from the Church’s worship to its faith. The Gnostics he opposed despised matter and denied that the flesh is saved. Yet they too offered bread and wine and called them the Lord’s body and blood. Irenaeus answers that the rite they share with the Church contradicts what they teach: “Let them, therefore, either alter their opinion, or cease from offering.” Then he states the rule: “our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion.” The argument is about the Eucharist, not intercessory prayer, and it has no word for “law.” But it already treats a rite that every party holds as a test that a doctrine must pass.

Claims & evidence

The heretics’ own offering contradicts their teaching

Nor, again, do any of the conventicles (synagogae) of the heretics [offer this]. For some, by maintaining that the Father is different from the Creator, do, when they offer to Him what belongs to this creation of ours, set Him forth as being covetous of another's property, and desirous of what is not His own. Those, again, who maintain that the things around us originated from apostasy, ignorance, and passion, do, while offering unto Him the fruits of ignorance, passion, and apostasy, sin against their Father, rather subjecting Him to insult than giving Him thanks. But how can they be consistent with themselves, [when they say] that the bread over which thanks have been given is the body of their Lord, and the cup His blood, if they do not call Himself the Son of the Creator of the world, that is, His Word, through whom the wood fructifies, and the fountains gush forth, and the earth gives “first the blade, then the ear, then the full corn in the ear.”

— Against Heresies IV.18.4, source.1 The chapter is about sacrifice. The Church alone, Irenaeus says, offers the Creator a pure oblation, “offering to Him, with giving of thanks, [the things taken] from His creation.” The heretics offer the same bread and cup, but their doctrine makes nonsense of the act. If the Father is not the Creator, offering Him bread is offering Him someone else’s property. If the world is the fruit of a fall, the offering insults Him. And if the bread is the Lord’s body, the Lord must be the Son of the God who makes wheat grow.

“Our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion”

Then, again, how can they say that the flesh, which is nourished with the body of the Lord and with His blood, goes to corruption, and does not partake of life? Let them, therefore, either alter their opinion, or cease from offering the things just mentioned. But our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion. For we offer to Him His own, announcing consistently the fellowship and union of the flesh and Spirit. For as the bread, which is produced from the earth, when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly; so also our bodies, when they receive the Eucharist, are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection to eternity.

— Against Heresies IV.18.5, source.2 The key sentence survives in Greek as well as Latin: Ἡμῶν δὲ σύμφωνος ἡ γνώμη τῇ εὐχαριστίᾳ, καὶ ἡ εὐχαριστία βεβαιοῖ τὴν γνώμην; Nostra autem consonans est sententia Eucharistiae, et Eucharistia rursus confirmat sententiam nostram. It runs both ways. First, the Church’s teaching agrees with what it does at the altar. Then the rite, in turn, confirms (βεβαιοῖ, confirmat) the teaching. The doctrine it confirms here is the salvation of the flesh. The same body that is fed with the Lord’s body and blood cannot simply rot away and be lost. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the sentence to call the Eucharist “the sum and summary of our faith.”3 For the eucharistic doctrine itself, see Irenaeus on the real presence.

The same argument in Book V: the cup and the bread prove the flesh can be saved

But vain in every respect are they who despise the entire dispensation of God, and disallow the salvation of the flesh, and treat with contempt its regeneration, maintaining that it is not capable of incorruption. But if this indeed do not attain salvation, then neither did the Lord redeem us with His blood, nor is the cup of the Eucharist the communion of His blood, nor the bread which we break the communion of His body. … When, therefore, the mingled cup and the manufactured bread receives the Word of God, and the Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made, from which things the substance of our flesh is increased and supported, how can they affirm that the flesh is incapable of receiving the gift of God, which is life eternal, which [flesh] is nourished from the body and blood of the Lord, and is a member of Him?

— Against Heresies V.2.2–3, source.4 Here the premise is Paul’s wording in 1 Corinthians 10:16, “the communion of the blood … the communion of the body,” as well as the rite itself. The structure is the same as in Book IV. Everyone receives the cup and the bread, so whoever denies the salvation of the flesh denies what the rite says. The doctrine is defended at length on the resurrection-of-the-body timeline.

Notes

  • Is this the principle before the formula? In part. Irenaeus does what Prosper and Augustine later did: he takes a rite that the whole Church performs and that his opponents cannot drop, and he argues that a doctrine which the rite contradicts must be false. In that sense his sentence is the earliest clear statement that worship confirms belief. But three things differ. First, the rite is the eucharistic offering, not the Church’s prayers of petition, so the argument concerns what the Church does with bread and wine, not what it asks. Second, the sentence runs in both directions. Belief agrees with the Eucharist and the Eucharist confirms belief. Neither is said to establish the other, as Prosper’s statuat would. Third, there is no lex. Irenaeus’s rule of faith is the creed handed down from the apostles, and the Eucharist is an agreeing witness to it, not its source.
  • An argument ad hominem. The force of the argument depends on the heretics’ practising the rite themselves. It shows that their teaching contradicts their own worship (“either alter their opinion, or cease from offering”). It does not, by itself, show that the Church’s worship can settle a question on which the rite is silent. Tertullian makes the same move against Marcion a generation later (see Tertullian).
  • Related pages. Irenaeus on the real presence (the same passage, read for its eucharistic doctrine); Irenaeus on the resurrection of the body; the lex orandi timeline.
