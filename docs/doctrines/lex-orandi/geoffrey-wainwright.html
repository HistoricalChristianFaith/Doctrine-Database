Geoffrey Wainwright on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1980 (Doxology; timeline anchor) · 1939–2020 · Wikipedia
Summary: Geoffrey Wainwright, a British Methodist minister who taught at the Queen’s College, Birmingham, at Union Theological Seminary in New York (from 1979) and at Duke Divinity School (1983–2012), wrote Doxology: The Praise of God in Worship, Doctrine and Life (1980), a systematic theology organized around worship. Two of its chapters bear the two halves of the adage as titles, “Lex Orandi” and “Lex Credendi.” He held that the relation runs both ways: “worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship.” He also described the confessional pattern the timeline shows. Roman Catholicism characteristically argues from the liturgy to doctrine, and Protestantism from doctrine to the liturgy. He wanted Protestants to take up the theme and Catholics to answer critical questions about how they had used it.

Claims & evidence

The tag “may be construed in two ways” (1980)

The Latin tag lex orandi, lex credendi may be construed in two ways. The more usual way makes the rule of prayer a norm for belief: what is prayed indicates what may and must be believed. But from the grammatical point of view it is equally possible to reverse subject and predicate and so take the tag as meaning that the rule of faith is the norm for prayer: what must be believed governs what may and should be prayed. The linguistic ambiguity of the Latin tag corresponds to a material interplay which in fact takes place between worship and doctrine in Christian practice: worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship. Much of the present book is taken up with explorations of that interplay.

— Doxology (London: Epworth; New York: Oxford University Press, 1980), ch. VII “Lex Orandi,” §1, p. 218, source.1 The book is built on this view. A few lines later he sets out the plan: “According to what criteria is worship allowed to influence doctrine and vice versa? Chapter VII will treat chiefly the influence of worship on doctrine. Chapter VIII will treat chiefly the influence of doctrine on worship.” The reversibility he means is that of the two-word tag. It is not true of Prosper’s full sentence, whose verb fixes the direction. Wainwright himself traces the tag to that sentence, legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, in the so-called capitula Coelestini.2

The Reformers’ “doctrinal control over worship” (1980)

Yet it was the policy of the Reformers to establish doctrinal control over worship, and the critical primacy of doctrine in relation to liturgy has remained characteristic of Protestantism. … The theological neglect of the lex orandi, lex credendi theme among Protestant writers may in fact be due to the use which Roman Catholic theologians have made of the tag in predominantly the reverse direction: Catholics have appealed to past and present liturgical practice in order to justify doctrinal positions and developments which Protestants have considered unacceptable. One of my purposes in writing has been to rescue the interplay of worship and doctrine—with both its problems and its opportunities—as an area of interest for Protestant theology. Some critical questions must certainly be put to Roman Catholics concerning the use they have made of the movement from worship to doctrine. On the other hand, the Protestant practice of doctrine needs to recover a more explicit doxological dimension.

— Doxology, ch. VII, §1, pp. 218–219, source.3 The passage opens with the observation that “It is a fairly rare occurrence for the theme of lex orandi, lex credendi to be treated by a Protestant writer.” “The reverse direction” here means from worship to doctrine, the reverse of the Protestant habit. The Reformers’ practice is on the timeline under Luther and Cranmer. Wainwright treats it as one side of a real two-way relation, not as a mistake.

Mediator Dei read as a “two-way relation” (1980)

Wainwright quotes Pius XII’s encyclical using the tag in both directions: first “Let the law of prayer establish the law of belief,” and then “the law of our faith must establish the law of our prayer.” He then comments:

… In other words: the liturgy is ‘subject to the Church’s supreme teaching authority’. The two-way relation between worship and doctrine which these passages expound is grounded in the assumption, and requirement, that ‘the whole liturgy contains the catholic faith, in as much as it is a public profession of the faith of the Church’. The encyclical Mediator Dei raises several questions to which we shall return in our systematic discussions: for example, the relation between the authority of the worship of the Church as such and the authority of the teaching office in the Church; or the question of whether there is an exact correspondence between the worship of the Church and Christian truth …

— Doxology, ch. VII, §1, pp. 223–224, source.4 Just before this he calls an earlier passage of the encyclical “a most significant passage from the pen of a pope who, three years after the encyclical on the liturgy, Mediator Dei, was to give dogmatic definition to the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Not only is development affirmed, but the right of the magisterium to control the liturgy is also asserted.” He does not reject Mediator Dei’s reversal. He reads the encyclical as stating a two-way relation and asks what it leaves open: whose authority decides, the Church’s worship as such or its teaching office.

“Catholics and Protestants”: two characteristic directions (1980)

Roman Catholicism characteristically appeals to existing liturgical practice for proof in matters of doctrine. There lex orandi, lex credendi is most readily taken to make the (descriptive) pattern of prayer a (prescriptive) norm for belief, so that what is prayed indicates what may and must be believed. Protestantism characteristically emphasizes the primacy of doctrine over the liturgy. The phrase lex orandi, lex credendi is not well known among Protestants, but they would most easily take the dogmatic norm of belief as setting a rule for prayer, so that what must be believed governs what may and should be prayed. … Although such a distinction can properly be made between the directions in which Catholicism and Protestantism characteristically see the relations between worship and doctrine, yet Catholicism and Protestantism each know also the reverse relationship between the two. Catholicism also seeks to control the liturgy doctrinally.

— Doxology, ch. VIII “Lex Credendi,” §1 “Catholics and Protestants,” p. 251, source.5 The omitted sentence glosses “may” and “should”: “may” is “in critical distinction from what may not,” and “should” reflects that Protestantism “also desires positively to promote true worship.” The last sentence is the counterweight. In Mediator Dei the Catholic magisterium claims the doctrinal control over the liturgy that the Reformers exercised.

Pius IX gave “a new twist to Prosper’s dictum” (1980)

Among other things it will be noted that the pope gave a new twist to Prosper’s dictum: a new lex orandi is seen by Pius to have been deliberately introduced by his papal predecessors in order to promote (that would seem to be the meaning here of establish) a particular doctrine.

— Doxology, ch. VII, pp. 238–239, source.6 The comment introduces his quotation of Pius IX’s bull on the Immaculate Conception, Ineffabilis Deus (8 December 1854). The bull says that earlier popes promoted the feast and its liturgy “atque adeo lex credendi ipsa supplicandi lege statueretur” (“and so that the law of belief itself might be established by the law of prayer”). In Prosper the Church’s intercession is a given that settles a doctrinal dispute. Pius describes popes shaping the liturgy so that it would later support a doctrine. Nicholas Jesson calls the argument “circular” on this basis.7 Whether the feasts led to the definitions is weighed on its own page: Did the Marian feasts lead to the 1854 and 1950 definitions?

Notes

  • Which way it runs. Both ways. Wainwright calls the prayer-to-belief reading “the more usual” and allows the reverse on grammatical grounds, but his thesis is the interplay: “worship influences doctrine, and doctrine worship.” Of the modern witnesses on this timeline he is the main advocate of the reciprocal answer. Schmemann and Kavanagh hold the prayer-to-belief answer, and Pius XII made belief the rule “in absolute and general terms.” The three answers are weighed in the timeline’s Arguments section.
  • On Prosper. Wainwright is one of two writers, with Paul De Clerck, on whom Jesson relies for the history of the formula. Both, Jesson says, “find the key to Prosper’s argument in the content of the supplications that are enjoined upon all Christians,” the intercessions for all people that the Prosper page sets out. Jesson also reports that Wainwright points to the next chapter of the Capitula, which argues from the rites of exorcism before baptism. Wainwright’s comment is that “Doctrinal appeal is … made not only to the word-content of prayers but also to a complex ritual act” (p. 227, as quoted by Jesson).8
  • Elsewhere on the timeline. Wainwright’s generalization about Protestant practice is cited on the Luther page.
  • The text. Doxology is quoted from the scan of the 1980 Oxford University Press (New York) printing. The Epworth (London) printing of the same year matches the text checked here. A few obvious scanning errors are silently corrected (“tule” for “rule,” “assetted” for “asserted”), and Wainwright’s bracketed note numbers are omitted.
