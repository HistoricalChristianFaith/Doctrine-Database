Pope Francis on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: 16 July 2021 (the motu proprio Traditionis Custodes and its letter to the bishops; timeline anchor) · Desiderio desideravi, 29 June 2022 · pope 2013–2025 · Wikipedia
Summary: Traditionis Custodes reversed Benedict XVI’s settlement in the same legal vocabulary. Its first article declares the liturgical books of Paul VI and John Paul II “the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite”; the Latin unica expressio and the Italian l’unica espressione mean “the only expression.” Earlier norms were abrogated. The older Missal may still be used, but only with the bishop’s permission and within limits. Francis gave two reasons. A consultation of the bishops had found the older Missal used to reject the Second Vatican Council, and Pius V had done the same after Trent, establishing “a single Missale Romanum” for the sake of unity. In 2022 he called the question “primarily ecclesiological”: to accept the Council is to accept its liturgical reform.

Claims & evidence

“The unique expression of the lex orandi” (arts. 1 and 8)

Art. 1. The liturgical books promulgated by Saint Paul VI and Saint John Paul II, in conformity with the decrees of Vatican Council II, are the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite.

…

Art. 8. Previous norms, instructions, permissions, and customs that do not conform to the provisions of the present Motu Proprio are abrogated.

— Traditionis Custodes (16 July 2021), arts. 1 and 8, source.1 The Latin of art. 1 is “Libri liturgici a sanctis Pontificibus Paulo VI et Ioanne Paulo II promulgati, iuxta decreta Concilii Vaticani II, unica expressio ‘legis orandi’ Ritus Romani sunt.” The article answers Summorum Pontificum art. 1 term for term. Benedict had named an “ordinary” and an “extraordinary expression” of one lex orandi; Francis names one. Both documents use lex orandi in the same way, as the status of a liturgical book in law. They disagree about which books have it.

Why: the consultation and the Council (letter to the bishops)

With the passage of thirteen years, I instructed the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith to circulate a questionnaire to the Bishops regarding the implementation of the Motu proprio Summorum Pontificum. The responses reveal a situation that preoccupies and saddens me, and persuades me of the need to intervene. … An opportunity offered by St. John Paul II and, with even greater magnanimity, by Benedict XVI, intended to recover the unity of an ecclesial body with diverse liturgical sensibilities, was exploited to widen the gaps, reinforce the divergences, and encourage disagreements that injure the Church, block her path, and expose her to the peril of division.

…

But I am nonetheless saddened that the instrumental use of Missale Romanum of 1962 is often characterized by a rejection not only of the liturgical reform, but of the Vatican Council II itself, claiming, with unfounded and unsustainable assertions, that it betrayed the Tradition and the “true Church.”

— Francis, Letter to the Bishops of the Whole World accompanying Traditionis Custodes (16 July 2021), source.2 This is the adage applied in reverse to Benedict’s use of it. Benedict had said that two forms of prayer would not divide the lex credendi. Francis judged from the consultation that in practice they had: the older rite had become the banner of a different view of the Council and the Church.

The precedent of Pius V

Responding to your requests, I take the firm decision to abrogate all the norms, instructions, permissions and customs that precede the present Motu proprio, and declare that the liturgical books promulgated by the saintly Pontiffs Paul VI and John Paul II, in conformity with the decrees of Vatican Council II, constitute the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite. I take comfort in this decision from the fact that, after the Council of Trent, St. Pius V also abrogated all the rites that could not claim a proven antiquity, establishing for the whole Latin Church a single Missale Romanum. For four centuries this Missale Romanum, promulgated by St. Pius V was thus the principal expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite, and functioned to maintain the unity of the Church.

— Letter to the Bishops (2021), source.3 Paul VI had drawn the same parallel in 1976, when he said that the new Order of Mass was promulgated “to take the place of the old,” as Pius V had ordered his revised Missal used after Trent (see the Vatican II page). Pius V’s bull Quo primum, quoted on Trent’s page, allowed rites more than two hundred years old to continue. The letter also says that whoever wishes to pray according to earlier forms “can find in the reformed Roman Missal according to Vatican Council II all the elements of the Roman Rite, in particular the Roman Canon.”

“The problematic is primarily ecclesiological” (2022)

The problematic is primarily ecclesiological. I do not see how it is possible to say that one recognizes the validity of the Council — though it amazes me that a Catholic might presume not to do so — and at the same time not accept the liturgical reform born out of Sacrosanctum Concilium, a document that expresses the reality of the Liturgy intimately joined to the vision of Church so admirably described in Lumen gentium. For this reason, as I already expressed in my letter to all the bishops, I have felt it my duty to affirm that “The liturgical books promulgated by Saint Paul VI and Saint John Paul II, in conformity with the decrees of Vatican Council II, are the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite.”

— Francis, apostolic letter Desiderio desideravi (29 June 2022), §31, source.4 Here the link between prayer and belief is explicit. The reformed liturgy expresses the Council’s vision of the Church, so the rite one prays is a sign of the Church one believes in. The Congregation for Divine Worship’s Responsa ad dubia (December 2021) put the same aim as “ecclesial communion,” expressed by recognising the reformed books as “the unique expression of the lex orandi of the Roman Rite,” and called the remaining use of the older books “a concession” and “not an opportunity to promote the previous rite.”5

Notes

  • “Unique” or “only”? The official English “unique” is softer than the Latin and Italian. The Oxford philosopher Joseph Shaw, chairman of the Latin Mass Society, argued that “only” is the right rendering, and that a document allowing both Missals to be used while naming one as the only lex orandi is “self-contradictory.” The Benedictine Anthony Ruff read it more narrowly: the 1962 Missal reflects the Roman rite’s lex orandi “to the extent that it reflects the Church’s liturgy as found in the 1970 Missal.”6 The question is weighed at Are the post-conciliar books the only expression of the Roman Rite’s lex orandi?
  • The rival position. Benedict’s view, that the older Missal remained an expression of the same lex orandi, is weighed at Did the older Roman Missal remain an expression of the Church’s lex orandi?
