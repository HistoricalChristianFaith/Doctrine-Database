Prosper Guéranger on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1840 (Institutions liturgiques, vol. 1; timeline anchor) · polemical letters 1843–1847 · 1805–1875 · Wikipedia
Summary: Dom Prosper Guéranger refounded the Benedictine priory of Solesmes in 1833 and led the campaign to bring the French dioceses back from their eighteenth-century “neo-Gallican” liturgies to the Roman rite. He opened his Institutions liturgiques (1840) with the adage, credited to Pope Celestine, and glossed it with the line that made it a programme: the liturgy is “tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity.” For him the argument ran from the rite to the faith. Heretics who wish to change a doctrine must first change the books, and a bishop who replaces an ancient liturgy with a new one disarms his people’s faith. When a French bishop proposed in 1847 to turn the adage round, so that belief rules prayer, Guéranger refused.

Claims & evidence

The liturgy is “tradition itself at its highest degree of power” (1840)

De là, l’importance si grande pour l’intelligence du dogme, donnée dans tous les temps aux paroles et aux faits de la Liturgie. On connaît l’axiome : Legem credendi statuat lex supplicandi. C’est dans la Liturgie que l’esprit qui inspira les Ecritures sacrées parle encore ; la Liturgie est la tradition même à son plus haut degré de puissance et de solennité.

— Institutions liturgiques, vol. 1 (1840), part I, ch. 1, p. 3, source.1 In English: “Hence the great importance for the understanding of dogma that has in every age been given to the words and acts of the Liturgy. The axiom is well known: Legem credendi statuat lex supplicandi. It is in the Liturgy that the Spirit who inspired the sacred Scriptures still speaks; the Liturgy is tradition itself at its highest degree of power and solemnity” (this page’s translation). The often-quoted description of the liturgy is a gloss on the adage, which Guéranger quotes in the sentence before it. The second edition (1878) keeps the passage unchanged.

The maxim of the popes (1840)

… les mêmes Pontifes ne négligèrent aucune occasion de montrer l’union intime de la Foi et de la Liturgie, en sorte qu’ils proclamèrent dès-lors leur grande maxime : Legem credendi statuat lex supplicandi ; maxime dont nous ne cesserons de voir l’application dans toute la suite de cette histoire ;

— Institutions liturgiques, vol. 1 (1840), p. 157, source.2 “… the same Pontiffs neglected no occasion of showing the intimate union of Faith and Liturgy, so that they proclaimed from that time their great maxim, Legem credendi statuat lex supplicandi, a maxim whose application we shall never cease to see in the whole course of this history” (this page’s translation). His chapter on the fifth century renders the Capitula in French, “so that the rule of believing flows from the rule of praying,” and introduces it as what “Saint Celestine writes to the bishops of the Gauls … against the error of the Pelagians.” The Latin is printed in a note as Celestine’s letter 21 in Coustant’s edition of the papal letters.3 Whether Celestine wrote it is weighed on its own page.

The “anti-liturgical heresy” (1840)

1° Le premier caractère de l’hérésie Anti-liturgiste est la haine de la Tradition dans les formules du culte divin. … Tout sectaire voulant introduire une doctrine nouvelle, se trouve infailliblement en présence de la Liturgie, qui est la tradition à sa plus haute puissance, et il ne saurait avoir de repos qu’il n’ait fait taire cette voix, qu’il n’ait déchiré ces pages qui récèlent la foi des siècles passés. En effet, comment le Luthéranisme, le Calvinisme, l’Anglicanisme se sont-ils établis et maintenus dans les masses? Il n’a fallu pour cela que la substitution de livres nouveaux et de formules nouvelles, aux livres et aux formules anciennes, et tout a été consommé. Rien ne gênait plus les nouveaux Docteurs ; ils pouvaient prêcher tout à leur aise : la foi des peuples était désormais sans défense.

— Institutions liturgiques, vol. 1 (1840), ch. 14 “De l’hérésie anti-liturgiste,” p. 414, source.4 “The first character of the anti-liturgical heresy is hatred of Tradition in the formulas of divine worship. … Every sectary wishing to introduce a new doctrine finds himself inevitably face to face with the Liturgy, which is tradition at its highest power, and he can have no rest until he has silenced that voice, until he has torn up those pages that hold the faith of past centuries. For how did Lutheranism, Calvinism and Anglicanism establish and maintain themselves among the masses? It took only the substitution of new books and new formulas for the old books and formulas, and all was accomplished. Nothing hindered the new Doctors any longer; they could preach as they pleased: the faith of the peoples was henceforth defenceless” (this page’s translation). This is the adage turned into a theory of heresy. If the rite carries the faith, the way to change a faith is to change its books.

“The bulwark of the faith”: the letter to the archbishop of Reims (1843)

Tout le monde connaît, ou doit connaître cet axiome de la théologie : LEGEM CREDENDI STATUAT LEX SUPPLICANDI : la règle de croire découle de la règle de prier. Il est promulgué par le grand Pape saint Célestin, dans une épître aux évêques des Gaules, contre l’erreur des Pélagiens.

— Lettre à Mgr l’archevêque de Reims sur le droit de la liturgie (1843), reprinted in Institutions liturgiques, 2nd ed., vol. 3 (1883), p. 459, source.5 “Everyone knows, or ought to know, this axiom of theology: the rule of believing flows from the rule of praying. It was promulgated by the great Pope Saint Celestine, in a letter to the bishops of the Gauls, against the error of the Pelagians” (this page’s translation). In the same letter the liturgy’s universality makes it “the invincible bulwark of the faith,” and its formulas are “the terror of heresies, the bulwark of the faith.” The argument is the one he made against the diocesan liturgies. Because the liturgy’s authority comes from its universality, a diocese that sets aside the common books gives up the witness on which the faith rests.6

The adage refused its reversal (1846–1847)

The bishop of Orléans, Jean-Jacques Fayet, answered Guéranger in defence of the French liturgies. In his second reply, the Nouvelle défense des Institutions liturgiques, Guéranger defends reading the sentence as “une proposition absolue, … un principe universel, … un axiôme” (“an absolute proposition, … a universal principle, … an axiom”). He grants that “plusieurs ont pensé que saint Prosper avait tenu la plume” (“several have thought that Saint Prosper held the pen”), but treats the text as Celestine’s act all the same. Fayet had proposed to turn the formula round, Legem deprecandi statuat lex credendi, “let the law of believing establish the law of praying.” Guéranger’s reply was short: “Il ne s’agit pas ici de ce que saint Célestin n’a pas dit, mais bien de ce que saint Célestin a dit” (“The question here is not what Saint Celestine did not say, but what Saint Celestine did say”).7 A century later Pius XII made the same inversion, with the same Latin word order, in Mediator Dei.

Notes

  • The campaign succeeded. The return to the Roman books began before Guéranger wrote, with Bishop Parisis of Langres in 1839, and it spread diocese by diocese. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that Guéranger “fought to have the Roman liturgy substituted for the diocesan liturgies, and he lived to see his efforts in this line crowned with complete success.” Orléans, Fayet’s own see, was among the last to give up its neo-Gallican books, in the mid-1870s.8 For the history of the rule, the campaign is a case of one received rite replacing many local ones in the name of the lex orandi. The 2007–2021 dispute over the Roman Missal, set out on the timeline, turns on the same two values, the unity of the rite and the authority of what has been handed down.
  • Which way it runs. Guéranger reads the adage as Prosper wrote it, prayer establishing belief, and he widens Prosper’s “law” to the whole ancient liturgy. Prosper’s own sense was narrower, the apostolic precept of intercession that the whole Church obeys; that sense is set out on the timeline’s Prosper page. Guéranger also treats the axiom as papal law, on the strength of the Celestine attribution, though he knew that Prosper was thought to be its author. The direction is weighed at Does the law of prayer establish the law of belief? (contested). His own theory of heresy, in which a new doctrine rewrites the books, describes the reverse mechanism, and it is cited at the belief-governs-prayer argument.
  • Not in the Année liturgique. His devotional commentary on the liturgical year (from 1841) does not appear to quote the adage. A search of its general preface found no instance. The axiom belongs to his polemical and historical work.
