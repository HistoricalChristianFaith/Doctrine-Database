Cyprian of Carthage on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 252 (On the Lord’s Prayer; timeline anchor) · the letters below run from 253 to 256 · Cyprian c. 200/210–258, bishop of Carthage from 248/249 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian calls the Lord’s Prayer a “law.” In his treatise on it the lex orationis is the norm the prayer sets for those who pray it: it is said in common, for the whole people, and from it each Christian learns “what he ought to be.” That law governs prayer and life rather than doctrine. But in the dispute over baptism by heretics (255–256) Cyprian made a real argument from rite to doctrine. The candidate is asked, “Dost thou believe in eternal life and remission of sins through the holy Church?” So, he argued, sins are forgiven only in the Church, and heretics who use the same question “lie in their interrogatory.” He even speaks of the “law of the Creed” (symboli lex). In the same dispute, though, his opponent Pope Stephen appealed to received practice, and Cyprian answered that “custom without truth is the antiquity of error.” Cyprian appealed to the words of the rite, but not to custom as such.

Claims & evidence

“This law of prayer”: the Lord’s Prayer is said in common (On the Lord’s Prayer 8)

Our prayer is public and common; and when we pray, we pray not for one, but for the whole people, because we the whole people are one. The God of peace and the Teacher of concord, who taught unity, willed that one should thus pray for all, even as He Himself bore us all in one. This law of prayer the three children observed when they were shut up in the fiery furnace, speaking together in prayer, and being of one heart in the agreement of the spirit.

— On the Lord’s Prayer 8 (252), source.1 The Latin is Hanc orationis legem servaverunt tres pueri.2 The “law” is drawn from the wording of the prayer Christ taught. It says “Our Father” and “give us,” not “my” and “me,” so Christian prayer must be common prayer, and the unity of the Church is written into it. Cyprian’s treatise follows Tertullian’s On Prayer closely (see Tertullian).

From the law of prayer each learns “what he ought to be” (On the Lord’s Prayer 20)

For which result, that every one of us may be able to prepare himself, let him thus learn to pray, and know, from the character of the prayer, what he ought to be.

— On the Lord’s Prayer 20, source.3 The English “from the character of the prayer” translates de orationis lege, “from the law of the prayer.” Paul De Clerck singles out this sentence and chapter 8 as the two places where Cyprian calls the Lord’s Prayer the lex orationis, and he notes how strikingly the treatise’s opening words, “among His other saving admonitions and divine precepts,” echo the liturgical introduction to the Lord’s Prayer.4 The context is the petition for daily bread, which Cyprian reads as a warning against riches. So what the law of prayer teaches here is a way of life. Elsewhere in the treatise the forgiveness clause is called a law in the same sense: Christ “added a law” (addidit legem), binding us to forgive as we ask to be forgiven (ch. 23).

“Thy will be done … in earth” is a prayer for unbelievers (On the Lord’s Prayer 17)

… so pray and ask by the admonition of Christ as to make our prayer for the salvation of all men; that as in heaven—that is, in us by our faith—the will of God has been done, so that we might be of heaven; so also in earth—that is, in those who believe not—God’s will may be done, that they who as yet are by their first birth of earth, may, being born of water and of the Spirit, begin to be of heaven.

— On the Lord’s Prayer 17, source.5 Cyprian offers this as a second way of reading the petition. He draws no conclusion about grace from it. It became important later. Augustine quoted this chapter against those who held that the first step of faith is ours: if the Church asks God that unbelievers may believe, then faith is God’s gift. So Cyprian’s commentary supplied one of the prayers from which the argument behind the adage was built.

“The very interrogation which is put in baptism is a witness of the truth” (Epistle 69)

But, moreover, the very interrogation which is put in baptism is a witness of the truth. For when we say, “Dost thou believe in eternal life and remission of sins through the holy Church?” we mean that remission of sins is not granted except in the Church, and that among heretics, where there is no Church, sins cannot be put away. Therefore they who assert that heretics can baptize, must either change the interrogation or maintain the truth; unless indeed they attribute a church also to those who, they contend, have baptism.

— Epistle 69.2 (Oxford/CSEL ed. 70), to Januarius and the Numidian bishops (255), source.6 Here Cyprian argues from a liturgical text to a doctrine. The words of the baptismal question tie the forgiveness of sins to the Church. Anyone who says that heretics baptize validly must either drop those words or grant the conclusion. The letter goes straight on to a second argument from the rite. The baptized must be anointed with oil “sanctified on the altar,” and a heretic “who has neither an altar nor a church” cannot sanctify it.

“There is not one law of the Creed” for the Church and schismatics (Epistle 75)

But if any one objects, by way of saying that Novatian holds the same law which the Catholic Church holds, baptizes with the same symbol with which we baptize, knows the same God and Father, the same Christ the Son, the same Holy Spirit, and that for this reason he may claim the power of baptizing, namely, that he seems not to differ from us in the baptismal interrogatory; let any one that thinks that this may be objected, know first of all, that there is not one law of the Creed, nor the same interrogatory common to us and to schismatics. For when they say, “Dost thou believe the remission of sins and life eternal through the holy Church?” they lie in their interrogatory, since they have not the Church.

— Epistle 75.7 (Oxford/CSEL ed. 69), to Magnus (255), source.7 The objector had argued from the rite too. The Novatianists use the same creed and ask the same questions, so their baptism should stand. Cyprian replies that the creed and the questions are not the same when the one asking has no Church: non esse unam nobis et schismaticis symboli legem neque eandem interrogationem.8 The “law of the Creed” here is the creed as a rule put to the candidate in baptism. It is the nearest pre-Nicene phrase to a lex credendi, and it lives inside the rite.

Custom must answer to Christ’s truth (Epistles 62 and 73)

There is then no reason, dearest brother, for any one to think that the custom of certain persons is to be followed, who have thought in time past that water alone should be offered in the cup of the Lord. … Wherefore, if Christ alone must be heard, we ought not to give heed to what another before us may have thought was to be done, but what Christ, who is before all, first did. Neither is it becoming to follow the practice of man, but the truth of God.

— Epistle 62.14 (Oxford/CSEL ed. 63), to Caecilius (253), source.9 Some bishops offered only water in the eucharistic cup. Cyprian corrects their practice by what Christ did at the Supper. Here the direction is the reverse of an argument from prayer: the dominical tradition judges the liturgy. He used the same principle against Stephen of Rome, who had ruled that converts from heresy be received by the laying on of hands:

If any one, therefore, come to you from any heresy whatever, let nothing be innovated (or done) which has not been handed down, to wit, that hands be imposed on him for repentance.

Nor ought custom, which had crept in among some, to prevent the truth from prevailing and conquering; for custom without truth is the antiquity of error.

— Epistle 73.1 (quoting Stephen) and 73.9 (Oxford/CSEL ed. 74), to Pompey (256), source.10 Stephen’s rule is itself an appeal to received practice: do nothing that has not been handed down. Cyprian treats it as mere custom and sets Scripture and truth above it. See Stephen of Rome for the Roman side of the dispute.

Notes

  • Both sides argued from the rite. In the baptismal controversy the liturgy was evidence for both parties. Cyprian argued from the words of the baptismal question. Stephen argued from the received way of receiving converts. The Council of Arles (314), which ruled against the African practice, also decided by the rite. A convert from heresy was to be asked the creed. If he had been baptized “in the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit,” he received only the laying on of hands; if he could not answer “this Trinity,” he was to be baptized. The canon calls the African practice their “own law” (propria lege).11 So the argument from the rite settled nothing by itself here. Each side read a different part of the rite as the decisive one: the clause on the Church, or the Trinitarian names. Cyprian’s conclusion was not received, though his standing as a saint was untouched (see Cyprian was wrong on rebaptism yet a saint).
  • Which way does it run? For Cyprian neither prayer nor practice stands on its own. The Lord’s Prayer is a law because Christ gave it, and the baptismal question binds because it states the faith the Church holds. Where a custom cannot show that warrant, it is “the antiquity of error.” This is the same balance found in Tertullian, whom Jerome reports Cyprian called “the master” (see Cyprian on Tertullian), and later in Augustine, whose prayer argument rests on prayers that Scripture commands.
