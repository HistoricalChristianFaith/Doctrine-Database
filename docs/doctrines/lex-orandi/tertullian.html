Tertullian on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 210 (The Chaplet and On the Resurrection of the Flesh, 208–212; timeline anchor) · the passages below run from an early treatise on prayer to c. 213 · Tertullian c. 155–220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian of Carthage defends the Church’s worship as a body of unwritten law. In The Chaplet (De corona) he lists customs that no Scripture commands, among them the renunciation and triple immersion at baptism, the offerings for the dead, and the sign of the cross. He says tradition originated them, custom confirmed them and faith keeps them, and that “in civil matters custom is accepted as law.” But in that passage he is arguing from practice to practice, to justify a Christian soldier’s refusal of the laurel crown, not from practice to doctrine. Elsewhere he does argue from the rites to doctrine. Christians are immersed three times, once at each name, so the Father, Son and Spirit are three persons. The flesh is washed, anointed and fed with Christ’s body, so the flesh will share in salvation. And Marcion’s own sacraments use the Creator’s water, oil and bread. Yet he also sets a firm limit. The rule of faith is “immoveable and irreformable,” and “Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom.”

Claims & evidence

Unwritten customs held “on the ground of tradition alone” (The Chaplet 3–4)

If no passage of Scripture has prescribed it, assuredly custom, which without doubt flowed from tradition, has confirmed it. For how can anything come into use, if it has not first been handed down? … To deal with this matter briefly, I shall begin with baptism. When we are going to enter the water, but a little before, in the presence of the congregation and under the hand of the president, we solemnly profess that we disown the devil, and his pomp, and his angels. Hereupon we are thrice immersed, making a somewhat ampler pledge than the Lord has appointed in the Gospel. … As often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead as birthday honours. … At every forward step and movement, at every going in and out, when we put on our clothes and shoes, when we bathe, when we sit at table, when we light the lamps, on couch, on seat, in all the ordinary actions of daily life, we trace upon the forehead the sign.

If, for these and other such rules, you insist upon having positive Scripture injunction, you will find none. Tradition will be held forth to you as the originator of them, custom as their strengthener, and faith as their observer. … But even in civil matters custom is accepted as law, when positive legal enactment is wanting; and it is the same thing whether it depends on writing or on reason, since reason is, in fact, the basis of law.

— The Chaplet, or De Corona 3–4 (208–212), source.1 This is the passage the surveys of lex orandi usually cite. A Christian soldier had refused to wear the laurel wreath at an imperial donative, and other Christians objected that no Scripture forbids it. Tertullian answers that much of what the Church does has no written warrant. Baptism, the Eucharist taken before dawn, the anniversary offerings for the dead, no kneeling on Sundays or between Easter and Pentecost, and the sign of the cross all rest on tradition and custom. The Latin brings out the legal framing: Harum et aliarum eiusmodi disciplinarum si legem expostules, “if you demand a law for these and other such disciplines,” and consuetudo autem etiam in civilibus rebus pro lege suscipitur.2 So custom can have the force of law. But what it rules here is disciplina, the Church’s practice, and the conclusion drawn from it is another practice. No doctrine is proved from the rites in these chapters. On the anniversary offerings, see Tertullian on purgatory.

Three immersions, at the three names, show three persons (Against Praxeas 26)

After His resurrection He promises in a pledge to His disciples that He will send them the promise of His Father; and lastly, He commands them to baptize into the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost, not into a unipersonal God. And indeed it is not once only, but three times, that we are immersed into the Three Persons, at each several mention of Their names.

— Against Praxeas 26 (c. 213), source.3 This is a true argument from rite to doctrine. Praxeas held that Father and Son are one person under two names. Tertullian’s chief proof is Scripture, ending with the command to baptize in Matthew 28:19. But he adds the rite as a further witness: the Church does not immerse once into one name but three times, once at each name. The Latin is nec semel sed ter, ad singula nomina in personas singulas, tinguimur.4 The same triple immersion appears in The Chaplet among the customs that go beyond what the Gospel commands. So the unwritten custom of the one book is used as evidence for doctrine in the other. See Tertullian on the Trinity.

The sacraments are given to the flesh, so the flesh will be saved (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 8)

The flesh, indeed, is washed, in order that the soul may be cleansed; the flesh is anointed, that the soul may be consecrated; the flesh is signed (with the cross), that the soul too may be fortified; the flesh is shadowed with the imposition of hands, that the soul also may be illuminated by the Spirit; the flesh feeds on the body and blood of Christ, that the soul likewise may fatten on its God. They cannot then be separated in their recompense, when they are united in their service.

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh 8 (208–212), source.5 The chapter’s heading in the English edition is “Christianity, by Its Provision for the Flesh, Has Put on It the Greatest Honour.” The rites of initiation, the washing, anointing, signing and laying on of hands, and then the Eucharist, are all done to the body. A body so used in God’s service cannot be shut out of the reward. This is Irenaeus’s argument from the Eucharist (Against Heresies IV.18.5, V.2.3), widened to the whole rite of initiation. See Tertullian on the resurrection of the body.

Marcion’s own sacraments use the Creator’s elements (Against Marcion I.14)

Indeed, up to the present time, he has not disdained the water which the Creator made wherewith he washes his people; nor the oil with which he anoints them; nor that union of honey and milk wherewithal he gives them the nourishment of children; nor the bread by which he represents his own proper body, thus requiring in his very sacraments the “beggarly elements” of the Creator.

— Against Marcion I.14 (second edition, 207–208), source.6 Marcion taught that the good God is a stranger to the world the Creator made. Tertullian points out that Marcion’s god still uses the Creator’s water, oil, milk and honey, and bread in his sacraments. It is the same argument ad hominem that Irenaeus turned on the Gnostics: the heretic’s own rite gives the lie to his doctrine.

The widow’s prayers for her husband show the marriage bond still holds (On Monogamy 10)

Indeed, she prays for his soul, and requests refreshment for him meanwhile, and fellowship (with him) in the first resurrection; and she offers (her sacrifice) on the anniversaries of his falling asleep. For, unless she does these deeds, she has in the true sense divorced him, so far as in her lies.

— On Monogamy 10 (c. 213 or later), source.7 This is an argument from a practice of prayer to a moral conclusion. By now a Montanist, Tertullian held that a widow may not remarry. His proof here is what the widow already does. She prays for her husband’s soul and makes the anniversary offering, so she treats him as still her husband, and she would be wronging him by taking another. The conclusion, against second marriage, is one the wider Church did not accept. See Tertullian on purgatory for the prayers for the dead and Tertullian on remarriage for the conclusion.

Custom does not overrule truth, and the rule of faith does not change (On the Veiling of Virgins 1)

But our Lord Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom. … Whatever savours of opposition to truth, this will be heresy, even (if it be an) ancient custom. … The rule of faith, indeed, is altogether one, alone immoveable and irreformable; the rule, to wit, of believing in one only God omnipotent, the Creator of the universe, and His Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin Mary, crucified under Pontius Pilate, raised again the third day from the dead, received in the heavens, sitting now at the right (hand) of the Father, destined to come to judge quick and dead through the resurrection of the flesh as well (as of the spirit). This law of faith being constant, the other succeeding points of discipline and conversation admit the “novelty” of correction.

— On the Veiling of Virgins 1 (c. 213), source.8 Here custom is on the other side. The churches did not agree on whether unmarried women should be veiled. Tertullian notes that most churches of Greece veil them, as do some in Africa, while others do not, and he will not let the matter be settled by custom: Christus veritatem se, non consuetudinem cognominavit. He also names the creed itself a law: lege fidei manente, “this law of faith being constant.”9 So for Tertullian the law of faith is fixed and comes first. Discipline, including the Church’s customs, is what can be corrected.

The Lord’s Prayer is “an epitome of the whole Gospel” (On Prayer 1–2)

For it has embraced not only the special duties of prayer, be it veneration of God or petition for man, but almost every discourse of the Lord, every record of His Discipline; so that, in fact, in the Prayer is comprised an epitome of the whole Gospel.

— On Prayer 1, an early treatise from before his Montanist years, source.10 His commentary then reads each petition for its doctrine. The first words, “Our Father,” are “a testimony to God, and … the reward of faith,” for “we at once pray to God, and commend faith” (ch. 2). Here the prayer carries the faith in compressed form. Cyprian’s treatise on the Lord’s Prayer follows this one closely (see Cyprian).

Notes

  • Two kinds of argument. Tertullian is cited as a forerunner of lex orandi for two different reasons, and they should be kept apart. The Chaplet 3–4 shows that unwritten liturgical custom had the standing of law in the Church. But it uses that law to settle a point of practice. Against Praxeas 26, On the Resurrection of the Flesh 8 and Against Marcion I.14 are the passages where a rite is evidence for a doctrine. In the last two, as in Irenaeus, the opponent shares the rite, so the argument shows that his doctrine contradicts his own worship.
  • Which way does it run? In On the Veiling of Virgins the law of faith is fixed and custom answers to truth. This is closer to the later reversal, “let the law of belief determine the law of prayer,” than to Prosper’s formula. In Tertullian the two are not in conflict. Custom that agrees with the rule of faith is authoritative, and custom against the truth is “heresy, even (if it be an) ancient custom.” He does not say how one would tell a custom of the first kind from one of the second, except by the truth it must answer to. The three answers to the direction question are weighed under which way the adage runs.
