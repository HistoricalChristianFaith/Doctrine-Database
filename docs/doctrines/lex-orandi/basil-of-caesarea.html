Basil of Caesarea on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 375 (On the Holy Spirit; timeline anchor) · the letters below are placed in 373 · Basil c. 330–379, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia from 370 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil’s On the Holy Spirit began with a dispute about a prayer. Leading the people in prayer, he had used two forms of the doxology: glory to the Father “with the Son together with the Holy Ghost,” and glory to the Father “through the Son in the Holy Ghost.” Opponents charged him with innovation. His defence sets out an order in three steps: “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.” Baptism into the three names, which Christ commanded, fixes the creed, and the creed fixes the doxology. So the doxology does not ground belief for Basil; it follows from it. He also argued that much of what the Church does in its rites comes from unwritten apostolic tradition, and he gathered old witnesses, including a hymn the people sang at the lighting of the lamps, to show that the doxology “with the Spirit” was not new.

Claims & evidence

The dispute began with a doxology (On the Holy Spirit 1.3)

Lately when praying with the people, and using the full doxology to God the Father in both forms, at one time “with the Son together with the Holy Ghost,” and at another “through the Son in the Holy Ghost,” I was attacked by some of those present on the ground that I was introducing novel and at the same time mutually contradictory terms.

— On the Holy Spirit 1.3, trans. Blomfield Jackson, NPNF² vol. 8, source.1 The treatise was written for Amphilochius, bishop of Iconium, about 375.2 The objectors held that “in the Spirit” was the only proper form, and that “with” set the Spirit on a level with the Father and the Son. Both sides treated the wording of a prayer as a statement of doctrine. That is why Basil spends a treatise on a few syllables: their “petty exactitude” about prepositions, he says, served a claim that the Spirit is unlike the Father and the Son in nature (2.4).

“As we were baptized, so we profess our belief” (Letters 159 and 125)

As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise. As then baptism has been given us by the Saviour, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, so, in accordance with our baptism, we make the confession of the creed, and our doxology in accordance with our creed. We glorify the Holy Ghost together with the Father and the Son, from the conviction that He is not separated from the Divine Nature; for that which is foreign by nature does not share in the same honors.

— Letter 159.2, to Eupaterius and his daughter (placed about 373), source.3 The same order appears in the statement of faith Basil drew up for Eustathius of Sebasteia to sign in 373: “we are bound to be baptized in the terms we have received and to profess belief in the terms in which we are baptized, and as we have professed belief in, so to give glory to Father, Son, and Holy Ghost.”4 The chain has three links, and they run one way. The rite of baptism, given by Christ, comes first. The creed follows from it. The doxology comes last. Honour in worship is given because of what the Spirit is: “that which is foreign by nature does not share in the same honors.”

The confession of faith is “the origin and parent of the doxology” (On the Holy Spirit 10.26, 27.68)

We have deemed ourselves under a necessary obligation to combine in our confession of the faith Him who is numbered with Them at Baptism, and we have treated the confession of the faith as the origin and parent of the doxology. What, then, is to be done? They must now instruct us either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed. Let any man prove if he can that the relation of sequence in these acts is not necessary and unbroken; or let any man deny if he can that innovation here must mean ruin everywhere.

— On the Holy Spirit 27.68, source.5 Earlier in the treatise Basil had charged his readers to “keep the Spirit undivided from the Father and the Son, preserving, both in the confession of faith and in the doxology, the doctrine taught them at their baptism” (10.26). Just before that he calls the ranking of the Spirit with the Father “the law prescribed by the Lord” in the command to baptize (10.25).6 The structure is close to the one later scholars find in Prosper. The binding “law” is a command of Christ recorded in Scripture, and the Church’s practice of that command is what carries the argument (see the timeline on De Clerck’s reading). The one difference is the rite. For Prosper it is the intercessions; for Basil it is baptism.

Unwritten traditions have “the same force” (On the Holy Spirit 27.66–67)

Of the beliefs and practices whether generally accepted or publicly enjoined which are preserved in the Church some we possess derived from written teaching; others we have received delivered to us “in a mystery” by the tradition of the apostles; and both of these in relation to true religion have the same force. … For instance, to take the first and most general example, who is thence who has taught us in writing to sign with the sign of the cross those who have trusted in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ? What writing has taught us to turn to the East at the prayer? Which of the saints has left us in writing the words of tim invocation at the displaying of the bread of the Eucharist and the cup of blessing? … Moreover we bless the water of baptism and the oil of the chrism, and besides this the catechumen who is being baptized. On what written authority do we do this? Is not our authority silent and mystical tradition? Nay, by what written word is the anointing of oil itself taught? And whence comes the custom of baptizing thrice? And as to the other customs of baptism from what Scripture do we derive the renunciation of Satan and his angels?

— On the Holy Spirit 27.66 (the NPNF text reads “tim invocation”, i.e. “the invocation”), source.7 Basil goes on to the most striking case. Even “the very confession of our faith in Father, Son, and Holy Ghost,” he asks, “what is the written source?” If his opponents accept the baptismal confession without a text, they should accept the doxology too, “a single word which has come down to us from the Fathers” (27.67).8 This passage is one of the classic texts for unwritten tradition. Its subject, though, is liturgical usage: the sign of the cross, the direction of prayer, the words of the eucharistic invocation, the blessing of the font, triple immersion. Shapland notes that Basil appeals to unwritten tradition “upon a point of liturgical usage,” and does not in fact rest the doctrine of the Spirit on it.9

The lamp-lighting hymn and the old doxologies (On the Holy Spirit 29.71–75)

It seemed fitting to our fathers not to receive the gift of the light at eventide in silence, but, on its appearing, immediately to give thanks. Who was the author of these words of thanksgiving at the lighting of the lamps, we are not able to say. The people, however, utter the ancient form, and no one has ever reckoned guilty of impiety those who say “We praise Father, Son, and God’s Holy Spirit.”

— On the Holy Spirit 29.73, source.10 Basil pleads like “a defendant who is indicted on a charge of innovation.” He brings written witnesses who closed with glory “with” the Spirit: Dionysius of Alexandria, who says he received the form “from the presbyters who were before us”; Julius Africanus; and Origen, whose own views on the Spirit were “not always and everywhere sound,” so that “the powerful influence of tradition frequently impels men to express themselves in terms contradictory to their own opinions.” Then he turns to unwritten usage: the people’s evening hymn (usually identified with the Phos hilaron), the doxology instituted by Gregory the Wonderworker at Neocaesarea, and the idiom of the Mesopotamian churches. He concludes that the form comes from “whole nations, cities, custom going back beyond the memory of man.”11 Here the Church’s worship is evidence for the antiquity of a belief. Basil uses it to rebut the charge of novelty. The positive case for the Spirit’s rank still rests on Scripture and on the baptismal command (29.75).

Notes

  • Which way does it run? Basil states the order outright: baptism, then belief, then praise. The doxology is defended because it agrees with the faith, and the faith is defended because it agrees with the baptism Christ commanded. So for Basil the lex orandi in the narrow sense, the wording of the doxology, follows the lex credendi. But the rite at the head of the chain is liturgical too. If that is counted, his argument runs from the font to the creed and from the creed to the prayer. Old prayers like the lamp-lighting hymn serve a different purpose. They show that the Church had long prayed this way without being thought impious.
  • A prayer of Basil’s own name. About 520 the Scythian monks cited the anaphora that goes under Basil’s name, “which almost the whole East uses,” in their argument from prayer on grace (see the timeline).
