Aidan Kavanagh on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1984 (On Liturgical Theology, the Hale Memorial Lectures given in 1981; timeline anchor) · “Response: Primary Theology and Liturgical Act,” 1983 · 1929–2006 · Saint Meinrad obituary
Summary: Fr Aidan Kavanagh, a Benedictine monk of Saint Meinrad Archabbey, taught liturgy at Saint Meinrad and Notre Dame, and from 1974 at Yale Divinity School. His Hale Lectures, published as On Liturgical Theology, made the strongest modern case for reading Prosper’s sentence as he wrote it. The assembly’s worship is theologia prima, “primary theology,” and doctrinal reflection is secondary theology. The verb statuat makes the law of supplicating the foundation of the law of believing. To turn the sentence round “is to cancel out the meaning of the maxim.” He grants that belief “does indeed shape and influence” worship, but holds that it does not found it.

Claims & evidence

The assembly’s “adjustment” is theologia prima (1984)

This is how liturgies grow. Their growth is a function of adjustment to deep change caused in the assembly by its being brought regularly to the brink of chaos in the presence of the living God. It is the adjustment which is theological in all this. I hold that it is theology being born, theology in the first instance. It is what tradition has called theologia prima.

— On Liturgical Theology (New York: Pueblo, 1984), p. 74, source.1 The argument leads up to this from the page before. What results “in the first instance” from the liturgical experience “is deep change in the very lives of those who participate in the liturgical act. And deep change will affect their next liturgical act, howsoever slightly. To detect that change in the subsequent liturgical act will be to discover where theology has passed, rather as physics detects atomic particles in tracks of their passage through a liquid medium.” The next act differs from the last “because those who do the next act have been unalterably changed.”2 Theology, on this account, is first the worshippers’ adjustment to the God they have met. The adjustment shows over time in the rites themselves.

“Charwomen and shopkeepers”: who does primary theology (1984)

To argue with minds accustomed to thinking of theology in such a manner that theology at its genesis is communitarian, even proletarian; that it is aboriginally liturgical in context, partly conscious and partly unconscious; that it stems from an experience of near chaos; that it is long term and dialectical; and that its agents are more likely to be charwomen and shopkeepers than pontiffs and professors—all this …

— On Liturgical Theology, pp. 74–75, source.3 Later in the book he gives the primary theologian a name:

Mrs. Murphy and her pastor do not fail to be theologians at the point where the seminary professor who taught the pastor succeeds in being one. The professor is a secondary theologian. Mrs. Murphy and her pastor are primary theologians whose discourse in faith is carried on not by concepts and propositions nearly so much …

— On Liturgical Theology, pp. 146–147, source.4 The closing summary puts it as a thesis: “the true primary theologian in the Church is the liturgical assembly in each and every one of its members.”

The verb statuat: the law of worship “constitutes or founds” the law of belief (1984)

There is no doubt that the law of belief does indeed shape and influence the law of worship. But the maxim does not say this, nor does it need to. It says only that the latter constitutes or founds the former. To reverse this is to cancel out the meaning of the maxim in its original formulation. The law of belief does not constitute the law of worship. Thus the creeds and the reasoning which produced them are not the forces which produced baptism. Baptism gave rise to the trinitarian creeds. So too the eucharist produced, but was not produced by, a scriptural text, the eucharistic prayer, or all the various scholarly theories concerning the eucharistic real presence. Influenced by, yes. Constituted or produced by, no.

— On Liturgical Theology, p. 92, source.5 Kavanagh’s point is grammatical. In Prosper’s clause lex supplicandi is the subject of statuat and legem credendi its object, so the slogan “lex orandi, lex credendi,” which drops the verb, loses the direction the sentence had. His Worship article the year before made the same point. The adage becomes a mere tag “when the two laws are allowed to float free from each other by the removal of the verb which originally united them. That verb was statuat, as in lex supplicandi legem statuat credendi: The law of worshiping founds the law of believing” (⚠ unverified, as quoted by O. T. I. Wright).6 Wright notes that this Latin is not Prosper’s exact wording, which is ut legem credendi lex statuat supplicandi, though it keeps the same subject and object. The book’s claim that baptism produced the Trinitarian creeds is the one the timeline’s fourth-century witnesses make in their own terms. Basil’s “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief” is the classic instance.

“A shambles of the dialectic of revelation” (1984)

To reverse the maxim, subordinating the standard of worship to the standard of belief, makes a shambles of the dialectic of revelation. It was a Presence, not faith, which drew Moses to the burning bush, and what happened there was a revelation, not a seminar. It was a Presence, not faith, which drew the disciples to Jesus, and what happened then was not an educational program but his revelation to them of himself as the long-promised Anointed One, the redeeming because reconciling Messiah-Christos. … Here is where their lives are regularly being constituted and reconstituted under grace. Which is why lex supplicandi legem statuat credendi.

— On Liturgical Theology, p. 92, source.7 The sentence before it gives the reason: the lex credendi “is thus subordinated to the lex supplicandi because both standards exist and function only within the worshipping assembly’s own subordination of itself to its ever-present Judge, Savior, and unifying Spirit” (pp. 91–92).

The reversal reduces the liturgy to “a doxological envoi” (1984)

The liturgy no longer serves as the constitutive foundation for secondary theology, but is reduced to a doxological envoi which concludes the secondary enterprise and is wholly controlled by it. Lex supplicandi legem statuat credendi is effectively reversed, with the law of belief founding and constituting the law of worship.

— On Liturgical Theology, p. 83, source.8 This is his description of what happens when secondary theology takes over. A prayer or hymn then only closes a doctrinal argument, and the doctrine controls it. The consequence he draws is that the assembly “now must be regarded as a theological cipher drawing whatever theological awareness it has not from its own response to its graced encounter with the living God, but from sources found in ecclesiastical bureaucracies and within the walls of academe. The served has become servant, mistress has become handmaid.”

The two laws “subtly correlative” (1984)

In this view, lex supplicandi and lex credendi are not detachable or opposable laws but subtly correlative, the first founding the second, the second affecting (although not founding) the first. Each law functions in concert with the other within the discourse of primary theology.

— On Liturgical Theology, p. 150, source.9 The passage goes on to say that the lex credendi is not merely the work of secondary theologians in their studies. It “is constantly being worked out, sustained, and established as the faithful in assembly are constantly working out, sustaining, and establishing their lex supplicandi from one festive, ordered, aesthetic, canonical, and eschatological liturgical act to the next under grace.” Wright counts this as a shift in position by the end of the book and notes that it “is often missed.” It is the same view as the p. 92 “Influenced by, yes”: belief affects prayer, but only prayer founds belief.

Notes

  • Which way it runs. From prayer to belief, with an asymmetric qualification. Prayer founds belief. Belief can “shape and influence” prayer but never founds it, and making belief the standard for worship is the move he calls “a shambles.” Of the modern witnesses on this timeline he holds the prayer-to-belief direction most firmly, and he rests it on Prosper’s grammar more explicitly than any of them. Whether Prosper’s lex meant “worship” in Kavanagh’s broad sense, or the narrower apostolic precept of intercession, is a separate question; see the Prosper page and the argument on the original sense.
  • The target of the critique. One reviewer reads the p. 92 passage as a veiled criticism of a pope.10 The passages quoted here name no pope and no encyclical. The reversal they reject is, in wording, the one Pius XII made in Mediator Dei (1947), and the one Bishop Fayet had proposed to Guéranger a century before.
  • Against. Kavanagh’s critics, Nicholas Jesson among them, object that most believers form their convictions from catechesis, Scripture study and preaching as much as from the rite. They argue that his account of the assembly’s “adjustment” does not explain this.11 His position is weighed with the other answers to the direction question in the timeline’s Arguments section.
  • The text. The book is quoted from the scan of the 1984 Pueblo edition (pagination also used by the Liturgical Press reprint). Several secondary quotations of these passages differ slightly from the printed text, for example “however slightly” for “howsoever slightly” and the omission of “wholly” on p. 83. The 1983 Worship article is quoted only through Wright.
