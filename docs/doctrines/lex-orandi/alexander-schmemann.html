Alexander Schmemann on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1961 (Introduction to Liturgical Theology, Russian original, and “Theology and Eucharist”; timeline anchor) · later essays 1963–1972 · 1921–1983 · Wikipedia
Summary: Fr Alexander Schmemann, a Russian émigré priest who taught at the Orthodox Institute of St Sergius in Paris and then at St Vladimir’s Seminary in New York, founded the modern discipline he called “liturgical theology.” He read the adage as lex orandi est lex credendi and took it to run from prayer to belief: the Church’s worship is the source of its theology, and the law of prayer “determines” the law of faith. His charge against “school” theology, Eastern as well as Western, was that it had turned worship from a source into an object. He denied, though, that the liturgy is one “authority” to be ranked against others. For him it is the condition under which theology is possible at all.

Claims & evidence

The “rule of prayer” “determining” the “rule of faith” (1961)

The neglect of this source in scholastic theology is explained by a narrowing down of the concepts both of Tradition and of the Church. But the early Church firmly confessed the principle lex orandi lex est credendi. Therefore the science of liturgics cannot fail to be a theological science by its very character and purpose; and theology as a whole cannot do without the science of liturgics.

— Introduction to Liturgical Theology, trans. Asheleigh E. Moorhouse (London: Faith Press, 1966), ch. 1, source; Russian original, Введение в литургическое богословие (Paris, 1961).1 “This source” is the tradition of the sacraments and sacred rites, which, he notes, Metropolitan Philaret’s catechism counts as part of Tradition. Where the translation has “scholastic theology,” the Russian has школьное богословие, “school theology,” the phrase the translation keeps elsewhere in the book. A few lines earlier the chapter sets out the threefold task of liturgical theology. The third part is “to present the separate data of liturgical experience as a connected whole, as, in the last analysis, the ‘rule of prayer’ dwelling within the Church and determining her ‘rule of faith.’”2 The same chapter defines liturgical theology as “the elucidation of the meaning of worship.”

“The lex orandi must be recovered as the lex credendi” (1961)

Such reintegration is possible only when one ceases to abstract the Eucharist as “sacrament,” “sacrifice” or “communion” from the Eucharistic leitourgia, from the action in which all these aspects can be understood in their proper perspective and in their organic relation with one another. The lex orandi must be recovered as the lex credendi. The rediscovery of the Eucharist as the Sacrament of the Church is, in other words, the rediscovery of the Church in actu, the Church as the Sacrament of Christ, of His “parousia”— the coming and presence of the Kingdom which is to come.

— “Theology and Eucharist,” St. Vladimir’s Seminary Quarterly 5, no. 4 (1961), p. 22, source.3 The essay calls the Eucharist “the organic source and the necessary ‘term of reference’ of theology” and blames a long “Western captivity” for having made theology the business of “the ‘school’ alone” instead of “the whole Church.” The same page names the result: “It was precisely such a transformation of the Eucharist into an ‘object’ that obscured its function as the source of theology.”4 The scholastic treatises on “sacrament,” “sacrifice” and “communion” are the case in point. They take the rite as material for doctrine, where Schmemann wants doctrine read out of the rite.

Worship turned “from a source” into “an object” (1963) — ⚠ unverified

Theology here has an independent, rational status; it is a search for a system of consistent categories and concepts: intellectus fidei. The position of worship in relation to theology is reversed: from a source it becomes an object, which has to be defined and evaluated within the accepted categories (e.g., definitions of sacraments). Liturgy supplies theology with “data,” but the method of dealing with these data is independent of any liturgical context.

— “Theology and Liturgical Tradition” (1963), in Liturgy and Tradition, ed. Thomas Fisch (1990), p. 13, as quoted by David Fagerberg and Nicholas Jesson.5 “Scholastic” here names no single school or period. Schmemann says he means “a theological structure which existed in various forms in both the West and the East, and in which all ‘organic’ connection with worship is severed.” Jesson also quotes the essay’s next step. The Reformers, it says, replaced the medieval doctrine of worship with “another theology of worship, so that in the Protestant Churches the leitourgia remained a function of its theological conception and interpretation.”6 On his account, then, Protestant and scholastic theology make the same move: doctrine is set over the rite.

Not an “authority” but the “ontological condition” of theology (1963) — ⚠ unverified

The problem of the relationship between liturgy and theology is not for the Fathers a problem of priority or authority. Liturgical tradition is not an “authority” or a locus theologicus; it is the ontological condition of theology, of the proper understanding of kerygma, of the Word of God, because it is in the Church, of which the leitourgia is the expression and the life, that the sources of theology are functioning as precisely “sources.”

— “Theology and Liturgical Tradition,” in Liturgy and Tradition, p. 18, as quoted by Fagerberg.7 On the same page, as Fagerberg quotes it: “The formula lex orandi est lex credendi means nothing else than that theology is possible only within the Church.” This is the qualification that keeps his reading from being a simple reversal of Mediator Dei. He does not rank the liturgy above doctrine as a higher court. He makes it the setting in which Scripture and the Fathers work as sources at all.

“The Church’s faith itself” and the “original sin” of Western theology (1969) — ⚠ unverified

The ‘essence’ of the liturgy or lex orandi is ultimately nothing else but the Church’s faith itself or, better to say, the manifestation, communication and fulfillment of that faith. It is in this sense that one must understand, it seems to me, the famous dictum lex orandi est lex credendi.

— “Liturgical Theology, Theology of Liturgy, and Liturgical Reform,” St. Vladimir’s Theological Quarterly 13 (1969), in Liturgy and Tradition, pp. 38–39, as quoted by Fagerberg.8 The same essay, as Fagerberg quotes it, calls it “the ‘original sin’ of the entire western theological development that it made ‘texts’ the only loci theologici, the extrinsic ‘authorities’ of theology, disconnecting theology from its living source: liturgy and spirituality” (p. 42). Theology suffered, it says, “because theology ceased to seek in the lex orandi its source and food, because liturgy ceased to be conducive to theology” (pp. 46–47).9

“Lex orandi constitutes the lex credendi” (later essays) — ⚠ unverified

Theologians have forgotten the essential principle that lex orandi constitutes the lex credendi; they have forgotten the absolutely unique function of Christian worship within all theological speculation.

— “Liturgy and Eschatology,” in Liturgy and Tradition, p. 95, as quoted by Fagerberg.10 Fagerberg quotes the same direction from two other essays. “Liturgy and Theology” says that “Theology must rediscover as its own ‘rule of faith’ the Church’s lex orandi, and the liturgy reveal itself again as the lex credendi” (p. 68). “Liturgical Theology: Remarks on Method” calls the liturgy “that lex orandi in which the lex credendi finds its principal criterion and standard” (p. 138).11

Notes

  • Which way it runs. From prayer to belief. The Introduction has the rule of prayer “determining” the rule of faith, the 1961 essay wants the lex orandi “recovered as the lex credendi,” and the later essays say it “constitutes” it. He rarely quotes Prosper’s sentence. His form of the adage is the identity lex orandi est lex credendi, which he credits to “the early Church” without discussing its author or its first sense. That sense, the apostolic precept of intercession, is set out on the Prosper page. Whether that is what Prosper meant is weighed on its own argument page.
  • Not a contest of authorities. His readers disagree about how far to press the direction. Fagerberg writes that “Schmemann is not proposing that the liturgical tail wags the doctrinal dog.” He adds that the “proving ground” view which Pius XII rejected in Mediator Dei was, in his own view, an error the pope “was justly correcting.”12 None of the Schmemann essays surveyed here names Mediator Dei or its reversed formula. The contrast with the encyclical is drawn by his readers. His own target is wider: any theology, Orthodox included, that treats the rite as data for a system built elsewhere.
  • The Protestant case. His description of the Reformation, in which the rite became “a function of its theological conception,” matches what the timeline records of Luther and Cranmer, who rewrote the rites to fit a revised doctrine. Schmemann counts that as the same mistake as scholasticism. The Methodist Geoffrey Wainwright, on the timeline, describes the same pattern without condemning it.
  • Editions. Most of the essays are collected in Liturgy and Tradition: Theological Reflections of Alexander Schmemann, ed. Thomas Fisch (Crestwood, NY: St Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1990). “Theology and Eucharist” is reprinted there too (p. 79 onward). A further essay, “Theology and Liturgy,” Greek Orthodox Theological Review 17 (1972), reprinted in Church, World, Mission (1979), complains that theology cut off from the liturgy “is imprisoned in its own ‘data’ and ‘propositions’” (p. 94, as quoted by Fagerberg).13 The quotations marked ⚠ unverified are given as Fagerberg and Jesson print them, with their page references. They have not yet been checked against the 1990 volume.
