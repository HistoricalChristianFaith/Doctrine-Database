Thomas Aquinas on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1270 (Summa Theologiae II-II and III; timeline anchor) · Thomas 1225–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas never quotes the clause about the law of supplicating, but the Summa argues from the Church’s practice to doctrine in several places, and it still cites the Capitula as Pope Celestine’s. From the exorcisms before baptism, on “Pope Celestine’s” authority (chapter 9 of the Capitula), he concludes that the unbaptized are under the devil’s power through original sin. From the feast of Mary’s birth he concludes that she was sanctified in the womb. He holds that “the custom of the Church has very great authority,” more than any single doctor, and that in the rites of the Mass “the Church cannot err, since she is taught by the Holy Ghost.” But he also sets limits. A feast kept by some churches and only tolerated by Rome, the feast of Mary’s conception, “does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception.”

Claims & evidence

The exorcisms before baptism, on “Pope Celestine’s” authority (ST III q.71 a.2)

On the contrary, Pope Celestine says (Epist. ad Episcop. Galliae): “Whether children or young people approach the sacrament of regeneration, they should not come to the fount of life before the unclean spirit has been expelled from them by the exorcisms and breathings of the clerics.”

— Summa Theologiae III q.71 a.2, sed contra, source.1 This is chapter 9 of the Capitula, the argument from the baptismal rite that follows the argument from prayer in chapter 8. Aquinas cites it, as the canonical collections did, as part of Celestine’s letter to the Gallic bishops. The article asks whether exorcism should precede baptism, and the conclusion is the one the Capitula drew: “all who are not baptized are subject to the power of the demons, at least on account of the guilt of original sin” (ad 1).

The feast of Mary’s birth proves her sanctification in the womb (ST III q.27 a.1)

On the contrary, The Church celebrates the feast of our Lady’s Nativity. Now the Church does not celebrate feasts except of those who are holy. Therefore even in her birth the Blessed Virgin was holy. Therefore she was sanctified in the womb.

— Summa Theologiae III q.27 a.1, sed contra, source.2 The authority Aquinas sets against the objections is a feast, with a premise about what feasts mean: the Church keeps feasts only of the holy. It is the argument Bernard had made a century earlier.

But a tolerated feast does not define (ST III q.27 a.2 ad 3)

The same premise was turned against him. The third objection to the next article argues that “no feast is celebrated except of some saint,” that “some keep the feast of the Conception of the Blessed Virgin,” and that therefore she was holy in her very conception. Aquinas replies:

Although the Church of Rome does not celebrate the Conception of the Blessed Virgin, yet it tolerates the custom of certain churches that do keep that feast, wherefore this is not to be entirely reprobated. Nevertheless the celebration of this feast does not give us to understand that she was holy in her conception. But since it is not known when she was sanctified, the feast of her Sanctification, rather than the feast of her Conception, is kept on the day of her conception.

— Summa Theologiae III q.27 a.2 ad 3.3 Two distinctions limit the argument from a feast. One is between the practice of the Roman Church and a local custom Rome only tolerates. The other is between what a feast is called and what it celebrates: a feast on the day of the conception can honour her sanctification, whenever that came. Aquinas’s denial of the immaculate conception itself is set out on his Immaculate Conception page.

“The custom of the Church has very great authority” (ST II-II q.10 a.12)

I answer that, The custom of the Church has very great authority and ought to be jealously observed in all things, since the very doctrine of catholic doctors derives its authority from the Church. Hence we ought to abide by the authority of the Church rather than by that of an Augustine or a Jerome or of any doctor whatever. Now it was never the custom of the Church to baptize the children of the Jews against the will of their parents …

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.10 a.12, source.4 The question is a moral one, whether the children of Jews and other unbelievers may be baptized against their parents’ will. Aquinas answers no, and the first ground he gives is the Church’s custom: powerful Christian emperors and holy bishops never did it, though they could have. The custom outweighs the opinion of any single doctor, because the doctors’ authority itself comes from the Church. Here practice settles a question of right, not a point of doctrine about God, but the principle is general: what the Church does is a stronger authority than what any one teacher says.

The rites of the Mass: “the Church cannot err” (ST III q.83 a.5)

On the contrary, The custom of the Church stands for these things: and the Church cannot err, since she is taught by the Holy Ghost.

— Summa Theologiae III q.83 a.5, sed contra, source.5 The article defends the ceremonies of the Mass (the washing of hands, the signs of the cross, the priest’s turning to the people) against objections that they are superfluous or unfitting. Its whole answer rests on the Church’s custom, and the reply explains what each action signifies. The direction here runs from the Church’s authority to the rite: because the Church is taught by the Spirit, its rite is fitting. In the previous article, on the words of the Mass, Aquinas notes that Christ’s lifting up His eyes at the Supper is not written in the Gospels, but “the Roman Church had it by tradition from the apostles.”6

Notes

  • The Capitula as canon law. Aquinas’s citation of chapter 9 as “Pope Celestine” is the attribution the canonical collections had carried since Dionysius Exiguus (see the authorship argument). In the Summa he uses only the baptismal chapter, not chapter 8 and its clause on the law of supplicating.
  • Which way does it run? For Aquinas the Church’s worship and custom are an authority because the Church is taught by the Holy Spirit. That makes received, universal practice a real source of argument, sometimes the deciding one (the feast of the Nativity, the custom on Jewish children). But the authority belongs to the Church, not to any rite as such. A local custom Rome merely tolerates proves nothing, and the meaning of a feast is fixed by doctrine, not the other way round. Like Bernard, he argues from the received feast and declines to argue from the new one. His reply on the Conception feast is part of the case weighed at Did the Marian feasts lead the Church to define the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption? (plausible).
