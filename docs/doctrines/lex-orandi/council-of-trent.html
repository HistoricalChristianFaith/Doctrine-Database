The Council of Trent on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1562 (Session 22, 17 September 1562, Doctrine on the Sacrifice of the Mass; timeline anchor) · Session 21, 16 July 1562 · council 1545–1563 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Reformers had cut the Roman Canon because of what it said about sacrifice. Trent defended it on both grounds at once. It first defined the doctrine, that the Mass is a true and propitiatory sacrifice, and then declared the Canon that expresses it “so pure from every error” that nothing in it does not savour of holiness, since it is made “out of the very words of the Lord, the traditions of the apostles, and the pious institutions also of holy pontiffs.” Anyone who says the Canon “contains errors, and is therefore to be abrogated” is anathema. The council also defended the ceremonies, the low voice and the Latin. In the previous session it had stated the Church’s power to change the rites of the sacraments, “their substance being untouched.” For Trent the received rite is a witness to the faith, and the Church, not private judgement, decides what in the rite may change.

Claims & evidence

The Canon is “pure from every error” (Session 22, ch. 4)

And whereas it beseemeth, that holy things be administered in a holy manner, and of all holy things this sacrifice is the most holy; to the end that it might be worthily and reverently offered and received, the Catholic Church instituted, many years ago, the sacred Canon, so pure from every error, that nothing is contained therein which does not in the highest degree savour of a certain holiness and piety, and raise up unto God the minds of those that offer. For it is composed, out of the very words of the Lord, the traditions of the apostles, and the pious institutions also of holy pontiffs.

— Council of Trent, Session 22, Doctrine on the Sacrifice of the Mass, ch. 4, trans. J. Waterworth (1848), pp. 155–156, source.1 The Latin is “sacrum canonem multis ante saeculis instituit, ita ab omni errore purum, ut nihil in eo contineatur, quod non maxime sanctitatem ac pietatem quandam redoleat.” The chapter comes after three chapters that define the sacrifice: the Mass is the sacrifice Christ left to his Church, in which “that same Christ is contained and immolated in an unbloody manner” who once offered Himself on the cross. The Canon is then defended as the Church’s own prayer of that sacrifice. Its authority is its sources, the Lord’s words, apostolic tradition and papal institution, not any single text read in isolation.

The canons: the Canon, the ceremonies, the low voice

CANON III.—If any one saith, that the sacrifice of the mass is only a sacrifice of praise and of thanksgiving; or, that it is a bare commemoration of the sacrifice consummated on the cross, but not a propitiatory sacrifice; or, that it profits him only who receives; and that it ought not to be offered for the living and the dead for sins, pains, satisfactions, and other necessities; let him be anathema.

…

CANON VI.—If any one saith, that the canon of the mass contains errors, and is therefore to be abrogated; let him be anathema.

CANON VII.—If any one saith, that the ceremonies, vestments, and outward signs, which the Catholic Church makes use of in the celebration of masses, are incentives to impiety, rather than offices of piety; let him be anathema.

…

CANON IX.—If any one saith, that the rite of the Roman Church, according to which a part of the canon and the words of consecration are pronounced in a low tone, is to be condemned; or, that the mass ought to be celebrated in the vulgar tongue only; or, that water ought not to be mixed with the wine that is to be offered in the chalice, for that it is contrary to the institution of Christ; let him be anathema.

— Session 22, canons 3, 6, 7 and 9, Waterworth pp. 158–159, source.2 Each canon answers a Reformation change point for point. Canon 3 rejects the doctrine for which Luther had cut the Canon: the Mass is no “bare commemoration.” Canon 6 (“canonem Missae errores continere ideoque abrogandum esse”) rejects the cut itself. Canon 9 defends three things that Luther’s Formula Missae had changed or questioned: the Words of Institution said in a low voice, the Latin, and the water mixed with the wine. A first draft of chapter 4, from 1552, named the charge it was answering: some “impiously teach that it is full of errors, falsehood and distortion.”3

The ceremonies teach (ch. 5) and the Mass is to be explained (ch. 8)

And whereas such is the nature of man, that, without external helps, he cannot easily be raised to the meditation of divine things; therefore has holy Mother Church instituted certain rites … She has likewise employed ceremonies, such as mystic benedictions, lights, incense, vestments, and many other things of this kind, derived from an apostolical discipline and tradition, whereby both the majesty of so great a sacrifice might be recommended, and the minds of the faithful be excited, by those visible signs of religion and piety, to the contemplation of those most sublime things which are hidden in this sacrifice.

— Session 22, ch. 5, Waterworth p. 156, source.4 The rite is here a teacher of the doctrine: its visible signs commend the sacrifice and lead the faithful to what is “hidden” in it. Chapter 8 concedes that “the mass contains great instruction for the faithful people,” but keeps the Latin. To make up for it, pastors are to “expound … some portion of those things which are read at mass, and that, amongst the rest, they explain some mystery of this most holy sacrifice.” The council framed the canons as a defence of “this ancient faith, which is based on the sacred Gospel, the traditions of the Apostles, and the doctrine of the holy Fathers” (ch. 9).5

The Church may change the rites, “their substance being untouched” (Session 21, ch. 2)

It furthermore declares, that this power has ever been in the Church, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient, for the profit of those who receive, or for the veneration of the said sacraments, according to the difference of circumstances, times, and places.

— Session 21, Doctrine on Communion under Both Kinds, ch. 2, Waterworth pp. 141–142, source.6 The Latin is “salva illorum substantia.” The decree defends communion under one kind, a change the Latin Church had itself made in the Middle Ages (see Trent on communion under one kind). So Trent did not hold that the rite is untouchable. The Church may reshape it, within the limit of the sacrament’s substance. What it denied was that the Reformers’ doctrine gave them the right to reshape it.

The rite fixed: Pius V’s Missal (1570)

… We order and enjoin that nothing must be added to Our recently published Missal, nothing omitted from it, nor anything whatsoever be changed within it under the penalty of Our displeasure.

— Pius V, Quo primum (14 July 1570), source.7 The council had left the reform of the Missal to the pope. The bull that promulgated it required “all everywhere” to follow “what has been handed down by the Holy Roman Church,” and that Masses not be said “according to any other formula than that of this Missal.” (Rites more than two hundred years old were allowed to continue.) After Trent the Canon was fixed as a standard of the faith.

Notes

  • Both sides read the rite as doctrine. The Catholic liturgist Josef Jungmann put the quarrel this way: “Taking as his principle the Bible alone, Luther denied the sacrificial character of the Mass … All prayers in the Mass-liturgy in which there is any mention of this, particularly of sacrifice — like the canon — are bad human additions and must be dropped.” Trent’s answer, in his words, was that the council “clearly distinguished between truth and error and declared the objective character of the Sacrifice of the Mass … Thus the foundations of Catholic liturgy were secured.”8 Adrian Fortescue wrote that “during the Reformation and always the Mass has been the test.”9 Neither side cites Prosper’s formula in these debates. But each acts on one direction of it. The Reformers let the doctrine they found in Scripture rewrite the prayer. Trent defined the doctrine first, then vindicated the prayer that had carried it for centuries.
  • Which way does it run? Trent does not argue that the Canon proves the sacrifice. Its proofs in chapters 1–2 are from Scripture (Malachi 1:11, Hebrews, the Last Supper) and tradition. The Canon’s freedom from error is asserted after the doctrine is defined, as a consequence of it, and as a matter of the Church’s authority over its own rite. Jungmann notes that even a Catholic bishop at the council, Friedrich Nausea of Vienna, proposed changing the Canon.10 The same council’s Session 13 (1551) on the real presence and the worship of the sacrament is set out on the real-presence timeline. The three answers to the direction question are weighed under which way the adage runs.
