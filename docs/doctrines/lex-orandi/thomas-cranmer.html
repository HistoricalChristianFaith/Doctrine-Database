Thomas Cranmer on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 1549–1552 (the first and second Books of Common Prayer; timeline anchor) · Cranmer 1489–1556, archbishop of Canterbury from 1533 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cranmer rewrote the English rite in two stages, and each stage followed his doctrine. In his Defence of 1550 he called transubstantiation, the real presence “of Christ’s flesh and blood in the sacrament of the altar,” and the sacrifice offered by the priest “the roots of the weeds,” which had to be pulled up if the reform was to last. The 1549 Book still asked that the bread and wine “maie be unto us the bodye and bloude” of Christ. The 1552 Book asked instead that the communicants “maye be partakers of his most blessed body and bloud,” and gave the bread with the words “feede on him in thy hearte by faythe.” Its “Black Rubric” denied any adoration of the elements or any “reall and essencial presence” of Christ’s natural flesh in them. The prayer was reshaped so that it could not be read as teaching the doctrine it replaced. Whether the doctrine it then taught was Zwinglian, Calvinist or Cranmer’s own is still disputed.

Claims & evidence

The 1549 Book: “that they maie be unto us the bodye and bloude”

Heare us (O merciful father) we besech thee; and with thy holy spirite and worde, vouchsafe to blesse and sanctifie these thy gyftes, and creatures of bread and wyne, that they maie be unto us the bodye and bloude of thy moste derely beloved sonne Jesus Christe.

— The Book of Common Prayer (1549), the Supper of the Lord, prayer of consecration, source.1 The first English rite kept the shape of the old Canon, in English, and much of its language. The same prayer insists that Christ made on the cross “(by his one oblacion once offered) a full, perfect, and sufficient sacrifyce, oblacion, and satysfaccyon, for the sinnes of the whole worlde,” so the sacrifice of the Mass has gone. But the invocation over the gifts could still be read as a prayer for a change in them. The words of administration were “The body of our Lorde Jesus Christe whiche was geven for thee, preserve thy bodye and soule unto everlasting lyfe.”

The Defence (1550): pull up the roots

But what availeth it to take away beads, pardons, pilgrimages, and such other like popery, so long as two chief roots remain unpulled up? … The rest is but branches and leaves, the cutting away whereof is but like topping and lopping of a tree, or cutting down of weeds, leaving the body standing and the roots in the ground; but the very body of the tree, or rather the roots of the weeds, is the popish doctrine of transubstantiation, of the real presence of Christ’s flesh and blood in the sacrament of the altar (as they call it), and of the sacrifice and oblation of Christ made by the priest, for the salvation of the quick and the dead. Which roots if they be suffered to grow in the Lord’s vineyard, they will overspread all the ground again with the old errors and superstitions.

— A Defence of the True and Catholic Doctrine of the Sacrament (1550), Preface to the Reader, Parker Society ed. p. 6, source.2 This is the doctrinal program behind the second Book. The abuses of practice (beads, pardons, pilgrimages) are only branches. The roots are doctrines about the Eucharist, and a rite that leaves them standing will let the old errors grow back. Cranmer reprinted the preface in his Answer (1551) to Stephen Gardiner, who had cited passages of the 1549 Book as teaching that communicants “receive with their bodily mouth the Body and Blood of Christ.” Cranmer called that “a plain untruth.” Gregory Dix judged that “every word” of the 1549 prayer was “compatible with, and for the most part clearly expressed,” Cranmer’s own doctrine, but that Gardiner’s reading was “a reasonable inference from these passages taken alone.” So, Dix wrote, Cranmer “in 1552 took pains to alter every point in 1549 to which Gardiner had appealed.”3

The 1552 Book: “feede on him in thy hearte by faythe”

Heare us O mercyefull father wee beeseche thee; and graunt that wee, receyving these thy creatures of bread and wyne, accordinge to thy sonne our Savioure Jesus Christ’s holy institucion, in remembraunce of his death and passion, maye be partakers of his most blessed body and bloud:

…

Take and eate this, in remembraunce that Christ dyed for thee, and feede on him in thy hearte by faythe, with thankesgeving.

— The Book of Common Prayer (1552), the Lord’s Supper, prayer of consecration and words of administration, source.4 The petition is no longer that the bread and wine “be unto us” Christ’s body and blood, but that the communicants who receive them “be partakers” of it. The invocation of the Spirit and the word over the gifts is gone. The cup is given with “Drinke this in remembraunce that Christ’s bloude was shed for thee, and be thankefull.” The words point away from the elements to the communicant’s faith and to the cross. Dix wrote that in 1552 Cranmer “made radical changes … in order to bring out the doctrinal implications of 1549.”

The “Black Rubric”: “they are in heaven and not here”

… we dooe declare that it is not ment thereby, that any adoracion is doone, or oughte to bee doone, eyther unto the Sacramentall bread or wyne there bodily receyved, or unto anye reall and essencial presence there beeyng of Christ’s naturall fleshe and bloude. For as concernynge the Sacramentall bread and wyne, they remayne styll in theyr verye naturall substaunces, and therefore may not be adored, for that were Idolatrye to be abhorred of all faythfull christians. And as concernynge the naturall body and blood of our saviour Christ, they are in heaven and not here. For it is agaynst the trueth of Christes true natural bodye, to be in moe places then in one, at one tyme.

— The Book of Common Prayer (1552), declaration on kneeling at the end of the Communion, source.5 The rubric was added late, in the autumn of 1552, by order of the Privy Council, after John Knox and others had argued that communicants should sit. The first copies were already printed, and it was pasted into them. It explains why the people still received “kneling.” Here doctrine is written into the rubrics to fix how a gesture of prayer may be understood. Kneeling was kept, and its meaning was fixed by a statement of belief. Dropped from the Elizabethan Book of 1559, the rubric was restored in 1662 with one telling change: “no Adoration is intended … unto any Corporal Presence of Christ’s natural Flesh and Blood,” where 1552 had denied a “reall and essencial presence.”6

“Of Ceremonies”: what blinds the people is cut away

This our excessive multitude of Ceremonies, was so great, and many of them so darke: that they dyd more confounde and darken, then declare and sette forth Christes benefites unto us. … which not onely for their unprofitablenesse, but also because they have muche blynded the people, and obscured the glory of God, are worthy to be cut awaye, and cleane rejected.

— The Book of Common Prayer (1549), “Of Ceremonies, why some be abolished and some retayned,” source.7 The preface gives the rule by which the rite was judged. A ceremony is kept if it “declares and sets forth” Christ’s benefits and cut away if it darkens them. Its “most weightye cause” for abolishing ceremonies was that they had been abused “by the supersticious blyndenes of the rude and unlearned.” The preface stood, nearly word for word, in the 1552 and 1662 Books.

The Articles: the sacrifices of Masses “blasphemous fables”

Wherefore the sacrifices of Masses, in the which it was commonly said, that the Priest did offer Christ for the quick and the dead, to have remission of pain or guilt, were blasphemous fables, and dangerous deceits.

— Articles of Religion, Art. XXXI, source.8 The Articles descend from Cranmer’s Forty-Two Articles of 1553, revised as the Thirty-Nine (1563, 1571). Article XXVIII adds that the sacrament “was not by Christ’s ordinance reserved, carried about, lifted up, or worshipped.” Each clause matches a ceremony the Prayer Book had removed: reservation, procession, elevation and adoration. The Articles state as doctrine what the rite already enacted.

Notes

  • Which doctrine did 1552 teach? Gregory Dix (1945) called the doctrine of 1552 “the full Zwinglian doctrine.” Others have answered him. C. W. Dugmore argued that Cranmer made concessions to more radical Reformers. Peter Brooks read him as essentially Calvinist or Bucerian, Cyril Richardson as nearer Zwingli than Brooks allowed. Diarmaid MacCulloch’s biography (1996) is widely taken to have answered Dix.9 The dispute is about which belief the rite expressed, not about whether the belief shaped it. On that, Dix and his critics agree.
  • The 1662 settlement. The 1662 Book gave the bread with both formulas, 1549’s and 1552’s together: “The Body of our Lord Jesus Christ, which was given for thee, preserve thy body and soul unto everlasting life: Take and eat this in remembrance that Christ died for thee, and feed on him in thy heart by faith with thanksgiving.”10 That Book, by Act of Parliament and later by canon, became itself a standard of the Church of England’s doctrine (see the timeline). So a rite written to express a doctrine became in time a source from which the doctrine is learned.
  • Elsewhere on this wiki. At Wittenberg Luther had cut the Canon for the same reason, the sacrifice. Unlike Cranmer, he kept the bodily presence. Both are answered by the Council of Trent’s defence of the Roman Canon (1562). The real-presence timeline sets out Luther, Zwingli and Calvin on the presence itself.
