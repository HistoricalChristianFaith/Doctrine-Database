Augustine of Hippo on lex orandi, lex credendi

Dates: c. 427 (Letter 217, to Vitalis of Carthage; timeline anchor) · the passages below run from 412 to 429 · Augustine 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine never wrote the formula, but he made the argument it compresses. Against the Pelagians, and then against Christians who held that the first step toward faith is our own, he argued from what the Church actually prays. The priest at the altar asks God to convert unbelievers, to give catechumens the desire for baptism, and to keep the faithful to the end. The faithful answer “Amen.” If these things were in our own power, the prayers would be empty. So faith and perseverance are God’s gifts, and the Church, which has always prayed this way, has always believed it. He made a parallel argument from the baptismal rite: the Church exorcises infants and breathes on them to drive out the devil, so they must be under his power by original sin. Prosper of Aquitaine, his disciple, set both arguments side by side in chapters 8 and 9 of the Capitula, where the first received its famous formula.

Claims & evidence

The apostle’s command to pray is kept in the Church’s liturgy

I prefer to understand by these words what the entire, or almost the entire, church observes: that we take as supplications those prayers which are said in celebrating the mysteries, before we begin to consecrate what lies on the table of the Lord. Prayers are said when it is blessed and sanctified and broken for distribution; and the whole church, for the most part, closes this complete petition with the Lord's Prayer.

— Letter 149.16 (to Paulinus of Nola, 414), on 1 Timothy 2:1, source.1 Paulinus had asked how Paul’s four words for prayer in 1 Timothy 2:1 differ. Augustine maps them onto the parts of the Eucharist. The supplications come before the consecration, the prayers during it, the intercessions at the blessing of the people, when the bishops “as advocates” lay hands on them, and the thanksgiving at the end. He then explains why the point matters. The verse goes on to say “for all men,” and no one should think the Church may skip praying for its persecutors, since God “will have all men to be saved” (1 Tim 2:4).2 This is the bridge Prosper would later use. The apostle’s precept is not just advice to individuals; the whole Church carries it out in its eucharistic prayer.

“Prayer itself is the clearest testimony of grace”

For whoever prays and says, “Lead us not into temptation,” is certainly not praying to be a man, which he is by nature; nor praying to have free choice, which he already received when that nature itself was created; nor praying for the remission of sins, because that is said earlier, “Forgive us our debts”; nor praying to receive the commandment, but plainly praying to keep the commandment. … Prayer itself, then, is the clearest testimony of grace. Let him confess this, and we shall rejoice in him, whether he was right all along or has been put right.

— Letter 177.4 (to Pope Innocent I, 416), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.3 The letter went to Rome over five African bishops’ names, Aurelius of Carthage, Alypius, Augustine, Evodius and Possidius, asking Innocent to examine Pelagius’s teaching. Pelagius called grace free will, or forgiveness, or the law. None of these, the bishops answer, is what the Christian asks for in the Lord’s Prayer. Paul De Clerck singles out the sentence as typically Augustinian and as the patristic root of the later adage: the “liturgical argument,” he writes, is one Augustine uses abundantly, and his chief prayer-text is the Lord’s Prayer, read through Cyprian’s commentary on it.4

The priest at the altar prays that God convert unbelievers (Letter 217)

Say, then, quite openly that we ought not to pray for those to whom we preach the Gospel that they may believe, but only preach to them. Bring out your arguments against the prayers of the Church. When you hear the priest of God at the altar exhorting the people of God to pray for unbelievers, that God may convert them to the faith; and for catechumens, that He may breathe into them the desire for regeneration; and for the faithful, that by His gift they may persevere in what they have begun to be: mock those pious words, and say that you do not do what he urges, that is, that you do not ask God for unbelievers that He make them believers, because these are not benefits of the divine mercy but duties of the human will.

— Letter 217.2 (to Vitalis of Carthage, c. 427), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.5 Vitalis was a Carthaginian layman who held that believing is “not God’s gift, but ours, from our own will.” God works in us, he said, only by setting His law and Scriptures before us; whether we consent is up to us. Augustine’s whole letter answers from the liturgy. Vitalis also condemns Cyprian, says Augustine, since Cyprian’s commentary on the Lord’s Prayer teaches that “Thy will be done on earth as in heaven” asks faith for unbelievers. Then he closes the trap:

If, then, you do not refuse that these things must be asked of the Lord, what remains, brother Vitalis, but that you confess that they are given by Him of whom you agree they must be asked? But if you deny that we ought to ask these things of Him, you contradict that very teaching of His; for this too we learned in it, that we should ask these things of Him.

— Letter 217.5, the wiki’s translation.6 The last clause matters for how the argument runs. The prayer is binding because Scripture commands it: Christ said “pray for your enemies” (Matt 5:44), and Paul prayed for the Jews (Rom 10:1). Augustine returns to the point at the end of the letter, after setting out twelve propositions on grace that he asks Vitalis to accept:

When you hear the priest of God at His altar exhorting the people to pray to God, or himself praying aloud, that He compel the unbelieving nations to come to His faith, will you not answer “Amen”? … God makes those who do not yet believe believe through the prayers of believers, in order to show that it is He who does it. For no one is so unskilled, so carnal, so slow of mind, as not to see that God does what He commands that He be asked to do.

— Letter 217.26–27, the wiki’s translation.7 He adds that thanksgiving says the same as petition: “prayer for unbelievers, thanksgiving for believers. For He who must be asked to do it is the one to be thanked when He has done it” (§28). And he narrows the dispute to its real point, the beginning of faith (§29). Vitalis granted that God gives the later goods of the Christian life to those who already believe and ask. What he denied was that God gives the first act of believing. That is why, Augustine replies, the Church prays for unbelievers that they may believe.8 This is the same question Prosper’s chapter 8 settles, the beginning of faith.

The Church has always prayed this way, so it has always believed it (The Gift of Perseverance)

And I wish that those who are slow and weak of heart, who cannot, or cannot as yet, understand the Scriptures or the explanations of them, would so hear or not hear our arguments in this question as to consider more carefully their prayers, which the Church has always used and will use, even from its beginnings until this age shall be completed. … For when was not prayer made in the Church for unbelievers and its opponents that they should believe? … As, therefore, the Church has both been born and grows and has grown in these prayers, so it has been born and grows and has grown in this faith, by which faith it is believed that God's grace is not given according to the merits of the receivers. For, certainly, the Church would not pray that faith should be given to unbelievers, unless it believed that God converts to Himself both the averse and adverse wills of men.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 23.63 (428/429, to Prosper and Hilary), source.9 This is the fullest statement of the principle in Augustine, and it was written for the two men who had told him about the resistance in Provence. One of them was Prosper. The argument is aimed at readers who cannot follow the exegesis. Even they know what the Church prays, and the prayer carries the doctrine. Augustine then takes the step from prayer to the Church’s faith across time:

Let him, therefore, say that the Church at any time has not had in its belief the truth of this predestination and grace, which is now maintained with a more careful heed against the late heretics; let him say this who dares to say that at any time it has not prayed, or not truthfully prayed, as well that unbelievers might believe, as that believers might persevere. And if the Church has always prayed for these benefits, it has always believed them to be certainly God's gifts.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 23.65.10 Augustine also argues here from the Lord’s Prayer, which he follows Cyprian in reading as a prayer for perseverance. “Why is that perseverance asked for from God if it is not given by God?” (§3).11 His fuller teaching on perseverance as a gift is set out on his predestination page.

The baptismal exorcism shows that infants are under the devil’s power

What shall I say of the actual form of this sacrament? I only wish some one of those who espouse the contrary side would bring me an infant to be baptized. What does my exorcism work in that babe, if he be not held in the devil's family?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins 1.34.63 (412), source.12 The same rite-to-doctrine move runs through the whole Pelagian controversy, with the rite now the exorcism before baptism. The sponsor answers for the infant that he renounces the devil, turns to God, and believes in the forgiveness of sins. If the infant had no sin and no devil to renounce, these answers would be false. Augustine spells it out in On Marriage and Concupiscence:

… whosoever denies this, is convicted by the truth of the Church's very sacraments, which no heretical novelty in the Church of Christ is permitted to destroy or change … It is true, then, and in no way false, that the devil's power is exorcised in infants, and that they renounce him by the hearts and mouths of those who bring them to baptism, being unable, to do so by their own … What is that, therefore, within them which keeps them in the power of the devil until they are delivered from it by Christ's sacrament of baptism? What is it, I ask, but sin? … But infants have committed no sin of their own since they have been alive. Only original sin, therefore, remains …

— On Marriage and Concupiscence 1.20.22 (419), source.13 In the second book he adds the antiquity of the rite. Julian of Eclanum had said that anyone who teaches original sin is a Manichee. Augustine answers that infants “about to be baptized” were “exorcised with exsufflation” in the Church long before Mani, and he calls it “that most ancient tradition of the Church.” To blame original sin on Mani is to accuse the Church that exorcises infants “everywhere” and “spread over the world.”14 He presses it again against Julian himself:

… yet what has been preached and believed from of old with true catholic faith by the whole Church is true; for the Church would neither exorcise nor breathe upon the children of the faithful if it were not rescuing them from the power of darkness and from the prince of death. … You were afraid to mention it, as though you yourself would be breathed out by the whole world if you had chosen to contradict this exsufflation, by which the prince of this world is cast out even from little ones. … How does [grace] do this, if it breathes upon one in whom it knows there is nothing to drive out, if it washes one in whom it knows there is nothing to wash away?

— Against Julian 6.5.11 (c. 421), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.15 “Exsufflation” was the minister’s breathing or blowing on the candidate to drive out the evil spirit. The argument has the same shape as the one from intercession: the rite is universal and ancient, it would be a lie if the doctrine were false, so the doctrine is the Church’s faith. It is the argument from infant baptism itself, weighed on the original-sin-ground argument, applied to one part of the rite.

Notes

  • Prosper’s two chapters are Augustine’s two arguments. Chapter 8 of the Capitula argues from the intercessions for unbelievers, Jews, heretics, the lapsed and catechumens; chapter 9 argues from the exorcisms and exsufflations of every candidate for baptism. Both are Augustine’s, in that order of importance (see Prosper of Aquitaine). Letter 217 already names the priest at the altar praying for unbelievers, catechumens and the faithful, and it rests the argument on the Church saying “Amen.” Prosper’s list is longer, and he adds one thing Augustine never wrote: the formula, with its legal word lex and its strong verb statuat. Augustine speaks of the Church’s prayers (orationes Ecclesiae) and its sacraments, not of a law of praying.
  • Which way does it run? Augustine’s argument goes from prayer to belief. Yet the prayers he cites are commanded in Scripture (1 Tim 2:1–4; Matt 5:44; the Lord’s Prayer), and he says the Church “learned” to ask from Christ’s own teaching (Letter 217.5). So the force of the argument is not that the liturgy can create doctrine. The Church’s universal obedience to a scriptural command shows how that command is to be understood. This fits Paul De Clerck’s reading of Prosper’s lex as the apostolic precept, not “the liturgy” in general.
  • An asserted universality. Augustine claims the Church “has always” prayed for unbelievers and “everywhere” exorcises infants. He did not survey other churches to establish this. As De Clerck notes of both Augustine and Prosper, they reasoned a priori that practices handed down from the apostles must be universal. For the intercessions he could at least point to the apostle’s own command (1 Tim 2:1). For the exorcism of infants he offers less. His proof of its age is that it predates Mani, which he asserts rather than documents.16
  • Related pages. The rite-to-doctrine arguments sit beside Augustine’s treatment of original sin and of infant baptism, where the proof from baptism “for the remission of sins” carries the main weight. The prayer argument is the patristic root of the adage traced on the lex orandi timeline.
