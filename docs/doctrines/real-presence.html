The Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

When Christians gather to "break bread," what is received? In Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran usage, Real Presence names the belief that, in the Eucharist, the consecrated bread and wine are — not merely symbolize — the body and blood of Christ, so that the communicant truly partakes of the Lord himself; Reformed teaching speaks of Christ's body truly received, spiritually, by faith (see John Calvin below). This timeline records how the Eucharist has been described and disputed, earliest extant attestation first. The communions and their teachers state their positions in these terms:

  • Symbolic — the bread and wine are signs that represent Christ's body and blood and call the Supper to mind; the true body is present "by the contemplation of faith," not in the elements (Huldrych Zwingli).1
  • Spiritual / "pneumatic" presence — Christ is truly received, but spiritually, by the faithful, the Holy Spirit lifting the communicant to feed on the body and blood; the elements remain bread and wine (John Calvin).2
  • Sacramental union — Christ's body and blood are present "in and under" the bread and wine, which remain bread and wine (the Lutheran teaching; often called "consubstantiation" by others).3
  • Transubstantiation — the substance of the bread and wine is changed into the body and blood of Christ, the outward appearances alone remaining (the Fourth Lateran Council, 1215, and the Council of Trent, 1551, for the Catholic Church).4
  • The change of the gifts, its manner undefined — the bread and wine become in truth the body and blood of Christ by a change (metabolē) whose manner is not explained (the Eastern Orthodox teaching as stated by Kallistos Ware and John Breck).5

This page presents the witnesses in date order; the disputed readings below set out how particular texts and stages of this history have been read.

Related doctrines: The Eucharist and the ministry — see also the ministerial priesthood · communion under one kind · the communion of infants

The Scriptural texts

The doctrine is argued from three bodies of New Testament text:

  • The Bread of Life discourse — "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you … For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed" (John 6:53–58), with the qualifying word "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing" (John 6:63).6
  • The Words of Institution — "Take, eat; this is my body … this is my blood of the new testament" (Matthew 26:26–28; Mark 14:22–24; Luke 22:19–20, which adds "this do in remembrance of me").7
  • Paul's "communion of the body and blood" — "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16) — and his warning that to eat or drink "unworthily" is to be "guilty of the body and blood of the Lord," failing to discern "the Lord's body" (1 Corinthians 11:23–29).8

The positions listed above read this language differently, as literal or as figurative.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first. This timeline is in its early stages and will be filled out as the doctrine's development across the centuries is traced.

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Writing on his way to martyrdom, Ignatius is the earliest extant witness here after the New Testament. He marks out the Docetists by their refusal of the Eucharist: they "abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins, and which the Father, of His goodness, raised up again" (Smyrnaeans 7).9 Longing for martyrdom, he writes that he desires "the bread of God, the heavenly bread, the bread of life, which is the flesh of Jesus Christ … and … the drink of God, namely His blood" (Romans 7).10 And he calls the one bread the Church breaks together "the medicine of immortality, and the antidote which prevents us from dying" (Ephesians 20).11

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Defending Christian worship to the emperor, Justin gives the earliest extant statement here that the consecrated elements are not ordinary food. Of the food "called among us the Eucharist" he writes that "not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise… the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word… is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" (First Apology 66).12 He grounds the claim in the Incarnation: as the Word took flesh, the food consecrated by his word is that flesh and blood. In the Dialogue with Trypho he calls "the Eucharist of the bread and the cup" the "pure sacrifice" foretold by Malachi, offered "in every place" among the Gentiles (Dialogue 41, 117),13 a passage also cited on the ministerial-priesthood timeline.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180–189)

Against the Gnostics, who scorned the flesh, Irenaeus makes the Eucharist an argument for the goodness and resurrection of the body. The bread "which is produced from the earth, when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly; so also our bodies, when they receive the Eucharist, are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection" (Against Heresies IV.18.5).14 When "the mingled cup and the manufactured bread receives the Word of God," he writes, "the Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made," from which "the substance of our flesh is increased and supported" — so the flesh, "nourished from the body and blood of the Lord," cannot be incapable of eternal life (Against Heresies V.2.2–3).15

Clement of Alexandria (c. 198)

In The Instructor (Paedagogus), Clement ascribes effects to the eucharistic cup. "The blood of the Lord is twofold," he writes, "and to drink the blood of Jesus, is to become partaker of the Lord's immortality."16 The mingled cup of water and the Word "is called Eucharist, renowned and glorious grace; and they who by faith partake of it are sanctified both in body and soul."17 Clement writes as an allegorist, layering wine, water, blood and Word as symbols, and ascribes to the Eucharist immortality and the sanctification of the whole person, body as well as soul.

Tertullian (c. 211)

In North Africa, Tertullian describes the church's handling of the consecrated elements. Listing customs that, he argues, rest on tradition without a written precept — the baptismal renunciations, offerings for the dead, the sign of the cross — he records that the faithful receive the Eucharist "from the hand of none but the presidents" and "feel pained should any wine or bread, even though our own, be cast upon the ground" (De Corona 3). It is the earliest extant witness here to how the elements were handled.18

Origen (c. 248)

Answering the pagan Celsus — who urged thank-offerings to the demons set over the things of this world — Origen sets against it the Christian's thanksgiving to the Creator: "we also eat the bread presented to us; and this bread becomes by prayer a sacred body, which sanctifies those who sincerely partake of it" (Against Celsus VIII.33).19 Origen elsewhere reads the eating of Christ's flesh allegorically, in commenting on John 6 and on Leviticus (see the detail page); here the bread becomes "a sacred body" by prayer and sanctifies those who partake.

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 253)

Cyprian writes from a disciplinary quarrel: against the "Aquarians," who used water alone in the cup, he insists the mingled cup is the blood of Christ. Because "that was wine which He called His blood," it follows that "the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup, nor the Lord's sacrifice celebrated with a legitimate consecration unless our oblation and sacrifice respond to His passion" (Epistle 62.9).20 He calls the Eucharist a sacrifice — "the Lord's passion is the sacrifice which we offer" — and says the priest "offers a true and full sacrifice… when he proceeds to offer it according to what he sees Christ Himself to have offered" (Epistle 62.14, 17).21

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Explaining the sacraments to the newly baptized, Cyril devotes a catechetical lecture to the Eucharist. In On the Body and Blood of Christ he tells them to "consider… the Bread and the Wine not as bare elements, for they are, according to the Lord's declaration, the Body and Blood of Christ… Judge not the matter from the taste, but from faith be fully assured" (Mystagogic 4.6).22 Walking them through the liturgy, he locates the change at the invocation of the Spirit over the gifts, "that He may make the Bread the Body of Christ, and the Wine the Blood of Christ; for whatsoever the Holy Ghost has touched, is surely sanctified and changed" (Mystagogic 5.7) — an early witness to a consecratory epiclesis.23 Cyril also calls the elements a "figure" and "antitype" of the body and blood.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 383)

In The Great Catechism Gregory writes of a change in the bread. The Body Christ took was maintained by bread and yet "transmuted to the dignity of Godhead" by the indwelling Word; "rightly, then, do we believe that now also the bread which is consecrated by the Word of God is changed into the Body of God the Word" — and changed "at once… by means of the Word," not by being eaten (ch. 37).24 He uses a verb for it: God "transelements" (metastoicheiōsis) "the natural quality of these visible things to that immortal thing".

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

In his mystagogical sermons Ambrose speaks of a conversion of the elements: "this is not what nature made, but what the blessing consecrated… because by blessing nature itself is changed" (On the Mysteries 9.50).25 He locates the moment and cause of the change in the words of institution: "Before the blessing of the heavenly words another nature is spoken of, after the consecration the Body is signified… Before it is consecrated, it is bread; but when the words of Christ have been spoken, it becomes the body of Christ" (On the Mysteries 9.54; On the Sacraments 4).26

John Chrysostom (c. 392)

The Antiochene preacher Chrysostom uses concrete language of the altar. Standing the reader before it in his Treatise on the Priesthood, he marvels that "He who sitteth on high with the Father is at that hour held in the hands of all, and gives Himself to those who are willing to embrace and grasp Him" (3.4).27 Preaching on Paul's "communion of the blood," he identifies the contents of the cup with the blood of the cross — "this which is in the cup is that which flowed from His side" — and asks, "what is the bread? The Body of Christ" (Homilies on First Corinthians 24).28 He uses this language to call his hearers to awe and to worthy reception.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 412)

Augustine was cited by both sides of the later dispute. To the newly baptized he says that "the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ," bidding them "be what you see, and receive what you are" (Sermon 272); and expounding "fall down before His footstool" he says that the flesh given to eat is the very flesh born of Mary, so that "no one eateth that flesh, unless he hath first worshipped … we sin in not worshipping" (Expositions on the Psalms 98.9).29 He also makes John 6's "eat my flesh" his example of a figure — a command that, taken literally, "seems to enjoin a crime … it is therefore a figure" (On Christian Doctrine 3.16) — reads the eating as incorporation into the body of Christ, the Church ("O sign of unity! … Let him draw near, let him believe; let him be embodied, that he may be made to live"), and tells his hearers "believe, and thou hast eaten already" (Tractates on John 25.12).30 His definition of a sacrament, a sign in which "one thing is seen, another is understood," is read in different ways by realist and figurative interpreters; see How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read?

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440)

Against a sect who held that the consecrated Eucharist lost its power if kept and consumed on a later day, Cyril holds that the consecration's power does not lapse with time: "Christ is not altered, nor is his holy body changed, but the power of the consecration and his life-giving grace is perpetual in it" (Letter to Calosyrius). The letter bears on the keeping of the consecrated Eucharist after the assembly disperses.31

John of Damascus (c. 743)

In his summary of Greek theology, John writes of the Eucharist in the chapter "Concerning the holy and immaculate Mysteries." He teaches that "the bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood," "supernaturally changed by the invocation and presence of the Holy Spirit," placing the change at the invocation as Cyril of Jerusalem had done. He denies that they are "merely figures of the body and blood of Christ (God forbid!) … for the Lord has said, 'This is My body,' not, this is a figure of My body"; the consecrated bread "is not plain bread but bread united with divinity." On the "antitype" language of earlier writers, he says that when Basil and others called the elements antitypes, "they said so not after the consecration but before the consecration" (Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.13).32

Paschasius Radbertus (c. 831)

The abbot of Corbie, Paschasius, wrote the earliest extant full-length Latin treatise on the Eucharist. He teaches a strict identity: what is received on the altar is "the true body of Christ, which was crucified and buried," made present by the priest's consecration "with the word of Christ through the Spirit" (De Corpore et Sanguine Domini, c. 831). The eucharistic flesh, he teaches, is the very flesh born of the Virgin, the Incarnation the pattern of the daily miracle.33

Ratramnus of Corbie (c. 843)

At the same abbey and the same king's request, Ratramnus answered his confrere: "that Bread and that Wine, figuratively, are the Body and Blood of Christ," with "a great difference betwixt the visible appearance, and the invisible substance"; and yet they are "not mere empty figures, or naked signs." Philip Schaff read the treatise as making the elements Christ's body and blood "only in a spiritual sense to the faith of believers," and recorded that it "produced a change in Archbishop Cranmer"; Michael Ott (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1913) and, in 1527, John Fisher read it as consistent with Catholic doctrine (see the detail page).34

Berengar of Tours (c. 1070)

Master of the school of Tours, Berengar denied a material change: after the consecration the bread and wine remain, and are the "sign" or "sacrament" of Christ's body — "the reality of the sign" received by faith, not the flesh physically present on the altar. Condemned at a string of synods, he was made in 1059 to swear an oath that the faithful "crush" Christ's body "with their teeth," and in 1079 a second one introducing "substantial conversion."35

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

The council convoked by Innocent III uses the verb in a conciliar profession of faith. Its opening confession professes that Christ's body and blood are "truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the forms of bread and wine, the bread being changed (transsubstantiatio) by divine power into the body, and the wine into the blood" — and, in the same sentence, reserves the confecting to "the priest who has been duly ordained," joining the Real Presence and the ministerial priesthood in a single clause.36

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas gives a scholastic account of how the change takes place. In the Summa Theologiae (III, q. 75) he holds that the substance of the bread and wine does not remain; that "the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body," a conversion so unlike any natural change that it must be "called by a name of its own, 'transubstantiation'"; and that the sensible accidents (appearances) remain. Whether this account states the belief of the Fathers or a later doctrine is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?37

Huldrych Zwingli (c. 1525)

Zwingli of Zürich read the "is" of "This is my body" as signifies: the bread and wine are signs that represent Christ's body and call his once-for-all sacrifice to mind — "those things are called the body and blood of Christ which are the symbols of the true body." Christ's true body ascended and sits at the Father's right hand, so it is received "by the contemplation of faith," not "masticated with our mouth and teeth." He relied on "the flesh profiteth nothing" (John 6:63), and at the Marburg Colloquy (1529) he and Luther agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles but not on this one.38

Martin Luther (c. 1529)

Against Zwingli, Luther insisted the consecrated bread and wine are the true body and blood of Christ, present "in and under" the elements, on the force of the words of institution — "distinct, clear… words, which certainly are no trope." At Marburg he chalked Hoc est corpus meum on the table and refused the Swiss the hand of fellowship. His reading is sacramental union, not transubstantiation: he left the substances of bread and wine standing — "I do not argue whether the wine remains wine or not… let it be with the wine as God wills" — grounding the presence in the Word rather than in a change of substance.39

John Calvin (c. 1541)

Calvin of Geneva taught a position distinct from both Zwingli's reading and Luther's bodily presence. Christ is truly received in the Supper, he taught, but spiritually: the Holy Spirit lifts the believer up to feed on the body and blood of the ascended Christ in heaven, so the elements remain bread and wine yet are no bare signs but efficacious instruments — the Supper conveys its grace "not by presenting a vain or empty sign, but by there exerting an efficacy of the Spirit." Against Zwingli the sign "truly exhibits" what it signifies; against Catholic and Lutheran teaching he denied that Christ's body is affixed to or enclosed in the bread, or present in a manner he called carnal; and of the mode he confessed he "rather feel[s] than understand[s] it." His spiritual-presence doctrine became the standard Reformed teaching (⚠ unverified).40

The Council of Trent (1551)

Answering the Reformation, Trent's thirteenth session defines the conversion as transubstantiation — "a conversion… of the whole substance of the bread into the substance of the body of Christ" — and draws out its worship corollary: because Christ "the true God" is wholly present, "all the faithful of Christ may… render in veneration the worship of latria… to this most holy sacrament," reserving it, venerating it, and bearing it in procession, on pain of anathema to any who would call such adoration idolatry (Session 13, chs. 4–6 and Canon 6).41
