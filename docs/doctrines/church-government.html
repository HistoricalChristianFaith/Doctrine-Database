The Government of the Church

How was the church actually ordered — who governs it, what its offices are, and how a man comes to hold one? This timeline traces the development of church government across time: the offices the earliest congregations knew (the bishop or overseer, the presbyter or elder, and the deacon), and the rival forms of government that grew from them — congregational (each local body governs itself), presbyterian (rule by a college of elders), and episcopal (rule by bishops set over the presbyters), the last maturing in the West into the Roman papacy. The witnesses are set out earliest first — each slotted by date, with his own words where they survive — so that the drift from the fluid ministry of the New Testament and the Didache toward the settled threefold order of Ignatius and Cyprian can be read off the timeline itself, which then runs on — past the conciliar polity of Nicaea — to the Reformation-era English quarrel over whether that episcopal order binds by divine right (iure divino) or is an arrangement the church may alter.

Of the three offices the deacon is the least disputed — the one order every polity grants is plainly scriptural, present in the earliest witnesses (Philippians 1:1; the Didache; 1 Clement) and rooted in the Seven appointed to serve tables in Acts 6. The single genuinely live question about it — whether the early church ordained women to the diaconate (Phoebe the deacon of Cenchrea, Romans 16:1) — cuts across the timeline like the questions of form, and so is weighed in the Arguments below.

The contested forms are not argued on the timeline. Because every side claims the same handful of passages (Acts 20:17 / 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1; 1 Clement 44) and the same early witnesses (Clement, Ignatius, the Didache, Cyprian, Jerome), the questions on which they divide — whether episkopos and presbyteros named one office or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic or a later development, whether office passes by an unbroken succession, and whether corporate government by council and synod is the apostolic pattern — cut across the timeline rather than sitting at any one point on it. They are therefore weighed adversarially, each on its own merits, in the Arguments section below, not in the chronological list.

Related doctrines: Church order and government — see also the primacy of Rome (whether the episcopal order climaxes in one bishop, the pope, set over the whole church by Petrine succession). The Eucharistic and sacramental side of the ministry is traced under the ministerial priesthood (who may confect the sacraments, ordination, apostolic succession as a channel of grace); a rival free-church succession model is weighed under Baptist successionism; and the apostolic office itself — Matthias chosen by lot to fill Judas’s episkopē (Acts 1:20) — sits on the apostles hub.

Timeline

The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) (c. 49)

The New Testament’s own picture of church government at work — and the scriptural charter every later conciliar and synodal polity appeals to. When the circumcision controversy divided Antioch, the question was referred not to a single ruling officer but “unto the apostles and elders,” who “came together for to consider of this matter” and settled it in open assembly after “much disputing”; the decree went out from “the apostles and elders, with the whole church,” in the name of “the apostles and elders and brethren” (Acts 15:2, 6, 22–23).1 The governing body is a plurality deciding corporately — Peter and James each speaking as members of the assembly — and its verdict is issued as a common, Spirit-guided sentence: “it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us” (Acts 15:28), which Chrysostom reads as a council binding its own judgment to the Spirit’s, “the Spirit’s legislating” yet issued by the gathered church.2 Whether this corporate pattern establishes council-government as the apostolic form, checks the single bishop, or merely furnishes a precedent that presupposes the apostles’ own (unrepeatable) authority is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

The earliest post‑New‑Testament church document, and so the earliest sub‑apostolic witness to church order. Writing in the name of the Roman church to Corinth to quell a revolt that had wrongfully deposed its presbyters, Clement describes a ministry of bishops and deacons appointed by the apostles — a two‑order local ministry (Philippians 1:1), traced in a descending line of authorization “God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons” (1 Clement 42).3 Yet across the same letter he uses “bishop” (episkopos) and “presbyter” (presbyteros) of the same officers: the strife is over “the office of the episcopate,” to depose the officers is to “eject from the episcopate,” and those same deposed men are in the next breath “those presbyters” whom it is sin to remove (1 Clement 44) — the same fluid, two-terms-one-office usage found in the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7).4 The governing body at Corinth is a college — “the presbyters set over” the flock, in the plural — and no single ruling bishop of Corinth is named or appealed to anywhere in the letter; the remedy is to “submit yourselves to the presbyters” (1 Clement 54, 57).5 On how a man comes to office, Clement holds together what later readings pull apart: office passes by an orderly succession from the apostles (“when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry”), and a lawful appointment carries “the consent of the whole Church” (1 Clement 44).4 So 1 Clement attests, c. 96, a two-order ministry — not yet the threefold, monarchical episcopate — continued by an apostolically arranged succession: the anchor witness for the parity reading and the seed of the apostolic-succession reading alike. Which of those the passage establishes, and whether the single-bishop order that appears a decade later in Ignatius is apostolic or a development, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

The Didache (c. 100)

The earliest surviving church-order manual — the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, an anonymous manual of practice whose date is debated (c. 50–150; anchored here at the conventional c. 100, its church-order section widely held to preserve a very early stratum). It catches a congregation at the hinge between two ministries. A still-living order of itinerant, charismatic apostles, prophets, and teachers is tested for authenticity and supported from the first-fruits — the prophets ranked highest, “for they are your high priests” (chs. 11–13).6 Beside it stands a settled local ministry the community is told to provide for itself: “Appoint [cheirotonēsate, elect by show of hands] for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord… for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” (ch. 15).7 The offices are plural and local — the same two-order pairing as Philippians 1:1 and 1 Clement 42 — chosen by congregational election, not laying-on of hands; and, strikingly, no single presiding bishop is named and the word “presbyter” never appears in the manual at all.7 So the Didache documents, from the inside, the drift from a charismatic to an appointed leadership — the stage between the fluid ministry of the New Testament and 1 Clement and the settled threefold order of Ignatius. Whether its silence on the monarch-bishop and the presbytery marks an earlier stage of the ministry or merely a different region, and what its congregational election shows for how office is filled, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Carried under guard from Antioch to martyrdom at Rome, Ignatius writes to the churches of Asia Minor and gives the earliest unambiguous picture of the monarchical (single-bishop) episcopate: in each city one bishop presides, distinct from and set over the presbytery (the college of elders), with the deacons a third order beneath both. He states the threefold order and makes it constitutive of the church itself: “let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ… and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church” (Trallians 3); the bishop “presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons” (Magnesians 6).8 Obedience to the bishop he casts as obedience to God — “we should look upon the bishop even as we would upon the Lord Himself” (Ephesians 6) — and makes the bishop the visible centre of the church’s unity: “Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church” (Smyrnaeans 8).9 Where 1 Clement, a decade earlier, still seems to use “bishop” and “presbyter” of one office, Ignatius everywhere presupposes the three ranked orders — the sharpest early evidence for the episcopal shape of the ministry. Whether that shape is apostolic, universal by c. 108, or a development drawn out of the New Testament’s two-order usage is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 125)

Writing from Smyrna to the church at Philippi — a short time after Ignatius passed through on his way to martyrdom, whose letters Polycarp forwards and whose fate he asks after (chs. 9, 13) — Polycarp addresses a ministry of presbyters and deacons and names no single bishop of Philippi. He greets the church corporately, as “Polycarp, and the presbyters with him,” sets out the duties of its deacons and presbyters, and directs the whole congregation to be “subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ” — the very formula of obedience Ignatius reserves for the bishop, here given to the plural presbyters and deacons together (Philippians 5–6).10 The occasion of the letter confirms the picture: Valens, “once a presbyter among you,” had fallen into avarice, and Polycarp has the congregation deal with him — “call them back… that ye may save your whole body” — not a ruling bishop (Philippians 11).11 Polycarp is himself the bishop of Smyrna, so this is not ignorance of the office but the two cities at different stages: Smyrna with its bishop, Philippi still ordered by presbyters and deacons a generation after Paul, at the very moment Ignatius is urging the single bishop on the churches of Asia. Whether that uneven, region-by-region spread marks the episcopate as a development or merely a regional lag in a substantially apostolic order is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140–155)

A long, popular apocalypse written at Rome in the mid-second century — and its incidental picture of the Roman church’s order is strikingly collective. Hermas is charged to read his book to the congregation “along with the presbyters who preside over the Church” — a college of presiding elders, in the plural, and beside them Clement, named not as a monarch-bishop but as the officer to whom “permission has been granted” to send the church’s writings “to foreign countries” (Visions 2.4.3).12 When he lists the church’s leaders he speaks of “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” (Visions 3.5.1) and of “bishops given to hospitality” who received the servants of God into their houses and sheltered the widows and the destitute (Similitudes 9.27) — bishops in the plural, ranged alongside teachers and deacons as overlapping ministries of service, with no distinction drawn between “bishop” and “presbyter” and no single ruling bishop of Rome anywhere in view.13 Coming from Rome around c. 140–155 — a generation after 1 Clement’s presiding presbytery and half a century after Ignatius urged the single bishop on the churches of Asia — this is a key counter-datum: the monarchical episcopate has not, on the evidence of this Roman book, reached Rome itself, its leadership still spoken of only as a college. Whether that uneven, region-by-region spread marks the episcopate as a development or merely a regional lag in a substantially apostolic order is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Against the Gnostics—who claimed a secret tradition passed privately from the apostles—Irenaeus forges the succession argument into its first developed form. The real apostolic teaching, he answers, is public and traceable: it is “within the power of all… to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles… and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times” (Against Heresies III.3.1). He offers the Roman line (Linus → Anacletus → Clement → … → Eleutherius, “the twelfth place from the apostles”) and Polycarp at Smyrna as specimens any church could match (III.3.2–4).14 For Irenaeus the succession guarantees doctrinal continuity—public teaching against private novelty—and he keeps it honest by anchoring it in fidelity: successors are a boon “if they discharged their functions honestly, but if they should fall away, the direst calamity” (III.3.1), and the presbyters to obey are those who have the succession and “that which is sound… in conduct… and incorrupt in speech” (IV.26.2, 5). Tellingly, that same succession is a succession “of the presbytery” as readily as of the episcopate—the two-terms-one-office fluidity of 1 Clement and the New Testament, a generation after Ignatius’s sharp threefold order.15 Whether the monarchical office the lists presuppose is apostolic, and whether the succession guarantees the faith mechanically or only evidences its doctrinal continuity, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian gives the succession argument its legal edge. Borrowing the Roman lawyer’s praescriptio—an objection that halts a suit before its merits are heard—he tells the heretics to “unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning” until their first bishop can show “for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles”; the Gnostics can produce no such roll and are thereby non-suited (Prescription 32, instancing Smyrna’s Polycarp from John and Rome’s Clement from Peter).16 Yet in the same breath he makes plain that the test is finally doctrine, not pedigree: churches with no apostolic founder—“of much later date, for they are in fact being founded daily”—are “not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine” (32), for “we hold communion with the apostolic churches because our doctrine is in no respect different from theirs” (21). His two-name registers, and his placing Clement directly after Peter—against Irenaeus’s Linus–Anacletus–Clement—show the rolls pressed into anti-heretical service rather than kept as exact archives.17 Whether the argument bears the heavier weight later hung on it—succession as a mechanical or jurisdictional guarantee—is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215)

The earliest surviving ordination manual puts in a rubric what the New Testament leaves open: who confers office by the laying on of hands. The Apostolic Tradition keeps the people’s election and the ministry’s ordination as two distinct acts — a bishop is “chosen by all the people,” but “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him, and the presbytery shall stand by in silence” (Apostolic Tradition 2).18 It then grades the three orders precisely by their part in the rite of conferral: at a presbyter’s ordination the bishop lays on his hand “while the presbyters touch him,” yet the presbyter “has only the power to receive; but he has no power to give… a presbyter does not ordain the clergy”; and a deacon “the bishop alone shall make,” for “he is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop” (Apostolic Tradition 8–9).19 So the ordaining power runs downward from the episcopate and does not circulate among the presbyters or up from the people: bishops make a bishop, the bishop makes a presbyter (the presbyters only “sealing”), the bishop alone makes a deacon. (The Eucharistic/sacerdotal side of these same prayers — the bishop consecrated “high priest” to offer the Church’s gifts — is treated under the ministerial priesthood.) Whether ordaining is the reserved act of a bishop as a distinct order, or of a presbytery of equals, and how election and ordination together fill an office, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

Cyprian supplies the most developed third-century theology of church order — and a deliberately double-edged one. On the office he is emphatically episcopal: the bishop is the hinge of the church’s unity. But on the relations among bishops he is conciliar and egalitarian. The episcopate is one, held collegially — “the episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur) — and in ch. 4 Peter is the emblem of that one office, the other apostles “endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power” (On the Unity of the Church 4–5, received text; a second, more Rome-exalting “Primacy Text” recension of ch. 4 also survives — weighed with the primacy question, not here).20 From this he draws the sharpest ancient denial of a monarchical papacy: convening eighty-seven African bishops to decide the rebaptism dispute in council, each casting his own sentence, he opens — against Stephen of Rome — “neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops… since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another” (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256).21 And on how a man comes to office he is congregation-involving: the people “have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones,” and no ordination is “just and legitimate” unless a bishop is “chosen in the presence of the people… by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled” — the people’s consent and the provincial college’s together (Epistle 67, to the Spanish churches; the same double consent to Cornelius in Epistle 54).22 So Cyprian is at once strongly episcopal on the office, conciliar-egalitarian against any single ruling see, and congregation-involving on election — a combination every later polity claims a piece of. Which inferences the material actually bears — whether “no bishop of bishops” tells against Roman primacy, and whether the people’s suffrage supports congregational or presbyterian election — is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

The Council of Nicaea (325)

The first ecumenical council did more than settle the Arian question: its canons wrote the corporate, conciliar polity of the provinces into the law of the whole church. A bishop is to be consecrated by “all the bishops in the province” (or at least three, with the absent bishops’ written suffrage), “the ratification of what is done” left to the metropolitan (canon 4), and the bishops of each province must assemble in synod “twice a year” to try disputed cases (canon 5).23 The great sees of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch keep their customary spheres over the surrounding provinces — Rome’s named as one instance of the same “custom,” not as a rule above the rest (canon 6) — the germ of the later Eastern pentarchy. So the standing organ of government is not the single bishop alone but the assembled bishops of the province under their metropolitan, the greatest sees first among them.24 (The same council’s canon on the eucharistic ordering of the clergy is treated under the ministerial priesthood.) Whether corporate, conciliar government is the apostolic pattern or a later organizational overlay is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

John Calvin (1559)

With the Reformation the two-order, collegial ministry of the early church is consciously recovered and given its systematic Reformed form. In the definitive Institutes (Book IV, chs. 3–4) Calvin reads “bishop,” “presbyter,” and “pastor” as scriptural synonyms — names given “indiscriminately… on the authority of Scripture, which uses the words as synonymous” — and orders each church by a college of elders of one order: pastors and teachers who preach, beside “governors” or “seniors selected from the people to unite with the bishops in pronouncing censures and exercising discipline” (the ruling elder), with deacons for the care of the poor. “From the beginning… each church had its senate, composed of pious, grave, and venerable men” (Institutes IV.3.8).25 On how a man comes to office he makes the people’s consent constitutive — “ministers are legitimately called… when those who may have seemed fit are elected on the consent and approbation of the people,” other pastors presiding over the election (IV.3.15) — and the single presiding bishop set over the presbyters he judges a matter of the church’s custom, not divine right: in the ancient church “these presbyters selected one of their number to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest… from equality dissension should arise” (IV.4.2), the distinction “introduced by human arrangement,” following Jerome that a bishop is greater than a presbyter by custom rather than by the Lord’s appointment. From Calvin’s Geneva the polity is confessionalized and extended upward into graded assemblies (session → presbytery → synod) — the Second Helvetic Confession (1566) and the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645).26 So Calvin furnishes the mature presbyterian case, the systematic counter-pole to episcopacy iure divino. Whether collegial elder-rule of one order is the divinely-fixed apostolic form, and whether the people’s election makes the minister, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

John Whitgift (1574)

With the Reformation the question of form becomes a public quarrel of the realm, and the English exchange opens it. Answering the presbyterian Admonition to the Parliament and Thomas Cartwright, Whitgift — later Archbishop of Canterbury — sets out the adiaphorist case: Scripture prescribes no one form of church government as necessary or unalterable. “I find no one certain and perfect kind of government prescribed or commanded in the scriptures to the church of Christ” — nor any set down “to be perpetual for all times, persons, and places, without alteration” — so polity is a thing indifferent the Christian magistrate may order, and episcopacy is retained by the realm’s authority and the church’s antiquity, not by divine command (The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition, 1574).27 Cartwright held the contrary — that a presbyterian discipline is the divinely-fixed form the church may not alter — so the Reformation debate is joined at the root: does the New Testament command a polity at all? Whether it does, and whether the episcopal order binds iure divino or is alterable, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Robert Browne (1582)

The congregational answer enters with the founding manifesto of English Separatism. Against both the bishops and the presbyterians who would wait on Parliament to reform the church, Browne argues in A Treatise of Reformation without Tarrying for Any that a true church is not the baptized nation under its prelates but a company gathered by covenant under Christ — “the Lord’s people is of the willing sort” — which holds Christ’s keys directly and may order and reform itself at once, “without tarrying for any” magistrate or bishop. The keys, the discipline, and the choice of officers belong to the gathered congregation as functions of the body, not of an external authority — the conforming ministry’s error, on his telling, was to surrender that spiritual discipline to the civil power. Two men were hanged in 1583 for distributing the tract, and Browne himself later conformed; but the congregational principle he first stated blunt outlived his own conformity and matured into the Independent tradition (the Cambridge Platform, the Savoy Declaration, and Owen, below). Where Calvin gives the presbyterian pole and the English bishops the episcopal, Browne opens the third: final authority in the gathered assembly itself. Whether the congregation holds final authority, or only a real share under a presiding ministry, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.28

Richard Bancroft (1589)

Against the presbyterian platform and the anonymous Marprelate tracts, Bancroft — the future Archbishop — presses the hard reply. In his Paul’s Cross sermon of 9 February 1588/9 he grounds the threefold ministry of “Bishops, Priests, and Deacons” on the church’s practice “ever since the apostles times,” and brands the denial of any difference between bishop and presbyter the ancient Aerian heresy — the parity argument from the New Testament’s interchange of titles being, on his telling, the very error Epiphanius called “full of follie.”29 The sermon has long been remembered as the first English assertion of episcopacy by divine right, though that fame is itself contested: on the text Bancroft argues the office’s antiquity and apostolic origin, and the strict jure divino claim is better located slightly later (Saravia, Bilson, and Bancroft’s own 1593 Survay, hardening under Laud). Whether episcopacy binds iure divino, and whether tarring the parity case as heresy answers it, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Richard Hooker (c. 1594)

Hooker gives the episcopal case its classic and most moderate statement. A bishop, he holds, is a distinct order — given “a further power to ordain… and a power of chiefty in government over Presbyters” — and the office is of divine institution: “the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God, the Holy Ghost was the author of it” (Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII).30 Yet he refuses the church-unmaking edge Bancroft’s party would give it: episcopacy is not so absolutely necessary that its absence unchurches, for “there may be sometimes very just and sufficient reason to allow ordination made without a bishop,” and he will “not simply without exception” require an unbroken episcopal succession — the ground of his charity toward the non-episcopal Reformed churches.31 His nuanced iure divino, conceding more than Bancroft’s, is the form steelmanned below in the Arguments. (Book VII was printed only posthumously, 1662.)

John Owen and the Congregational platforms (1689)

A century after Browne, the congregational way receives its mature, systematic statement — and its two corporate constitutions. In The True Nature of a Gospel Church and its Government (published posthumously, 1689) John Owen — Cromwell’s chaplain, Dean of Christ Church, vice-chancellor of Oxford — lodges all church power in Christ and its ordinary seat in the particular congregation: this power “in actu primo, or fundamentally, is in the church itself; in actu secundo, or its exercise, in them that are especially called thereunto,” for “the first, original grant of all church power and privileges is made unto believers as such.” The rule of the officers is real but ministerial and derived, not a lordship standing over the body. The polity is confessionalized in the two great Congregational standards: the New-England Cambridge Platform (1648), which divides ordinary church power into the elders’ “power of office” and the brethren’s “power of privilege” — the latter reaching to choosing their own officers, admitting their own members, and the “public censure” of offenders — and the Savoy Declaration (1658, of which Owen was a principal author), whose “Institution of Churches” makes each particular church “the seat of that power which he is pleased to communicate to his saints” and has its officers “chosen… by the common suffrage of the church itself,” synods being for counsel and communion, not jurisdiction. So the timeline closes with the third form matured to a system, the congregational counter-pole to Calvin’s presbyterian elder-rule and to episcopacy iure divino. Tellingly, these standards distribute power between elders and brethren rather than vest it in the assembly alone — whether that amounts to final congregational authority is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.32

Arguments

The contested forms of church government — whether bishop and presbyter are one order or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic or a later development, whether office passes by an unbroken succession, whether corporate government by council and synod is the apostolic pattern, and whether episcopacy binds by divine right (iure divino) or is an arrangement the church may alter — cut across the timeline above rather than sitting at any one point on it, so they are weighed here, each on its own page.

Related arguments — the emergence of the episcopate

How the second-century episcopate came to be, how it spread, whether its chief witness’s own insistence betrays it as new, and what its authority rested on are four questions best read together. The first asks whether the settled one-bishop-over-presbyters order is apostolic or a later development; the second, whether that single-bishop form was universal by c. 110 or arose unevenly, region by region; the third, whether the very intensity of Ignatius’s exhortations proves the office a novelty he was pressing rather than a settled fact he described; the fourth, whether the episcopal succession the developed order claimed guarantees the apostolic faith. All turn on the same evidence — the two-order presbyter-bishop college of 1 Clement and the Didache, the sudden threefold order of Ignatius, the collegial witness of Rome and Philippi, and the earliest bishop-lists — read once forward (how the office grew and spread) and once backward (what the lines prove).

Is the threefold ministry apostolic, or a second-century development? (assessment: contested)

The developmental thesis of Lightfoot and later scholarship — that the settled threefold ministry (one bishop, a distinct order of presbyters, deacons) is not laid down in the New Testament but emerged across the late-first and second century out of an originally two-fold, plural ministry of presbyter-bishops and deacons. On the historical evidence the developmental account is strong and now the majority reading: “bishop” and “presbyter” interchange in the apostolic writings (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1), the earliest sub-apostolic documents still show that plural two-order ministry (1 Clement 42, 44; Didache 15), Ignatius’s single bishop appears abruptly c. 108 and is urged rather than assumed, and it had reached neither Rome nor Philippi even in his own day. Yet the thesis proves the development of a structure and a title, not the un-apostolicity of the presiding function: plural presbyters admit a president, and a trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, James at Jerusalem, the delegates Timothy and Titus) already stands over the local elders in the New Testament — so the rival substance-apostolic reading survives as a coherent, independently-held position the same evidence does not defeat. Strong by the historian’s lights that the settled order is a development, coherent by the theologian’s that its substance is apostolic: no clear winner (contested).33

Was the monarchical episcopate universal by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? (assessment: sound)

The reading that the single (“monarchical”) bishop arose unevenly across the churches rather than everywhere at once — a companion to the question just above, but narrower: it asks only about the geographic and chronological spread of the single-bishop form, not whether the presiding office is apostolic in substance. The evidence is a genuine patchwork: firm and early at Antioch and in Ignatius’s Asian correspondents (c. 108), yet at Rome a presbyteral college still in 1 Clement (c. 96) and in the Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140–155), and at Philippi no bishop at all in Polycarp’s letter (c. 125). For that spread-claim the reading is well-grounded and near the historical consensus (sound), resting for its strongest case (Rome) on the church’s own collegial self-descriptions, not on silence; the rival “universal by c. 110” must re-read positive evidence as accidental silence, and Ignatius’s own hortatory pressure tells against a settled universal institution. Two limits are folded rather than split off, since no one advances them as a positive counter-thesis: the argument from silence is soft at the margins (Ignatius’s letter to Rome is explained by its purpose — a plea against intervention in his martyrdom — and Philippi turns partly on silence), and the dates of Hermas and Polycarp are elastic. What the reading proves is the uneven spread of a form; that this makes the office un-apostolic does not follow — uneven spread is compatible with an apostolic origin diffusing at different rates — which is the separate, contested substance question in this group.34

Does Ignatius describe an established episcopate, or urge a novelty? (assessment: weak)

The reading that the sheer intensity of Ignatius’s exhortations to obey the one bishop is itself evidence the monarchical episcopate was not yet an established fact but a novelty he was pressing — so that his letters read as prescriptive advocacy, not description of settled practice. On the rhetoric this does not hold (weak): the inference from emphatic exhortation to institutional novelty is a non-sequitur — one urges obedience to an authority precisely because it exists — and Ignatius’s own letters everywhere presuppose the office as in place at the churches he addresses, each with its named bishop (Damas, Polybius, Onesimus, Polycarp), its presbytery already “fitted… as the strings are to the harp,” and a compliance he says they are “indeed” already practising. His strongest complaint — of those who “give one the title of bishop, but do all things without him” (Magnesians 4) — presupposes a recognized bishop being bypassed, i.e. resistance to an existing office, not its novelty. A genuine kernel survives and is credited: the intensity does show the bishop’s monarchical claims were contested where Ignatius wrote — but contestation of an office is materially weaker than novelty. The real evidence that the single-bishop form was not yet universal (collegial Rome, bishop-less Philippi) is external to Ignatius’s tone and weighed, soundly, in the companion uneven-emergence argument; this page rates only the rhetorical inference, which the text will not bear.35

Does apostolic succession guarantee the apostolic faith? (assessment: sound)

The anti-Gnostic succession argument of Irenaeus and Tertullian — that the true apostolic faith is the one the churches can trace in a public, unbroken line of teachers from the apostles, so that the heretics, who can show no such line, stand exposed as innovators. In the form its authors actually advance it — a guarantee of doctrinal continuity, publicly checkable teaching against secret novelty — the argument is well-grounded and largely unrefuted for its era (sound), and both fathers keep it honest by anchoring the guarantee in fidelity of teaching: Irenaeus warns of successors who “fall away” (Against Heresies III.3.1) and Tertullian counts churches with no apostolic founder “not less apostolic… because they are akin in doctrine” (Prescription 32). What does not follow — the flagged overreach — is the later inflation into a mechanical or sacramental guarantee (a valid line of ordination securing a see’s orthodoxy or its jurisdiction of itself): the earliest single-line list is partly a retrojection onto a college of presbyter-bishops, and apostolic sees have in fact taught heresy (Antioch under Paul of Samosata, Constantinople under Nestorius, Rome under Honorius), the church judging the bishop by the faith and not the reverse. That heavier, jurisdictional/sacramental form is weighed under the ministerial priesthood; the argument is kept distinct from the modern Baptist successionism thesis, which inverts it into a chain of dissenting congregations.36

Related arguments — the parity of bishop and presbyter

Both of these arguments read the New Testament’s own vocabulary the same way — that episkopos (“bishop/overseer”) and presbyteros (“presbyter/elder”) named one office, not two — and both are rated sound for that philological core. The first takes the case from the New Testament vocabulary at large (the plural presbyter-bishops of Philippi, the elders “in every city,” the deacons as a second order); the second is the classic patristic statement of the same identity, from Jerome. Each flags the same overreach: that the identity of the two words settles a complete form of government or excludes any presiding office — which it does not.

Do the New Testament office-terms settle a biblical polity? (assessment: sound)

The philological argument from the New Testament’s own office-vocabulary — that presbyteros and episkopos name one local office, appointed in plurality in each church, with deacons as the settled second order (a two-office ministry, not a single bishop over the presbyters). The two titles interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7), Paul greets plural “bishops and deacons” in one city (Philippians 1:1), elders are appointed as a college “in every city” (Titus 1:5; Acts 14:23), and Chrysostom and Theodoret read the text the same way — so the two-office core is well-grounded (sound), the shared foundation on which the presbyterian and congregational cases both build. What does not follow from the vocabulary alone — the flagged overreach — is that the terms therefore fix a polity: a distinct trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, James, Timothy, Titus) stands above the local presbyter-bishops, plurality is not strict parity of function (1 Tim 5:17, “the elders that rule well… they who labour in the word and doctrine”), and the presbyterian and congregational readings rest on these same verses yet divide over where final authority sits — so the words settle the offices, not the form of government.37

Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office? (assessment: sound)

Jerome’s thesis that episkopos and presbyteros named one and the same office — the classic patristic statement of the parity reading. In the New Testament the two titles interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7), Paul greets “bishops” in the plural in one city (Philippians 1:1), and 1 Clement 44 uses both words of the same officers — so the terminological identity in the apostolic and earliest church is well-grounded (sound), the common ground of modern scholarship across confessions. What does not follow from the lexical fact alone — and is the flagged overreach — is the further inference that the single presiding bishop is therefore a merely human custom without apostolic warrant: a distinct trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, James at Jerusalem, Timothy and Titus) stands above the local presbyter-bishops in the same New Testament, and Jerome himself keeps the bishop’s reserved power of ordination and his rank “by the custom of the Church.” That larger question — whether the monarchical episcopate is apostolic or a later development, and whether it binds iure divino — is weighed just above in the emergence of the episcopate.38

Related arguments — the forms of government

The three rival forms of church government — episcopal (a bishop set over the presbyters), presbyterian (rule by a college of elders), and congregational (final authority in the gathered assembly) — are best read together, because each is a total account built on the same handful of under-determining texts (Acts 6; Acts 15; Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 5; Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:17). Each has a well-grounded core and a defining overreach, and each is rated contested: the same evidence that grounds one reading’s strength is claimed, on identical grounds, by its rivals.

Is episcopacy of divine right (iure divino)? (assessment: contested)

The reading that government by bishops is of divine right — a distinct order set over the presbyters, given the church by God and not at its free disposal to lay aside — advanced in the Elizabethan debates by Hooker (moderate) and Bancroft (hard), against the adiaphorism of Whitgift. The interpretation splits by strength. In its moderate form — episcopacy apostolic in root, primitive, all but universal from the second century, and divinely approved — it is well-grounded, overlapping the substance-apostolic reading of the threefold-ministry question and the doctrinal-continuity form of apostolic succession (both above); Hooker’s own case concedes the New Testament parity of the two titles and rests on the office’s apostolic origin and Spirit-guided universality. In its strict form — a distinct order by divine command whose absence unchurches — it is weak: no such order is commanded in the New Testament (the parity of episkopos and presbyteros is sound), the monarchical bishop emerged unevenly rather than as a delivered blueprint (the threefold-ministry question is contested), the adiaphorist reply that Scripture fixes no one perpetual polity stands, and the English church’s own reception of non-episcopally-ordained Reformed ministers — with Hooker’s concession of “ordination made without a bishop” in necessity — refuse the church-unmaking edge. Between a sound moderate reading and a weak strict one, no clear winner (contested); the episcopal leg of the three-way dispute over the form of government, its rivals the presbyterian and congregational readings (below).39

Is rule by a college of elders the apostolic form of government? (assessment: contested)

The presbyterian thesis — that in the New Testament every church was governed by a plurality (college) of elders of one order, so that collegial elder-rule (not a single presiding bishop, and not the gathered congregation) is the apostolic form of government, extended upward through graded assemblies (session → presbytery → synod) — the presbyterian leg of the three-way dispute, advanced by Calvin and the Reformed standards (the Second Helvetic Confession, 1566; the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government, 1645). The reading splits. Its plurality-of-elders core is well-grounded: the apostolic writings appoint elders in the plural in every church — “elders in every church” (Acts 14:23), “elders in every city” (Titus 1:5), “bishops and deacons” in one city (Philippians 1:1), the plural “elders” called “overseers” at Ephesus (Acts 20:17, 28) — and even the fathers who most exalt the bishop concede it (Chrysostom on Philippians 1:1, “he called the Presbyters” bishops; Jerome’s “common council of the presbyters”), Peter numbering himself among the elders whose collegial oversight he bids (1 Peter 5:1–2). But the leap from plurality to the defining presbyterian claims the same texts will not carry: a college admits a president (a primus inter pares — James at Jerusalem, the single “angel” of each church), so plurality is not strict parity; 1 Timothy 5:17’s “especially they who labour in the word” may grade one office rather than found a distinct order of lay ruling elders; a trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, Timothy, Titus, told to appoint elders) stands over the local college; and Acts 15 is an apostolic council wielding the apostles’ own unrepeatable authority, not self-evidently a template for standing synods. Above all the proof-texts under-determine the question, claimed equally by the episcopal and congregational readings. Between a sound plurality core and an iure divino synodal superstructure the evidence will not bear, genuinely disputed (contested); its rivals are the episcopal reading (just above) and the congregational reading (just below).40

Does the gathered congregation hold final authority? (assessment: contested)

The congregational (or independent) thesis — that in the New Testament final authority in discipline, doctrine, and the choosing of officers rests in the whole gathered local congregation, not in a bishop or even a college of elders acting over it — the congregational leg of the three-way dispute over the form of government, advanced by the Separatist and Independent tradition (Robert Browne, 1582; the Cambridge Platform, 1648; the Savoy Declaration, 1658; John Owen). The reading splits. Its corporate-discipline core is well-grounded: the local congregation really is the disciplining body — the offense is told “unto the church” and the keys are given to it (Matthew 18:17–18), the assembly gathers to “deliver such an one unto Satan” and to “put away from among yourselves” the offender (1 Corinthians 5:4–5, 13), the penalty is “inflicted of many” (2 Corinthians 2:6), and the multitude chooses the Seven (Acts 6:2–5) — a share the earliest exegetes grant (Paul “makes them also partners in the sentence,” Chrysostom; the ejection “by common consent,” Ambrosiaster), and Cyprian did nothing “without the consent of the people.” But the leap from that to final congregational authority the same texts will not carry: Matthew 18’s “church” is not fixed as the assembled multitude (the early readers construe it as the church acting through its officer), Paul presides over the Corinthian sentence (“my spirit… with the power of our Lord”), a trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, James, Timothy, Titus) stands over the local body, and the Jerusalem decree issues in “the apostles and elders” name and binds other churches (Acts 15:22). Above all the proof-texts under-determine the question: the presbyterian reading claims every one of them for the eldership, and the congregational tradition’s own standards distribute power between the elders’ “power of office” and the brethren’s “power of privilege” rather than vesting it in the assembly alone. Between a sound corporate-discipline reading and a final-authority claim the evidence will not carry, genuinely disputed (contested); its rivals are the episcopal reading (iure divino, just above) and the presbyterian reading of the same verses.41

Related arguments — the making of a minister

How a man came to hold ministerial office turned on two acts — the church’s choice of the man and the conferral of office upon him by the laying on of hands — and the polities divide over two questions about them. The first asks who performs the ordaining act: the standing ministry, or the congregation? The second asks which act is constitutive: does the people’s election make the minister, ordination merely ratifying, or is office conferred from above, the people nominating and consenting? Both read the same seam of texts — the multitude choosing the Seven while the apostles lay on hands (Acts 6), the elders “ordained” (cheirotoneō) in every church (Acts 14:23), the Didache’s “elect for yourselves,” Cyprian’s people’s suffrage joined to the bishops’ sentence — and reach opposite emphases.

Who confers ministerial office by the laying on of hands? (assessment: sound)

The reading that the conferral of office by the laying on of hands is an act of the standing ministry, distinct from the congregation’s election — the people (may) choose the man, but those already in office ordain him. In Acts the multitude chooses the Seven and the apostles lay hands on them (Acts 6:5–6); Paul recalls the gift given “with the laying on of the hands of the presbytery” (1 Timothy 4:14) and “by the putting on of my hands” (2 Timothy 1:6); Paul and Barnabas “ordained… elders in every church” (Acts 14:23) and Titus is left to do the same (Titus 1:5); and the earliest manuals reserve the act to the ministry — a bishop is “chosen by all the people” but the bishops lay on hands, a presbyter “does not ordain the clergy” (Apostolic Tradition 2, 8), “a presbyter… lays on hands, but does not ordain” (Apostolic Constitutions VIII.28). For that core the reading is well-grounded and near a cross-confessional consensus (sound). Three overreaches are flagged: that the congregation’s cheirotoneō (Acts 14:23; Didache 15) makes its vote the ordaining act — the word means “appoint,” and marks election, not ordination; that 1 Timothy 4:14 (“presbytery”) or 2 Timothy 1:6 (“my hands”) alone fixes the ordainer’s rank — they most likely report one ordination from two sides, Paul presiding and the elders associating; and that Acts 13:3 bears on the conferral of office at all — it commissions men already in office. The remaining live dispute — which minister ordains, a bishop as a distinct order or a presbytery of equals — is left to the parity of bishop and presbyter and the emergence of the episcopate.42

Were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? (assessment: contested)

The reading that a minister was made by the church’s own election — the people choosing the man, ordination ratifying a decision already taken — against office conferred from above by an ordaining authority, the people merely nominating and consenting. Its participation core is well-grounded: the multitude chooses the Seven (Acts 6:3–5; “again it is the people that choose,” Chrysostom), cheirotoneō plainly carries an election sense in the New Testament’s own usage (the brother “chosen of the churches,” 2 Corinthians 8:19), and the Fathers guarded the people’s suffrage and their veto over an unworthy man (the Didache 15; Cyprian, Ep. 67; the Apostolic Tradition). But the strong claim — that election was the decisive act, ordination mere ratification — the same texts will not carry: cheirotoneō in Acts 14:23 has Paul and Barnabas, not the crowd, for its subject (and the ancient exegetes hear an ordination), Matthias is chosen by lot not vote (Acts 1:26), the apostles keep “the fixing the number, and the ordaining” while making the choice over to the people (Chrysostom), Titus is told to ordain elders (Titus 1:5), and Cyprian couples the people’s suffrage with “the sentence of the bishops” by which office is conferred. Between a sound participation strand and a decisive-election claim the under-determining texts will not carry (contested); its companion is the who-ordains argument just above, and it borders the congregational-authority reading of the same verses.43

Is government by council the apostolic pattern? (assessment: sound)

The conciliar reading — that corporate, deliberative government by council and synod is the apostolic pattern — rooted in the Jerusalem council of Acts 15 (“the apostles and elders… with the whole church”; “it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us”), embodied in Cyprian’s collegial episcopate and the African synods, and enacted as ecumenical law in Nicaea’s provincial-synod canons (4–6). As an account of a real, apostolic, continuously-practised organ of government — and a genuine corporate check on the single bishop — the reading is well-grounded and near the historical consensus (sound), and the “late administrative overlay” dismissal is false to the record. Two opposite overreaches are flagged: Acts 15 does not establish a council-only polity that excludes any presiding office (the deciding body wielded the apostles’ own, unrepeatable authority, and James presides in framing the decree, v. 19), and the mirror-image reduction of councils to a mere human convenience of no apostolic standing is equally wrong — because every council after the apostles is an assembly of bishops, conciliar government crowns the standing ministry and limits it, but presupposes it. The seam between conciliar equality and the later Roman claim to jurisdiction — “no bishop of bishops,” Nicaea canon 6’s plural spheres — is where this argument bears on the primacy of Rome.44

Did the early church ordain women to the diaconate? (assessment: sound)

The case that the ancient church kept an ordained order of women deacons — Phoebe the “deacon [diakonos] of the church which is at Cenchrea” (Romans 16:1), the “women” read as deaconesses by the Greek exegetes (1 Timothy 3:11), Pliny’s two ministrae (c. 112), and the mature order of the Apostolic Constitutions and the councils. For the core historical claim — that the church, and the Greek East unmistakably, recognized and ordained a female diaconate, with a bishop’s laying-on of hands (cheirotonia), a dedicated ordination prayer, and defined duties (Apostolic Constitutions III / VIII; Nicaea c. 19; Chalcedon c. 15) — the reading is well-grounded and near the historical consensus (sound). Two overreaches are flagged: the two proof-texts do not by themselves settle it (diakonos carries a generic sense, and 1 Timothy 3:11 is genuinely disputed — Ambrosiaster and Aquinas read it of deacons’ wives and deny the office), so the order rests on the church-orders and councils; and the female diaconate was a distinct, subordinate office — ministry to women, ranked below the deacons, barred from the blessing and the altar — not simply the male office opened to women, so its ordination is not evidence that men and women held one interchangeable order.45
