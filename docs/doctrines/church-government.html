The Government of the Church

How was the church actually ordered — who governs it, what its offices are, and how a man comes to hold one? This timeline traces the development of church government across time: the offices the earliest congregations knew (the bishop or overseer, the presbyter or elder, and the deacon), and the rival forms of government that grew from them — congregational (each local body governs itself), presbyterian (rule by a college of elders), and episcopal (rule by bishops set over the presbyters), the last maturing in the West into the Roman papacy. The witnesses are set out earliest first — each slotted by date, with his own words where they survive — so that the drift from the fluid ministry of the New Testament and the Didache toward the settled threefold order of Ignatius and Cyprian can be read off the timeline itself.

The contested forms are not argued on the timeline. Because every side claims the same handful of passages (Acts 20:17 / 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1; 1 Clement 44) and the same early witnesses (Clement, Ignatius, the Didache, Cyprian, Jerome), the questions on which they divide — whether episkopos and presbyteros named one office or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic or a later development, whether office passes by an unbroken succession — cut across the timeline rather than sitting at any one point on it. They are therefore weighed adversarially, each on its own merits, in the Arguments section below, not in the chronological list.

Related doctrines: Church order and government — the Eucharistic and sacramental side of the ministry is traced under the ministerial priesthood (who may confect the sacraments, ordination, apostolic succession as a channel of grace); a rival free-church succession model is weighed under Baptist successionism; and the apostolic office itself — Matthias chosen by lot to fill Judas’s episkopē (Acts 1:20) — sits on the apostles hub.

Timeline

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Carried under guard from Antioch to martyrdom at Rome, Ignatius writes to the churches of Asia Minor and gives the earliest unambiguous picture of the monarchical (single-bishop) episcopate: in each city one bishop presides, distinct from and set over the presbytery (the college of elders), with the deacons a third order beneath both. He states the threefold order and makes it constitutive of the church itself: “let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ… and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church” (Trallians 3); the bishop “presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons” (Magnesians 6).1 Obedience to the bishop he casts as obedience to God — “we should look upon the bishop even as we would upon the Lord Himself” (Ephesians 6) — and makes the bishop the visible centre of the church’s unity: “Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church” (Smyrnaeans 8).2 Where 1 Clement, a decade earlier, still seems to use “bishop” and “presbyter” of one office, Ignatius everywhere presupposes the three ranked orders — the sharpest early evidence for the episcopal shape of the ministry. Whether that shape is apostolic, universal by c. 108, or a development drawn out of the New Testament’s two-order usage is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.
