The Government of the Church

How was the church actually ordered — who governs it, what its offices are, and how a man comes to hold one? This timeline traces the development of church government across time: the offices the earliest congregations knew (the bishop or overseer, the presbyter or elder, and the deacon), and the rival forms of government that grew from them — congregational (each local body governs itself), presbyterian (rule by a college of elders), and episcopal (rule by bishops set over the presbyters), the last maturing in the West into the Roman papacy. The witnesses are set out earliest first — each slotted by date, with his own words where they survive — so that the drift from the fluid ministry of the New Testament and the Didache toward the settled threefold order of Ignatius and Cyprian can be read off the timeline itself.

The contested forms are not argued on the timeline. Because every side claims the same handful of passages (Acts 20:17 / 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1; 1 Clement 44) and the same early witnesses (Clement, Ignatius, the Didache, Cyprian, Jerome), the questions on which they divide — whether episkopos and presbyteros named one office or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic or a later development, whether office passes by an unbroken succession — cut across the timeline rather than sitting at any one point on it. They are therefore weighed adversarially, each on its own merits, in the Arguments section below, not in the chronological list.

Related doctrines: Church order and government — the Eucharistic and sacramental side of the ministry is traced under the ministerial priesthood (who may confect the sacraments, ordination, apostolic succession as a channel of grace); a rival free-church succession model is weighed under Baptist successionism; and the apostolic office itself — Matthias chosen by lot to fill Judas’s episkopē (Acts 1:20) — sits on the apostles hub.

Timeline

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

The earliest post‑New‑Testament church document, and so the earliest sub‑apostolic witness to church order. Writing in the name of the Roman church to Corinth to quell a revolt that had wrongfully deposed its presbyters, Clement describes a ministry of bishops and deacons appointed by the apostles — a two‑order local ministry (Philippians 1:1), traced in a descending line of authorization “God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons” (1 Clement 42).1 Yet across the same letter he uses “bishop” (episkopos) and “presbyter” (presbyteros) of the same officers: the strife is over “the office of the episcopate,” to depose the officers is to “eject from the episcopate,” and those same deposed men are in the next breath “those presbyters” whom it is sin to remove (1 Clement 44) — the same fluid, two-terms-one-office usage found in the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7).2 The governing body at Corinth is a college — “the presbyters set over” the flock, in the plural — and no single ruling bishop of Corinth is named or appealed to anywhere in the letter; the remedy is to “submit yourselves to the presbyters” (1 Clement 54, 57).3 On how a man comes to office, Clement holds together what later readings pull apart: office passes by an orderly succession from the apostles (“when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry”), and a lawful appointment carries “the consent of the whole Church” (1 Clement 44).2 So 1 Clement attests, c. 96, a two-order ministry — not yet the threefold, monarchical episcopate — continued by an apostolically arranged succession: the anchor witness for the parity reading and the seed of the apostolic-succession reading alike. Which of those the passage establishes, and whether the single-bishop order that appears a decade later in Ignatius is apostolic or a development, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

The Didache (c. 100)

The earliest surviving church-order manual — the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, an anonymous manual of practice whose date is debated (c. 50–150; anchored here at the conventional c. 100, its church-order section widely held to preserve a very early stratum). It catches a congregation at the hinge between two ministries. A still-living order of itinerant, charismatic apostles, prophets, and teachers is tested for authenticity and supported from the first-fruits — the prophets ranked highest, “for they are your high priests” (chs. 11–13).4 Beside it stands a settled local ministry the community is told to provide for itself: “Appoint [cheirotonēsate, elect by show of hands] for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord… for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” (ch. 15).5 The offices are plural and local — the same two-order pairing as Philippians 1:1 and 1 Clement 42 — chosen by congregational election, not laying-on of hands; and, strikingly, no single presiding bishop is named and the word “presbyter” never appears in the manual at all.5 So the Didache documents, from the inside, the drift from a charismatic to an appointed leadership — the stage between the fluid ministry of the New Testament and 1 Clement and the settled threefold order of Ignatius. Whether its silence on the monarch-bishop and the presbytery marks an earlier stage of the ministry or merely a different region, and what its congregational election shows for how office is filled, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Carried under guard from Antioch to martyrdom at Rome, Ignatius writes to the churches of Asia Minor and gives the earliest unambiguous picture of the monarchical (single-bishop) episcopate: in each city one bishop presides, distinct from and set over the presbytery (the college of elders), with the deacons a third order beneath both. He states the threefold order and makes it constitutive of the church itself: “let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ… and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church” (Trallians 3); the bishop “presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons” (Magnesians 6).6 Obedience to the bishop he casts as obedience to God — “we should look upon the bishop even as we would upon the Lord Himself” (Ephesians 6) — and makes the bishop the visible centre of the church’s unity: “Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church” (Smyrnaeans 8).7 Where 1 Clement, a decade earlier, still seems to use “bishop” and “presbyter” of one office, Ignatius everywhere presupposes the three ranked orders — the sharpest early evidence for the episcopal shape of the ministry. Whether that shape is apostolic, universal by c. 108, or a development drawn out of the New Testament’s two-order usage is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Against the Gnostics—who claimed a secret tradition passed privately from the apostles—Irenaeus forges the succession argument into its first developed form. The real apostolic teaching, he answers, is public and traceable: it is “within the power of all… to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles… and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times” (Against Heresies III.3.1). He offers the Roman line (Linus → Anacletus → Clement → … → Eleutherius, “the twelfth place from the apostles”) and Polycarp at Smyrna as specimens any church could match (III.3.2–4).8 For Irenaeus the succession guarantees doctrinal continuity—public teaching against private novelty—and he keeps it honest by anchoring it in fidelity: successors are a boon “if they discharged their functions honestly, but if they should fall away, the direst calamity” (III.3.1), and the presbyters to obey are those who have the succession and “that which is sound… in conduct… and incorrupt in speech” (IV.26.2, 5). Tellingly, that same succession is a succession “of the presbytery” as readily as of the episcopate—the two-terms-one-office fluidity of 1 Clement and the New Testament, a generation after Ignatius’s sharp threefold order.9 Whether the monarchical office the lists presuppose is apostolic, and whether the succession guarantees the faith mechanically or only evidences its doctrinal continuity, is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian gives the succession argument its legal edge. Borrowing the Roman lawyer’s praescriptio—an objection that halts a suit before its merits are heard—he tells the heretics to “unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning” until their first bishop can show “for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles”; the Gnostics can produce no such roll and are thereby non-suited (Prescription 32, instancing Smyrna’s Polycarp from John and Rome’s Clement from Peter).10 Yet in the same breath he makes plain that the test is finally doctrine, not pedigree: churches with no apostolic founder—“of much later date, for they are in fact being founded daily”—are “not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine” (32), for “we hold communion with the apostolic churches because our doctrine is in no respect different from theirs” (21). His two-name registers, and his placing Clement directly after Peter—against Irenaeus’s Linus–Anacletus–Clement—show the rolls pressed into anti-heretical service rather than kept as exact archives.11 Whether the argument bears the heavier weight later hung on it—succession as a mechanical or jurisdictional guarantee—is weighed below in the Arguments, not on this timeline.

Arguments

The contested forms of church government — whether bishop and presbyter are one order or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic or a later development, whether office passes by an unbroken succession — cut across the timeline above rather than sitting at any one point on it, so they are weighed here, each on its own page.

Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office? (assessment: sound)

Jerome’s thesis that episkopos and presbyteros named one and the same office — the classic patristic statement of the parity reading. In the New Testament the two titles interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7), Paul greets “bishops” in the plural in one city (Philippians 1:1), and 1 Clement 44 uses both words of the same officers — so the terminological identity in the apostolic and earliest church is well-grounded (sound), the common ground of modern scholarship across confessions. What does not follow from the lexical fact alone — and is the flagged overreach — is the further inference that the single presiding bishop is therefore a merely human custom without apostolic warrant: a distinct trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, James at Jerusalem, Timothy and Titus) stands above the local presbyter-bishops in the same New Testament, and Jerome himself keeps the bishop’s reserved power of ordination and his rank “by the custom of the Church.” That larger question — whether the monarchical episcopate is apostolic or a later development, and whether it binds iure divino — is weighed separately.12

Does apostolic succession guarantee the apostolic faith? (assessment: sound)

The anti-Gnostic succession argument of Irenaeus and Tertullian — that the true apostolic faith is the one the churches can trace in a public, unbroken line of teachers from the apostles, so that the heretics, who can show no such line, stand exposed as innovators. In the form its authors actually advance it — a guarantee of doctrinal continuity, publicly checkable teaching against secret novelty — the argument is well-grounded and largely unrefuted for its era (sound), and both fathers keep it honest by anchoring the guarantee in fidelity of teaching: Irenaeus warns of successors who “fall away” (Against Heresies III.3.1) and Tertullian counts churches with no apostolic founder “not less apostolic… because they are akin in doctrine” (Prescription 32). What does not follow — the flagged overreach — is the later inflation into a mechanical or sacramental guarantee (a valid line of ordination securing a see’s orthodoxy or its jurisdiction of itself): the earliest single-line list is partly a retrojection onto a college of presbyter-bishops, and apostolic sees have in fact taught heresy (Antioch under Paul of Samosata, Constantinople under Nestorius, Rome under Honorius), the church judging the bishop by the faith and not the reverse. That heavier, jurisdictional/sacramental form is weighed under the ministerial priesthood; the argument is kept distinct from the modern Baptist successionism thesis, which inverts it into a chain of dissenting congregations.13
