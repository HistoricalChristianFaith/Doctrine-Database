The Government of the Church

How has the church been ordered — who governs it, what its offices are, and how a person comes to hold one? This timeline sets out the witnesses to church government, earliest extant attestation first: the offices named in the earliest congregations (the bishop or overseer, the presbyter or elder, and the deacon), and the forms of government the communions have held — episcopal (rule by bishops, with presbyters and deacons), presbyterian (rule by a college of elders), and congregational (each local body governing itself). Each witness is slotted by date, with his own words where they survive, from the Jerusalem assembly of Acts 15 and the earliest church orders, through Ignatius, Irenaeus, Cyprian and the Council of Nicaea, to the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Reformed and English writers who disputed whether the episcopal order binds by divine right (iure divino) or is an arrangement the church may alter.

The deacon appears in the earliest witnesses (Philippians 1:1; the Didache; 1 Clement). Whether the early church ordained women to the diaconate (Phoebe, “servant [diakonos] of the church which is at Cenchrea”, Romans 16:1) is set out with the other questions of interpretation under Disputed readings below.

The traditions appeal to the same passages (Acts 20:17 / 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1; 1 Clement 44) and the same early witnesses (Clement, Ignatius, the Didache, Cyprian, Jerome). Several questions about how they are to be read — whether episkopos and presbyteros named one office or two, whether the threefold ministry is apostolic in origin or a later development, how widely the single bishop was established by the early second century, and how a minister was made — cut across the timeline rather than sitting at one point on it. They are set out, with each reading attributed to its holders, under Disputed readings below.

Related doctrines: Church order and government — see also the primacy of Rome (the place of the bishop of Rome among the bishops). The Eucharistic and sacramental side of the ministry is traced under the ministerial priesthood (who may celebrate the sacraments, ordination, apostolic succession as a channel of grace); a free-church succession model is traced under Baptist successionism; and the apostolic office itself — Matthias chosen by lot to fill Judas’s episkopē (Acts 1:20) — sits on the apostles hub.

Timeline

The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) (c. 49)

Acts 15 narrates how the circumcision controversy at Antioch was settled at Jerusalem. The question was referred “unto the apostles and elders,” who “came together for to consider of this matter” and settled it after “much disputing”; the decree went out from “the apostles and elders, with the whole church,” in the name of “the apostles and elders and brethren” (Acts 15:2, 6, 22–23).1 The decision is issued as “it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us” (Acts 15:28), which Chrysostom glosses as “the Spirit’s legislating,” the “to us” showing that the apostles “also themselves admit the Gentiles.”2

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

One of the earliest extant Christian writings outside the New Testament. Writing in the name of the Roman church to Corinth, where a revolt had deposed the presbyters, Clement describes bishops and deacons appointed by the apostles (cf. Philippians 1:1), in a descending line of authorization “God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons” (1 Clement 42).3 In ch. 44 the strife is over “the office of the episcopate,” to depose the officers is to “eject from the episcopate,” and the deposed are in the next breath “those presbyters” whom it is sin to remove; how this usage relates to the same pairing in the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7) is set out under Disputed readings.4 The letter speaks of “the presbyters set over” the flock in the plural, urges the Corinthians to “submit yourselves to the presbyters,” and names no individual bishop of Corinth (1 Clement 54, 57).5 On how a man comes to office, Clement speaks both of an orderly succession from the apostles (“when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry”) and of appointment with “the consent of the whole Church” (1 Clement 44).4 How the letter bears on the parity of bishop and presbyter and on the origin of the single-bishop order is set out under Disputed readings.

The Didache (c. 100)

The earliest extant church-order manual — the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, an anonymous manual of practice whose date is debated (c. 50–150; anchored here at the conventional c. 100). It describes two kinds of ministry side by side. Itinerant apostles, prophets, and teachers are tested for authenticity and supported from the first-fruits — the prophets ranked highest, “for they are your high priests” (chs. 11–13).6 Beside them the community is told to provide a local ministry for itself: “Appoint [cheirotonēsate] for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord… for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” (ch. 15).7 The offices are named in the plural; no single presiding bishop is named, and the word “presbyter” does not occur in the manual.7 Whether the verb cheirotonēsate here denotes election by the community or appointment, and whether the manual’s silence on a single bishop and on presbyters reflects an earlier stage of the ministry or a regional difference, are among the questions under Disputed readings.

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Carried under guard from Antioch to martyrdom at Rome, Ignatius writes to the churches of Asia Minor. His letters are the earliest extant texts to describe, in each city he addresses, a single bishop presiding over the presbytery (the college of elders), with the deacons named as a third order. He names the three together and writes: “let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ… and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church” (Trallians 3); the bishop “presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons” (Magnesians 6).8 He calls for obedience to the bishop — “we should look upon the bishop even as we would upon the Lord Himself” (Ephesians 6) — and ties the church’s gatherings to him: “Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church” (Smyrnaeans 8).9 1 Clement, about a decade earlier, uses “bishop” and “presbyter” in the one chapter of the deposed Corinthian officers; Ignatius distinguishes the two titles throughout. Whether this order is apostolic in origin, how widely it was established by c. 108, and whether the letters describe it or urge it are set out under Disputed readings.

Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 125)

Writing from Smyrna to the church at Philippi, in a letter that forwards the letters of Ignatius and asks after his fate (ch. 13), implying a date shortly after Ignatius passed through on his way to martyrdom, while ch. 9 treats him as dead (P. N. Harrison, 1936, divides the letter into two) — Polycarp addresses a ministry of presbyters and deacons and names no single bishop of Philippi. He greets the church as “Polycarp, and the presbyters with him,” sets out the duties of its deacons and presbyters, and directs the congregation to be “subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ” (Philippians 5–6).10 The occasion of the letter is the case of Valens, “once a presbyter among you,” who had fallen into avarice; Polycarp addresses the whole church about him — “call them back… that ye may save your whole body” (Philippians 11).11 Polycarp himself is named bishop of Smyrna in the letter’s manuscript title. Why the letter names no bishop of Philippi is among the questions under Disputed readings.

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140–155)

A long apocalypse written at Rome in the mid-second century. Hermas is charged to read his book in the city “along with the presbyters who preside over the Church,” and Clement is named as the one who will send the book “to foreign countries,” for “permission has been granted” him to do so (Visions 2.4.3).12 When he lists the church’s leaders he speaks of “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” (Visions 3.5.1) and of “bishops given to hospitality” who received the servants of God into their houses and sheltered the widows and the destitute (Similitudes 9.27); the word appears in the plural, and the book names no individual bishop of Rome.13 How this Roman evidence bears on the spread of the single-bishop order is among the questions under Disputed readings.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Against the Gnostics, who claimed a secret tradition passed privately from the apostles, Irenaeus argues from the succession of bishops that the apostolic teaching is public and traceable: it is “within the power of all… to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles… and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times” (Against Heresies III.3.1). He gives the Roman line (Linus → Anacletus → Clement → … → Eleutherius, “the twelfth place from the apostles”) and Polycarp at Smyrna as examples (III.3.2–4).14 He conditions the succession on fidelity: successors are a boon “if they discharged their functions honestly, but if they should fall away, the direst calamity” (III.3.1), and the presbyters to obey are those who have the succession and “that which is sound… in conduct… and incorrupt in speech” (IV.26.2, 5). In Book IV the succession is ascribed to the presbyters, where Book III speaks of bishops.15 How the bishop-lists bear on the origin and spread of the single-bishop office is among the questions under Disputed readings.

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian puts the succession argument in legal form. Borrowing the Roman lawyer’s praescriptio — an objection that halts a suit before its merits are heard — he tells the heretics to “unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning” until their first bishop can show “for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles”; the Gnostics, he argues, can produce no such roll (Prescription 32, instancing Smyrna’s Polycarp from John and Rome’s Clement from Peter).16 In the same work he also grounds apostolicity in agreement of doctrine: churches with no apostolic founder — “of much later date, for they are in fact being founded daily” — are “not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine” (32), for “we hold communion with the apostolic churches because our doctrine is in no respect different from theirs” (21). He places Clement directly after Peter at Rome, where Irenaeus’s list has Linus and Anacletus before Clement.17

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215)

The Apostolic Tradition, attributed to Hippolytus, is the earliest extant ordination manual (its authorship and date are discussed on the detail page). It keeps the people’s election and the ministry’s ordination as two acts: a bishop is “chosen by all the people,” but “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him, and the presbytery shall stand by in silence” (Apostolic Tradition 2).18 It distinguishes the three orders by their part in the rite: at a presbyter’s ordination the bishop lays on his hand “while the presbyters touch him,” yet the presbyter “has only the power to receive; but he has no power to give… a presbyter does not ordain the clergy”; and a deacon “the bishop alone shall make,” for “he is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop” (Apostolic Tradition 8–9).19 (The Eucharistic side of these same prayers — the bishop consecrated “high priest” to offer the Church’s gifts — is treated under the ministerial priesthood.) How election and ordination together made a minister is among the questions under Disputed readings.

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, wrote at length on church order. He describes the episcopate as one, held in common by the bishops — “the episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole” (episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur) — and in ch. 4 Peter is the emblem of that one office, the other apostles “endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power” (On the Unity of the Church 4–5, received text; a second recension of ch. 4, the “Primacy Text,” also survives, and its readings are taken up under the primacy of Rome).20 At the council of eighty-seven African bishops he convened on the rebaptism dispute, each bishop giving his own sentence, he opened — in his dispute with Stephen of Rome — with: “neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops… since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another” (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256).21 On how a man comes to office he writes that the people “have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones,” and that no ordination is “just and legitimate” unless a bishop is “chosen in the presence of the people… by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled” (Epistle 67, to the Spanish churches; the same double consent to Cornelius in Epistle 54).22 How “no bishop of bishops” bears on the see of Rome is taken up under the primacy of Rome; how the people’s suffrage and the bishops’ sentence relate in the making of a bishop is among the questions under Disputed readings.

The Council of Nicaea (325)

Besides its creed, the first ecumenical council issued canons on the government of the provinces. A bishop is to be consecrated by “all the bishops in the province” (or at least three, with the absent bishops’ written suffrage), “the ratification of what is done” left to the metropolitan (canon 4), and the bishops of each province are to assemble in synod “twice a year” to try disputed cases (canon 5).23 Canon 6 confirms the customary jurisdiction of the bishop of Alexandria over the surrounding provinces, “since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also,” and the privileges of Antioch and the other provinces; how the canon bears on the primacy of Rome is taken up on that timeline.24 (The same council’s canon on the eucharistic ordering of the clergy is treated under the ministerial priesthood.)

John Calvin (1559)

The definitive Latin edition of Calvin’s Institutes (Book IV, chs. 3–4) sets out the Reformed order of ministry. Calvin reads “bishop,” “presbyter,” and “pastor” as names given “indiscriminately… on the authority of Scripture, which uses the words as synonymous,” and describes each church as governed by pastors and teachers who preach, beside “governors” or “seniors selected from the people to unite with the bishops in pronouncing censures and exercising discipline” (the ruling elder), with deacons for the care of the poor: “From the beginning… each church had its senate, composed of pious, grave, and venerable men” (Institutes IV.3.8).25 On how a man comes to office he writes that “ministers are legitimately called… when those who may have seemed fit are elected on the consent and approbation of the people,” other pastors presiding over the election (IV.3.15). The single presiding bishop set over the presbyters he describes as a matter of the church’s custom: in the ancient church “these presbyters selected one of their number to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest… from equality dissension should arise” (IV.4.2), the distinction “introduced by human arrangement,” and he cites Jerome that a bishop is greater than a presbyter by custom rather than by the Lord’s appointment. The Second Helvetic Confession (1566) and the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645) set out Reformed polity with graded assemblies (session, presbytery, synod).26 How the people’s election relates to ordination in the making of a minister is among the questions under Disputed readings.

John Whitgift (1574)

In the Elizabethan controversy over church government, Whitgift — later Archbishop of Canterbury — answered the presbyterian Admonition to the Parliament and Thomas Cartwright. He argues that Scripture prescribes no one form of church government as necessary or unalterable: “I find no one certain and perfect kind of government prescribed or commanded in the scriptures to the church of Christ” — nor any set down “to be perpetual for all times, persons, and places, without alteration” — so that, on his account, polity is a thing indifferent the Christian magistrate may order, and episcopacy is retained by the realm’s authority and the church’s antiquity (The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition, 1574).27 Cartwright held the contrary, that a presbyterian discipline is the form the church may not alter; the detail page sets out the exchange.

Robert Browne (1582)

Against both the bishops and the presbyterians who would wait on Parliament to reform the church, Browne argues in A Treatise of Reformation without Tarrying for Any, a manifesto of English Separatism, that a true church is not the baptized nation under its prelates but a company gathered by covenant under Christ — “the Lord’s people is of the willing sort” — which holds Christ’s keys directly and may order and reform itself at once, “without tarrying for any” magistrate or bishop. On his account the keys, the discipline, and the choice of officers belong to the gathered congregation, and the conforming ministry’s error was to surrender that spiritual discipline to the civil power. Two men were hanged in 1583 for distributing the tract, and Browne himself later conformed. The later congregational statements of the Cambridge Platform, the Savoy Declaration and John Owen are under Owen, below.28

Richard Bancroft (1589)

Against the presbyterian platform and the anonymous Marprelate tracts, Bancroft — later Archbishop of Canterbury — preached at Paul’s Cross on 9 February 1588/9. He grounds the threefold ministry of “Bishops, Priests, and Deacons” on the church’s practice “ever since the apostles times,” and calls the denial of any difference between bishop and presbyter the ancient Aerian heresy, the error Epiphanius called “full of follie.” W. D. J. Cargill Thompson (1969) notes that the sermon “has traditionally been associated with the first appearance in Anglican theology of the jure divino theory of episcopacy,” in an article reconsidering that association, and cites Saravia (1590), Bilson (1593) and Bancroft’s own Survay (1593) from the later Elizabethan debate.29

Richard Hooker (c. 1594)

Hooker holds that a bishop is a distinct order — given “a further power to ordain… and a power of chiefty in government over Presbyters” — and that the office is of divine institution: “the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God, the Holy Ghost was the author of it” (Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII).30 He also allows exceptions: “there may be sometimes very just and sufficient reason to allow ordination made without a bishop,” and he will “not simply without exception” require an unbroken episcopal succession.31 (Book VII was printed only posthumously, 1662.)

John Owen and the Congregational platforms (1689)

In The True Nature of a Gospel Church and its Government (published posthumously, 1689) John Owen — Cromwell’s chaplain, Dean of Christ Church, vice-chancellor of Oxford — places all church power in Christ and its ordinary seat in the particular congregation: this power “in actu primo, or fundamentally, is in the church itself; in actu secundo, or its exercise, in them that are especially called thereunto,” for “the first, original grant of all church power and privileges is made unto believers as such.” On his account the officers’ rule is ministerial and derived. Two Congregational standards set out the same polity: the New-England Cambridge Platform (1648), which divides ordinary church power into the elders’ “power of office” and the brethren’s “power of privilege” — the latter reaching to choosing their own officers, admitting their own members, and the “public censure” of offenders — and the Savoy Declaration (1658, of which Owen was a principal author), whose “Institution of Churches” makes each particular church “the seat of that power which he is pleased to communicate to his saints” and has its officers “chosen… by the common suffrage of the church itself,” synods being for counsel and communion, not jurisdiction.32

Disputed readings

How the early evidence for the offices of the church, and for the making of its ministers, has been read by the different traditions.

The emergence of the episcopate

How the single bishop over the presbyters came to be, and how widely it was established in the early second century.

  • Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? — how the bishop–presbyter interchange of the New Testament and 1 Clement relates to Ignatius’s three orders.33
  • Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? — the evidence of Antioch and Asia set beside that of Rome and Philippi.34
  • Do Ignatius’s letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one? — what the insistence of his exhortations implies about the office.35

Other questions

  • Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office? — the interchange of the two titles in Acts, Titus, Philippians and 1 Clement, and Jerome’s reading of it.36
  • Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? — the choosing of the Seven, cheirotoneō, the Didache, the Apostolic Tradition and Cyprian.37
  • Did the early church ordain women to the diaconate? — Phoebe, 1 Timothy 3:11, and the deaconesses of the Eastern church orders and councils.38
