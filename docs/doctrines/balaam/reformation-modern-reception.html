The Reformation to the modern pulpit: Balaam the mercenary preacher

Dates: c. AD 1520–1900 (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Protestant and later homiletic re-reading of Balaam, from Luther’s and Calvin’s sixteenth-century exegesis through the English pulpit and Joseph Butler’s Fifteen Sermons of 1726 to the figure’s afterlife in the language) · Butler, Sermon VII (anglicanhistory.org)
Summary: Where the Fathers wrestled Balaam as a metaphysical puzzle — how could a wicked diviner utter true prophecy? — the Reformation and after put him to a plainer, sharper use: he became the standing type of the corrupt minister, the man who serves God with his lips for gain. Calvin makes him the portrait of the covetous hypocrite; Luther turns him on the venal clergy of his own day and coins “Balaamites” for them; Matthew Henry and the English pulpit fix him as the preacher who “loved the wages of unrighteousness”; and Bishop Butler’s landmark sermon “Upon the Character of Balaam” reads him as the classic case of moral self-deceit — a man who wished to die the death of the righteous without living the righteous life. The figure even seeped into everyday English. This page gathers that post-medieval reception; it reports how preachers and moralists used Balaam, and takes no position on the historical or exegetical questions weighed elsewhere on the timeline.

Where it sits

By the sixteenth century the great ancient and medieval readings were long set: the rabbinic “Balaam the wicked,” the patristic diviner-who-prophesied, the medieval legend that made the magi his heirs. What the Reformation adds is not a new answer to the old cruxes but a new application. The Reformers read Scripture to reform the church, and in the New Testament’s three tags — the “way,” the “error,” and the “doctrine” of Balaam (2 Peter 2:15; Jude 11; Revelation 2:14) — they found a ready-made mirror for the mercenary preacher and the prophet-for-hire. So this stratum sits on the timeline after the medieval and Islamic reception and before the modern critical survey: it is the last stretch of the figure’s living use in the pulpit, before scholarship turned him into an object of study.

Claims & evidence

Calvin: the hypocrite whose show of scruple masks his greed

John Calvin (1509–1564) treats Balaam at length in his Commentaries on the Four Last Books of Moses (the “Harmony of the Law”), where the whole episode of Numbers 22 becomes a study in religious hypocrisy. Balaam’s careful refusal to move without God’s leave is not piety, Calvin argues, but a bargainer’s cover for his covetousness:

At first sight he pretends a holy anxiety to obey, when he dares to attempt nothing without God’s permission, and refuses to stir a foot, until he shall have received His answer. Yet secret covetousness influences him to obtain from God, by bargaining as it were, what he still feels not to be right.

— Calvin, Harmony of the Law, on Numbers 22:9. From this he draws the lesson for “hypocrites” generally — that “they profit nothing by their vain pretences, although God may indulge them for a time, since He at length taketh the wily in their own craftiness” — and reduces the whole man to a single, memorable image of greed:

Balaam sinned, in that he was attracted, like a hound, by the scent of gain, to sell his curses for money.

— Calvin, Harmony of the Law, on Numbers 22:36, CCEL. This is the Reformation type in miniature: the outward scruples are real, but they serve an inward love of reward — the very “wages of unrighteousness” the New Testament pins on him.1

Luther: “Balaamites … our bishops” — the type turned on the clergy

Martin Luther (1483–1546), expounding 2 Peter 2:15, takes the same reading and aims it polemically. Where Peter compares the false teachers to Balaam “purely for the sake of gold,” Luther identifies the target with the clergy of his own church:

Of this St. Peter here speaks, and would say that our ecclesiastics are specially Balaam’s children and scholars; for just as he gave evil counsel to set up an idol so that the children of Israel should be brought to sin … so have our bishops also set up an idol.

He then coins a name for the venal churchman that captures the whole type in a word:

Such Balaamites are our bishops and ecclesiastics, who are the throat of the devil, by which he draws so many souls to himself, and swallows them down.

— Luther, Commentary on the Epistles of St. Peter and St. Jude, on 2 Peter 2:15, CCEL. “Balaamites” for the mercenary clergy is the sharpest form of the Reformation reading — the biblical prophet-for-hire made a live charge against the contemporary church.2

The English pulpit: Matthew Henry and “the wages of unrighteousness”

The reading crosses into English devotional commentary, where Matthew Henry (1662–1714) gives it its most quotable pastoral form. On 2 Peter 2:15–16 he makes Balaam the case study in how the love of the world unseats a man who knows better:

The inordinate love of the good things of this world turns men out of the way which leads to the unspeakably better things of another life; the love of riches and honour turned Balaam out of the way of his duty, although he knew that the way he took displeased the Lord.

— Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on 2 Peter 2:15–16, Bible Study Tools. Commenting on Numbers 22 he catches the same duplicity in Balaam’s pious talk — “It is an easy thing for bad men to speak very good words” — the double-minded man whose mouth “makes a show of piety” while his heart runs after reward.3

Butler’s “Upon the Character of Balaam” (1726): the anatomy of self-deceit

The century’s most searching treatment is not a commentary but a sermon: Joseph Butler (1692–1752), the Anglican moral philosopher and later Bishop of Durham, devoted the seventh of his Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel to “the Character of Balaam,” taking as his text Balaam’s own wish, “Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my last end be like his” (Numbers 23:10). Butler reads the whole episode as a study in the human capacity for moral self-deception — a man who genuinely reveres goodness and wants a good end, yet will not pay the price of a good life, and so contrives to have both his conscience and his reward:

So that the object we have now before us is the most astonishing in the world: A very wicked man, under a deep sense of God and religion, persisting still in his wickedness, and preferring the wages of unrighteousness, even when he had before him a lively view of death.

The mechanism, for Butler, is that a man in Balaam’s state does not simply choose evil; he negotiates with his own conscience, seeking indulgences and evasions until he can act against what he knows:

He wanted to do what he knew to be very wicked, and contrary to the express command of God; he had inward checks and restraints, which he could not entirely get over; he therefore casts about for ways to reconcile this wickedness with his duty.

— Butler, Fifteen Sermons, Sermon VII, “Upon the Character of Balaam” (1726), anglicanhistory.org. Butler’s Balaam is the enduring one for modern moral thought: not a monster but an ordinary self-deceiver, which is precisely what makes the character “astonishing” and universally applicable.4

Balaam in the language: the idiom and its afterlife

The reach of the tradition finally passes out of the pulpit and into ordinary English. By the mid-seventeenth century “a Balaam” had become a byword for exactly the mercenary-religion type the Reformers preached — in the Online Etymology Dictionary’s summary, figuratively “one who makes profession of religion for the sake of gain,” a usage traced “from the 1640s.” The phrase “Balaam’s ass” kept alive the donkey of the story — the dumb beast given a voice to rebuke its rider (Numbers 22:28–30), the proverbial figure of the unlikely mouth made to speak the truth. And in the jargon of the old printing house the word narrowed to a curiosity all its own: “Balaam” or “Balaam’s ass” came to mean, in Brewer’s Dictionary of Phrase and Fable, spare “matter kept in type for filling up odd spaces” in a newspaper — stray copy pressed into service to plug a short column, an oddly fitting last echo of the seer whose words came out other than he intended.5

Notes

  • Use, not exegesis. This stratum is valuable as reception, not as argument. Calvin, Luther, Henry, and Butler are not weighing whether Balaam was a true prophet or a diviner (the question weighed on the “prophet or diviner?” argument), nor whether he engineered the apostasy at Peor (the “doctrine of Balaam” argument); they take the New Testament’s dark verdict as given and put the figure to homiletic work. Their interest is the moral type, and it is on that ground — not on the historical cruxes — that this page reports them.
  • Bunyan — a parallel, not a citation. John Bunyan’s satire of using religion for gain, above all the character “By-ends” and his companion “Mr. Money-love” in The Pilgrim’s Progress, is the same mercenary-religion type in narrative dress; but Bunyan does not there name Balaam (Christian’s rebuttal invokes Hamor and Shechem, Judas, Simon Magus, and Gehazi instead), so the By-ends episode is best read as a thematic parallel to the way of Balaam rather than as Bunyan invoking the seer.
  • What is not asserted. A popular claim that “Balaamite” became English for a paid political mouthpiece could not be verified in the standard references and is not asserted here; the attested coinage is Luther’s “Balaamites” for venal clergy. The “dumb truth-teller” sense of “Balaam’s ass” is a transparent allusion to Numbers 22:28–30 itself rather than a dictionary idiom.

Bingham, 1852–55) via CCEL; Luther from the Lenker edition of the Commentary on the Epistles of St. Peter and St. Jude via CCEL; Matthew Henry from his Commentary on the Whole Bible; Butler from Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel (1726).
