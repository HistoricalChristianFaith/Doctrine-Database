Josephus on Balaam

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (Jewish priest, soldier, and historian; the Antiquities of the Jews completed c. AD 93–94) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Antiquities of the Jews IV.6 (§§102–158) Josephus gives the fullest first-century retelling of the Balaam episode. He elevates Balaam higher than the biblical text does — “the greatest of the Prophets at that time” — dramatises the speaking ass and the angel, and expands Israel’s blessing into a grand oration; yet he pointedly declines to reproduce the “star out of Jacob,” flattening the fourth oracle into vague predictions about the fates of kings and cities. Above all he turns the Torah’s bare after-the-fact notice (Numbers 31:16) into a set-piece scene: unable to curse Israel, Balaam counsels Balak and the Midianites to send their most beautiful women to seduce the Hebrew youths into idolatry — the narrative aetiology of the New Testament’s “doctrine of Balaam.” Josephus ends on a studied ambivalence: this counsellor of ruin was still so great a prophet that Moses did him the honour of recording his oracles rather than claiming them as his own.

Where it sits

Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100), a priest of Jerusalem and general in the Jewish revolt who became a client of the Flavian emperors at Rome, completed his twenty-book Antiquities of the Jews about AD 93–94 — a paraphrase of Israel’s history from creation to his own day, aimed at a Graeco-Roman readership. The Balaam episode falls in Book IV, chapter 6 (Whiston’s sections; §§102–158 in the Niese numbering), inserted where Numbers places it: after the conquest of the Transjordan and before the war on Midian. Written roughly half a century after Philo’s Life of Moses, it is the second great first-century Jewish retelling on this timeline — and, because Josephus writes as a historian defending his nation’s antiquity and virtue, his handling of Balaam is shaped throughout by apologetic and political care.1

Claims & evidence

“The greatest of the Prophets at that time”

Now these Midianites, knowing there was one Balaam, who lived by Euphrates, and was the greatest of the Prophets at that time, and one that was in friendship with them, sent some of their honourable Princes along with the ambassadors of Balak, to intreat the Prophet to come to them; that he might imprecate curses to the destruction of the Israelites.

— Antiquities IV.6.2 (William Whiston). Josephus rates Balaam higher than any biblical writer does: not a mere qosem (“soothsayer,” as Joshua 13:22 brands him) but the foremost prophet of the age. And the elevation is God’s doing on Josephus’s telling: when Balaam relays the divine refusal to the ambassadors, he speaks of “that God who had raised him to great reputation on account of the truth of his predictions.” The double portrait the Bible leaves in tension — true oracle and hireling diviner — Josephus keeps, but he tilts the first half upward: Balaam is a genuine, God-honoured prophet whose gift is real.2

The speaking ass and the angel in the road

But when the divine Angel met him in the way: when he was in a narrow passage, and hedged in with a wall on both sides, the ass on which Balaam rode, understood that it was a divine spirit that met him; and thrust Balaam to one of the walls; without regard to the stripes which Balaam, when he was hurt by the wall, gave her… by the will of God she made use of the voice of a man, and complained of Balaam, as acting unjustly to her; that whereas he had no fault find with her in her former service to him, he now inflicted stripes upon her.

— Antiquities IV.6.3 (Whiston). Josephus retells the famous pericope of Numbers 22:22–35 straight, without allegorising it away: the ass sees the “divine spirit” her master cannot, speaks “by the will of God,” and the angel then appears to rebuke Balaam, telling him “the brute creature was not in fault; but that he was himself come to obstruct his journey, as being contrary to the will of God.” God sends him on but binds his mouth — “that he should declare nothing, but what he himself should suggest to his mind.” The miracle stands as narrated; Josephus, elsewhere so ready to rationalise, does not blunt it here.3

The blessing of Israel, expanded — but the star withheld

Happy is this people, on whom God bestows the possession of innumerable good things; and grants them his own providence to be their assistant, and their guide!… And you shall be sufficiently numerous to supply the world in general, and every region of it in particular with inhabitants out of your stock… And that more in number than are the stars of heaven.

— Antiquities IV.6.4 (Whiston). Balaam’s forced blessing Josephus amplifies into a sweeping oration of Israel’s future glory, dominion, and inexhaustible increase, closing his own editorial gloss on it: “Thus did Balaam speak by inspiration; as not being in his own power; but moved to say what he did by the divine spirit” (IV.6.5). Yet the most telling feature is an omission. Where Philo read the fourth oracle frankly as a coming king, and where Numbers gives “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (24:17), Josephus quotes no star and no sceptre. He compresses the climactic prophecy into a vague notice: Balaam “fell… upon his face, and foretold what calamities would befal the several Kings of the nations, and the most eminent cities… which events have come to pass … till my own memory.” A historian writing under Flavian patronage, within a generation of a Jewish war inflamed by messianic hope, had reason not to publicise an oracle of a world-ruler rising out of Israel; whatever the motive, Josephus is the first-century witness who conspicuously declines the royal-messianic reading of the star that Philo and later Christians embraced.4

The counsel of Balaam: seduction as strategy

Do you therefore set out the comeliness of such of your daughters as are most eminent for beauty, and proper to force and conquer the modesty of those that behold them; and these decked and trimmed to the highest degree you are able. Then do you send them to be near the Israelites camp; and give them in charge, that when the young men of the Hebrews desire their company, they allow it them. And when they see they are enamoured of them, let them take their leaves; and if they intreat them to stay, let them not give their consent, till they have persuaded them to leave off their obedience to their own laws, and the worship of that God who established them; and to worship the Gods of the Midianites and Moabites: for by this means God will be angry at them.

— Antiquities IV.6.6 (Whiston). This is the load-bearing passage. The Torah says only, after the fact, that “these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor” (Numbers 31:16). Josephus, like Philo before him, unpacks that lone phrase into a deliberate scheme — but he stages it as a farewell speech: rebuffed and sent home unrewarded, Balaam turns back to Balak and the Midianite princes and, unbidden, hands them the one strategy that can wound Israel. Since God will not permit their destruction “by war, nor by plague, nor by scarcity,” the only opening is to make Israel forfeit her own protection by breaking her laws — so seduce the young men through the beauty of the women into idolatry, “for by this means God will be angry at them.” Josephus then dramatises the plan working: the Midianite women’s coy speeches, the youths’ capitulation, Zimri’s defiant public harangue against Moses, and Phinehas’ zeal ending the plague (IV.6.7–12). This scene — Balaam as the cunning author of Israel’s seduction — is precisely the profile the New Testament crystallises as “the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel, to eat things sacrificed unto idols, and to commit fornication” (Revelation 2:14).5

Josephus’s verdict: the villain honoured as a prophet

although this Balaam, who was sent for by the Midianites to curse the Hebrews; and when he was hindred from doing it, by divine providence, did still suggest that advice to them, by making use of which our enemies had well nigh corrupted the whole multitude of the Hebrews with their wiles… yet did he do him great honour, by setting down his prophecies in writing. And while it was in his power to claim this glory to himself… he still gave his attestation to him.

— Antiquities IV.6.13 (Whiston). Josephus closes the episode with a remarkable editorial coda in praise of Moses, but it doubles as his final judgement on Balaam. Balaam is unambiguously the villain — his counsel “well nigh corrupted the whole multitude of the Hebrews.” And yet Moses, Josephus says, could have suppressed Balaam’s name and passed off the oracles as his own prophecies, “there being no one that could be a witness against him”; instead he “did him the honour to make mention of him.” The ambivalence the whole retelling carries is here made explicit: Balaam is at once the greatest prophet of his age and the self-serving architect of Israel’s near-ruin, and Josephus lets both stand — “but let every one think of these matters as he pleases.”6

Notes

  • The bridge from “counsel” to “doctrine.” With Philo, Josephus is one of the two first-century Jewish sources that convert the Torah’s terse retrospective note (Israel sinned at Peor “through the counsel of Balaam”) into a full narrative of Balaam deliberately advising the seduction. That developed scene — not the bare biblical phrase — is what stands behind the New Testament’s naming of “the doctrine of Balaam” (Rev 2:14), “the way of Balaam” (2 Pet 2:15), and “the error of Balaam” (Jude 11). Josephus is a key witness that this reading was current in first-century Judaism, not a purely Christian invention.
  • The withheld star. Josephus’s refusal to reproduce “a Star out of Jacob” as a royal oracle is the mirror image of Philo, who read it plainly as a coming king. Whether from Flavian political caution or from a general reticence about messianic prophecy after AD 70, Josephus flattens the very verse that Christian readers would later tie to the star of the wise men. On whether the magi were heirs of Balaam’s oracle, see the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion behind Matthew, weak for the historical transmission); Josephus is evidence that the messianic reading was not universal even among first-century Jews.
  • Josephus the dramatist. The stretch at IV.6.7–12 — the Midianite women’s scripted seductions, and above all Zimri’s long, defiant speech accusing Moses of tyranny and defending his own right to “live according to his own will” — is Josephus’s own composition, with no basis in the terse text of Numbers 25. It is a good specimen of how he expands scriptural narrative with invented speeches in the manner of Graeco-Roman historiography, and a caution that his “Balaam” is a literary reconstruction, not an independent source.
  • Text and edition. Greek in B. Niese, Flavii Iosephi Opera, vol. I (Berlin, 1887); the English quoted here is William Whiston’s translation (1737), The Antiquities of the Jews IV.6, as digitised at the University of Chicago’s Penelope archive. Whiston’s chapter-and-section divisions (IV.6.1–13) run alongside the Niese/Loeb continuous numbering §§102–158.
