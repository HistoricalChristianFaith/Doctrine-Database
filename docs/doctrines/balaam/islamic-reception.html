The Qur’an and the Islamic exegetes: Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura

Dates: c. AD 610–1373 (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Qur’anic revelation of Sūrat al-A‘rāf in the earliest seventh century, and the classical exegetical tradition that read Q 7:175–176 of Balaam — al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273), and Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373)) · Qur’an 7:175–176 · Ibn Kathīr’s Tafsīr
Summary: The third of the great Abrahamic traditions to remember Balaam is Islam, and it remembers him exactly as the New Testament and the rabbis did — the man given true knowledge from God who threw it away for the world. The Qur’an tells, without naming him, of “him to whom We gave Our signs, but he detached himself from them; so Satan pursued him… his example is like that of a dog” (7:175–176); and the dominant strand of the classical commentary, on the authority of Ibn Mas‘ūd, Ibn ‘Abbās, Mujāhid, and ‘Ikrima, identifies him as Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’ — Balaam son of Beor, whose Arabic name preserves both the seer and the patronymic of Numbers 22–24. The tafsir even keeps two threads of the biblical episode — the donkey that halts on the road, and a man of famed prayer sent to curse God’s people who is overruled — and turns the whole into Islam’s “error of Balaam”: the type of the gifted man who forfeits his gift. This page also scopes the thinner Samaritan handling of Balaam. Whether Q 7:175 is originally about Balaam or a contemporary of Muḥammad is a genuinely disputed question, weighed on the “is the man of Qur’an 7:175 Balaam?” argument (rated sound for the identification’s grounding).

Where it sits

Like the Latin medieval legend before it, this is a reception stratum rather than one dated author: the Qur’anic verse of the earliest seventh century, and the layered commentary (tafsīr) that grew around it over the next seven hundred years. It is slotted here, in the reception tail after the Fathers, because its interpretive weight belongs to that later commentary tradition — though the Qur’anic verse itself (c. AD 610–632) is older than the Golden Legend on the page before it, and roughly contemporary with the close of the rabbinic and Targumic strata. What Islam adds to the tradition is not a new reading of the Numbers narrative but a fresh crystallisation of its moral: Balaam becomes a stock Qur’anic parable of apostasy, the learned man who “clung to the earth” and lost both worlds.

The Qur’anic verse: the man who cast off God’s signs

The Qur’an tells the story in two verses of Sūrat al-A‘rāf (“The Heights”), without giving the man a name:

And relate to them ﷼O Prophet﷽ the story of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he abandoned them, so Satan took hold of him, and he became a deviant. If We had willed, We would have elevated him with Our signs, but he clung to this life—following his evil desires. His example is that of a dog: if you chase it away, it pants, and if you leave it, it ﷼still﷽ pants. This is the example of the people who deny Our signs. So narrate ﷼to them﷽ stories ﷼of the past﷽, so perhaps they will reflect.

— Qur’an 7:175–176 (Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran); Sahih International renders 7:175 “him to whom We gave [knowledge of] Our signs, but he detached himself from them; so Satan pursued him, and he became of the deviators.” The figure is anonymous in the Qur’an; a name is supplied only by the commentary.1

The exegetical identification: Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura

Classical tafsīr overwhelmingly fills the blank with Balaam. Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373), gathering the early authorities, records that on the report of ‘Abdullāh ibn Mas‘ūd the verse “is about Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’ a man from the Children of Israel,” and that Ibn ‘Abbās said the same — “He is Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’… whom Allah had given the knowledge of His Ayat” — with Mujāhid and ‘Ikrima concurring; “it is well-known,” Ibn Kathīr concludes, “that this honorable Ayah was revealed about a man from the Children of Israel in ancient times, according to Ibn Mas‘ud and several others among the Salaf.” The Arabic name is a near-exact transcription of the Hebrew: Bal‘am is Balaam (Bil‘am), and Ba‘ūrā’ preserves Beor (Numbers 22:5) — the same patronymic the New Testament garbles to “Bosor” (2 Peter 2:15).2

The commentary preserves recognisable threads of the Numbers episode. Balaam is “one of the scholars of the Children of Israel whose supplication was acceptable” — a man to whom Allah had “taught… His Greatest Name” (al-ism al-a‘ẓam) — who is prevailed on to invoke God against Moses and his people. On the way to curse them the beast beneath him fails: “when he proceeded on the Mount for a while, the donkey sat down and refused to proceed. He got off the donkey and struck it until it stood up again” — a clear echo of the ass of Numbers 22:22–35. When he does at last try to curse Israel, “Allah made his tongue mention his people with evil and the Children of Israel with good instead” — the biblical overruling of the curse into a blessing — and he confesses, “It is against my will. This is a matter that Allah has decided.” His end is the ruin the Qur’an’s dog-simile pictures: “as his tongue was made to loll out of his mouth,” he says, “Now I have lost this life and the Hereafter.”3

Islam’s “error of Balaam”

Read against the rest of the tradition, the Qur’anic Balaam completes a striking three-way agreement. The New Testament made him the type of the teacher who “loved the wages of unrighteousness” — the “way,” “error,” and “doctrine” of Balaam (2 Peter 2:15–16; Jude 11; Revelation 2:14); the rabbis made him “Balaam the wicked,” first of the commoners barred from the World-to-Come. The Qur’an’s image is the sharpest of the three: a man raised up by God’s own signs who “clung to the earth” and so sank to the level of a panting dog, the very emblem of appetite that cannot be satisfied. All three traditions, that is, seized on the same tension the Torah left unresolved — the true oracle and the deadly greed — and each resolved it the same way, by making Balaam the pattern of the gifted man who sells or forfeits his gift for the world. The tafsir’s stray details (the acceptable prayer, the Greatest Name, the king of Midian who buys him off with land and gifts) are downstream elaborations in the manner of the rabbinic and Josephan legends, not independent history.

The rival identifications

The identification with Balaam was dominant but never unanimous, and the honesty of the reading requires naming the alternatives the exegetes themselves weighed. Because the Qur’an leaves the man nameless, some early authorities applied the verse to a contemporary of Muḥammad: on the report of ‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr and Zayd ibn Aslam it was said to describe Umayya ibn Abi al-Ṣalt, the pre-Islamic Arabian poet and would-be prophet who had read the Jewish and Christian scriptures, expected a coming prophet, and turned against Muḥammad when the office fell to another. Others named Sayfi ibn al-Rāhib or a priest called Abū ‘Āmir. The classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī) accordingly conclude that the man’s precise identity is neither fixed by the Qur’an nor essential: the verse is an archetype of “the one-time believer who recants,” whoever first sat for the portrait. That range of candidates is a real limit on any claim that the verse is originally about the biblical seer — but it does not touch the reception-historical fact that the mainstream Islamic reading of Q 7:175 is Balaam son of Beor, and identifies him by his own biblical name. That distinction is the subject of the “is the man of Qur’an 7:175 Balaam?” argument.4

The Samaritan tradition (a thinner stream)

The Samaritans — who keep only the Torah (in their own Samaritan Pentateuch) and stand outside the rabbinic corpus — leave a far slighter Balaam. Their handling is mainly text-critical, and runs in the opposite direction from the Jewish, Christian, and Islamic legends: rather than expand Balaam into a villain, the Samaritan Pentateuch softens the narrative in its characteristic way, replacing direct divine contact with an angelic intermediary — at Numbers 23:4 its text reads “the angel of God found Balaam,” where the Masoretic Hebrew has “God met Balaam.” At Numbers 24:7 the Samaritan text shares the “Gog” reading found also in the Septuagint (against the Masoretic “Agag”) — a datum that bears on whether that eschatological heightening was a deliberate translator’s choice or an inherited Hebrew variant, weighed on the Septuagint-heightening argument. But the early Samaritan interpretive corpus — above all the fourth-century homiletic Memar Marqah — develops no distinctive Balaam legend, and Samaritan messianic hope rests not on Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” but on the Taheb (“the Restorer”), drawn from the prophet-like-Moses of Deuteronomy 18:18. So where synagogue, church, and mosque all made Balaam a moral type, the Samaritan tradition largely left him as the Torah’s ambivalent seer, touched only by the scribal smoothing of the whole Pentateuch.5
