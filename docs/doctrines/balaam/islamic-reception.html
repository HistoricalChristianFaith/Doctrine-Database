The Qur’an and the Islamic exegetes: Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura

Dates: c. AD 610–1373 (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Qur’anic revelation of Sūrat al-A‘rāf in the earliest seventh century, and the classical exegetical tradition that read Q 7:175–176 of Balaam — al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273), and Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373)) · Qur’an 7:175–176 · Ibn Kathīr’s Tafsīr
Summary: Islamic exegesis also remembers Balaam, as the man given true knowledge from God who threw it away for the world — a portrait close to those of the New Testament and the rabbis. The Qur’an tells, without naming him, of “him to whom We gave Our signs, but he detached himself from them; so Satan pursued him… his example is like that of a dog” (7:175–176); and the main strand of the classical commentary reported by Ibn Kathīr, on the authority of Ibn Mas‘ūd, Ibn ‘Abbās, Mujāhid, and ‘Ikrima, identifies him as Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’ — Balaam son of Beor, whose Arabic name preserves both the seer and the patronymic of Numbers 22–24. The tafsir keeps two threads of the biblical episode — the donkey that halts on the road, and a man of famed prayer sent to curse God’s people who is overruled — and makes Balaam the type of the gifted man who forfeits his gift. This page also scopes the thinner Samaritan handling of Balaam. Whether Q 7:175 is originally about Balaam or a contemporary of Muḥammad is disputed; see “Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor?”

Placement on the timeline

Like the Latin medieval legend before it, this is a reception stratum rather than one dated author: the Qur’anic verse of the earliest seventh century, and the layered commentary (tafsīr) that grew around it over the next seven hundred years. It is slotted here, in the reception tail after the Fathers, because its identification of the figure as Balaam comes from that later commentary tradition — though the Qur’anic verse itself (c. AD 610–632) is older than the Golden Legend on the page before it, and roughly contemporary with the close of the rabbinic and Targumic strata. In the commentary the Qur’anic parable of apostasy is read as Balaam’s: the learned man who “clung to the earth” and lost both worlds.

The Qur’anic verse: the man who cast off God’s signs

The Qur’an tells the story in two verses of Sūrat al-A‘rāf (“The Heights”), without giving the man a name:

And relate to them ﷼O Prophet﷽ the story of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he abandoned them, so Satan took hold of him, and he became a deviant. If We had willed, We would have elevated him with Our signs, but he clung to this life—following his evil desires. His example is that of a dog: if you chase it away, it pants, and if you leave it, it ﷼still﷽ pants. This is the example of the people who deny Our signs. So narrate ﷼to them﷽ stories ﷼of the past﷽, so perhaps they will reflect.

— Qur’an 7:175–176 (Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran); Sahih International renders 7:175 “him to whom We gave [knowledge of] Our signs, but he detached himself from them; so Satan pursued him, and he became of the deviators.” The figure is anonymous in the Qur’an; a name is supplied only by the commentary.1

The exegetical identification: Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura

Classical tafsīr, as Ibn Kathīr gathers it, fills the blank with Balaam. Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373), gathering the early authorities, records that on the report of ‘Abdullāh ibn Mas‘ūd the verse “is about Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’ a man from the Children of Israel,” and that Ibn ‘Abbās said the same — “He is Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’… whom Allah had given the knowledge of His Ayat” — with Mujāhid and ‘Ikrima concurring; “it is well-known,” Ibn Kathīr concludes, “that this honorable Ayah was revealed about a man from the Children of Israel in ancient times, according to Ibn Mas‘ud and several others among the Salaf.” The Arabic name is a near-exact transcription of the Hebrew: Bal‘am is Balaam (Bil‘am), and Ba‘ūrā’ preserves Beor (Numbers 22:5) — the patronymic that 2 Peter gives as “Bosor” (2 Peter 2:15).2

The commentary preserves recognisable threads of the Numbers episode. Balaam is “one of the scholars of the Children of Israel whose supplication was acceptable” — a man to whom Allah had “taught… His Greatest Name” (al-ism al-a‘ẓam) — who is prevailed on to invoke God against Moses and his people. On the way to curse them the beast beneath him fails: “when he proceeded on the Mount for a while, the donkey sat down and refused to proceed. He got off the donkey and struck it until it stood up again” — a parallel to the ass of Numbers 22:22–35. When he does at last try to curse Israel, “Allah made his tongue mention his people with evil and the Children of Israel with good instead” — the biblical overruling of the curse into a blessing — and he confesses, “It is against my will. This is a matter that Allah has decided.” His end, in the tafsir, matches the Qur’an’s dog-simile: “as his tongue was made to loll out of his mouth,” he says, “Now I have lost this life and the Hereafter.”3

The tafsir’s Balaam beside the New Testament and the rabbis

The tafsir’s Balaam shares the main features of the New Testament and rabbinic portraits. The New Testament made him the type of the teacher who “loved the wages of unrighteousness” — the “way,” “error,” and “doctrine” of Balaam (2 Peter 2:15–16; Jude 11; Revelation 2:14); the rabbis made him “Balaam the wicked,” first of the commoners barred from the World-to-Come. The Qur’anic image, as the tafsir applies it, is a man raised up by God’s own signs who “clung to the earth” and so sank to the level of a panting dog, the very emblem of appetite that cannot be satisfied. In all three, the Torah’s pairing of the true oracle and the deadly greed becomes the pattern of the gifted man who sells or forfeits his gift for the world. The tafsir’s further details (the acceptable prayer, the Greatest Name, the king of Midian who buys him off with land and gifts) are expansions of the kind found in the rabbinic and Josephan retellings.

The rival identifications

The identification with Balaam was not unanimous; the exegetes themselves recorded alternatives. Because the Qur’an leaves the man nameless, some early authorities applied the verse to a contemporary of Muḥammad: on the report of ‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr and Zayd ibn Aslam it was said to describe Umayya ibn Abi al-Ṣalt, the pre-Islamic Arabian poet and would-be prophet who had read the Jewish and Christian scriptures, expected a coming prophet, and turned against Muḥammad when the office fell to another. Others named Sayfi ibn al-Rāhib or a priest called Abū ‘Āmir. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī are cited for this range of names. Gibril Fouad Haddad, citing al-Rāzī, writes that the man’s identity “is of little consequence since he is an archetype of the one time believer who recants,” and S. A. Maududi that “the identity of that person has not been established by the Qur’an or the Hadith.” The range of candidates bears on whether the verse is originally about the biblical seer; the reading of Q 7:175 as Balaam son of Beor, which Ibn Kathīr calls “well-known,” identifies him by his biblical name. The question is the subject of “Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor?”4

The Samaritan tradition (a thinner stream)

The Samaritans — who keep only the Torah (in their own Samaritan Pentateuch) and stand outside the rabbinic corpus — leave a far slighter Balaam. Their handling is mainly text-critical, and runs in the opposite direction from the Jewish, Christian, and Islamic legends: rather than expand Balaam into a villain, the Samaritan Pentateuch, here as elsewhere, replaces direct divine contact with an angelic intermediary: at Numbers 23:4 its text reads “the angel of God found Balaam,” where the Masoretic Hebrew has “God met Balaam.” At Numbers 24:7 the Samaritan text shares the “Gog” reading found also in the Septuagint (against the Masoretic “Agag”) — a datum that bears on whether the Greek “Gog” was a translator’s choice or an inherited Hebrew variant; see “Does the Septuagint read Balaam’s oracles more messianically than the Hebrew?” But the early Samaritan interpretive corpus — above all the fourth-century homiletic Memar Marqah — develops no distinctive Balaam legend, and Samaritan messianic hope rests not on Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” but on the Taheb (“the Restorer”), drawn from the prophet-like-Moses of Deuteronomy 18:18. So where synagogue, church, and mosque all made Balaam a moral type, the Samaritan tradition largely left him as the Torah’s ambivalent seer, touched only by the scribal smoothing of the whole Pentateuch.5

Reception and debate

  • Reception, not a claim about Numbers. This page reports how Islam and the Samaritans remembered Balaam, not a fresh reading of the historical episode. The Qur’anic Balaam is a moral archetype; its narrative details are expansions of the kind found in the Josephan and rabbinic legends.
  • The name preserves Beor. The Arabic Ba‘ūrā’ corresponds to the Hebrew Beor of Numbers rather than to the Greek “Bosor” of 2 Peter 2:15.

The Qur’an is quoted from Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran (7:175–176), with Sahih International noted; the tafsir from Ibn Kathīr’s commentary on 7:175–177 as translated in the standard abridged English edition. The Samaritan Pentateuch variants are cited from the standard text-critical literature.
