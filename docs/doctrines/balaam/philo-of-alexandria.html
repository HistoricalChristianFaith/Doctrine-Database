Philo of Alexandria on Balaam

Dates: c. 20 BC – AD 50 (Alexandrian Jewish philosopher and exegete) · Wikipedia
Summary: Philo’s Life of Moses I.263–304 is the earliest surviving interpretive reworking of the Balaam episode, and the seed of a reading that would echo for centuries. He casts Balaam as a magos — a Mesopotamian “magician” and master diviner — who is nonetheless seized by an authentic prophetic spirit that drives his own divination out of him, so that he blesses Israel against his will and utters a messianic oracle he does not understand. Yet Philo makes him a villain throughout: blind where his own donkey sees, “even more wicked than the king,” and finally the author of the counsel to seduce Israel with Moabite women — Philo’s dramatised source of the later “doctrine of Balaam.”

Where it sits

Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – AD 50) was a Greek-speaking Jewish philosopher of the largest Jewish community in the Diaspora, who read the Pentateuch through the lens of Middle-Platonic and Stoic philosophy. His two-book De Vita Moysis (On the Life of Moses) retells the Torah narrative for a partly Gentile readership; the Balaam episode falls in Book I, among Philo’s illustrations of Moses’ prophetic office, at sections 263–304. Because it is a first-century Jewish retelling — expanding, moralising, and interpreting the bare narrative of Numbers 22–24 — it is the earliest witness on this timeline to read Balaam rather than merely name him, and it stands roughly a generation before Josephus’ parallel treatment.1

Claims & evidence

Balaam the magos: a Mesopotamian master of divination

Now there was a man at that time very celebrated for his skill in divination, dwelling in Mesopotamia, who was initiated in every branch of the soothsayers’ art. And he was celebrated and renowned above all men for his experience as a diviner and prophet, as he had in many instances foretold to many people incredible and most important events.

— Life of Moses I.264 (C. D. Yonge). Philo introduces Balaam not as a prophet of Israel but as a professional Mesopotamian diviner — a weather-seer and prognosticator whose fame rests on his art. The Greek word Philo uses of him is magos: at I.276, when Balak leads him up the hill overlooking the Hebrew camp, Yonge renders the term literally — the vantage “was shown to the magician from this point, as if from a watch tower.” This is the same word the Greek Bible and Matthew use of the magi, and it is Philo’s label for Balaam that later readers would seize on to make him the ancestor or archetype of the wise men of Matthew 2.2

The prophetic spirit seizes him and drives his magic out

So, having gone forth, immediately he became inspired, the prophetic spirit having entered into him, which drove all his artificial system of divination and cunning out of his soul; for it was not possible that holy inspiration should dwell in the same abode with magic.

— Life of Moses I.277 (Yonge). This is the hinge of Philo’s portrait, and a subtle one. The true oracle does not come through Balaam’s magic; it comes by displacing it — the divine spirit expels his “artificial system of divination” because “holy inspiration” and “magic” cannot share one soul. Balaam prophesies truly only when he has, for the moment, stopped being a magos at all. The inspiration is entirely involuntary: at a later oracle he is “suddenly filled by divine inspiration, and, without at all understanding the words which he uttered, spoke everything that was put into his mouth” (I.283), and the angel in the road had already told him, “you must say what I prompt you, without any thoughts of your own” (I.274). Balaam is a mouthpiece overruled, not a willing herald.3

The moralised villain: blind, resentful, “even more wicked than the king”

Throughout, Philo heaps blame on Balaam’s character even as God commandeers his tongue. When the donkey shies from the angel Balaam cannot see, Philo draws the moral: he “was thus shown to be inferior to a brute beast in the power of sight, at a time when he was boasting that he could see, not only the whole world, but also the Creator of the world” (I.272). As Balak drags him from hill to hill for a third attempt, Philo calls the seer “even more wicked than the king,” for though the words in his mouth were another’s, “in his secret thoughts he was oppressed by a heavy feeling of evil, yet still desired in his mind to curse this people, though he was forbidden to do so with his mouth” (I.286). The blessing is wrung from a man who inwardly craves to curse.4

The star oracle, rendered as a coming king

A man shall hereafter come forth out of thee who shall rule over many nations, and his kingdom shall increase every day and be raised up to heaven.

— Life of Moses I.290 (Yonge). This is Philo’s paraphrase of the fourth oracle, “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17). Philo drops the astral imagery and reads the verse frankly as the prediction of a coming ruler whose “kingdom shall increase… and be raised up to heaven” — an individual, royal, and expanding dominion. It is one of the earliest datable witnesses to the messianic-royal reading of Balaam’s oracle, the very reading that later Christian writers would connect to the star of the magi. Philo’s wording — “a man … rule over many nations … his kingdom shall increase” — in fact tracks the Greek of Numbers 24:7 rather than the Hebrew, evidence that the Septuagint’s personalized rendering was already being read as prophecy of a coming ruler in the first century (weighed on the “does the Septuagint read the oracles more messianically?” argument).5

The counsel to seduce Israel: Philo’s seed of the “doctrine of Balaam”

All that I have hitherto uttered have been oracles and words of God; but what I am going to say are merely the suggestions of my own mind… As he knew that the only way by which the Hebrews could be subdued was by leading them to violate the law, he endeavoured to seduce them by means of debauchery and intemperance… “O king! the women of the country surpass all other women in beauty, and there are no means by which a man is more easily subdued than by the beauty of a woman.”

— Life of Moses I.294–296 (Yonge). The Torah reports only, after the fact, that “these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor” (Numbers 31:16). Philo dramatises that bare notice into a full scene: Balaam, his inspiration spent, takes Balak aside and, of his “own mind,” scripts the plan — have the most beautiful Moabite women lure the Hebrew youths into idolatrous sacrifice through sexual enticement, playing hard-to-get to inflame desire, until the men “endure to do and to suffer anything.” Balak “repealed the law against adulteries,” the plan works, and Phinehas’ zeal ends it (I.295–304). This elaboration — Balaam as the deliberate architect of Israel’s seduction into idolatry — is precisely the profile the New Testament crystallises as “the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock” (Revelation 2:14), and Philo is among its earliest narrative sources.6

Notes

  • A double figure held in one frame. Philo does not resolve the Bible’s tension between the blesser and the villain; he sharpens it. Balaam is a genuine seer whom God can and does use, and a greedy, resentful magician whose true oracles are torn from him against his will and who, left to his own mind, invents Israel’s ruin. The prophetic gift and the moral corruption sit side by side — the inspiration overrides the man without reforming him.
  • Why this matters for the magi. Philo’s magos Balaam — a Gentile diviner from the east who receives a true revelation and foretells a coming king — is the closest pre-Matthean template for the wise men of Matthew 2, and Raymond Brown and others cite exactly this passage in reconstructing the literary background of the magi story. Whether the historical magi were Balaam’s heirs is weighed on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion behind Matthew, weak for the claim that the historical magi possessed his prophecy). Note the caution Philo himself supplies: on his telling the true oracle comes by expelling Balaam’s magic, not by exercising it — so “a magos receives a true oracle” is, in Philo, a claim about God overruling magic, not about magic reaching God.
  • Text and edition. Greek in F. H. Colson, Philo, vol. VI (Loeb Classical Library, 1935); the English quoted here is C. D. Yonge’s translation, The Works of Philo (rev. ed., Hendrickson, 1993). The section numbers (I.263–304) follow the standard Cohn–Wendland division; Yonge’s chapter divisions (XLVIII–LV) run alongside them.
