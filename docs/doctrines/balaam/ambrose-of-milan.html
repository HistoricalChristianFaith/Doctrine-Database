Ambrose of Milan on Balaam — a covetous seer who prophesied Christ

Dates: c. 378–397 (Ambrose’s Balaam remarks are scattered across the sermons and treatises of his Milan episcopate — On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus [378], Concerning Repentance [c. 384–390], Letter 41 [c. 388], the Commentary on Luke, Exposition on Psalm 118, and On the Duties of the Clergy [c. 389–391], and the Commentaries on the Twelve Davidic Psalms); Ambrose b. c. 339, bishop of Milan 374, d. 397 · Wikipedia
Summary: The first great Latin churchman to touch the story, Ambrose inherits the Torah’s double figure and keeps both halves at full strength — but where Origen had refused Balaam the name of prophet, Ambrose does the opposite: he calls him a man “inspired by the spirit of prophecy” who saw Christ’s death and resurrection in his own oracle, and who fathered the line of the magi through the “star out of Jacob.” Yet the same Ambrose brands Balaam the very type of the prophet corrupted by greed — the seer Balak thought money could hire to curse, the magician whose art God simply overruled, the source of the “doctrine of Balaam” that Revelation condemns. His most characteristic move is exegetical: he returns again and again to Balaam’s wish “let my soul die the death of the righteous” (Numbers 23:10), reading it now as a true prophecy of Christ’s rising, now as the covetous man’s own longing to be rid of his sin.

Claims & evidence

The star oracle, and the magi as Balaam’s heirs (Commentary on Luke)

Commenting on Matthew’s wise men, Ambrose takes up the tradition — already stated by Origen — that the magi were Balaam’s own successors, custodians of the star-oracle he had spoken. He treats the descent as a matter of record (“a certain history”) and makes Balaam and the magi heirs of one prophecy across the centuries:

But who are these Magi, if not those who, as a certain history teaches, are descended from Balaam, from whom it was prophesied: A star shall come forth from Jacob? Therefore, these are no less heirs of faith than of succession. He saw the star in the spirit, they saw with their eyes and believed. They saw a new star, which had not been seen by any creature in the world.

— Ambrose, Commentary on Luke II.48 (on Numbers 24:17).1 Balaam “saw the star in the spirit” — a frank grant of genuine prophetic sight — and the magi are “heirs of faith” as well as “of succession.” Ambrose even keeps the note that the seer knew his own craft was ending: “the wise man therefore understands his own arts to cease.” This is the Western echo of Origen’s magi–Balaam link, and it feeds the medieval legend that made Balaam the wise men’s ancestor; whether the historical magi actually held and preserved his prophecy is weighed on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion behind Matthew, weak for the historical transmission).

“Let my soul die the death of the righteous” — Balaam prophesies Christ (On the Decease of Satyrus)

Ambrose’s most striking claim is that Balaam, blessing Israel, was granted a true vision of Christ. Consoling himself over his brother’s death, he seizes on Balaam’s wish in Numbers 23:10 and reads it as an unqualified prophecy of the resurrection:

This Balaam wished for as the greatest good for himself, when, inspired by the spirit of prophecy, he said: “Let my soul die in the souls of the righteous, and let my seed be like the seed of them.” And in truth he wished this according to the spirit of prophecy, for as he saw the rising of Christ, so also he saw His triumph, he saw His death, but saw also in Him the everlasting resurrection of men, and therefore feared not to die as he was to rise again.

— Ambrose, On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus II.43.2 Nothing could be further from Origen, who athetized the very clause “the spirit of God came upon him” rather than lodge God’s Spirit in a diviner: Ambrose says twice over that Balaam spoke “inspired by the spirit of prophecy” and “according to the spirit of prophecy,” and grants him a Christophany besides. The same verse anchors a second, moral line of reading that runs through Ambrose’s other works — Balaam’s wish becomes the pattern of the sinner’s longing to be rid of his sin: in the Exposition on Psalm 118 Balaam seeks that his soul “forget his own error … lay aside what was his own and take on what belonged to justice and equity,” and in the Commentaries on the Twelve Davidic Psalms the wish means “that his fall and certain wicked uses should die, and that he should become accustomed to the life of the just.” In the Commentary on Luke the same words become the death of baptism, in which “not nature, but sins perish.”3

The covetous seer: the love of money and the “doctrine of Balaam”

For all the honour Ambrose pays the oracle, he is unsparing about the man. Balaam is his stock example of the prophet whom money corrupts — the very declaration of the divine law, he says, already had to legislate against the vice that Balaam embodies:

Love of money, then, is an old, an ancient vice, which showed itself even at the declaration of the divine law; for a law was given to check it. On account of love of money Balak thought Balaam could be tempted by rewards to curse the people of our fathers. Love of money would have won the day too, had not God bidden him hold back from cursing.

— Ambrose, On the Duties of the Clergy II.130.4 The blessing, on this reading, is a near thing: greed “would have won the day” but for God’s restraint — the New Testament’s “wages of unrighteousness” (2 Peter 2:15) put in a moralist’s frame. Ambrose also carries forward the darkest of the New Testament’s three phrases. Arguing that the church may not pray away the “sin unto death,” he cites the risen Christ’s charge to Pergamum verbatim — “thou hast there those that hold the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to put a stumbling-block before the children of Israel, to eat things sacrificed unto idols, and to commit fornication” (Revelation 2:14) — taking “the doctrine of Balaam” as the settled name for the teaching that seduces God’s people into idolatry and immorality.5

The magician God overruled (Letter 41)

A third strand keeps Balaam’s pagan craft in view. Rehearsing God’s benefits to Israel in the voice of the Lord addressing his people, Ambrose lists the thwarting of Balaam among the deliverances of the Exodus generation, and names his craft plainly as sorcery:

Remember what Balaam conceived against thee, seeking the aid of magic art, but I suffered him not to hurt thee.

— Ambrose, Letter 41.24.6 Balaam “sought the aid of magic art” — the diviner of Joshua 13:22 — and his hostile intent was real (“what Balaam conceived against thee”); only God’s overruling turned the curse aside. So the man who “saw the star in the spirit” is, in the same corpus, a hired sorcerer with murderous designs whom grace alone kept from succeeding.

Notes

  • The mirror image of Origen. On the one question that divides the tradition — was the true word a work of God’s Spirit in Balaam, or a word forced past a mere diviner? — Ambrose stands opposite Origen. Origen denied Balaam the title of prophet and struck the clause “the spirit of God came upon him” from his text; Ambrose says without hesitation that Balaam spoke “inspired by the spirit of prophecy” and even “saw the rising of Christ.” The two Fathers thus stake out the poles the later “true prophet or diviner?” question moves between — and Ambrose is closer to Josephus (“the greatest of the Prophets”) and Philo (in whom the prophetic spirit enters and drives the magic out) than to the Alexandrian who preceded him in the church.
  • The double figure held whole. Ambrose does not resolve the tension between the true prophet and the covetous sorcerer; he keeps both at full strength and lets the context decide which Balaam he needs. Consoling the bereaved, Balaam is the seer who saw the resurrection; preaching against avarice, he is the prophet-for-hire; teaching that some sins are past praying for, he is the author of the “doctrine of Balaam.” This is the Torah’s own double portrait — the mouth of a true oracle and the author of a deadly seduction — carried intact into Latin preaching.
  • Numbers 23:10 as Ambrose’s signature text. Where Origen’s Balaam was fixed on the star (24:17), Ambrose’s is fixed on the wish of 23:10, “let my soul die the death of the righteous,” which he quotes across at least four works. It lets him do two things at once: read Balaam as a genuine prophet of Christ’s resurrection, and turn the covetous seer’s own words into a model of the sinner’s repentance — the death of sin in baptism and the soul’s longing to “forget his own error.”
