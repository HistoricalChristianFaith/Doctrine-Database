The medieval legend: the magi as Balaam’s own descendants

Dates: c. AD 380–1483 (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Latin hardening of the patristic reading, from the fourth-century Questions on the Old and New Testament through Jerome and the Carolingian schoolmen to Aquinas’s Catena Aurea and the Golden Legend, c. 1260, whose English printing by Caxton dates to 1483) · Fordham Medieval Sourcebook
Summary: The Fathers had already made the magi Balaam’s spiritual heirs — his “successors” or “school” in the East, custodians of his “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) — a reading seeded by Irenaeus, worked up by Origen, and taken as flat history by Eusebius. In the Latin Middle Ages that spiritual succession hardened into a literal genealogy: the wise men were cast as Balaam’s own descendants, the “sons of Balaam,” who had kept the seer’s star-prophecy in the family and travelled because they had read it. This page traces that hardening through its datable Latin anchors — Ambrosiaster’s cautious “the tradition from Balaam,” Jerome’s “successors,” the Carolingian commentator Remigius of Auxerre’s blunt “descendants of Balaam … which is more probable,” the gathering of the whole chain in Aquinas’s Catena Aurea, and its lodging in the most-read book of the later Middle Ages, Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend, where the three kings are “successors of Balaam … by the prophecy of their father.” It is presented here as legend and reception, not history: the claim that the historical magi possessed and preserved Balaam’s oracle rests on no attested channel and is rated weak where the wiki weighs it (the magi timeline’s “heirs of Balaam?” argument). And it was never the only pedigree the West told: a rival tradition made the magi the sons of Seth instead (see the Revelation of the Magi).

Where it sits

The link between the magi and Balaam is very old, but it grew in stages, and only the last of them is the “legend” this page tracks. The earliest form is a bare identification of the stars: the star the magi followed is the star Balaam foretold. That is already in Justin Martyr and Irenaeus in the second century, and it says nothing about who the magi were. Origen (c. 248) turns the identification into a transmission: the magi “possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam … and finding among them the prophecy about the star” recognised what the sign meant — and, in his Homilies on Numbers, he makes them Balaam’s “seed” and “school” in the East. Eusebius (Demonstratio Evangelica 9.1, c. 314) states it as history — “Balaam’s successors … (for the prediction was preserved most likely among them).” What the Latin West adds is the final turn of the screw: successors becomes descendants, a school becomes a bloodline, and Balaam becomes the magi’s “father.”1

This page is therefore best read not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum — the deposit of what the Latin schools and the medieval church said about the magi’s ancestry — slotted on the timeline at its medieval culmination, after the fifth-century Fathers, even though its patristic Latin seeds (Ambrosiaster, Jerome) are themselves fourth-century and so precede Theodoret in strict chronology.

Claims & evidence

The patristic Latin seed: “the tradition from Balaam,” “whose successors they were”

The soft form of the claim enters Latin exegesis in the later fourth century. The anonymous Roman writer known as Ambrosiaster — whose Questions on the Old and New Testament circulated for centuries among the works of Augustine, hence the old label “Pseudo-Augustine” — grounds the magi’s knowledge in a Balaam tradition, carefully hedged:

These Chaldean Magi inspected the stars, not with malevolence, but with the true desire of knowledge; following, it may be supposed, the tradition from Balaam; so that when they saw this new and singular star, they understood it to be that of which Balaam had prophesied, as marking the birth of a King of Judaea.

— Ambrosiaster, Questions on the Old and New Testament q. 63, as gathered in Aquinas’s Catena Aurea on Matthew 2:1. “It may be supposed” is doing real work: this is still a tradition the magi followed, not yet a lineage they belonged to.2 A generation later Jerome, in his Commentary on Matthew (398), tightens “tradition” toward kinship — the magi “knew that such a star would rise by the prophecy of Balaam, whose successors they were” — the same “successors of Balaam” that stands on Jerome’s own detail page as one of his contributions to the Balaam tradition.3

The Carolingian hardening: “descendants of Balaam … which is more probable”

By the Carolingian schools the hedge is gone and the succession has become descent. The most explicit statement comes from Remigius of Auxerre (c. 841–908), the great monastic teacher whose Gospel commentaries fed the standard medieval Gloss. Weighing the various opinions about who the magi were, he ranks the Balaam pedigree first:

Another and more probable opinion is, that they were descendants of Balaam, who having his prophecy, “There shall rise a Star out of Jacob,” as soon as they saw the star, would know that a King was born.

— Remigius of Auxerre, as gathered in the Catena Aurea on Matthew 2:2. Where Ambrosiaster had “it may be supposed” and Jerome “successors,” Remigius has plain “descendants” (Latin de genere Balaam) — and rates it the more probable account of the wise men’s identity. The magi are now Balaam’s own line, born custodians of the family oracle.4

The chain gathered: Aquinas’s Catena Aurea (c. 1263)

All of these — Ambrosiaster’s “tradition,” Jerome’s “successors,” Remigius’s “descendants” — are set side by side in Thomas Aquinas’s Catena Aurea (the “Golden Chain,” his verse-by-verse anthology of patristic comment on the Gospels, the Matthew portion compiled c. 1262–1263 for Pope Urban IV), where they read as a settled consensus on Matthew 2:1–2. The Catena is not itself a new claim but the medieval clearing-house that made the Balaam-descent reading a schoolroom commonplace, one link in the chain that every later preacher and glossator could draw on.5

The legend in the age’s most-read book: the Golden Legend (c. 1260)

The reading reaches its widest audience in Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea — compiled about 1260 and, in William Caxton’s English of 1483, one of the most widely read books of the later Middle Ages. Its chapter on the Epiphany fixes the three kings, supplies their names in three languages, and states the Balaam pedigree flatly:

These kings were successors of Balaam, and came at the vision and sight of the star, by the prophecy of their father, which said that a star shall be born or spring out of Jacob, and a man shall arise of the lineage of Israel.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Epiphany of Our Lord” (trans. Caxton). The decisive phrase is “the prophecy of their father”: Balaam is no longer merely the seer whose oracle the magi inherited, but their ancestor, the head of their line, whose family prophecy they act on when they see the star. Voragine gives the kings their now-canonical Latin names — “Jaspar, Melchior, and Balthasar” (with Hebrew and Greek variants) — so that the anonymous Eastern seers of Matthew stand fully transformed into three named, crowned heirs of Balaam.6

The rival pedigree the West also told: the sons of Seth

One honest limit belongs here. The Balaam-descent legend was never the only ancestry the medieval West imagined for the magi. A parallel and older tradition made them the descendants of Seth, third son of Adam, keepers of a prophecy of the star handed down from Adam himself and watched for from a sacred peak, the “Mountain of Victories.” This Sethite pedigree is the whole burden of the Syriac Revelation of the Magi, and it entered the Latin West through the fifth-century Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum (a commentary long ascribed to John Chrysostom, now assigned to an anonymous Arian author), which summarises exactly that story — magi of the East, descendants of Seth, a watch on the Mons Victorialis. Voragine’s Golden Legend in fact draws on both streams. So the medieval magi carried two competing family trees, and the “sons of Balaam” reading traced here is the one that grew out of the patristic star-prophecy exegesis, not out of the Seth apocrypha.7

Notes

  • Reception, not history. This page reports how the church’s memory of the magi developed, not what the historical wise men knew. The underlying claim — that the actual magi possessed Balaam’s oracle, kept it across some fifteen centuries, and journeyed because they had read it — is weighed adversarially on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam’s star out of Jacob?” argument, which rates the literary allusion behind Matthew plausible (indeed probable) but the historical transmission weak: no channel is attested, and the “book of Balaam” the Fathers lean on is a lost conjecture. The medieval legend is the pious downstream of that weak transmission-claim, taken as fact.
  • Successors → descendants. The interest of the Latin stratum is the small but real shift it records: the Greek Fathers’ “successors”/“school” (a line of learning) becomes, in Remigius and the Golden Legend, a line of blood (“descendants,” “their father”). Nothing in the sources warrants the tightening; it is the natural drift of a legend making a vivid story more concrete.
  • The named kings. The trio “Jaspar, Melchior, and Balthasar” is a separate late accretion, not part of Matthew and not required by the Balaam reading; on the names’ late and mutually contradictory attestation see the magi timeline’s naming-tradition page, and on the shrine, relics, and the fuller kings-romance the medieval cult of the Three Kings.
  • Editions. Ambrosiaster, Jerome, and Remigius are quoted here in the nineteenth-century Oxford translation of Aquinas’s Catena Aurea (J. H. Newman, ed., 1841–45); the Golden Legend in William Caxton’s English (1483), via the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.
