Why does 2 Peter call Balaam “son of Bosor,” not “son of Beor”? (2 Peter 2:15)

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: 2 Peter 2:15

Related readings: Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor? (the Arabic patronymic Ba‘ūrā’) · Does the Septuagint read Balaam’s oracles more messianically than the Hebrew? · Is the Balaam of Numbers 22–24 a literary composite?

The passage

“Which have forsaken the right way, and are gone astray, following the way of Balaam the son of Bosor, who loved the wages of unrighteousness.”

— 2 Peter 2:15 (KJV).1

The question

Everywhere in the Hebrew Bible and its Greek translation Balaam is “son of Beor” (Hebrew בְּעוֹר, LXX Βεωρ; Numbers 22:5), but at this one point the Greek New Testament calls him Balaam tou Bosor (Βοσóρ), a patronymic found nowhere else. The question is why the form Bosor stands here: as a place-name, a word-play, a pronunciation, or a scribal slip.

The manuscripts divide. Bosor stands in 𝓁72, in correctors of Sinaiticus (ℵ2) and Alexandrinus, in Ephraemi Rescriptus (C), and across the Byzantine tradition and lectionaries, hence the KJV; Beor stands in Vaticanus (B), the Sahidic and Armenian, and Augustine. The original hand of Sinaiticus reads Βεωορσóρ, which Bruce Metzger judged “no doubt due to the conflation of Βοσóρ with a marginal correction –εωρ.” The modern editions (NA28, UBS5) print Bosor, which Richard Bauckham calls “by far the best attested reading”; Westcott and Hort, following Vaticanus, printed Beor; Paul Himes treats Bosor as a test case for the rule of the harder reading. The one Hebrew name-play on Balaam’s father recorded in the Talmud connects Beor with be‘ir, “beasts” (b. Sanhedrin 105a).2

Bosor as the name of Balaam’s city

In this reading “Bosor” names the place Balaam came from.

Held by: the Venerable Bede (Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, 8th c.).3

Bede reads the word as a toponym: Bosor is “the name of the city from which Balaam is said to have come.” He joins the place-name to the onomastic tradition of the Latin church, adding that Bosor means “either of flesh or in tribulation.” On Bede’s reading the epistle names Balaam by his city, and the Hebrew sense of the word, fleshly or afflicted, belongs to that city’s name.

A play on bāśār, “son of flesh”

In this reading the author chose the harder form so that a Hebrew ear would hear Balaam called “son of flesh.”

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Jude, 2 Peter, WBC, 1983); Thomas R. Schreiner (1, 2 Peter, Jude, NAC, 2003); Paul A. Himes (TC, 2022).4

The letter is saturated with “the flesh” (2:10, 2:18). A reader who heard Bosor as bāśār would hear “son of flesh,” a fitting name for the carnal seer. Bauckham calls this “a play on the name and the word בשֶׂר [‘flesh’]” and calls it “more plausible” than the phonetic explanation; Schreiner says the form “likely derives from a pun on the word ‘flesh’ (basar)”; and Himes defends it at length as a sophisticated Aramaic pun, which is why the harder reading is original. Jerome’s Book of Hebrew Names, glossing 2 Peter, gives “Bosor: in tribulation, either fleshly or furry,” and Bede repeats “of flesh.” On this reading Bosor is the author’s own word-play, and the scribes who wrote Beor smoothed it back to the familiar Old Testament form.

A Galilean or Aramaic pronunciation of Beor

In this reading Bosor is the way a Galilean or Aramaic speaker sounded the Hebrew name.

Held by: John Gill (Exposition); Charles Ellicott and the Pulpit Commentary; Theodor Zahn (Introduction to the New Testament); J. N. D. Kelly (Black’s NTC, 1969).5

The interchange is unremarkable in Aramaic transmission: Gill explains the guttural ayin (ע) as represented by a sibilant, with a vowel-glide in the manner of “Boanerges,” a straightforward alternate transliteration of the same name. Ellicott’s commentary and the Pulpit Commentary describe it as “a peculiar (perhaps Galilaean) pronunciation of the guttural ע” and draw the inference “a slight confirmation of St. Peter’s authorship”: Peter was a Galilean, whose speech betrayed him (Matthew 26:73). Zahn developed the phonetic explanation in his Introduction, and Kelly carries it in his commentary. On this reading the form is an undesigned trace of the writer’s own speech, and the Beor of the Old Testament and the Bosor of the epistle are one name in two mouths.

An unintentional scribal replacement seeded by the Edomite king-list

In this reading Bosor replaced Beor by accident, prompted by a neighbouring place-name in Genesis 36.

Held by: Christopher M. Hays (Filología Neotestamentaria 17, 2004).6

Hays argues that the Balaam traditions and the Edomite king-lists are “tightly intertwined.” “Bela the son of Beor” of Genesis 36:32 stands one verse from “Bozrah,” LXX Bosorra (36:33); the same list recurs at 1 Chronicles 1:43; and LXX Job 42:17d re-identifies these kings. A scribe or author steeped in that material had Beor and Bosorra side by side in memory, and their proximity, in Hays’s words, “paved the way for the unintentional replacement of Βεωρ with Βοσóρ.” On this reading the form is a transmissional event with a traceable cause in the Greek Old Testament, and the epistle’s Balaam is the same son of Beor whom Genesis and Numbers name.
