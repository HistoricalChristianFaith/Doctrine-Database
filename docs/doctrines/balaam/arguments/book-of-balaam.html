Did a “book of Balaam” ever exist?

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witnesses: the Deir ‘Alla inscription, the rabbinic tradition, Origen

Related readings: Is “Balaam” a rabbinic cipher for Jesus? · How old is the “Star out of Jacob,” and was it read messianically before Christianity? · Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s “star out of Jacob”? · the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres · Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam?

The passage

The misfortunes of the book of Balaam s[on of Beo]r. A divine seer was he

— Deir ‘Alla plaster inscription, Combination I, incipit (c. 800 BC), in Baruch A. Levine’s translation.1

I myself saw the notebook (pinqas) of Balaam, and it was written therein: Balaam the lame was thirty-three years old when Pineḥas the highwayman killed him

— a min (sectarian) replying to Rabbi Ḥanina, in b. Sanhedrin 106b.2

possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam … and finding among them the prophecy about the star

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.59–60 (c. 248), of the magi of Matthew 2.3

The question

The Deir ‘Alla text styles itself a spr (“writing,” “inscription,” “book”) of Balaam, the Babylonian Talmud speaks of a “notebook of Balaam,” and Origen and Eusebius describe the magi as possessing Balaam’s prophecies. The question is whether a written collection of Balaam’s oracles, distinct from the account in Numbers 22–24, circulated and was handed down, or whether the written Balaam is an ancient motif with no transmitted corpus of the oracles behind it. The question bears on how the patristic account of the magi’s knowledge of the star is read; Anders Hultgård has written on “the lost books of Balaam,” and the wider question is surveyed in van Kooten and van Ruiten’s collection The Prestige of the Pagan Prophet Balaam.4

Balaam’s prophecies preserved in writing and handed down

In this reading the magi knew of the star because Balaam’s prophecies had been kept and passed on among his successors in the East.

Held by: Origen (Against Celsus 1.59–60, c. 248; Homilies on Numbers); Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica 9.1, c. 314).5

Origen describes the magi as “possessing” the prophecies of Balaam and finding the star prophecy among them, and develops the point in his Homilies on Numbers. Eusebius gives the transmission as history: Balaam’s “successors … (for the prediction was preserved most likely among them) … hastened to arrive at Palestine.” The idea of a written Balaam is old. On Levine’s reading, the Deir ‘Alla incipit already captions Balaam’s vision-report as his “book” c. 800 BC, copied out on a wall in Iron-Age Transjordan, and the rabbis, centuries later and in another language, assume a written record of his words in the notebook the min claims to have seen. Balaam is drawn as a professional mantic fetched from “Pethor by the river” whose blessings and curses are potent, transmissible formulae; the four poetic oracles of Numbers 23–24 are quotable units, and the “star out of Jacob” circulated as a detached proof-text in Second Temple Judaism, at Qumran, in the Targums, and around Bar Kokhba.6 The classical world also knew this circle of material: the Neopythagorean Numenius of Apamea (2nd c.) named the Egyptian “sacred scribes” Jannes and Jambres, whom a Jewish tradition made the sons of Balaam, in a fragment Eusebius preserved.7 On this reading a seer whose single oracle travelled so far is the kind of figure whose sayings were kept in writing, and the magi’s knowledge of the star had a channel in Balaam’s own successors.

A written Balaam as an ancient motif, without a transmitted corpus of the oracles

In this reading the idea of a book of Balaam is genuinely ancient, but no written collection of his oracles beyond Numbers is attested, and the patristic book is an inference rather than a document.

Held by: Anders Hultgård (The Magi and the Star, 1998; “The Star of the Magi and the Prophecy of Balaam,” 2015) ⚠ unverified.8

The one physical text that calls itself a book of Balaam is the Deir ‘Alla plaster, and it records a night-vision of the Shaddayin council decreeing darkness: no Balak, no donkey, no blessing of Israel, and no star-and-sceptre oracle. The Biblical Archaeology Society’s survey describes it as a text that “does not reflect an earlier or alternate version of the biblical story, as the two texts recount decidedly different events.”9 Among the writers surveyed here, no Father quotes a book of Balaam, describes contents beyond what Numbers supplies, or says who held it; its existence is reached by asking how the magi could have known of the star. The rabbinic notebook stands inside a haggadic death-scene, Balaam the lame killed by Phinehas the highwayman, whose figures are themselves read as ciphers in the debate on the rabbinic cipher page. Numenius’s fragment names Jannes and Jambres and no book of Balaam. On this reading the figure of a written Balaam is attested from Iron-Age Transjordan onward, in Judaism and in the church alike, while the star oracle itself is known from Numbers and the texts that quote it.
