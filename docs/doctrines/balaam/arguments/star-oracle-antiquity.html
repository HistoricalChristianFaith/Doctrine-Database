How old is the “Star out of Jacob,” and was it read messianically before Christianity?

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: Balaam’s fourth oracle, Numbers 24:15–24, and its readers in modern scholarship

The passage

there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth… Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion

— Numbers 24:17–19 (KJV).

Asshur… the ships from the coast of Chittim… and shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber

— Numbers 24:24 (KJV).1

The question

The date at which the Balaam oracles were composed is disputed: whether their archaic language places them among the oldest Hebrew verse, or whether they are a monarchic or layered composition in an archaic register. The answer bears on how far the poem precedes the earliest Jewish readings of it as a prophecy of a coming ruler, set out below. W. F. Albright’s early date of 1944 is the starting point of the modern discussion; later commentaries (Jo Ann Hackett, 1992; Baruch A. Levine, 2000) are cited for a more cautious view of the absolute date together with the poems’ relative antiquity to their prose frame.2

Early Jewish readings of the oracle

John J. Collins reads the star and sceptre, in the oracle’s own horizon, as royal emblems of a conquering Israelite king, most naturally David, who subdued Moab and Edom (2 Samuel 8).3 Jewish readers before and alongside early Christianity read the verse of a coming figure. The Septuagint (3rd–2nd c. BC) renders the Hebrew “sceptre” (šebeṭ) of 24:17 as “a man (ἄνθρωπος) shall spring out of Israel,” and at 24:7 reads “there shall come a man out of his seed, and he shall rule over many nations” (how far the Greek departs from the Hebrew is the subject of the Septuagint reading). At Qumran the Testimonia (4Q175) strings Numbers 24:15–17 among its eschatological proof-texts; the Damascus Document (CD 7:18–21) makes the star the Interpreter of the Law and the sceptre the Prince of the Congregation; the War Scroll (1QM 11:6–7) cites the verse as a battle-oracle; and the blessing of the Prince in 1QSb (1Q28b) 5:27 draws on its imagery. The Torah Targums render the star and sceptre as a king and the Messiah (Onkelos, “when a king shall arise out of Jacob, and the Meshiha be anointed from Israel”). And Rabbi Akiva, reading “a Star steps forth out of Jacob,” hailed Simon bar Kosiba as Bar Kokhba, “Son of the Star,” and King Messiah (AD 132–135). Collins takes the title of his study of Qumran messianism, The Scepter and the Star, from this verse.4

Archaic poetry from the earliest period of Hebrew verse

In this reading the oracles are among the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible, composed long before their prose setting.

Held by: W. F. Albright (“The Oracles of Balaam,” JBL, 1944); on the method, Na’ama Pat-El and Aren Wilson-Wright (Hebrew Studies, 2013).5

The Balaam oracles are poetry embedded in prose, and the poetry reads differently from the narrative around it. Albright stripped the Masoretic pointing back to the consonantal text and read the four oracles as Northwest-Semitic verse. Their orthography is defective in the old manner; their morphology is archaic; they name God by the old epithets El, Shaddai and Elyon; and they run parallel to Ugaritic poetry and to the oldest datable Hebrew poems, the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15), the Blessing of Jacob (Genesis 49), the Blessing of Moses (Deuteronomy 33) and the Song of Deborah (Judges 5). On these grounds Albright dated the oracles to roughly the 13th–10th century BC, lightly modernised in transmission, as a pre-monarchic or early-monarchic core older than its editorial setting. Pat-El and Wilson-Wright defend the diagnostic value of the archaic features of Biblical Hebrew for dating, the linguistic method on which this reading rests. On this reading the “star out of Jacob” belongs to the earliest stratum of Israel’s poetry, centuries before any of the figures later hailed as its fulfilment.

A monarchic or layered composition in archaic style

In this reading the oracles’ old-looking language does not fix their date, and their content points to the monarchy or later.

Held by: Robyn C. Vern (Dating Archaic Biblical Hebrew Poetry, 2011).6

Archaic-looking Hebrew can be archaizing: a later poet writing in a deliberately old, elevated register can reproduce early forms, and some features may reflect dialect rather than date. Vern presses this against the linguistic arguments for dating archaic Biblical Hebrew poetry, and the wider debate over the linguistic dating of biblical texts turns on the same point. The oracle’s content then supplies the date. The star and sceptre “shall smite the corners of Moab” and, in the sequel, possess Edom and Seir (24:18), the conquests 2 Samuel 8 credits to David, which fits a composition of the monarchic era celebrating the Davidic house, written in whole or part after the events it foretells (vaticinium ex eventu). The close of the fourth oracle names “Asshur” (Assyria) and the ships of “Chittim” afflicting Asshur and Eber (24:24); “Kittim,” originally Cyprus, is the term Daniel 11:30 and the Qumran Pesher Habakkuk use for the western sea-power, Greece and then Rome. On this reading the fourth oracle’s horizon reaches into the Assyrian age or beyond, and the closing lines form a later expansion of an older core.7
