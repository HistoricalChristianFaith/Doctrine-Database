Are the Nicolaitans of Revelation 2:15 the “doctrine of Balaam”?

Type: exegetical identification (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound (that the two names denote one and the same libertine teaching — idol-food and fornication) / weak (that “Nicolaitans” is a coined Greek calque of “Balaamites,” so the two are literally one sect under two names, secured by a bilingual pun). The risen Christ’s two rebukes to Pergamum — those who hold “the doctrine of Balaam” (Revelation 2:14) and those who hold “the doctrine of the Nicolaitans” (2:15) — level the identical charge (things sacrificed to idols + fornication), and the Greek coordinates them tightly (οῡτως…ο̇μοιως, “so also…likewise”); that the two teachings are one error is close to consensus. But the further, genuinely-advocated claim that the very name was invented to translate “Balaam” into Greek — so that there was no real Nicolaus and the “Nicolaitans” simply are the Balaamites — does not hold: the pun is inexact, it is unattested in antiquity, and every early Father who touches the sect derives it instead from a literal founder, Nicolaus of Antioch.

Related arguments: the doctrine of Balaam — see also Did Balaam engineer the apostasy at Peor?

The question

In the letter to Pergamum the risen Christ names two evils in one breath. First: “thou hast there them that hold the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel, to eat things sacrificed unto idols, and to commit fornication” (Revelation 2:14). Then, immediately: “So hast thou also them that hold the doctrine of the Nicolaitans, which thing I hate” (2:15). The two clauses stand in exact parallel and carry the same indictment — idol-food and immorality — and the second opens by pointing back to the first (“so also…likewise”). From that juxtaposition a long-standing reading concludes that the “doctrine of Balaam” and the “doctrine of the Nicolaitans” are the same heresy under two names — and the reading is often clinched by an etymological pun: Hebrew Bil‘am can be heard as bela‘ ‘am / billa‘ ‘am (“he swallowed / consumed / wore down the people”), and Greek Nikolaos parses transparently as nika-laos (“conqueror of the people”); on this view “Nicolaitans” is a Greek calque of “Balaamites,” a symbolic name John coined for the same group.1

Two questions must be kept apart, and both answered. (i) Do the two names label the same libertine teaching? — yes, and near-uncontestedly. (ii) Is “Nicolaitans” a symbolic renaming of “Balaamites,” so that the two are literally one sect and there was no historical Nicolaus? — here the answer is negative, and the gap between the two is the whole argument.

The case for the identification

The reading is not built on air. Four things carry it.

(1) The parallel structure of 2:14–15. The two “doctrine of …” clauses are laid out as a matched pair, and the charge in each is verbally identical — eating things offered to idols and committing fornication. A single congregation is rebuked for a single kind of laxity, described twice.

(2) The Greek coordinates them. Verse 15 opens οῡτως ἔχεις καὶ σύ…ο̇μοιως (“so you also have…likewise”). The paired adverbs can be read as explanatory — v. 15 restating v. 14 — so that “the teaching of the Nicolaitans” simply is “the teaching of Balaam” just described. David Aune takes the adverbs to coordinate the verses “by way of… explanation,” and G. K. Beale renders the pair “in the same manner…in the same way,” treating the two as one libertine movement.2

(3) Both names yield “…the people” — and the Hebrew half is anciently attested. The Greek Nikolaos (νίκη “victory” + λαος “people”) means “conqueror of the people” on its face. And the matching Hebrew play on Bil‘am is not a modern invention: the Babylonian Talmud already derives the name as belo ‘am (“without a people”) and billa‘ ‘am (“he wore down / consumed a people”) at Sanhedrin 105a — the same rabbinic name-midrash that Jerome knew. So a reader with both languages could hear “Balaam” and “Nikolaos” as saying the same thing about a corrupter of God’s people.3

(4) It is genuinely advocated. The identification is no fringe curiosity: it was proposed by Johannes Cocceius in the seventeenth century, argued at length by Campegius Vitringa, glossed by J. D. Michaelis (who paired Nikolaos, “conqueror of the people,” with Balaam, “destroyer of the people”), and made famous by E. W. Hengstenberg; in the twentieth century W. M. Mackay pressed it to the conclusion that no literal sect existed at all — the “swallowers of the people” and the “conquerors of the people” are one symbolic pair.4

The counter-case

Against the strong form of the reading — that the name is a coined cipher and the two are one sect — stand three observations, and together they set its ceiling.

(1) The pun is inexact. The bridge phrase “…the people” papers over a real mismatch. Greek nikaō is conquer / be victorious over; Hebrew bala‘ is swallow / devour / destroy. “Be victorious over the people” and “devour the people” are not translations of each other; the equivalence survives only if both are loosened to a vague “dominate or ruin the people.” Michaelis himself had to slide “Balaam” from “swallow” to “destroy” to make the two names rhyme. This is a homiletical resonance, not the rigorous bilingual equivalence of genuine pairs such as Abaddon / Apollyon (Revelation 9:11), where the Greek really does render the Hebrew.5

(2) It has no ancient footing — and the whole early tradition runs the other way. No Church Father proposes the pun or the name-identity; it first appears in post-Reformation scholarship. Every early writer who addresses the sect instead derives it from a literal person: Nicolaus of Antioch, one of the seven deacons of Acts 6:5. Irenaeus reports the Nicolaitans as “the followers of that Nicolas who was one of the seven first ordained to the diaconate by the apostles,” who “lead lives of unrestrained indulgence”; Clement of Alexandria tells the story of Nicolaus’ wife and defends the man as chaste while blaming his followers for perverting his “abuse the flesh” into fornication; Hippolytus and Eusebius follow suit. Crucially, these writers keep Balaam and Nicolaus distinct — Balaam is the Old Testament type of the sin (as 2:14 makes him), Nicolaus the founder of the present party — and the early Latin and Greek commentators on Revelation 2:15 read the two “doctrines” the same way, as parallel but separate (Victorinus, Oecumenius, Andreas of Caesarea, Bede, Alcuin). The critical suggestion that the Fathers merely inferred a founder from Revelation itself does not rescue the pun: even if inferred, what they inferred was always a person, never a Balaam-calque, so their unanimous testimony still tells against the cipher reading.6

(3) The grammar of 2:15 reads at least as naturally as comparison. The paired adverbs are ambiguous, and the phrase καὶ σύ (“you also”) most naturally adds the Nicolaitans as a further item rather than renaming the first — “just as you have Balaam-holders, so you also have Nicolaitan-holders likewise.” A named present party (“Nicolaitans”) standing where an Old Testament type (“Balaam”) stood in v. 14 reads as a comparison of a contemporary sect to a scriptural precedent, not as one group described twice. Albert Barnes already objected that 2:15 “expressly distinguishes” the two. The syntax firmly establishes that the two teachings are the same in kind; it does not establish that the two names denote one and the same group.7

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the Nicolaitans and the “Balaam” faction were charged with the same libertine error — eating idol-food and fornication — is on the face of the text and is the near-consensus of the commentators. On this level the letter treats “the doctrine of Balaam” and “the doctrine of the Nicolaitans” as one moral tendency in the Asian churches, Balaam supplying the Old Testament template for it (as the parallel rebuke to Thyatira supplies a “Jezebel,” 2:20). A reader who says “the Nicolaitans held the doctrine of Balaam” is saying something the text warrants.

What is overstated. The strong thesis — that “Nicolaitans” is a coined Greek translation of “Balaam,” so the two names are one sect and no real Nicolaus stands behind them — outruns the evidence at every point. The pun it rests on is not an exact equivalence but a loose gloss that has to bend “swallow” into “conquer”; it is unattested in antiquity, appearing only after the Reformation; it runs against the unanimous early derivation of the sect from a literal founder; and it requires the weaker of two possible construals of 2:15, against the natural additive force of “you also.” That a Greek-and-Hebrew reader could hear the resonance does not show that John coined the name to carry it — possibility is not attestation. The identification is a suggestive literary reading that a minority of serious scholars entertain, not a demonstrated fact; the major critical and evangelical commentaries treat the Nicolaitans as a real proto-Gnostic party whose teaching is likened to Balaam’s, which is a weaker and safer claim than the pun requires.

Assessment

Sound that the two “doctrines” name a single libertine teaching (idol-food + fornication): the parallel of Revelation 2:14 and 2:15, the identical charge, and the coordinating “so also…likewise” put this close to consensus, with Balaam as the Old Testament type of the sin. Weak that “Nicolaitans” is a coined Greek calque of “Balaamites,” so the two are literally one sect under two names: the underlying pun (bela‘ ‘am “consume the people” = nika-laos “conquer the people”) is inexact, it has no ancient attestation, every early Father instead derives the sect from a literal Nicolaus of Antioch (Acts 6:5), and the grammar of 2:15 reads at least as naturally as a comparison of two analogous groups. The honest verdict keeps the two apart: the identity of the error is secure; the identity of the groups, and the cipher-etymology offered to prove it, is a coherent but unsupported conjecture. This is distinct from the question of whether Balaam actually engineered the apostasy at Peor, the other argument that turns on the “doctrine of Balaam.”
