Is “Balaam” a rabbinic cipher for Jesus?

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: the rabbis on Balaam (b. Sanhedrin 106b; b. Gittin 57a)

Related readings: Did a “book of Balaam” ever exist? · Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor?

The passage

… from what is written: “Bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days” (Psalms 55:24), [it emerges] that he was thirty-three or thirty-four years old. He said to him: You have spoken well; I myself saw the notebook of Balaam, and it was written therein: Balaam the lame was thirty-three years old when Pineḥas the highwayman killed him.

— b. Sanhedrin 106b, a min (“heretic”) answering Rabbi Ḥanina on Balaam’s age at death.

Come and see the difference between the sinners of Israel and the prophets of the nations of the world. As Balaam, who was a prophet, wished Israel harm, whereas Jesus the Nazarene, who was a Jewish sinner, sought their well-being.

— b. Gittin 57a, closing the scene in which Onkelos raises Titus, Balaam and Jesus by necromancy.1

The question

Since the nineteenth century some scholars have read certain rabbinic “Balaam” passages as veiled references to Jesus under a biblical name; others read them as being about the seer of Numbers 22–24. The thesis has been stated broadly, that “Balaam” is a standing alias across the Talmud, and narrowly, of the age-and-notebook passage of b. Sanhedrin 106b alone; the answer bears on what the Talmud records of early Jewish–Christian controversy. R. Travers Herford’s Christianity in Talmud and Midrash (1903) gathered the material under the headings “Jesus and Balaam,” “Jesus and Balaam in Hell,” “The Age of Balaam” and “Balaam and the Name of God.”2 A popular form of the thesis holds that “Balaam” was a Christian censor’s substitute for “Jesus”; no named proponent of that form has been located.

The history of the question

The identification of hostile figures in the Talmud with Jesus has a medieval history. At the Disputation of Paris (1240) the convert Nicholas Donin laid thirty-five charges against the Talmud before the papal court, many of them alleging blasphemy against Jesus and Mary; Rabbi Yechiel of Paris, defending, argued in part that the offending figures were not the Christian Jesus; and some twenty-four cartloads of Talmud manuscripts were publicly burned in 1242.3 Later Christian censorship of the Talmud, intensifying after the burnings of 1553 and heaviest in the Basel edition of 1578–81, deleted or blanked the name “Yeshu ha-Notzri” where the manuscripts have it, or replaced it with a neutral phrase: at Gittin 57a the printed editions read “the sinners of Israel” where the Munich 95 manuscript reads “Yeshu.” Peter Schäfer’s Jesus in the Talmud (2007) works from the uncensored manuscripts.4

A covert reference to Jesus under the name of Balaam

In this reading some rabbinic Balaam passages, above all the Sanhedrin 106b exchange, allude to Jesus behind the biblical name.

Held by: R. Travers Herford (Christianity in Talmud and Midrash, 1903).5

Herford held that Balaam “is frequently used in the Talmud as a type for Jesus,” and his case is concentrated in the Sanhedrin 106b exchange, where the details correspond point by point to the Christian story. The questioner is a min, a sectarian whom this reading takes for a Christian or Jewish-Christian, asking a rabbi the age of a figure whose life is a live question between them. The answer is thirty-three, the traditional age of Jesus at the crucifixion. The “notebook (pinqas) of Balaam” is a written record of his sayings, a Gospel. The killer, “Pineḥas the highwayman” (or “robber,” listaʾah), is a distorted Pontius Pilate, under whom Jesus was executed. Taken together, the chain makes the passage a jab at Jesus folded into a midrash about Balaam’s premature death. The wider Babylonian Talmud speaks of Jesus in other places, in the ben Panḍera and ben Sṭaḍa traditions and the notice that he was “hanged on the eve of Passover” (Sanhedrin 43a), so the rabbis had Jesus in view; and under Christian rule a biblical name offered cover where plain speech was dangerous. On this reading a reader who finds Jesus behind the seer of Numbers in this passage is following the rabbis’ own correspondences of age, book and executioner.6

The biblical seer, distinct from Jesus

In this reading the rabbinic Balaam is the seer of Numbers, and Jesus is a separate figure in the Talmud.

Held by: Ephraim E. Urbach (Tarbiz, 1956); Johann Maier (Jesus von Nazareth in der talmudischen Überlieferung, 1978); John P. Meier (A Marginal Jew, 1991); Louis Ginzberg (1922).7

Gittin 57a names Balaam and Jesus in one scene as two different men: Balaam counsels against Israel and is punished in boiling semen, Jesus counsels for Israel, and the passage contrasts the gentile prophet with the Jewish sinner.8 The hostile rabbinic portrait continues a first-century Jewish tradition: Philo, Josephus and Pseudo-Philo already made Balaam a mercenary who counselled the seduction at Peor, and the Mishnah’s seer barred from the World-to-Come (m. Sanhedrin 10:2) and the foil to Abraham in Avot 5:19 grow out of the biblical text, the process Geza Vermes traced in the Balaam haggadah.9 Rabbi Ḥanina gives his own method for the age: “bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days” (Psalm 55:23), set against the seventy years of Psalm 90:10, yields “thirty-three or thirty-four”; Avot 5:19 already attaches the same Psalm to Balaam’s disciples, and the number varies across editions and manuscripts.10 The Encyclopaedia Judaica sets the passage in its social setting: “it is impossible to imagine that a Christian would ask a Jew how old Jesus was, and call the Gospel Balaam’s Chronicle, or that Pontius Pilate, who is not mentioned even once in the whole of rabbinic literature, should be referred to as Pineḥas the robber.”11 Maier treats the Balaam cipher as speculation, Ginzberg held that rabbinic literature knows no nicknames for Jesus, and Urbach reads the Balaam homilies as inner-Jewish exegesis within the Jewish–Christian debate. On this reading the Sanhedrin sugya is midrash on the seer of Numbers, drawn from Psalms and Torah.
