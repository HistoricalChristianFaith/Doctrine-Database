Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor?

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: the Qur’an and the Islamic exegetes

Related readings: Why does 2 Peter call Balaam “son of Bosor,” not “son of Beor”? · Is “Balaam” a rabbinic cipher for Jesus?

The passage

him to whom We gave Our signs… his example is that of a dog

— Qur’an 7:175–176.1

[On] Allah’s statement, ‘And recite to them the news of him to whom We gave Our Ayat, but he threw them away’ … ‘Abdullah bin Mas‘ud said [it] is about Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’, a man from the Children of Israel … It is well-known that this honorable Ayah was revealed about a man from the Children of Israel in ancient times.

— Ibn Kathīr, Tafsīr on Q 7:175–177.2

The question

The Qur’an tells of a man given God’s signs who cast them off and “clung to the earth,” but names no one, and the exegetes name him variously. The question is whether the verse refers to Balaam son of Beor, to a man of Muḥammad’s own time, or to a type rather than a determinate individual; it bears on whether the Qur’an carries a reception of Balaam alongside the New Testament’s “error of Balaam” and the rabbis’ “Balaam the wicked.”

Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’, the Balaam of Numbers

In this reading the verse was revealed about the Israelite-era seer Balaam son of Beor.

Held by: ‘Abdullāh ibn Mas‘ūd; Ibn ‘Abbās (in several chains); Mujāhid and ‘Ikrima; Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373).3

The narrations give the man one name, Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’, an exact transcription of the Hebrew “Balaam son of Beor”: Bil‘am and Ba‘ūrā’, patronymic and all, the form 2 Peter renders “Bosor.” The tafsir narrative keeps recognisable furniture from Numbers: a man of famed, answered prayer, who knew God’s Greatest Name, prevailed on to curse God’s people; a mount that “sat down and refused to proceed” on the road, as the ass of Numbers 22:22–35; and a tongue overruled to bless Israel against the speaker’s will (“It is against my will. This is a matter that Allah has decided”), Numbers’ own reversal of the curse. The Qur’anic theme, a man given God’s signs who forfeits them for the world, is the “wages of unrighteousness” profile the New Testament and the rabbis had already fixed on Balaam. Ibn Kathīr reports the identification as well known “according to Ibn Mas‘ud and several others among the Salaf.” On this reading the verse joins Islam to the synagogue and the church in remembering Balaam as the gifted man who sold his gift.4

A man of Muḥammad’s own time

In this reading the verse describes a contemporary known to its first hearers, most often named as the poet Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt.

Held by: Ibn ‘Abbās, in the chain through Qatāda (Sayfī son of al-Rāhib); ‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr and Zayd ibn Aslam (Umayya) ⚠ unverified; commentators collected in the classical tafsirs.5

The verse is addressed to the Prophet’s hearers (“recite to them the news of him”), and the classical commentators record identifications drawn from their own world. Gibril Fouad Haddad’s digest of al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī and others lists, beside Balaam, “the jinn-familiar, Arab poet and pseudo-prophet Umayya b. Abi al-Salt al-Thaqafi (d. 5/626), who had read the Scriptures and who taught the Quraysh the expression, ‘bismik Allahumma’,” and “a priest named Abu ‘Amir.” Ibn Kathīr records the narration from Ibn ‘Abbās through Qatāda that the man was Sayfī, son of al-Rāhib, and Maududi lists Umayya and Ṣayfī among the names commentators gave. On this reading the verse holds up to its first hearers a man of their own world who had been given the signs and cast them off.6

A type of the one who recants

In this reading the verse draws a pattern of apostasy, and the identity of any single man behind it is not established or not essential.

Held by: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb) ⚠ unverified; S. A. Maududi (Tafhīm al-Qur’an); Gibril Fouad Haddad (2011).7

The Qur’an names no one, and Maududi observes that God and His Messenger “usually mention evil without specific references to any individual,” since “those examples are meant for didactic purposes, and this is done without smearing anyone’s reputation.” He records the names commentators proposed and concludes that “in the absence of any authentic information about the identity of the persons under discussion, we might as well consider the description made here to fit a certain type of person.” Haddad, citing al-Rāzī, writes that the man’s identity “is of little consequence since he is an archetype of the one time believer who recants,” and, citing Ḥabannaka, more specifically of “the rank and file of learned Christians and Jews” who lapse “from Divine election into worldliness and unbelief.” On this reading the verse’s force lies in the pattern it draws, the man raised by God’s signs who clung to the earth and followed his desires, set before every hearer as a warning.
