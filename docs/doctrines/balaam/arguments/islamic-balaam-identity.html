Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the dominant classical identification is Balaam, and it is well-grounded (an exact onomastic match and two transferred Numbers motifs), even though the Qur’an leaves the man anonymous and the exegetes weighed rival referents · read alongside the Islamic-reception timeline block.

The claim

[On] Allah’s statement, ‘And recite to them the news of him to whom We gave Our Ayat, but he threw them away’ … ‘Abdullah bin Mas‘ud said [it] is about Bal‘am bin Ba‘ura’, a man from the Children of Israel … It is well-known that this honorable Ayah was revealed about a man from the Children of Israel in ancient times.

— Ibn Kathīr, Tafsīr on Q 7:175–177. The asserted claim: the unnamed apostate of Qur’an 7:175–176 — the man given God’s signs who “clung to the earth… like a dog” — is the Balaam of Numbers 22–24, so that the Qur’an carries an Islamic reception of Balaam parallel to the New Testament’s “error of Balaam” and the rabbis’ “Balaam the wicked.”

Who advances it

The identification is the mainstream of classical Sunni exegesis, traced to the earliest authorities: Ibn Mas‘ūd and Ibn ‘Abbās (with Mujāhid and ‘Ikrima) in the reports gathered by al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), and carried through al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273) and Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373). It is the standard reading reproduced in modern Qur’an commentary and reference works, which note the biblical Balaam of Numbers behind the name.1

The evidence for

Three lines converge. (1) The name. Every pro-Balaam narration gives the man the same name — Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’ — which is an exact transcription of the Hebrew “Balaam son of Beor”: Bil‘am (Balaam) and Ba‘ūrā’ (Beor), patronymic and all. It is in fact a cleaner witness to Beor than the New Testament’s Greek “Bosor” (2 Peter 2:15). (2) The transferred motifs. The tafsir narrative keeps recognisable furniture from Numbers: a man of famed, answered prayer prevailed on to curse God’s people; a mount that “sat down and refused to proceed” on the road (the ass of Numbers 22:22–35); and a tongue overruled to bless Israel against the speaker’s will (“It is against my will. This is a matter that Allah has decided”) — Numbers’ own reversal of the curse. (3) The theme. The Qur’anic point — a man given God’s signs who forfeits them for the world — is precisely the “wages of unrighteousness” profile the New Testament and the rabbis had already fixed on Balaam, so the reading joins a tradition rather than inventing one.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the mainstream classical identification of Q 7:175 is the biblical Balaam — and that it is well-grounded, not a bare guess — is not seriously in doubt: the name is an exact match, two distinctive Numbers motifs are carried over, and the moral profile fits the man the rest of the tradition already condemned. As a fact of reception history — that Islam remembers Balaam, and remembers him as the type of the gifted apostate — the claim is secure.

What is overstated or imprecise. The Qur’an itself names no one; the name is entirely the commentary’s. And the commentary is not unanimous: because the verse is anonymous, some early authorities (‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr, Zayd ibn Aslam) referred it to a contemporary of Muḥammad — Umayya ibn Abi al-Ṣalt — and others to Sayfi ibn al-Rāhib or Abū ‘Āmir, while the exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Maududi) concluded that the man’s exact identity is neither established nor essential, the verse being an archetype of the recanting believer. So a stronger claim — that Q 7:175 is originally and determinately about the biblical Balaam — runs past its evidence: the anonymity and the rival referents leave that genuinely open. The tafsir’s extra colour (the acceptable prayer, the Greatest Name, the king of Midian’s bribe) is legendary elaboration in the manner of the rabbinic and Josephan retellings, not independent attestation.3

Assessment

Sound. The claim this page rates — that the dominant Islamic reading of Qur’an 7:175–176 identifies its apostate as Balaam son of Beor, and does so on real grounds — holds: the name Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura is Balaam son of Beor exactly, the donkey and the overruled-curse motifs come straight from Numbers, and the theme matches the reception the New Testament and the rabbis had already built. What should not be inflated from this is a claim about the verse’s original referent: the Qur’an names no one, and the exegetical tradition itself weighed a contemporary of Muḥammad (Umayya ibn Abi al-Ṣalt) and others, treating the figure as a type — so the “originally Balaam” version of the claim is only plausible, not sound. The secure and sound result is the reception-historical one: Islam belongs, with synagogue and church, among the traditions that made Balaam the pattern of the gifted man who sold his gift.
