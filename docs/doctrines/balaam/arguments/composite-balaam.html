Is the Balaam of Numbers 22–24 a literary composite?

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: modern scholarship

Related readings: How old is the “Star out of Jacob,” and was it read messianically before Christianity? · Why does 2 Peter call Balaam “son of Bosor,” not “son of Beor”? · Does the Septuagint read Balaam’s oracles more messianically than the Hebrew?

The passage

If the men come to call thee, rise up, and go with them

— Numbers 22:20 (KJV).

God’s anger was kindled because he went

— Numbers 22:22 (KJV), the next verse.1

The question

God permits the journey and in the next verse is angry that Balaam went; the professional seer cannot see the angel that his donkey sees three times; four dense poetic oracles are set in flowing prose; and the Balaam who cannot “go beyond the word of the LORD… to do either good or bad of mine own mind” (24:13) and departs for home (24:25) is remembered elsewhere as the diviner whose counsel seduced Israel at Peor (31:16) and the “soothsayer” slain in war (Joshua 13:22). The question is whether these features mark a text that grew from several sources or strata, and if so whether those sources can be recovered, or whether the pericope is to be read as one narrative.

The question concerns the formation of the text. The Deir ‘Alla plaster text (8th c. BC) names “Balaam son of Beor… a seer of the gods” in a story independent of Numbers; Jo Ann Hackett, Michael S. Moore and John T. Greene treat the figure’s ambiguity as a feature of the Balaam traditions themselves; and Géza Vermes traces how later Jewish tradition hardened the ambivalent seer into “Balaam the wicked.”2

Two continuous sources, the Yahwist and the Elohist

In this reading the prose of Numbers 22–24 interweaves two parallel narratives, J and E, each of which once told the whole Balaam story in its own way.

Held by: Julius Wellhausen (Die Composition des Hexateuchs, 3rd ed., 1899); George Buchanan Gray (Numbers, ICC, 1903); Martin Noth (Numbers, OTL, 1968, with modifications).3

The prose carries doublets: two embassies of Moabite messengers, two nocturnal divine consultations, and two “will Balaam go?” sequences. Alongside them the divine names alternate between YHWH and Elohim. On the Documentary Hypothesis these repetitions and unevennesses are the overlap of two continuous documents, each with its own Balaam, which an editor combined, and the received text can be un-braided into two coherent, separately readable accounts of the seer. The same analysis that divides the rest of the Pentateuch into its sources accounts here for the double embassy and the shifting name of God, and it gives the composite figure, blesser and diviner, a source in two literary traditions that each remembered Balaam differently. On this reading the pericope is the meeting-place of J’s Balaam and E’s.

An old core expanded and re-evaluated in stages

In this reading the pericope grew around older material by later expansion and redaction, without dividing into two complete parallel documents.

Held by: Alexander Rofé (The Book of Balaam, 1979); Baruch A. Levine (Numbers 21–36, Anchor Bible, 2000).4

The four oracles (23:7–10, 18–24; 24:3–9, 15–24) are markedly archaic verse, with older orthography and morphology, the divine epithets El, Shaddai and Elyon, and parallels with the oldest datable Hebrew poetry; the prose builds up to oracles whose content overruns its frame, as pre-existing material around which the narrative was composed. The donkey pericope (22:22–35) is a self-contained unit with its own ironic point, the hired seer blind to what his ass perceives; it opens with God angry at a journey he has just authorised and closes with the angel’s “go with the men,” returning Balaam to the errand he was already on, which marks it as a later insertion. The obedient seer of chs. 22–24 who blesses Israel four times is overlaid by the hostile evaluation of 31:16, Joshua 13:22, Deuteronomy and Nehemiah. Verse-by-verse assignments to J and E diverge from one scholar to the next, and the divine names do not sort into two consistent threads in this pericope. On this reading the seams mark stages of growth around an old narrative core, not two whole Balaams.

A single narrative read as it stands

In this reading Numbers 22–24 is one story, whose tensions are features of the narrative to be read exegetically.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The patristic readers on this timeline treat Balaam as one figure whose doubleness calls for exegesis: Origen refuses him the name of prophet, Augustine keeps the Spirit-clause and calls him a compelled reprobate, and Gregory of Nyssa makes him a diviner overruled. Augustine takes the speech of the ass as a literal wonder, as 2 Peter 2:16 recounts it. Read as one narrative, the seer hired for his sight who cannot see what his donkey sees is the story’s own irony, and the prophet who blesses under constraint and the counsellor of Peor are one man seen at two moments. On this reading the received pericope is a single account of one seer, its tensions belonging to the story it tells.5
