Does the Septuagint read Balaam’s oracles more messianically than the Hebrew? (Numbers 24:7 and 24:17)

Disputed reading · on Balaam son of Beor · witness: the Septuagint of Numbers 24, read by Philo of Alexandria

Related readings: How old is the “Star out of Jacob,” and was it read messianically before Christianity? · Is the Balaam of Numbers 22–24 a literary composite? · Why does 2 Peter call Balaam “son of Bosor,” not “son of Beor”?

The passage

He shall pour the water out of his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters, and his king shall be higher than Agag, and his kingdom shall be exalted.

— Numbers 24:7, Hebrew (KJV).

There shall come a man out of his seed, and he shall rule over many nations; and the kingdom of Gog shall be exalted, and his kingdom shall be increased.

— Numbers 24:7, Septuagint, in Brenton’s translation.1

there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel

— Numbers 24:17, Hebrew (KJV).

a star shall rise out of Jacob, a man shall spring out of Israel; and shall crush the princes of Moab, and shall spoil all the sons of Seth.

— Numbers 24:17, Septuagint, in Brenton’s translation.2

The question

At both verses the Greek Pentateuch parts from the Hebrew: “a man” (ἄνθρωπος) for the water and buckets of 24:7 and for the “sceptre” of 24:17, “many nations” for the local realm, and Gog for the Amalekite king Agag. The question is how far these renderings carry a messianic reading of Balaam’s oracles, and whether that reading is the translators’ own, grounded in the Hebrew they read, or short of messianism in the later sense. John J. Collins’s The Scepter and the Star sets the Greek among the early Jewish evidence that the oracle was read of a ruler-figure; the oracle’s other pre-Christian readings, at Qumran, in the Targums and around Bar Kokhba, are set out on the star-oracle page.3

A reinterpretation grounded in the Hebrew

In this reading the Greek draws out a shift toward a single king already present in the Hebrew oracles, and its “Gog” may stand on a Hebrew reading.

Held by: John H. Sailhamer (The Pentateuch as Narrative, 1992) ⚠ unverified; the author of “Septuagintal Saturdays: Num. 24:7” (2008).4

The post’s author grants that “the LXX contains a reinterpretation” but does “not think it is divorced from the MT.” Citing Sailhamer, the post notes that “in several places Numbers 24 repeats oracles that in Numbers 23 were referring to the nation of Israel, and reapplies them to the single ‘king’ (cf. Num. 23:24 and Num. 24:8)”; the translator may be picking up that shift and rendering 24:7 of a single referent. For the king’s rival the Greek’s Γωγ has company: the Samaritan Pentateuch and the later Jewish revisers Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion, who elsewhere bring the Greek back toward the Hebrew, also read Gog, which points to a Hebrew mig-Gog (מגוג), one consonant from the Masoretic mē-ʾĀgāg (מאגג). On this reading the Greek’s future king belongs to the history of the Hebrew text, and, in the post’s words, “with a few reasonable emendations of the MT it is not hard to postulate a messianic ‘original reading’ not reflected in the MT.”5

A royal reading, distinct from developed messianism

In this reading the Greek speaks of a coming ruler, and whether that ruler is already the Messiah of later expectation is a further question the rendering does not settle.

Held by: Johan Lust (“Septuagint and Messianism, with a Special Emphasis on the Pentateuch,” 1997).6

A rendering that makes the oracle speak of a coming man who rules many nations is a royal, ruler-focused reading. Lust cautions against reading the later, full messianism back into every such Greek rendering. The personalizing “a man” and the universal “many nations” describe a coming sovereign; whether that sovereign is the Messiah is a further step. On this reading the Greek oracles of Numbers 24 are royal expectation, read on their own terms.

A deliberate reinterpretation toward a coming ruler

In this reading the Alexandrian translators reshaped Balaam’s oracles into a prophecy of a single end-time victor.

Held by: John W. Wevers (Notes on the Greek Text of Numbers, 1998); Martin Rösel (2006); Kyle A. Biersdorff (2014).7

Wevers judges the Greek of 24:7 “a complete reinterpretation…[that]…has little to do with a parent text”; Rösel treats the Greek Pentateuch’s Balaam renderings as deliberate interpretive choices; Biersdorff closes his study on the “messianic interpretation in the LXX Balaam oracles.” The Hebrew of both verses can be read of the Davidic realm in its own horizon: a king higher than Agag, the Amalekite of 1 Samuel 15, and a sceptre over Moab. Read with Gog, the kingdom is exalted over the adversary from the land of Magog who marshals the nations against God’s people (Ezekiel 38–39; later Revelation 20:8), a figure of “the latter days,” and the personalized “a man” of both verses gives that victor a single face. Philo already quotes 24:7 in its Greek form, “a man shall hereafter come forth… who shall rule over many nations,” as prophecy of a coming ruler. On this reading the translators read Balaam’s oracle as Qumran and the Targums would, of a coming deliverer.8
