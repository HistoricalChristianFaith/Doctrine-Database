Cyril of Alexandria on Balaam — a wicked augur who prophesied under constraint

Dates: c. 425 (Cyril’s Balaam remarks are scattered across his exegetical works, all written before the Nestorian controversy of 428 — the Commentary on Isaiah, the Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets, and the Commentary on the Gospel of John); Cyril b. c. 376, patriarch of Alexandria from 412, d. 444 · Wikipedia
Summary: The last of the great Alexandrian exegetes on this timeline reads Balaam with no ambivalence at all: he is “the wicked Balaam,” “a false prophet and an augur” hired from Mesopotamia to curse Israel by sorcery, whose curse God overruled into a blessing — and who, foiled, turned to a second malice, the seduction at Peor. Cyril’s theologically weightiest move is to pair Balaam with Caiaphas: like the high priest who “prophesied” Christ’s saving death without understanding it (John 11:51), Balaam spoke a true word “under constraint,” the prophecy belonging to the compulsion and not to the man — the same resolution Origen had drawn from the same Caiaphas verse two centuries before.

Claims & evidence

The false prophet overruled — and his second malice at Peor (Commentary on Isaiah)

Cyril’s fullest narrative comes in his Commentary on Isaiah, expounding the oracle against Moab (Isaiah 15–16). He rehearses the whole episode as a case of pagan magic defeated by God, and — like Philo and Josephus before him — treats the Peor seduction as Balaam’s deliberate fallback once the curse failed:

He hired Balaam from Mesopotamia; and this man was a false prophet and an augur; then he exhorts him, saying: Curse Israel for me. For I know, he says, that whom you bless is blessed, and whom you curse is cursed. And the methods of sorcery were practiced by him, with altars having been raised, and sacrifices having been offered. But Balak was disappointed of his hope, and missed his mark, as the one who was hired to curse unexpectedly blessed Israel. But since his attempt turned out contrary to his expectations, he again pursues his malice against them in another way. For he ensnared those of Israel, who were captivated by the beauty of the women, to turn away from their love for God, and to choose to worship Baal-peor.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Isaiah, Discourse 9 (on the burden of Moab).1 The pattern — curse thwarted, then seduction — is the developed aetiology of the New Testament’s “doctrine of Balaam” (Revelation 2:14) that the first-century Jewish retellings had already staged from the bare notice of Numbers 31:16. Across his prophetic commentaries Cyril keeps the same flat verdict: Balak “had Balaam summoned from Mesopotamia” to “curse those of Israel and [seek] knowledge of the future” (on Habakkuk); “he hired the wicked Balaam and even brought him up to a mountain, ‘saying, Come, curse Israel for me’” (on Nahum); Balaam “attempted to divine against those of Israel” through “unclean spirits” (on Hosea).2

Prophesying “under constraint” — Balaam and Caiaphas (Commentary on John)

Cyril’s answer to the tradition’s hardest question — how a wicked man could speak true prophecy — comes where he expounds Caiaphas’s unwitting prophecy that one man should die for the people (John 11:51). He explains it exactly as Balaam’s: a true word forced from an unworthy speaker, granted to the office he held rather than to his person.

For he proclaims beforehand of what good things the death of the Christ would become the source, saying that which he did not understand, and glorifying God (as Balaam did) under constraint, since he was holding the prerogative of the priestly order: the prophecy being as it were given, not to him personally, but to the outward representative of the priesthood.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on the Gospel of John, Book VII (on John 11:51).3 The pairing is the same one Origen made from the same verse — Caiaphas and Balaam as the two men who “prophesied” without being prophets — and Cyril’s formula (“under constraint… not to him personally”) is close to Gregory of Nyssa’s diviner “moved by divine inspiration” against his own will. The true word is real, but it is God’s act upon the man, not the product of his craft or his character.

Notes

  • No double figure — the dark half only. Where Ambrose and Jerome hold the covetous diviner and the clear prophet of Christ in tension, Cyril keeps almost wholly to the dark half: “the wicked Balaam,” the false prophet, the augur, the architect of Peor. The true prophecy is acknowledged, but always as something wrung from him “under constraint” — never as a mark of the man’s standing before God.
  • The Alexandrian line on the true-prophecy problem. Cyril’s Caiaphas–Balaam pairing shows how durable Origen’s reading of John 11:51 proved in Alexandria: two centuries on, the patriarch resolves the paradox the same way — the gift lodged in the office or the moment, not in the diviner. Together with Gregory of Nyssa and Theodoret, this makes the Greek consensus plain: Balaam remains a diviner throughout, past whom God speaks.
  • The star oracle. Cyril’s Balaam material here is about the figure — the augur, the Peor seduction, the constrained prophecy — not the “star out of Jacob,” which he treats within the magi tradition; the oracle’s afterlife is traced on The Star Prophecy and Its Claimed Fulfilments.
