Gregory of Nyssa on Balaam — a diviner overruled into prophecy

Dates: c. 390 (the Balaam narrative and its allegory fill the Life of Moses, one of Gregory’s last works, written in the early 390s; the shorter dogmatic remark is from On the Holy Trinity, c. 375–380); Gregory b. c. 335, bishop of Nyssa from c. 372, d. c. 395 · Wikipedia
Summary: The youngest of the Cappadocian Fathers gives Balaam his fullest Greek Christian reading between Origen and the fifth-century commentators. In the Life of Moses Gregory retells the episode twice — once as history (historia), once as spiritual contemplation (theoria) — and his answer to the tradition’s central puzzle is decisive: Balaam is a diviner whose ordinary power is frankly demonic (“with the cooperation of demons”), but who is “moved by divine inspiration instead of by demonic power” and, “leaving divination aside,” is made to serve “as an interpreter of the divine will.” The true oracle is not the fruit of his craft but a commandeering of it — God wringing truth from a hostile power, as the Gospel demons confess Christ against their will. From this Gregory draws his governing lesson: magic and cursing are simply powerless against those who live in virtue, so “the curse turns into a blessing.”

Claims & evidence

The historia: a demonic augur, moved to prophesy against his own power (Life of Moses I)

In the narrative half of the Life of Moses, Gregory introduces Balaam as a hired professional of the magic arts whose divination works “with the cooperation of demons.” The turn comes when this power proves “impotent against those who have God as their ally”: the diviner is overruled, and his very inability to harm becomes the occasion of true prophecy.

As he followed those who were leading him to the king of that nation, he learned by the voice of his ass that the way was not propitious for him. Having learned in a vision what was to be done, he found that any harm to be inflicted by working magic was impotent against those who have God as their ally. Moved by divine inspiration instead of by demonic power, he uttered such words as were a clear prophecy of better things which would later come to pass. What prevented his making use of his skill for evil also brought him an awareness of divine power. Leaving divination aside, he acted as an interpreter of the divine will.

— Gregory of Nyssa, Life of Moses I.74.1 “A clear prophecy of better things which would later come to pass” is Gregory’s allusion to the messianic oracle of Numbers 24 — he does not quote the “star out of Jacob” line itself here, but points to it. His resolution of the true-prophecy-from-a-diviner problem is thus close to Origen’s in substance while differently framed: not that prophesying differs from being a prophet, but that the true word comes only when the divine displaces the demonic — the diviner “leaving divination aside.” It is nearer still to Philo, in whom the prophetic spirit enters and “drove all his artificial system of divination… out of his soul.”

The theoria: the curse that cannot land — virtue is curse-proof (Life of Moses II)

In the contemplative half, Gregory turns the episode into a lesson about the impotence of evil against the virtuous soul. Balaam’s magic is the type of every hostile power that a life lived toward God simply defeats; the hired curse “actually turned into a blessing” because reproach finds no purchase on the righteous.

He calls in magic as his ally against those whom he assaults. The history says this magic is a diviner and augur who derived his presumably harmful power from the working of demons… and was paid by the ruler of the Midianites to curse those who live to God, but actually he turned the curse into a blessing. We perceive… that magic is ineffective against those who live in virtue; on the contrary, those fortified by divine help prevail over every onslaught.

— Gregory of Nyssa, Life of Moses II.292.2 He grounds the point in Balaam’s own words — “How shall I curse one whom God does not curse?” (Numbers 23:8) — glossing it, “How shall I revile someone… whose life is impervious to evil because he looks to God?” And he explains the mechanism of the true confession by an unwilling agent: the demonic power itself “taught Balaam that the people of God are invincible” — truth wrung from a hostile spirit, as when the Gospel demons cry out the identity of Christ.3 Near the end of the work Balaam returns under a fresh symbol: his “magic” becomes “the crafty deceit of this life,” the philtre of Circe that changes men “into the form of irrational animals.”4

“Balaam… is said in Scripture to receive counsel from God” (On the Holy Trinity)

Gregory also puts Balaam to dogmatic use. Arguing against the followers of Macedonius that the title “God” (theotēs) is not, by itself, a proof of the divine nature — since Scripture applies it even to idols and false gods — he cites Balaam as a case where even a devil-taught diviner is nonetheless described as dealing with the true God:

And again Balaam, being an augur and a seer, and engaging in divination, and having obtained for himself the instruction of devils and magical augury, is said in Scripture to receive counsel from God.

— Gregory of Nyssa, On the Holy Trinity, and of the Godhead of the Holy Spirit (to Eustathius).5 The point is incidental to Balaam — Gregory is defending the Spirit’s deity by an argument about how divine titles work — but it captures his settled view in a phrase: a genuine encounter with God granted to a man whose craft is demonic, the paradox the whole tradition circles.

Notes

  • The star oracle is alluded to, not quoted. In the Life of Moses Gregory says only that Balaam “uttered such words as were a clear prophecy of better things which would later come to pass” (I.74) — a pointer to the messianic content of Numbers 24, not a citation of the “star out of Jacob.” His explicit messianic use of the star belongs to the lineage traced on the magi timeline (Origen → Eusebius → the later readers), not to this work; so this page does not credit him with a verbatim star-prophecy. The oracle’s own afterlife is on The Star Prophecy and Its Claimed Fulfilments.
  • Balak and the Midianites. Gregory compresses the story and calls Balak “the ruler of the Midianites” (I.73; II.292), where Numbers makes Balak king of Moab and the Midianites the parallel adversary — a distortion his modern editors flag. The blurring of Moab and Midian is native to the biblical account itself (Numbers 22:4–7; 31), where the two peoples act together against Israel.
  • A diviner overruled — the Greek consensus forming. Gregory’s Balaam — a demon-empowered augur commandeered to speak the truth “against” his own power — stands between Origen’s “a diviner, not a prophet” and the fifth-century Antiochene reading of Theodoret and the Alexandrian Cyril. All four keep Balaam a diviner and refuse to make his divination the vehicle of the true word; the true oracle is God’s act upon him, not his craft’s achievement.
