Theodoret of Cyrus on Balaam — the Spirit in an unclean mouth

Dates: c. 453 (Theodoret’s Balaam material is a block of short questions-and-answers in his Questions on the Octateuch — the Questions on Numbers, Q. 39–44, 48 — a late work generally dated to the 450s, written near the end of his life at the request of his coadjutor Hypatius); Theodoret b. c. 393 at Antioch, bishop of Cyrus (Cyrrhus) from 423, d. c. 458/466 · Wikipedia
Summary: The last great Antiochene exegete gives Balaam the tradition’s most methodical treatment, working through the puzzles one by one in his terse question-and-answer form. His verdict on the man is severe — a false seer whose “reputation, though widespread, was false” and whose divination was a “futile art” — and yet his account of the true prophecy is the most exact on this timeline: “his unclean mouth received the power of the most Holy Spirit and uttered what he had not intended.” He resolves the tradition’s two hardest cruxes cleanly: God forbade then permitted the journey not because God changed his mind (“God is not like a man”) but as a concession to Balaam’s own greed; and God arranged the whole affair so that Israel could never blame a seer’s curse for the punishments God himself sent. He reads the fourth oracle of Christ — not, however, from the “star,” but from the line about “the children of Seth.”

Claims & evidence

A false seer whose curse had no power (Questions 39, 42)

Theodoret opens by asking why a seer would need to consult the Lord at all, and answers that Balaam got a true response from a God he did not know — God “desired to use the seer’s response as an opportunity to issue his commands.” When he reaches the decisive question — what power the hired curse actually had — his answer is that it had none, and that the entire episode was staged by God for Israel’s sake:

It had no power at all, for his reputation, though widespread, was false. Since, as a result of their constant lawlessness, the people kept suffering heaven-sent afflictions, it was with a view to their frailty that the Lord God arranged the events regarding Balaam. You see, in case they might imagine they were suffering disasters caused by the seer’s curse when they were really being chastised by God, he did not allow the seer to pronounce curses; he thus cut short any ground of argument that fools might latch onto.

— Theodoret, Questions on Numbers 42.1 The curse is emptied not by Balaam’s virtue but by God’s protective management of his people’s weakness — a distinctively pastoral reading of why the blessing had to come.

Forbidden, then permitted — the concession to greed (Question 40)

On the crux that had exercised Pseudo-Philo and Augustine — why God first refused Balaam the journey and then allowed it — Theodoret locates the reversal entirely in Balaam’s covetous self-deception, not in any change in God:

In his desire for the money promised him, Balaam, imagining that the Lord God could change his mind, again asked whether he should accompany the men sent to him, so God allowed him to go. It was as if he said, ‘Since you want to do it, go, but be sure to say whatever I choose.’

— Theodoret, Questions on Numbers 40.2 God’s permission is a real concession to a fixed will, immediately fenced (“say whatever I choose”) and then checked by the angel in the road — whom, Theodoret notes, “some commentators assert… was Michael, the protector of the people.” The point is guarded by the principle Theodoret states outright: “God is not like a man, that he should have doubts… he does not change his mind like human beings.”3

“His unclean mouth received the power of the most Holy Spirit” (Questions 42, 44)

Theodoret’s answer to how a wicked pagan could utter true prophecy is the timeline’s most precise statement of the instrumental view: the true word is the Holy Spirit’s, spoken through an unwilling and unclean instrument — exactly as God had made the donkey speak.

Therefore, his unclean mouth received the power of the most Holy Spirit and uttered what he had not intended. … these prophecies issued, not from false divination, but from the power of the most Holy Spirit. He who bade the donkey preternaturally adopt a human voice also used the seer’s tongue to foretell the future. Remember, Scripture says, ‘The spirit of God was in him.’ The same thing happened in the case of that criminal Saul; hence, the proverb that arose from that unexpected event: ‘Is Saul also among the prophets?’

— Theodoret, Questions on Numbers 44 (and 42).4 This is the mirror image of Origen, who athetized the clause “the spirit of God came upon him” rather than lodge God’s Spirit in a diviner: Theodoret keeps the clause and makes it the crux of his answer, siding — on the text, though not on the mechanism — with the Latin Augustine. He adds that Balaam himself, having learned “there was nothing to gain from this futile art,” at last “without recourse to the usual portents… directed his tongue to the service of God.”

The oracle of Christ — through “the children of Seth” (Question 44)

Theodoret reads the fourth oracle messianically, and defends the reading against contemporaries who denied it — but the load-bearing line for him is not the star; it is the oracle’s second half, “he will plunder… all the children of Seth” (Numbers 24:17), which he takes to mean all humanity:

You should know that some commentators have concluded that he made no prophecy regarding our Savior, but they should have understood that the coming of our God and Savior was revealed even to Nebuchadnezzar, despite his wickedness… He who was born of Judah according to the flesh plundered not only the princes of Moab but all the children of Seth. All human beings are children of Seth, since Noah was a descendant of Seth, and all the human race comes from Noah.

— Theodoret, Questions on Numbers 44.5 The rationale is his theory of why such a prophecy was given to a pagan at all: “so that prophecies such as these would be remembered among the gentiles.” Balaam, like Nebuchadnezzar, is a gentile mouthpiece by whom the salvation of the nations is announced to the nations. Foiled of the curse, the “false prophet continued his machinations” — he “plotted to draw them into a sin of lust,” advising the Midianites “to arm, not their men, but their women against Israel”: the counsel of Peor once again.6

Notes

  • The messianic reading, but not from the star. Unlike the magi lineage (Origen → Eusebius) and the Targums, Theodoret does not rest the Christ-prophecy on “a star shall come out of Jacob” — the word “star” does not appear in his Balaam questions. His christological reading hangs on the oracle’s other clauses (“the children of Seth”; the Kittim oracle of Numbers 24:24, which he refers to Alexander and Antiochus). So this page does not credit him with a star-quote; the star’s own afterlife is on The Star Prophecy and Its Claimed Fulfilments.
  • Balaam’s death is untreated. Theodoret’s questions run from the Peor counsel straight to the slaughter of the Midianite women (Q. 48, “schooled in the teachings of idolatry and apt to corrupt their captors”); he nowhere comments on Balaam’s own killing (Numbers 31:8).
  • The Greek consensus, stated most exactly. With Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, and Cyril, Theodoret keeps Balaam a false seer and refuses to make his divination the vehicle of the true word — the oracle is the Spirit’s act through an unwilling instrument, “the seer’s tongue” used like the donkey’s mouth. His pastoral twist — that God emptied the curse so Israel could not misread his own chastisements — is his own contribution to the tradition.
