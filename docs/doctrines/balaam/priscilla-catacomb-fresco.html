The Catacomb of Priscilla fresco

Dates: c. 230–240 (a painted-plaster ceiling fresco in the Catacomb of Priscilla, Via Salaria, Rome; dated on style and topography to the early third century, with some placing it late-second/early-third) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian image of Balaam, and — independent of the Adoration of the Magi — widely called the earliest known image of the Virgin and Child. A seated woman nurses an infant while a standing man in a philosopher’s pallium points up to a star. In the traditional and majority identification the pointing figure is Balaam, gesturing to the “Star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) read fulfilled at Bethlehem; the point of the whole scene, before the Council of Ephesus, is christological, not Marian. The identification is real but not unanimous (Isaiah, a magus, and a personified “prophecy” have each been proposed).

Related arguments: the antiquity and messianism of the oracle this fresco depicts is weighed on “How old is the Star out of Jacob?” (sound for the pre-Christian messianic reading), and whether the historical wise men actually inherited it on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion, weak for the transmission).

What it is and where it is

In a burial chamber of the Catacomb of Priscilla — the “queen of the catacombs” on the Via Salaria in Rome — a fresco painted on the plastered ceiling of a niche shows a seated woman with a child at her breast and, beside her, a standing man who lifts his hand toward a star above the pair. It belongs to the pioneering generation of Christian figural art: excavators and art historians date it, on painting style and on its position near the oldest part of the cemetery (the central arenarium), to the first third of the third century — commonly about 230–240, though some press it back toward the end of the second century. After the Adoration of the Magi in the so-called Greek Chapel, it is reckoned the oldest surviving image of the Nativity theme. The most celebrated fresco of the catacomb, it was conserved in a long restoration begun in 1992 by the Pontifical Commission for Sacred Archaeology.1

The pointing figure: the Balaam identification

The oracle in view is Balaam’s fourth: “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17). By the time the fresco was painted, that verse had long been read of the coming of Christ and joined to the star the wise men followed — a reading already voiced by Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 106), pressed by Irenaeus (“a star should arise… to announce the birth of Christ,” Against Heresies 3.9.2), and worked up a little later, on the same timeline as this fresco, by Origen, who made the magi the very heirs of Balaam’s star-prophecy. Against that background the standing prophet who points a nursing mother’s child toward a star is, in the traditional identification, Balaam himself — the pagan seer whose one great oracle the church claimed — drawn beside the prophecy’s fulfilment.

The identity of the third figure is less sure, but it’s most likely the Gentile prophet Balaam, who, in the power of God’s Spirit, prophesied to King Balak of Moab that “a star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel” (Num. 24:17).

— Victoria Emily Jones, “Catacomb of Priscilla,” Art & Theology. This has been the majority reading in the art-historical study of the catacomb, and it is why the fresco is routinely captioned a “Balaam” scene and a “Virgin and Child with Balaam the prophet.”2

The debate: Isaiah, a magus, or “prophecy” itself

The identification is genuinely contested, and the fresco carries no label to settle it. Because the accompanying figure of a prophet with a star could suit more than one Old Testament text, alternatives have been proposed: the prophet Isaiah, who foretold that “a virgin shall conceive” (Isaiah 7:14) and bade Zion “Arise, shine; for thy light is come” (Isaiah 60:1); a single magus, representing the wise men who followed the star; and even John the Baptist. A subtler proposal, associated with the archaeologist Pasquale Testini, reads the figure not as any one named prophet but as a personification of prophecy as such — the pointing gesture standing for the whole prophetic witness to Christ, of which Balaam’s star is the type. What is not in doubt is the scene’s logic: a prophet, a star, and the mother with her child, set together so that the reader sees the ancient prophecy and its fulfilment in one frame.3

Notes

  • Christological, not Marian. The image predates the Council of Ephesus (431) and the flowering of Marian devotion, and scholars caution that its meaning is christological: like the catacomb’s Adoration scenes, it points to Christ — here through the fulfilled star-prophecy — rather than honouring Mary in her own right. That it happens to be, so far as survives, the earliest picture of the Virgin and Child is a by-product of that christological purpose, not its aim.4
  • Where it sits on this timeline. By the age of the evidence (c. 230–240) the fresco is the first Christian witness to Balaam, slightly earlier than Origen’s exegesis and placed just before him. It is an image, not a “sustained reading” by an author, so it does not displace Origen as the first Christian to interpret Balaam at length; the two are near-contemporary, the picture and the commentary, and both turn on the same star.
  • A single image, three threads. The fresco is the one early monument where the Balaam, magi, and Marian traditions converge. Balaam supplies the star-oracle; the magi supply its claimed fulfilment (their star, and the tradition — seeded here and by Origen — that they were Balaam’s heirs); and the nursing mother is the earliest surviving image behind the later Marian doctrines traced on this wiki (Theotokos, Mary the New Eve, the perpetual virginity). It is a christological picture that later ages would read as the first Madonna.
