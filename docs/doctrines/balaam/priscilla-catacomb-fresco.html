The Catacomb of Priscilla fresco

Dates: c. 230–240 (a painted-plaster ceiling fresco in the Catacomb of Priscilla, Via Salaria, Rome; dated on style and topography to the early third century, with some placing it late-second/early-third) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant Christian image identified as Balaam, and — independent of the Adoration of the Magi — called by Stracke and Jones the earliest known image of the Virgin and Child. A seated woman nurses an infant while a standing man in a philosopher’s pallium points up to a star. In the identification Victoria Emily Jones calls “most likely,” and Richard Stracke also gives, the pointing figure is Balaam, gesturing to the “Star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) read fulfilled at Bethlehem; Stracke and Jones read the scene, painted before the Council of Ephesus, as christological. Isaiah, a magus, and a personified “prophecy” have also been proposed for the figure.

Related readings: “How old is the ‘Star out of Jacob,’ and was it read messianically before Christianity?” · on the magi timeline, “Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’?”

What it is and where it is

In a burial chamber of the Catacomb of Priscilla — the “queen of the catacombs” on the Via Salaria in Rome — a fresco painted on the plastered ceiling of a niche shows a seated woman with a child at her breast and, beside her, a standing man who lifts his hand toward a star above the pair. It belongs to an early generation of Christian figural art: excavators and art historians date it, on painting style and on its position near the oldest part of the cemetery (the central arenarium), to the first third of the third century — commonly about 230–240, though some press it back toward the end of the second century. After the Adoration of the Magi in the so-called Greek Chapel, it is reckoned the oldest surviving image of the Nativity theme. The most celebrated fresco of the catacomb, it was conserved in a long restoration begun in 1992 by the Pontifical Commission for Sacred Archaeology.1

The pointing figure: the Balaam identification

The oracle in view is Balaam’s fourth: “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17). By the time the fresco was painted, that verse had long been read of the coming of Christ and joined to the star the wise men followed — a reading voiced by Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 106) and by Irenaeus (“a star should arise… to announce the birth of Christ,” Against Heresies 3.9.2), and, a little later and near-contemporary with this fresco, by Origen, who made the magi heirs of Balaam’s star-prophecy. Against that background the standing figure who points a nursing mother’s child toward a star is, in the traditional identification, Balaam himself — the gentile seer whose oracle Christian writers read of Christ — drawn beside the prophecy’s fulfilment.

The identity of the third figure is less sure, but it’s most likely the Gentile prophet Balaam, who, in the power of God’s Spirit, prophesied to King Balak of Moab that “a star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel” (Num. 24:17).

— Victoria Emily Jones, “Catacomb of Priscilla,” Art & Theology. Stracke gives the same reading, and the fresco is commonly captioned a “Balaam” scene and a “Virgin and Child with Balaam the prophet.”2

The debate: Isaiah, a magus, or “prophecy” itself

The identification is disputed, and the fresco carries no label. Because the accompanying figure of a prophet with a star could suit more than one Old Testament text, alternatives have been proposed: the prophet Isaiah, who foretold that “a virgin shall conceive” (Isaiah 7:14) and bade Zion “Arise, shine; for thy light is come” (Isaiah 60:1); a single magus, representing the wise men who followed the star; and John the Baptist. Another proposal, associated with the archaeologist Pasquale Testini, reads the figure not as any one named prophet but as a personification of prophecy as such — the pointing gesture standing for the whole prophetic witness to Christ, of which Balaam’s star is the type. On each of these identifications the scene sets a prophet or witness, a star, and the mother with her child together, prophecy and fulfilment in one frame.3

Reception and debate

  • The scene’s meaning. The image predates the Council of Ephesus (431). Stracke and Jones read its meaning as christological: like the catacomb’s Adoration scenes, it points to Christ — here through the fulfilled star-prophecy. They also call it, so far as survives, the earliest picture of the Virgin and Child.4
  • Placement on the timeline. By the age of the evidence (c. 230–240) the fresco is the earliest extant Christian image of Balaam on this timeline, slightly earlier than Origen’s exegesis and placed just before him. It is an image, not a “sustained reading” by an author, and Origen remains the earliest extant Christian writer here to interpret Balaam at length; the two are near-contemporary, the picture and the commentary, and both turn on the same star.
  • A single image, three themes. In the fresco the Balaam, magi, and Marian themes meet. Balaam supplies the star-oracle; the magi are linked to its claimed fulfilment (their star, and the tradition stated by Origen that they were Balaam’s heirs); and the nursing mother is the image Stracke and Jones call the earliest known Virgin and Child. Marian teaching is traced on the Theotokos, Mary the New Eve, and perpetual virginity timelines.
