Pseudo-Philo on Balaam

Dates: c. AD 70 (the anonymous first-century Jewish Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum or “Biblical Antiquities,” a retelling of Israel’s history from Adam to Saul, later mis-attributed to Philo) · Wikipedia
Summary: In chapter 18 of the Biblical Antiquities the anonymous author known as Pseudo-Philo retells the Balaam episode with touches found in no other first-century source. His Balaam is a genuine seer who knows in advance that his gift is on loan and that obeying Balak will damn him — and goes anyway: “when I cursed I perished, and though I blessed I was not blessed.” God frames the whole affair as a test of the human will (“Wherefore, Lord, dost thou tempt the race of man?”), grounds Israel’s un-cursability in the merit of Abraham’s binding of Isaac (“for the blood of him did I choose this people”), and permits the journey while stamping it a sin (“Go with them, and thy journey shall be an offence”). Like Philo and Josephus, Pseudo-Philo expands the Torah’s bare “counsel of Balaam” into a full scene of seduction by the women of Midian; unlike Josephus he mutes the speaking donkey to a silent one and, like Josephus, omits the “star out of Jacob” entirely. The result is the most moralised and psychologically interior of the three great first-century Jewish retellings: a tragic prophet who prophesies his own doom.

Where it sits

The Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum (“Book of Biblical Antiquities,” conventionally abbreviated LAB) is an anonymous Jewish paraphrase of the biblical story from creation to the death of Saul, composed in Hebrew and surviving only through a Greek and then a Latin translation. By an accident of manuscript transmission the name of the first-century philosopher Philo of Alexandria became attached to it, so its unknown author is called “Pseudo-Philo” — but the work is not Philo’s, and one of the clearest signs of that is precisely its handling of Balaam, far removed from the Life of Moses. Most scholars date LAB to the first century AD, and many place it right around the destruction of the Temple in AD 70; it thus sits on this timeline between Philo (c. AD 40) and Josephus (c. AD 93), the third of the three great first-century Jewish retellings of the episode.1

Claims & evidence

“The spirit which is given unto us is given for a time”

And Balaam said: Lo, this is good in the sight of Balac, but he knoweth not that the counsel of God is not as man’s counsel. And he knoweth not that the spirit which is given unto us is given for a time, and our ways are not guided except God will. Now therefore abide ye here, and I will see what the Lord will say to me this night.

— Biblical Antiquities 18:3 (M. R. James). From his very first words Pseudo-Philo’s Balaam is set apart from the other retellings by a startling self-awareness. He knows that his prophetic spirit is not his own possession but a loan — “given for a time” — and that no diviner’s craft can steer it: “our ways are not guided except God will.” This is the seed of the tragedy the chapter will unfold: a man who understands exactly the terms on which he holds his gift, and forfeits it anyway. It also fixes the theology that governs the whole episode — the initiative is God’s throughout, and Balaam’s freedom is real only as the freedom to obey or to ruin himself.2

“Wherefore, Lord, dost thou tempt the race of man?” — the test, and the merit of Isaac

And in the night God said unto him: Who are the men that are come unto thee? And Balaam said: Wherefore, Lord, dost thou tempt the race of man? They therefore cannot sustain it, for thou knewest more than they, all that was in the world, before thou foundedst it. And now enlighten thy servant if it be right that I go with them.

And God said to him: Was it not concerning this people that I spake unto Abraham in a vision saying: Thy seed shall be as the stars of heaven, when I raised him up above the firmament and showed him all the orderings of the stars, and required of him his son for a burnt offering? and he brought him to be laid upon the altar, but I restored him to his father. And because he resisted not, his offering was acceptable in my sight, and for the blood of him did I choose this people.

— Biblical Antiquities 18:4–5 (James). Two distinctive touches sit in this exchange. First, Balaam reads God’s question as a test of the human will — “dost thou tempt the race of man?” — and confesses in the same breath that the outcome is foreknown, since God knew “all that was in the world, before thou foundedst it.” The freedom is genuine and the failure is foreseen: Pseudo-Philo holds both together, the “free will vs. God’s permission” problem stated by the pagan seer himself. Second, when God explains why Israel cannot be cursed, he does not appeal to the Sinai covenant or to Israel’s own righteousness but to the merit of Abraham’s binding of Isaac: God required Isaac “for a burnt offering,” Abraham “resisted not,” and “for the blood of him did I choose this people.” This grounding of Israel’s election in the Aqedah is a hallmark of Pseudo-Philo’s theology (the “merits of the fathers”), and it is put, remarkably, in the mouth of God to Balaam — making the pagan diviner the recipient of one of the book’s central revelations.3

God permits the journey — and calls it a sin

And God said to him: Go with them, and thy journey shall be an offence, and Balac himself shall go unto destruction. And he arose and went with them. And his she-ass came by the way of the desert and saw the angel, and he opened the eyes of Balaam and he saw the angel and worshipped him on the earth. And the angel said to him: Haste and go on, for what thou sayest shall come to pass with him.

— Biblical Antiquities 18:8–9 (James). Here the “permission” motif reaches its sharpest form. God grants Balaam leave to go, but in the same sentence brands the journey a sin — “thy journey shall be an offence” — and forecasts Balak’s ruin. Permission is not approval: God allows the free agent his course without endorsing it, and the guilt remains Balaam’s. Note too what Pseudo-Philo does with the famous donkey. Where Josephus retells the speaking-ass miracle of Numbers 22:22–35 at length, Pseudo-Philo mutes it: the she-ass merely sees the angel, God opens Balaam’s own eyes, and the beast never speaks. The interest has shifted entirely from the marvel of the talking animal to the interior drama of a prophet who is told, and knows, that he is doing wrong. (In an earlier exchange Balaam had himself scorned Balak’s bid to bribe God with sacrifices — “the son of Sephor is foolish, and knoweth not that he dwelleth hard by the dead” — only to succumb to the same lure of reward.)4

The tragic oracle: a prophet who curses himself

I am restrained in the speech of my voice and I cannot express that which I see with mine eyes, for but a little is left to me of the holy spirit which abideth in me, since I know that in that I was persuaded of Balac I have lost the days of my life.

But I shall gnash my teeth because I was deceived and did transgress that which was said to me in the night. Yet my prophecy shall remain manifest, and my words shall live, and the wise and prudent shall remember my words, for when I cursed I perished, and though I blessed I was not blessed. And when he had so said he held his peace.

— Biblical Antiquities 18:11–12 (James). This is the passage that makes Pseudo-Philo’s Balaam unique. Even as he pronounces Israel’s un-cursable glory, Balaam narrates his own damnation. He feels the holy spirit draining out of him — “but a little is left to me” — because “I was persuaded of Balac” and so “have lost the days of my life.” He will “gnash my teeth” — the New Testament’s image of the damned — “because I was deceived and did transgress.” And the aphorism that seals it is his own self-verdict: “when I cursed I perished, and though I blessed I was not blessed.” No source before or beside him gives Balaam this tragic interiority: he is a true prophet who sees his own ruin as clearly as Israel’s triumph and cannot avert it. Pseudo-Philo also joins Josephus in a conspicuous omission — there is no “Star out of Jacob,” no sceptre; the oracles are recast as apocalyptic visions of Israel’s “heritage” whose light “shineth above the brightness of lightning.”5

The counsel of Balaam: seduction by the women of Midian

Then Balaam said unto him: Come and let us advise what thou shalt do to them. Choose out the most comely women that are among you and that are in Midian and set them before them naked, and adorned with gold and jewels, and it shall be when they shall see them and lie with them, they will sin against their Lord and fall into your hands, for otherwise thou canst not subdue them. And so saying Balaam turned away and returned to his place. And thereafter the people were led astray after the daughters of Moab, for Balac did all that Balaam had showed him.

— Biblical Antiquities 18:13–14 (James). With Philo and Josephus, Pseudo-Philo unpacks the Torah’s terse retrospective note that Israel sinned “through the counsel of Balaam” (Numbers 31:16) into an explicit scheme: unable to curse Israel, Balaam hands Balak the one strategy that can work — parade the comeliest women of Moab and Midian before the Hebrews, “for otherwise thou canst not subdue them.” The logic is the same as Josephus’s: Israel can only fall by making itself sin and so forfeiting God’s protection. This developed scene — Balaam as the deliberate author of the seduction — is exactly the profile the New Testament crystallises as “the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel… to commit fornication” (Revelation 2:14). That all three first-century Jewish retellings independently supply this scene is strong evidence that the reading was current in Second-Temple Judaism, not a Christian invention.6

Notes

  • The Second-Temple Jewish cluster. Pseudo-Philo is the third member, with Philo and Josephus, of a tight first-century Jewish reception of Balaam. All three convert the Torah’s bare “counsel of Balaam” into a full seduction scene; Philo and Josephus both keep the speaking miracles while Pseudo-Philo mutes the donkey; Josephus and Pseudo-Philo both drop the star oracle where Philo reads it as a coming king. Read together they show that the New Testament’s villain-Balaam — the prophet-for-hire who teaches Israel to sin — was the common Jewish portrait of the age.
  • Free will and permission. Pseudo-Philo’s handling is the most theologically explicit of the three on the problem the episode raises: God tests the human will (“dost thou tempt the race of man?”), foreknows the outcome, and permits a course he condemns (“Go with them, and thy journey shall be an offence”). The permission does not transfer the guilt: Balaam knows he transgressed “that which was said to me in the night” and owns the ruin. It is a compact Second-Temple statement of divine sovereignty coexisting with real human responsibility.
  • The wages motive. The New Testament brands Balaam the man who “loved the wages of unrighteousness” (2 Peter 2:15). Pseudo-Philo has Balaam himself denounce Balak’s attempt to buy a verdict from God — Balak “desired to persuade the Most Mighty with gifts and to purchase decision with money” — only for Balaam to be undone by that same lure of reward, so that Balak can taunt him at the end, “Thy God hath defrauded thee of many gifts from me.” The seer who saw through the bribery of God still sold himself.
  • A “sympathetic” Balaam? Scholars note that Pseudo-Philo’s portrait is markedly more sympathetic — or at least more interior — than Philo’s hostile magos, and cite this among the reasons the work cannot be Philo’s. But “sympathetic” is easily overstated: this Balaam is a genuine seer granted real revelation, yet he is also a self-condemned transgressor who authors Israel’s seduction and knows he is damned. The sympathy is the sympathy of tragedy, not of exoneration.
  • Text and edition. The Latin survives in the manuscripts edited by G. Kisch (Pseudo-Philo’s Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum, Notre Dame, 1949); the English quoted here is Montague Rhodes James’s translation, The Biblical Antiquities of Philo (London: SPCK, 1917), chapter 18, pp. 123–127. A modern critical edition with commentary is H. Jacobson, A Commentary on Pseudo-Philo’s Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum (2 vols., Brill, 1996); the standard English study translation is D. J. Harrington in J. H. Charlesworth (ed.), The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha, vol. 2 (Doubleday, 1985).
