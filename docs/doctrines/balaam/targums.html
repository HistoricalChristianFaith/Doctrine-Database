The Aramaic Targums on Balaam

Dates: c. 2nd–8th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Aramaic synagogue paraphrases of the Torah, whose written redaction spans several centuries — Onkelos the earliest, Pseudo-Jonathan the latest) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Targums — the Aramaic renderings read alongside the Hebrew in the synagogue — do two things to Balaam at once. First, they read the fourth oracle messianically: where the Hebrew of Numbers 24:17 says only “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel,” all three Torah Targums turn the star and sceptre into a king, and two of them (Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan) name him the Messiah (Aramaic Meshiha) — Onkelos “when a king shall arise out of Jacob, and the Messiah be anointed from Israel,” Neofiti “a king… a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel,” and Pseudo-Jonathan expanding it into a full eschatological battle against “the armies of Gog.” Second, in the same paraphrases, Balaam is portrayed as a villain: Pseudo-Jonathan identifies him with Laban the Aramean, Jacob’s old enemy, calls him mad “from the vastness of his knowledge,” and stages his death as a duel of sorceries in which he flies off by magic only to be run down and beheaded by Phinehas for the counsel that killed twenty-four thousand Israelites. So the Targums that read his oracle of the King Messiah portray its speaker as a wicked sorcerer.

Placement on the timeline

A Targum (Aramaic for “translation”) is the running Aramaic rendering of Scripture that grew up in the synagogue once Aramaic had displaced Hebrew as the everyday language of Jewish Palestine and Babylonia; read aloud after the Hebrew, it was never a bare translation but a paraphrase that folded interpretation, expansion, and homily into the text. Three Targums to the Torah bear on Balaam. Onkelos is the authoritative Babylonian Targum — the most restrained and literal, its core reaching back to the second century and its final form fixed in Babylonia by roughly the fifth. Neofiti (Codex Neofiti 1, a complete Palestinian Targum whose lone manuscript was copied at Rome in 1504 and identified only in 1956 in the Vatican Library) is more expansive; its editor Alejandro Díez Macho dated it to the first century as part of a pre-Christian textual tradition, while Martin McNamara places its origin in the fourth century. Pseudo-Jonathan (Targum Yerushalmi I) is the latest and by far the most elaborate: its final redaction is dated to the seventh or eighth century (its text here names Constantinople), but it, too, preserves much ancient haggadah. Because a Targum’s wording may be centuries younger than the tradition it records, this page treats it not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum — the deposit of what the synagogue said about Balaam across the whole span — slotted on the timeline after the first-century retellings of Philo, Pseudo-Philo, and Josephus.1

Claims & evidence

The star oracle read as the King Messiah (Numbers 24:17)

The Hebrew of Balaam’s fourth oracle is imagery, not title: “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth” (Numbers 24:17, KJV). Every Torah Targum reads the star and sceptre as a person — a coming king, and in two of the three the Messiah by name. The most restrained is Onkelos:

I shall see him, but not now; I shall behold him, but he is not near. When a king shall arise out of Jakob, and the Meshiha be anointed from Israel, he will slay the princes of Moab, and reign over all the children of men.

— Targum Onkelos, Numbers 24:17 (Etheridge). In this rendering Onkelos has no star and no sceptre, but “a king” and “the Meshiha” (the Messiah), and where the Hebrew “destroys the children of Sheth” Onkelos has him “reign over all the children of men.” The more literal printed text of Onkelos, which keeps the star-and-sceptre picture in verse 17, renders verse 19 royally: “A ruler shall come from Jacob, who will annihilate the survivors of the city.”2

The Palestinian tradition, represented by Neofiti, is more expansive and adds the note of redemption:

A king shall arise from the house of Jacob, and a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel.

— Targum Neofiti, Numbers 24:17 (McNamara). “Redeemer and ruler” (the same doublet stands in the Fragment Targums of the Palestinian family) makes the figure not only a conquering king but a saviour.3

Pseudo-Jonathan goes furthest, unfolding the verse into an apocalyptic scene:

I shall see Him, but not now; I shall behold Him, but it is not near. When the mighty King of Jakob’s house shall reign, and the Meshiha, the Power-sceptre of Israel, be anointed, He will slay the princes of the Moabaee, and bring to nothing all the children of Sheth, the armies of Gog who will do battle against Israel, and all their carcases shall fall before Him.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:17 (Etheridge). Here the “Meshiha” is explicit, the sceptre becomes the “Power-sceptre of Israel,” and Balaam’s oracle is drawn into the end-time war of Gog and Magog: the King Messiah annihilates the eschatological enemy. All three Targums, Babylonian and Palestinian alike, read the oracle of a king or Messiah. The verse is also read messianically at Qumran (the Testimonia, 4Q175, and the Damascus Document, CD 7:18–21) and in Rabbi Akiva’s hailing of Simon “Bar Kokhba” — son of the star — on this very verse in the revolt of AD 132–135. Whether Matthew’s wise men were cast as Balaam’s heirs is a disputed reading on the magi timeline: “Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’?”4

Pseudo-Jonathan’s late horizon (Numbers 24:19)

And a prince of the house of Jakob will arise, and destroy and consume the remnant that have escaped from Constantina the guilty city, and will lay waste and ruin the rebellious city, even Kaiserin the strong city of the Gentiles.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:19 (Etheridge). This continuation bears on the dating of the layer. The Messiah’s enemies here are named “Constantina” (Constantinople) and “Kaiserin” (Caesarea / the imperial city) — cities of the Roman and Byzantine world. Such references are among the markers used to place Pseudo-Jonathan’s final redaction in the Byzantine or early-Islamic period. The messianic reading of the star oracle is attested at Qumran and in the Bar Kokhba acclamation; the particular details of a given Targumic expansion may be centuries younger than the tradition it carries.5

Balaam as Laban the Aramean and the sorcerer “insane from the vastness of his knowledge”

The Targums that read Balaam’s oracle messianically portray its speaker as an enemy. Pseudo-Jonathan opens the episode by identifying Balaam with Laban the Aramean, who in Genesis had cheated and pursued Jacob:

And he sent unto Laban the Aramite, who was Bileam — so called because he it was who sought to swallow up the people of the house of Israel — the son of Beor, who was insane from the vastness of his knowledge; and would not spare Israel, the descendants of his sons and daughters.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 22:5 (Etheridge). Three moves are packed into one verse. Balaam is Laban — an identification also found in the Talmud — so his hostility to Israel is ancestral; his name is etymologized as the man who “sought to swallow up” (Hebrew bala‘) the people; and his great learning is turned against him — he is “insane from the vastness of his knowledge.” In the Targum’s opening line he is a hereditary enemy of Israel.6

“The counsel of Balaam” and his death at the hands of Phinehas

Like Philo, Pseudo-Philo, and Josephus, the Targum makes Balaam the deliberate author of the seduction at Peor. As he leaves, Pseudo-Jonathan has Balak act on his advice:

And Bileam rose up and went to return to his place, and Balak also went upon his way, and appointed the daughters of the Midianites for the tavern booths at Beth Jeshimoth…

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:25 (Etheridge). The “tavern booths” staffed by Midianite women are the machinery of the seduction the Torah lays at Balaam’s door — “these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor” (Numbers 31:16). The reckoning comes at his death, which Pseudo-Jonathan stages as a contest of powers. Caught among the slain kings of Midian (Numbers 31:8), Balaam

made use of his magical arts, and flew in the air of the heavens.

Phinehas the priest “pronounced the Great and Holy Name,” flew after him, seized him by the head, and — rejecting Balaam’s plea for his life — recited his crimes: that he had sought to destroy Jacob, sent Amalek against Israel, and counselled Balak “to set his daughters in the way to make them go wrong, when there fell of them twenty-four thousand.” Then he “drew the sword and slew him.” The bare notice of Joshua — that Israel killed “Balaam… the soothsayer” with the sword (Joshua 13:22) — has become a duel of sorcery against holiness, in which the diviner’s flight by magic is overtaken by the priest’s invocation of the Name.7

Reception and debate

  • The two Targum families. Onkelos is the Babylonian Targum; Neofiti, the Fragment Targums, and Pseudo-Jonathan belong to the Palestinian family. The royal reading of the star oracle appears in both — the restrained Onkelos and the expansive Palestinian Targums read the star as a king or the Messiah. The villain-portrait, by contrast, is carried furthest by Pseudo-Jonathan.
  • How old is the messianism? The date of the Targumic redactions is disputed: Neofiti’s editor Alejandro Díez Macho argued for a first-century date within a pre-Christian textual tradition; Martin McNamara places its origin in the fourth century. The messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 is also attested at Qumran (4Q175; CD 7:18–21) and in the Bar Kokhba (“son of the star”) acclamation of AD 132–135.
  • Bearing on the magi. Whether Matthew’s “star” and Eastern seers allude to Numbers 24:17, read as the Targums read it, is a disputed reading on the magi timeline: “Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’?”
  • The seduction counsel in Jewish retellings. Like the three first-century retellings, the Targum develops the Torah’s brief “counsel of Balaam” into an explicit scheme of seduction — here the “tavern booths” of Midianite women — the profile Revelation calls “the doctrine of Balaam” (Revelation 2:14).
  • Text and editions. Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan are quoted here in J. W. Etheridge’s translation (The Targums of Onkelos and Jonathan Ben Uzziel on the Pentateuch, London, 1862–65); Neofiti in Martin McNamara’s (Targum Neofiti 1: Numbers, The Aramaic Bible 4, Collegeville, 1995). A survey of the Balaam material across the Targums and Second-Temple sources is G. Vermes, “The Story of Balaam,” in Scripture and Tradition in Judaism (Leiden: Brill, 1961), 127–177.
