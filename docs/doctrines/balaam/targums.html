The Aramaic Targums on Balaam

Dates: c. 2nd–8th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Aramaic synagogue paraphrases of the Torah, whose written redaction spans several centuries — Onkelos the earliest, Pseudo-Jonathan the latest — while the traditions they carry are older still) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Targums — the Aramaic renderings read alongside the Hebrew in the synagogue — do two things to Balaam at once, and both are load-bearing for his later reputation. First, they read the fourth oracle messianically: where the Hebrew of Numbers 24:17 says only “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel,” all three Torah Targums turn the star and sceptre into a king and, explicitly, the Messiah (Aramaic Meshiha) — Onkelos “when a king shall arise out of Jacob, and the Messiah be anointed from Israel,” Neofiti “a king… a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel,” and Pseudo-Jonathan expanding it into a full eschatological battle against “the armies of Gog.” Second, and in the same breath, they harden Balaam into a villain: Pseudo-Jonathan identifies him with Laban the Aramean, Jacob’s old enemy, calls him mad “from the vastness of his knowledge,” and stages his death as a duel of sorceries in which he flies off by magic only to be run down and beheaded by Phinehas for the counsel that killed twenty-four thousand Israelites. So the same tradition that made his oracle the standard proof-text of the King Messiah made its speaker the archetypal wicked sorcerer — the two faces the whole Balaam tradition holds in tension, pulled here to their extremes.

Where it sits

A Targum (Aramaic for “translation”) is the running Aramaic rendering of Scripture that grew up in the synagogue once Aramaic had displaced Hebrew as the everyday language of Jewish Palestine and Babylonia; read aloud after the Hebrew, it was never a bare translation but a paraphrase that folded interpretation, expansion, and homily into the text. Three Targums to the Torah bear on Balaam. Onkelos is the authoritative Babylonian Targum — the most restrained and literal, its core reaching back to the second century and its final form fixed in Babylonia by roughly the fifth. Neofiti (Codex Neofiti 1, a complete Palestinian Targum whose lone manuscript was copied at Rome in 1504 and identified only in 1956 in the Vatican Library) is more expansive; its traditions are old — some argue pre-Christian — though its redaction is usually placed later. Pseudo-Jonathan (Targum Yerushalmi I) is the latest and by far the most elaborate: its final redaction is dated to the seventh or eighth century (it betrays a post-Islamic horizon elsewhere, and here names Constantinople), but it, too, preserves much ancient haggadah. Because a Targum’s wording may be centuries younger than the tradition it records, this page is best read not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum — the deposit of what the synagogue said about Balaam across the whole span — slotted on the timeline after the first-century retellings of Philo, Pseudo-Philo, and Josephus.1

Claims & evidence

The star oracle read as the King Messiah (Numbers 24:17)

The Hebrew of Balaam’s fourth oracle is imagery, not title: “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth” (Numbers 24:17, KJV). Every Torah Targum reads the star and sceptre as a person — a coming king, and in two of the three the Messiah by name. The most restrained is Onkelos:

I shall see him, but not now; I shall behold him, but he is not near. When a king shall arise out of Jakob, and the Meshiha be anointed from Israel, he will slay the princes of Moab, and reign over all the children of men.

— Targum Onkelos, Numbers 24:17 (Etheridge). Onkelos removes the metaphor outright: no star, no sceptre, but “a king” and “the Meshiha” (the Messiah), and where the Hebrew “destroys the children of Sheth” Onkelos has him “reign over all the children of men.” Even the more literal printed text of Onkelos, which keeps the star-and-sceptre picture in verse 17, makes the royal sense unmistakable two verses on: “A ruler shall come from Jacob, who will annihilate the survivors of the city.”2

The Palestinian tradition, represented by Neofiti, is more expansive and adds the note of redemption:

A king shall arise from the house of Jacob, and a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel.

— Targum Neofiti, Numbers 24:17 (McNamara). “Redeemer and ruler” (the same doublet stands in the Fragment Targums of the Palestinian family) makes the figure not merely a conquering king but a saviour — the messianic reading in its fullest synagogue form.3

Pseudo-Jonathan goes furthest, unfolding the verse into an apocalyptic scene:

I shall see Him, but not now; I shall behold Him, but it is not near. When the mighty King of Jakob’s house shall reign, and the Meshiha, the Power-sceptre of Israel, be anointed, He will slay the princes of the Moabaee, and bring to nothing all the children of Sheth, the armies of Gog who will do battle against Israel, and all their carcases shall fall before Him.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:17 (Etheridge). Here the “Meshiha” is explicit, the sceptre becomes the “Power-sceptre of Israel,” and Balaam’s oracle is drawn into the end-time war of Gog and Magog: the King Messiah annihilates the eschatological enemy. That all three Targums — Babylonian and Palestinian alike — read the oracle of a king or Messiah is strong evidence that this was the standard synagogue understanding of the verse, not a fringe or Christian one. It converges with the same messianic reading at Qumran (the Testimonia, 4Q175, and the Damascus Document, CD 7:18–21) and with Rabbi Akiva’s hailing of Simon “Bar Kokhba” — son of the star — on this very verse in the revolt of AD 132–135. This is the reception that stands behind the wiki’s argument on whether the wise men were cast as Balaam’s heirs of the “star out of Jacob.”4

Pseudo-Jonathan’s late horizon (Numbers 24:19)

And a prince of the house of Jakob will arise, and destroy and consume the remnant that have escaped from Constantina the guilty city, and will lay waste and ruin the rebellious city, even Kaiserin the strong city of the Gentiles.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:19 (Etheridge). This continuation is worth flagging on its own, because it dates the layer. The Messiah’s enemies here are named “Constantina” (Constantinople) and “Kaiserin” (Caesarea / the imperial city) — Byzantine capitals that did not exist when Balaam spoke or when the oracle was written. An anachronism of this kind is one of the markers scholars use to place Pseudo-Jonathan’s final redaction in the Byzantine or early-Islamic period, and it is a useful caution for the whole page: the messianism of the star oracle is old (it is already in Onkelos, at Qumran, and in the Bar Kokhba acclamation), but the particular furniture of a given Targumic expansion can be centuries younger than the tradition it carries.5

Balaam hardened into a villain: Laban the Aramean, the mad sorcerer

The same Targums that exalt Balaam’s oracle blacken its speaker. Pseudo-Jonathan opens the episode by identifying Balaam with a figure the reader already hates — Laban the Aramean, who had cheated and pursued Jacob:

And he sent unto Laban the Aramite, who was Bileam — so called because he it was who sought to swallow up the people of the house of Israel — the son of Beor, who was insane from the vastness of his knowledge; and would not spare Israel, the descendants of his sons and daughters.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 22:5 (Etheridge). Three moves are packed into one verse. Balaam is Laban — the same shared rabbinic identification found in the Talmud — so his hostility to Israel is ancestral, not incidental; his name is etymologized as the man who “sought to swallow up” (Hebrew bala‘) the people; and his great learning is turned against him — he is “insane from the vastness of his knowledge,” the gifted seer ruined by his own gift. The reluctant instrument of blessing in Numbers has become, in the Targum’s opening line, a hereditary and half-mad enemy of God’s people.6

“The counsel of Balaam” and his death at the hands of Phinehas

Like Philo, Pseudo-Philo, and Josephus, the Targum makes Balaam the deliberate author of the seduction at Peor. As he leaves, Pseudo-Jonathan has Balak act on his advice:

And Bileam rose up and went to return to his place, and Balak also went upon his way, and appointed the daughters of the Midianites for the tavern booths at Beth Jeshimoth…

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 24:25 (Etheridge). The “tavern booths” staffed by Midianite women are the machinery of the seduction the Torah lays at Balaam’s door — “these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor” (Numbers 31:16). The reckoning comes at his death, which Pseudo-Jonathan stages as a contest of powers. Caught among the slain kings of Midian (Numbers 31:8), Balaam

made use of his magical arts, and flew in the air of the heavens.

Phinehas the priest “pronounced the Great and Holy Name,” flew after him, seized him by the head, and — rejecting Balaam’s plea for his life — recited his crimes: that he had sought to destroy Jacob, sent Amalek against Israel, and counselled Balak “to set his daughters in the way to make them go wrong, when there fell of them twenty-four thousand.” Then he “drew the sword and slew him.” The bare notice of Joshua — that Israel killed “Balaam… the soothsayer” with the sword (Joshua 13:22) — has become a duel of sorcery against holiness, in which the diviner’s flight by magic is overtaken by the priest’s invocation of the Name.7

Notes

  • Two Targum families, one verdict. Onkelos is the Babylonian Targum; Neofiti, the Fragment Targums, and Pseudo-Jonathan belong to the Palestinian family. It is significant that the messianic reading of the star oracle spans both traditions — the restrained Onkelos and the expansive Palestinian Targums agree that the star is a king or the Messiah — which tells strongly against treating it as a late or localized gloss. The villain-portrait, by contrast, is carried furthest by Pseudo-Jonathan.
  • How old is the messianism? Scholars divide on the date of the Targumic redactions (Neofiti’s editor Alejandro Díez Macho argued for pre-Christian traditions underlying it; Martin McNamara placed its core nearer the fourth century), but the messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 itself is demonstrably early: it is already at Qumran (4Q175; CD 7:18–21) and drives the Bar Kokhba (“son of the star”) acclamation of AD 132–135. The Targums are best cited as evidence that this reading was standard, not as the point of its origin.
  • Bearing on the magi. Because the synagogue read Balaam’s star as the King Messiah, a first-century Jew — and an Evangelist — encountering a “star” and Eastern seers would naturally hear Numbers 24:17 behind the scene. That is the milieu on which the magi argument turns; the Targums supply the firsthand primary evidence for it, weighed on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion, weak for the historical transmission).
  • The seduction counsel is not a Christian invention. The Targum joins the three first-century retellings in developing the Torah’s bare “counsel of Balaam” into an explicit scheme of seduction — here the “tavern booths” of Midianite women — independent evidence that the profile the New Testament calls “the doctrine of Balaam” (Revelation 2:14) was the common Jewish reading, not read back from the epistles.
  • Text and editions. Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan are quoted here in J. W. Etheridge’s translation (The Targums of Onkelos and Jonathan Ben Uzziel on the Pentateuch, London, 1862–65); Neofiti in Martin McNamara’s (Targum Neofiti 1: Numbers, The Aramaic Bible 4, Collegeville, 1995). The standard survey of the Balaam material across the Targums and Second-Temple sources is G. Vermes, “The Story of Balaam,” in Scripture and Tradition in Judaism (Leiden: Brill, 1961), 127–177.
