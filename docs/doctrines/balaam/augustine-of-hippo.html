Augustine of Hippo on Balaam — a reprobate compelled by the Spirit

Dates: c. 419 (Augustine’s only sustained treatment of Balaam is in the Questions on the Heptateuch, Book IV, on Numbers — a set of exegetical notes composed late in his career); Augustine b. 354 at Thagaste, bishop of Hippo Regius 395/6, d. 430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine is the great Latin doctor of the tradition, but he handles Balaam only once at length, in the terse Questions on the Heptateuch — and there he stakes out the position exactly opposite Origen’s on the tradition’s hardest verse. Where Origen had struck the clause “the Spirit of God came upon him” (Numbers 24:2) rather than lodge God’s Spirit in a diviner, Augustine keeps it and explains its grammar: the Spirit was not made but “came to be over” Balaam. His Balaam is a man truly compelled by the Spirit to prophesy what he did not wish, who nevertheless “remained a reprobate” who “loved the wages of iniquity” (2 Peter 2:15); whose real sin was to go back and consult God a second time, his greed showing through; whose talking donkey is a literal miracle worked to check his madness; and whose “malign counsel” engineered the seduction at Peor. Unlike the earlier witnesses on this timeline, Augustine passes over the “star out of Jacob” entirely.

On the sources. Augustine’s Balaam material survives in Latin only; it is quoted below verbatim from the Patrologia Latina text, with the sense given in English in the surrounding prose (the standard modern English translation, Lienhard and Doyle’s Questions on the Heptateuch, is in copyright and not freely online, so the renderings here are working translations, not that published version).

Claims & evidence

“The Spirit of God came upon him” — kept, not struck (Question 51, on Numbers 24:2)

The tradition’s sharpest textual crux is the notice that, as Balaam looked out over Israel, “the Spirit of God came upon him” (Numbers 24:2 LXX). Origen had athetized it — marked it with an obelus as absent from the other columns of his Hexapla — unwilling to say God’s Spirit rested on a diviner. Augustine takes the opposite path: he leaves the text standing and dissolves the difficulty grammatically, insisting that “came to be” (factus est) does not mean the Spirit was made or created, only that it came to be over Balaam:

Et factus est Spiritus Dei super illum; id est, super Balaam. Non factus est Spiritus Dei, tamquam factura sit Spiritus Dei; sed factus est super illum, id est, factum est ut super illum esset.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV (on Numbers), q. 51.1 That is: “And the Spirit of God came to be upon him — that is, upon Balaam. The Spirit of God was not made, as though the Spirit of God were a created thing; but it came to be upon him, that is, it came about that it was over him.” Augustine buttresses the reading with parallels where Scripture uses “was made” of the eternal — “He who comes after me was made before me” (John 1:15), “the Lord was made my helper” — each meaning not a coming-into-being but a coming-to-stand-in-a-relation. So the very clause Origen deleted to keep the Spirit clear of a diviner, Augustine retains and reads straight: God’s Spirit genuinely came upon Balaam, without thereby being a made thing or making Balaam holy.

Compelled by the Spirit, yet a reprobate: where Balaam really sinned (Question 48)

Augustine’s controlling theology of Balaam is that a true oracle and a lost soul are not in tension. Balaam’s answer to Balak’s envoys — that not for a house full of silver and gold could he transgress the word of the Lord — was, Augustine says, no sin at all; his sin lay in what followed, going back to consult God a second time when he already knew God’s will, so that his captured, gift-conquered greed showed through and God “permitted him to go” in order to check his avarice by the very donkey he rode. The load-bearing conclusion:

Nam omnino permissus non est dicere quod volebat, sed quod virtute Spiritus cogebatur. Et ipse quidem reprobus mansit: nam posterior de illo sancta Scriptura ita locuta est, ut quidam reprehensibiles et reprobi viam eius secuti dicerentur: Secuti, inquit, viam Balaam filii Beor, qui mercedem iniquitatis dilexit.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 48 (“In quo Balaam peccavit”).2 In sense: “For he was by no means permitted to say what he wished, but only what he was compelled to by the power of the Spirit. And he himself indeed remained a reprobate; for later holy Scripture spoke of him in such a way that certain blameworthy and reprobate men were said to have followed his way — ‘they have followed,’ it says, ‘the way of Balaam son of Beor, who loved the wages of iniquity’” (2 Peter 2:15). This is Augustine’s answer to the puzzle the whole tradition circles: the prophecy is real and God’s, squeezed out of Balaam by compulsion; the man is damned all the same. It converges on Origen’s verdict — a true word forced through an unworthy vessel — while declining Origen’s further step of denying that the Spirit touched him at all.

The talking donkey: a literal miracle, no soul given to the beast (Question 50)

Augustine reads the speaking ass (Numbers 22:22–33) as a plain miracle, and is careful about its mechanism: God did not confer a rational soul on the animal but made a voice sound from it to restrain Balaam’s madness — and he pauses to marvel, as Origen did over the same beast, that Balaam was so consumed by greed he was not even terrified by the wonder but answered “as if speaking to a man.” The miracle, Augustine adds, prefigures God’s way of working:

… ad illius vesaniam cohibendam: illud fortasse praefigurans, quia stulta mundi electurus erat Deus, ut confunderet sapientes.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 50.3 — the voice made to sound from the ass “to restrain his madness, prefiguring perhaps that God would choose the foolish things of the world to confound the wise” (1 Corinthians 1:27). The beast that rebukes the seer becomes an emblem of the divine preference for the lowly instrument — the same instinct by which God speaks a true word past the diviner himself.

The “malign counsel” and the seduction at Peor (Question 63, on Numbers 31:16)

On the dark half of the tradition — the “counsel of Balaam” that Revelation calls “the doctrine of Balaam” — Augustine glosses Moses’ charge that the Midianite women were kept alive though it was they who, “according to the word of Balaam,” had drawn Israel into the apostasy of Peor:

Haec enim fuerunt filiis Israel, secundum verbum Balaam, ut recedere facerent et despicere verbum Domini propter Phogor. Hoc consilium malignum …

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 63 (on Numbers 31:16).4 Augustine unpacks the “malign counsel” as the plan to set the women out as bait so that Israel “might fornicate not only bodily but also spiritually, in worshipping the idol” — the two-fold fornication, of the body and of the soul, being the whole point. He notes that Scripture never records when Balaam gave this counsel, “yet it is evident it happened, since it is recalled here.” So Augustine, like Philo and Josephus before him, reads the bare notice of Numbers 31:16 as the deliberate villainy the New Testament brands “the doctrine of Balaam.”

Notes

  • The exact counter to Origen on Numbers 24:2. The single most pointed thing about Augustine’s Balaam is the contrast with Origen over “the Spirit of God came upon him.” Origen the textual critic deleted the clause; Augustine the grammarian kept it and neutralized it by construing factus est. The two Fathers agree on the substance — a genuine prophecy issuing from a man who is not thereby made holy — but disagree on whether the words “Spirit of God” may stand in the text at all. It is a clean illustration of two patristic methods meeting the same problem: emend the text, or re-read it.
  • The silence on the star. Every earlier witness on this timeline — Philo, the Targums, and above all Origen and the magi tradition — makes the “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) the heart of the Christian interest in Balaam. Augustine, in the one place he treats Balaam at length, passes it over: his Questions move straight from the Spirit-clause of 24:2 to the vows and wars of the following chapters, with no question on the messianic oracle. The contrast with the Latin contemporary just before him on this timeline is stark — Ambrose had made the star and the magi-as-Balaam’s-heirs a centrepiece; Augustine, in this work, is silent on it.
  • Balaam appears nowhere else in Augustine’s major works. For so central a Father, the treatment is strikingly confined: Balaam is not named in the Reply to Faustus (where Augustine’s example of the wicked man who prophesies is Caiaphas, not Balaam), nor in the City of God (whose gentile witnesses to Christ are the Sibyl and Job), nor in the surviving sermons or the Enarrations on the Psalms. Augustine’s Balaam is the Questions on the Heptateuch Balaam — an exegete’s notes, not a preacher’s type.
