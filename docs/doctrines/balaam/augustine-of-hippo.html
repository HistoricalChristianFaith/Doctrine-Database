Augustine of Hippo on Balaam — a reprobate compelled by the Spirit

Dates: c. 419 (Augustine’s only sustained treatment of Balaam is in the Questions on the Heptateuch, Book IV, on Numbers — a set of exegetical notes composed late in his career); Augustine b. 354 at Thagaste, bishop of Hippo Regius 395/6, d. 430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, handles Balaam at length only once, in the terse Questions on the Heptateuch — and there, on the clause “the Spirit of God came upon him” (Numbers 24:2), he explains its grammar: the Spirit was not made but “came to be over” Balaam. His Balaam is a man compelled by the Spirit to prophesy what he did not wish, who nevertheless “remained a reprobate” who “loved the wages of iniquity” (2 Peter 2:15); whose sin, as Augustine reads it, was to go back and consult God a second time, his greed showing through; whose talking donkey is a literal miracle worked to check his madness; and whose “malign counsel” engineered the seduction at Peor. Like Josephus and Pseudo-Philo, and unlike Ambrose, Augustine passes over the “star out of Jacob” in the Questions.

On the sources. Augustine’s Balaam material survives in Latin only; it is quoted below verbatim from the Patrologia Latina text, with the sense given in English in the surrounding prose (the standard modern English translation, Lienhard and Doyle’s Questions on the Heptateuch, is in copyright and not freely online, so the renderings here are working translations, not that published version).

Claims & evidence

“The Spirit of God came upon him” — not made, but come upon him (Question 51, on Numbers 24:2)

As Balaam looked out over Israel, “the Spirit of God came upon him” (Numbers 24:2). Augustine explains the clause grammatically: on his reading “came to be” (factus est) does not mean the Spirit was made or created, only that it came to be over Balaam:

Et factus est Spiritus Dei super illum; id est, super Balaam. Non factus est Spiritus Dei, tamquam factura sit Spiritus Dei; sed factus est super illum, id est, factum est ut super illum esset.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV (on Numbers), q. 51.1 That is: “And the Spirit of God came to be upon him — that is, upon Balaam. The Spirit of God was not made, as though the Spirit of God were a created thing; but it came to be upon him, that is, it came about that it was over him.” Augustine buttresses the reading with parallels where Scripture uses “was made” of the eternal — “He who comes after me was made before me” (John 1:15), “the Lord was made my helper” — each meaning not a coming-into-being but a coming-to-stand-in-a-relation. On his reading God’s Spirit came upon Balaam, without thereby being a made thing or making Balaam holy.

Compelled by the Spirit, yet a reprobate: where Balaam sinned, on Augustine’s reading (Question 48)

Augustine holds that a true oracle and a lost soul are not in tension. Balaam’s answer to Balak’s envoys — that not for a house full of silver and gold could he transgress the word of the Lord — was, Augustine says, no sin at all; his sin lay in what followed, going back to consult God a second time when he already knew God’s will, so that his captured, gift-conquered greed showed through and God “permitted him to go” in order to check his avarice by the very donkey he rode. He concludes:

Nam omnino permissus non est dicere quod volebat, sed quod virtute Spiritus cogebatur. Et ipse quidem reprobus mansit: nam posterior de illo sancta Scriptura ita locuta est, ut quidam reprehensibiles et reprobi viam eius secuti dicerentur: Secuti, inquit, viam Balaam filii Beor, qui mercedem iniquitatis dilexit.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 48 (“In quo Balaam peccavit”).2 In sense: “For he was by no means permitted to say what he wished, but only what he was compelled to by the power of the Spirit. And he himself indeed remained a reprobate; for later holy Scripture spoke of him in such a way that certain blameworthy and reprobate men were said to have followed his way — ‘they have followed,’ it says, ‘the way of Balaam son of Beor, who loved the wages of iniquity’” (2 Peter 2:15). On Augustine’s reading the prophecy is God’s, drawn from Balaam by compulsion, and the man remains a reprobate. Origen had also read Balaam as a true word spoken through an unworthy vessel.

The talking donkey: a literal miracle, no soul given to the beast (Question 50)

Augustine reads the speaking ass (Numbers 22:22–33) as a miracle, and is careful about its mechanism: God did not confer a rational soul on the animal but made a voice sound from it to restrain Balaam’s madness — and he pauses to marvel, as Origen did over the same beast, that Balaam was so consumed by greed he was not even terrified by the wonder but answered “as if speaking to a man.” The miracle, Augustine adds, prefigures God’s way of working:

… ad illius vesaniam cohibendam: illud fortasse praefigurans, quia stulta mundi electurus erat Deus, ut confunderet sapientes.

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 50.3 — the voice made to sound from the ass “to restrain his madness, prefiguring perhaps that God would choose the foolish things of the world to confound the wise” (1 Corinthians 1:27). In Augustine’s reading the beast that rebukes the seer is an emblem of God’s choosing the lowly instrument.

The “malign counsel” and the seduction at Peor (Question 63, on Numbers 31:16)

On the other half of the tradition — the “counsel of Balaam” that Revelation calls “the doctrine of Balaam” — Augustine glosses Moses’ charge that the Midianite women were kept alive though it was they who, “according to the word of Balaam,” had drawn Israel into the apostasy of Peor:

Haec enim fuerunt filiis Israel, secundum verbum Balaam, ut recedere facerent et despicere verbum Domini propter Phogor. Hoc consilium malignum …

— Augustine, Questions on the Heptateuch IV, q. 63 (on Numbers 31:16).4 Augustine unpacks the “malign counsel” as the plan to set the women out as bait so that Israel “might fornicate not only bodily but also spiritually, in worshipping the idol” — the two-fold fornication, of the body and of the soul. He notes that Scripture never records when Balaam gave this counsel, “yet it is evident it happened, since it is recalled here.” Augustine, like Philo and Josephus before him, reads the notice of Numbers 31:16 as a deliberate plan, the one Revelation 2:14 calls “the doctrine of Balaam.”

Reception and debate

  • Augustine and Origen on “the Spirit of God came upon him.” Origen obelized these words where the Septuagint has them in Numbers 23 (placed in his Commentary on John 28 between Numbers 23:5 and 23:16; they end Numbers 23:6 in Brenton’s translation of the Septuagint), not finding them “in the remaining versions”; Augustine construes factus est in the same words at Numbers 24:2. Both read the prophecy as God’s, issuing from a man who is not thereby made holy.
  • The silence on the star. Earlier on this timeline Philo, the Targums, and Origen and the magi tradition give the “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) a central place. Augustine, in the one place he treats Balaam at length, passes it over: his Questions move straight from the Spirit-clause of 24:2 to the vows and wars of the following chapters, with no question on the messianic oracle. Ambrose, the Latin contemporary just before him on this timeline, had made the star and the magi-as-Balaam’s-heirs a centrepiece; Augustine, in this work, is silent on it.
  • Balaam elsewhere in Augustine’s works. Among the works surveyed here the treatment is confined to this one: Balaam is not named in the Reply to Faustus (where Augustine’s example of the wicked man who prophesies is Caiaphas, not Balaam), nor in the City of God (whose gentile witnesses to Christ are the Sibyl and Job), nor in the surviving sermons or the Enarrations on the Psalms. Augustine’s Balaam is the Questions on the Heptateuch Balaam — an exegete’s notes, not a preacher’s type.
