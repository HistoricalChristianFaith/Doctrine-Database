The Deir ‘Alla plaster inscription

Dates: c. 840–760 BC (a plaster wall-text; excavated 1967 at Tell Deir ‘Alla, Jordan) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable extra-biblical source to name a Balaam figure. Written in ink on the plastered wall of a building in the eastern Jordan Valley, it opens as “the book of Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods,” and recounts a night-vision in which a divine council of the Shaddayin resolves to darken the heavens. It shares the name, patronymic, calling, region, and even the divine title Shadday with the seer of Numbers 22–24 — but tells a different story and paints a frankly pagan seer, so it attests a regional Balaam tradition without confirming (or denying) the biblical narrative.

What it is and how it was found

In 1967 a Dutch expedition under Henk J. Franken, digging at Tell Deir ‘Alla on the east bank of the Jordan near the mouth of the Jabbok, found fragments of a text written in black and red ink on white plaster, fallen to the floor of a room whose walls it had once covered. A professional scribe had lettered it; an earthquake and fire brought the building down and shattered it. The pieces were reassembled into two groups — the better-preserved Combination I and the fragmentary Combination II — and dated by the destruction layer to about 800 BC (the late ninth to early eighth century), a horizon some have tied to the earthquake of Amos 1:1.1 The language is famously hard to place — a dialect “lying between Aramaic and Canaanite/Hebrew,” carrying Aramaic features alongside Hebrew and Canaanite ones — and the reconstruction of the broken lines is contested at many points; but the reading of the seer’s name is not in dispute.2

Claims & evidence

It names “Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods”

The misfortunes of the book of Balaam s[on of Beo]r. A divine seer was he.

— Deir ‘Alla, Combination I, opening line (Baruch A. Levine’s translation), “Balaam the Seer: From the Bible to the Deir ‘Alla Inscription,” TheTorah.com. The name and patronymic — bl‘m br b‘r, “Balaam son of Beor” — are exactly those of the seer of Numbers 22–24, and the figure’s calling (a visionary who receives oracles and reports them) and his Transjordanian setting match the biblical episode, which sets Balaam’s summons in the same region. Balaam is named again further down the text, so the identification does not hang on a single broken line.3

A night-vision: the divine council resolves to darken the heavens

The gods came to him at night / And he beheld in a vision in accordance with El’s utterance.

— Deir ‘Alla, Combination I (Levine), TheTorah.com. In the morning Balaam rises weeping and fasting, and when his people ask why, he discloses what he has seen: the Elohin gathered and the Shaddayin — a plurality of “Shadday-gods” — sat in assembly and commanded the sun to “sew up” and darken the sky, decreeing gloom and terror over the land. It is a scene of cosmic reversal handed down by a divine council under the head-god El, delivered through a distraught seer — a plot with no counterpart in the Balak-and-the-donkey narrative of Numbers.4

The shared divine title Shadday

The council of the Shaddayin is the inscription’s most striking point of contact with the Bible. The Deir ‘Alla text knows Shadday as a class of gods (plural, a type of divine being), whereas Israel’s Scriptures fuse the title into a single God; but the very word stands in Balaam’s own biblical oracles, where he is “he… which saw the vision of the Almighty” — the Hebrew behind “the Almighty” being Shadday (Numbers 24:4, 16). That the biblical Balaam receives “the vision of Shadday” and the epigraphic Balaam sees the council of the Shaddayin is a real shared thread of diction, and one of the reasons scholars connect the two figures to a common Transjordanian prophetic tradition rather than dismissing the overlap as coincidence.5

Notes

  • What it does and does not show. The inscription is an external, independently-dated attestation that a seer named Balaam son of Beor — and a body of story about him — was known in Iron-Age Transjordan; that much is plausible-to-sound. It does not preserve an earlier version of the Numbers episode (the events differ entirely), and its Balaam is a pagan seer of a polytheistic council, not the reluctant instrument of YHWH. Whether it is the same Balaam, and whether it corroborates anything about Israel’s conquest, are separate questions weighed on their own argument pages — see the conquest timeline’s “does the Deir ‘Alla Balaam corroborate the conquest?” argument (weak as conquest-proof; plausible-to-sound as attestation of the figure).
  • Where it sits on this timeline. By the age of the evidence (c. 800 BC) this is the earliest non-biblical witness to Balaam, and it is placed here as the first extra-biblical block after the scriptural baseline — the Torah portrait it may reflect being the canonical starting point already in view, not a source it postdates in composition.
  • Editions. The editio princeps is J. Hoftijzer & G. van der Kooij, Aramaic Texts from Deir ‘Alla (Brill, 1976); influential later treatments and translations include those of Jo Ann Hackett, P. Kyle McCarter, and Baruch A. Levine (quoted above), and Meindert Dijkstra’s reading of the text as a Transjordanian prophetic tradition.
