Jerome on Balaam — the diviner who spoke of Christ “more plainly than almost any prophet”

Dates: c. 390–414 (Jerome’s Balaam remarks are scattered across his exegetical career — the Book of Hebrew Names [Liber interpretationis Hebraicorum nominum, c. 390], the Commentary on Micah [c. 392], Letter 53 to Paulinus [394], the Commentary on Matthew [398], Letter 77 on Fabiola [399], and the Commentary on Ezekiel [c. 410–414]); Jerome b. c. 347 at Stridon, priest and translator of the Vulgate, d. 420 at Bethlehem · Wikipedia
Summary: The great Latin translator and philologist never devotes a full treatise to Balaam, but he touches him from every side of his learning, and he inherits the Torah’s double figure whole. On one side Jerome is emphatic that Balaam is a diviner — a ariolus, a false prophet “called a prophet in a derivative sense,” whom “the Lord sent… to deceive,” grouped with the lying spirit of Ahab and the Satan of Job among the instruments God permits against the faithless. On the other he is the source of the wiki’s strongest patristic claim for Balaam’s prophetic clarity: reporting Fabiola’s question, Jerome grants that “the soothsayer Balaam, in prophesying of the future mysteries of Christ, spoke more plainly of him than almost any other prophet.” His most distinctive contribution is philological: mining his Hebrew learning, he fixes the etymology of the name — Balaam, “without the people” or “empty people” — the very name-midrash the rabbis worked into belo ‘am, and turns it, with Beor (“on the skin”) and Balak, into a running allegory of the “empty people of the nations” who nonetheless come to bless.

Claims & evidence

“More plainly than almost any other prophet” (Letter 77, on Fabiola)

Jerome’s highest estimate of Balaam surfaces where he least intends a verdict on him — in the eulogy he wrote in 399 for the Roman noblewoman Fabiola, recalling the day she came to Bethlehem and questioned him over the book of Numbers. He reports her question as his own puzzle, and the terms are striking: a mere soothsayer is granted to have foretold Christ more clearly than the prophets of Israel.

One day we had before us the book of Numbers written by Moses, and [Fabiola] modestly questioned me as to the meaning of the great mass of names there to be found. Why was it, she inquired, that single tribes were differently associated in this passage and in that, how came it to be that the soothsayer Balaam, in prophesying of the future mysteries of Christ, spoke more plainly of him than almost any other prophet? I replied as best I could and tried to satisfy her inquiries.

— Jerome, Letter 77.7 (to Oceanus, on the death of Fabiola).1 The frame matters: Jerome does not soften “soothsayer” (ariolus, the diviner of Joshua 13:22) even while conceding the clarity of the prophecy — the same double figure he holds everywhere. He counted Balaam’s oracle among the buried “mysteries” of the Pentateuch: writing to Paulinus five years earlier on how the whole of Scripture repays spiritual reading, he lists “Balaam’s prophecy” beside the wilderness stations as one of the hidden figures of the book of Numbers.2

The name: Balaam, “without the people” (the Book of Hebrew Names)

Jerome’s Book of Hebrew Names, his glossary of the meanings of biblical proper nouns compiled from his Jewish teachers and from Origen’s onomastica, fixes an etymology of Balaam that would prove load-bearing for his allegory — and that runs exactly parallel to the rabbinic name-play. He gives it more than once, book by book:

Balaam: Without the people, either without their substance, or in them. … Beor: On the skin.

Balaam: Empty people, either hurrying them or without people. … Beor: on skin.

Balaam: Empty people, or their consumption. Bosor: In tribulation, either fleshly or furry.

— Jerome, Book of Hebrew Names (glosses on Numbers, on Micah, and on 2 Peter).3 “Without the people” / “empty people” renders the Hebrew as belî ‘am or belo ‘am — the very reading the Babylonian Talmud puts in the mouth of the rabbis at Sanhedrin 105a (“Balaam — one without a people [belo ‘am],” or “he who wore down the people”). Jerome, steeped in contemporary Jewish exegesis, carries the Hebrew name-midrash into Latin Christianity, and turns it into interpretation rather than mere insult.

The diviner God “sent to deceive” (Commentary on Ezekiel)

Where Origen answered the puzzle of true prophecy from a wicked man by distinguishing prophesying from being a prophet, Jerome answers it from a different verse and a harsher angle. Expounding Ezekiel 14:9 — “if the prophet be deceived… I the Lord have deceived that prophet” — he denies such a figure the name of prophet outright and lists Balaam among the deceivers God sends as a judgment on those who would rather hear lies:

When a prophet errs and speaks a word, I, the Lord, have deceived that prophet, we cannot say that he is a true prophet, but rather a false prophet, who is called a prophet in a derivative sense. … The Devil is also said to go about the earth in the book of Job, and it is said that he can receive power from the Lord over Job’s substance and then his body. And Balaam the diviner is sent by the Lord to deceive Balak.

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel IV (on Ezekiel 14:9).4 Balaam here keeps company with the lying spirit that entices Ahab (1 Kings 22) and the Satan permitted against Job: a “false prophet” through whom God nonetheless works his purpose. The Commentary on Micah makes the same identification in narrative form — “Balak, the king of Moab, hired Balaam, a diviner, to curse you and how, against his own will, Balaam, desiring to curse you, blessed you instead” — the diviner whose hostile will is overruled to speak blessing.5

“Let my soul die the death of the righteous,” and the oracles read of Christ

For all that, Jerome takes Balaam’s actual words as genuine prophecy and reads them, like Ambrose before him, of Christ and the Christian soul. On Ezekiel 18 he glosses Balaam’s wish in Numbers 23:10 spiritually, and folds it into the name-etymology:

But, that which is said by Balaam, ‘Let my soul die among the souls of the just’ (Numbers 23:10), has this meaning: that he desires to die to the world and to sin, and to live with the souls of the just, whose life is Christ… And if Balaam, as is likely, translated into our language, sounds empty to the people: it is clear that the empty people of the nations prior desire to have fellowship with the souls of the just, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel VI (on Ezekiel 18:3–4).6 He treats the other oracles as messianic testimony too: expounding Matthew 2:15 (“out of Egypt have I called my son”), he adduces Balaam’s “God has called him out of Egypt; His glory is like a unicorn” (Numbers 24:8) as a parallel witness, and in the Commentary on Ecclesiastes he cites “Balaam the prophet” for the oracle “behold the people shall rise up as a great” lion (Numbers 23:24).7

The magi as Balaam’s successors (Commentary on Matthew)

Jerome carries forward the tradition — from Origen and Ambrose — that the wise men of Matthew 2 were the heirs of Balaam’s star. Commenting on the star in the east, he sends the reader straight to Numbers 24:

To confound the Jews and make them understand the birth of Christ from the Gentiles, a star rises in the East, which the successors of Balaam, through prophecy, had known. Read the book of Numbers (Chapter XXIV). The magi, however, were guided by a star to Judea.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew I (on Matthew 2:2).8 Whether the historical magi actually held and preserved Balaam’s prophecy — as opposed to Matthew’s alluding to it — is weighed on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion behind Matthew, weak for the historical transmission), where Jerome takes his place in the star-oracle lineage. The afterlife of the oracle itself — how “the Star out of Jacob” became the great messianic proof-text — is traced on The Star Prophecy and Its Claimed Fulfilments.

Notes

  • The double figure, philologically anchored. Like Ambrose, Jerome keeps both halves of the Torah’s Balaam at full strength — the diviner God “sends to deceive” and the seer who prophesied Christ “more plainly than almost any prophet.” What is distinctive is that Jerome roots the villainy in the name: Balaam is “the empty people,” Beor “on the skin” (the fleshly), so that even the etymology preaches. His answer to the true-prophet-vs-diviner problem is closer to a hard verdict than Origen’s subtle distinction: such a man is “a false prophet, who is called a prophet in a derivative sense.”
  • A bridge to the Jewish name-midrash. Jerome’s “without the people” / “empty people” is the same reading of the consonants (bl‘m as belo ‘am) that the Babylonian Talmud preserves. Trained by Jewish teachers at Bethlehem, Jerome is one of the clearest channels by which the rabbinic reading of Balaam’s name passed into the Latin West — a witness, on this narrow point, to a shared Jewish–Christian exegesis rather than a polemic against it.
  • Provenance. Several of the quotations above are working English renderings of Jerome’s Latin commentaries (Micah, Matthew, Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes) from the edition linked in each note; the two letters are given in the standard NPNF translation. Where a rendering turns on a Hebrew or Latin play (Balaam “empty people”), the underlying word is noted.
