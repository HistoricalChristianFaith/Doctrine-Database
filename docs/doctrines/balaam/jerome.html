Jerome on Balaam — the diviner who spoke of Christ “more plainly of Him than almost any other prophet”

Dates: c. 390–414 (Jerome’s Balaam remarks are scattered across his exegetical career — the Book of Hebrew Names [Liber interpretationis Hebraicorum nominum, c. 390], the Commentary on Micah [c. 392], Letter 53 to Paulinus [394], the Commentary on Matthew [398], Letter 77 on Fabiola [399], and the Commentary on Ezekiel [c. 410–414]); Jerome b. c. 347 at Stridon, priest and translator of the Vulgate, d. 420 at Bethlehem · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome, Latin translator and philologist, never devotes a full treatise to Balaam, but he touches him from every side of his learning, and he keeps both sides of the Torah’s double figure. On one side Jerome is emphatic that Balaam is a diviner — a ariolus, a false prophet “who is called a prophet συνωνυμῶς [by a shared name],” who “is sent by the Lord to deceive Balak,” grouped with the lying spirit of Ahab and the Satan of Job among the instruments God permits against the faithless. On the other, reporting Fabiola’s question, Jerome grants that “the soothsayer Balaam in prophesying of the future mysteries of Christ … spoke more plainly of Him than almost any other prophet.” Philologically, drawing on his Hebrew learning, he gives the etymology of the name — Balaam, “without a people” or “vain people” — the same name-play found in the Talmud as belo ‘am — and turns it, with Beor (“in the skin”) and Balak, into a running allegory of “the vain people of the nations” who nonetheless come to bless.

Claims & evidence

“More plainly of Him than almost any other prophet” (Letter 77, on Fabiola)

Jerome’s remark on the clarity of Balaam’s prophecy comes in the eulogy he wrote in 399 for the Roman noblewoman Fabiola, recalling the day she came to Bethlehem and questioned him over the book of Numbers. He reports her question as his own puzzle: in it a soothsayer is said to have foretold Christ more clearly than the prophets of Israel.

One day we had before us the book of Numbers written by Moses, and [Fabiola] modestly questioned me as to the meaning of the great mass of names there to be found. Why was it, she inquired, that single tribes were differently associated in this passage and in that, how came it that the soothsayer Balaam in prophesying of the future mysteries of Christ [Nu. 24:15-19] spoke more plainly of Him than almost any other prophet? I replied as best I could and tried to satisfy her enquiries.

— Jerome, Letter 77.7 (to Oceanus, on the death of Fabiola).1 Jerome keeps “soothsayer” (ariolus, the diviner of Joshua 13:22) alongside the clarity of the prophecy — the same double figure he holds elsewhere. He counted Balaam’s oracle among the buried “mysteries” of the Pentateuch: writing to Paulinus five years earlier on how the whole of Scripture repays spiritual reading, he lists “Balaam’s prophecy” beside the wilderness stations as one of the hidden figures of the book of Numbers.2

The name: Balaam, “without a people” (the Book of Hebrew Names)

Jerome’s Book of Hebrew Names, his glossary of the meanings of biblical proper nouns compiled from his Jewish teachers and from Origen’s onomastica, gives an etymology of Balaam that he uses in his allegory, and that parallels the rabbinic name-play. He gives it more than once, book by book:

Balaam: without a people, or without their substance, or in them. Beor: in the skin.

Balaam: vain people, or throwing them headlong, or without people. Beor: in skin.

Balaam: a vain people, or his devouring. Bosor [Beor]: in tribulation, or fleshly, or made of skin.

— Jerome, Book of Hebrew Names (glosses on Genesis, on Numbers, and on 2 Peter).3 “Without a people” / “vain people” renders the Hebrew as belî ‘am or belo ‘am — the reading the Babylonian Talmud puts in the mouth of the rabbis at Sanhedrin 105a (“Balaam — one without a people [belo ‘am],” or “he who wore down the people”). Jerome, who studied with Jewish teachers, gives the Hebrew name-play in Latin and makes it the basis of an allegory.

The soothsayer “sent by the Lord to deceive” (Commentary on Ezekiel)

Where Origen answered the puzzle of true prophecy from a wicked man by distinguishing prophesying from being a prophet, Jerome answers it from a different verse. Expounding Ezekiel 14:9 — “if the prophet be deceived… I the Lord have deceived that prophet” — he denies such a figure the name of prophet and lists Balaam among the deceivers God sends as a judgment on those who would rather hear lies:

The prophet, when he errs and speaks a word, I the Lord have deceived that prophet; let us not think that this is said of a true prophet, but of a false prophet, who is called a prophet συνωνυμῶς [by a shared name]. … the devil too, in the book of Job, going around the earth, is said to stand in the sight of God and to receive power from the Lord, first over Job’s substance, then over his body; and Balaam the soothsayer also is sent by the Lord to deceive Balak the son of Beor.

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel IV (on Ezekiel 14:9).4 Balaam here keeps company with the lying spirit that entices Ahab (1 Kings 22) and the Satan permitted against Job: a “false prophet” through whom God nonetheless works his purpose. The Commentary on Micah makes the same identification in narrative form — “Balak king of Moab hired Balaam the diviner for a fee against you, and see how, against his own will, the soothsayer, wishing to curse you, blessed you” — the diviner whose hostile will is overruled to speak blessing.5

“Let my soul die the death of the righteous,” and the oracles read of Christ

Jerome also takes Balaam’s words as prophecy and reads them, like Ambrose before him, of Christ and the Christian soul. On Ezekiel 18 he glosses Balaam’s wish in Numbers 23:10 spiritually, and folds it into the name-etymology:

But what is said by Balaam, Let my soul die the death of the righteous, has this sense: that he desires to die to the world and to sin, and to live with the souls of the righteous, whose life is Christ… and if Balaam, translated into our language, means ‘a vain people’, which is most true, it is clear that the formerly vain people of the nations desired to have fellowship with the souls of the righteous Abraham, Isaac and Jacob…

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel VI (on Ezekiel 18:3–4).6 He treats the other oracles as messianic testimony too: expounding Matthew 2:15 (“out of Egypt have I called my son”), he adduces Balaam’s “God has called him out of Egypt, his glory is like that of the unicorn” (Numbers 24:8) as a parallel witness, and in the Commentary on Ecclesiastes he cites “the prophet Balaam” for the oracle “Behold, the people will rise up like a lion’s whelp, and like an exulting lion” (Numbers 23:24).7

The magi as Balaam’s successors (Commentary on Matthew)

Jerome repeats the tradition, found earlier in Origen and Ambrose, that the wise men of Matthew 2 were the heirs of Balaam’s star. Commenting on the star in the east, he sends the reader straight to Numbers 24:

To the confusion of the Jews, so that they might learn of Christ’s nativity from the Gentiles, a star rises in the east, which they knew was to come from the prophecy of Balaam (whose successors these men were). Read the book of Numbers. And the magi are brought by the guidance of the star into Judea…

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew I (on Matthew 2:2).8 Whether the historical magi held and preserved Balaam’s prophecy — as opposed to Matthew’s alluding to it — is disputed; see the magi timeline’s “Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’?” The later reception of the oracle itself — how “the Star out of Jacob” was read as a messianic proof-text — is traced on The Star Prophecy and Its Claimed Fulfilments.

Reception and debate

  • The double figure and the name. Like Ambrose, Jerome keeps both halves of the Torah’s Balaam — the soothsayer “sent by the Lord to deceive” and the seer who prophesied Christ “more plainly of Him than almost any other prophet.” Jerome also roots the villainy in the name: Balaam is “vain people,” Beor “in the skin” (the fleshly), so that the etymology serves the allegory. His answer to the true-prophet-or-diviner question differs from Origen’s distinction: such a man is “a false prophet, who is called a prophet συνωνυμῶς [by a shared name].”
  • Jerome and the Jewish name-play. Jerome’s “without a people” / “vain people” is the same reading of the consonants (bl‘m as belo ‘am) that the Babylonian Talmud preserves. Jerome, who studied with Jewish teachers at Bethlehem, gives the same reading of Balaam’s name in Latin; on this point Jewish and Christian exegesis share a reading.
  • Provenance. The quotations from Jerome’s Latin commentaries (Micah, Matthew, Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes) and the Book of Hebrew Names are given in the English translation at the edition linked in each note; the two letters are given in the standard NPNF translation. Where a rendering turns on a Hebrew or Latin play (Balaam “vain people”), the underlying word is noted.
