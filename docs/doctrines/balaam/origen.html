Origen on Balaam — a diviner who truly prophesied

Dates: c. 240–248 (the Balaam material spans the later Commentary on John [Book 28, at Caesarea, c. 240], the Homilies on Numbers [c. 240–244], and Against Celsus [c. 248]); Origen b. c. 185 at Alexandria, d. c. 253 at Tyre · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen is the first Christian writer to give Balaam a sustained reading, and he inherits the double figure the Jewish sources had already sharpened — the mouth of a true oracle and the author of a deadly seduction. His distinctive contribution is a precise resolution of the puzzle that a wicked pagan diviner could utter true prophecy, including the messianic “star out of Jacob.” Origen’s answer, worked out in his Commentary on John alongside the parallel case of Caiaphas, is that prophesying is not the same as being a prophet: Balaam “was not a prophet, for he is recorded as being a diviner,” and the true word he spoke came not from the Holy Spirit dwelling in him but was supplied from outside — by an angel, or forced out by the circumstance of seeing Israel encamped. He even athetized (marked with an obelus) the clause “the spirit of God came upon him.” Yet Origen also marvels at Balaam’s speaking ass, and, in Against Celsus and the Homilies on Numbers, makes Balaam the source of the magi: the Eastern seer whose star-prophecy his “school” kept in writing until they read its fulfilment in the Bethlehem star.

Claims & evidence

“He was not a prophet, for he is recorded as being a diviner” (Commentary on John 28)

Origen’s fullest treatment of Balaam is not a comment on Numbers at all but a digression in his Commentary on the Gospel of John, prompted by the evangelist’s note that Caiaphas “did not say this on his own authority, but being high priest that year he prophesied” (John 11:51). The wicked high priest who engineered Christ’s death nonetheless prophesied truly — and Balaam is Origen’s controlling parallel. The key move is a distinction between prophesying (an act) and being a prophet (a standing):

It is not the case that if someone prophesies, that person is necessarily a prophet. Thus, Caiaphas, being high priest that year, prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also that he might gather together the children of God who were scattered abroad, although he was not a prophet. If Balaam also prophesied what is recorded in Numbers, saying: “The word that God puts in my mouth, that I will speak,” and said from “Balak the king of Moab brought me from Aram,” and what follows, it is clear that he was not a prophet; for he is recorded as being a diviner. Thus, if anyone is a prophet, he certainly prophesies; but if anyone prophesies, he is not necessarily a prophet.

— Origen, Commentary on the Gospel of John 28 (on John 11:49–52), rendered from Migne’s Patrologia Graeca.1 Balaam is “recorded as being a diviner” — the Septuagint and Joshua 13:22 call him a soothsayer (mantis/qosem), never a prophet (navi) — and so, for Origen, the true words he spoke prove nothing about his standing before God. The gift of prophecy is not a certificate of holiness: “from what is recorded about Caiaphas, who prophesied about the Savior, it can be made clear that even a wicked soul sometimes receives the gift of prophecy.”

The word came from without — an angel, a circumstance, not the indwelling Spirit (Commentary on John 28)

Having denied Balaam the title of prophet, Origen presses the harder question — whose spirit spoke through him? He is careful not to lodge the Holy Spirit in so foul a vessel. Balaam’s true word, he suggests, was put in his mouth by the angel of Numbers 22, not breathed into his heart; and Origen goes so far as to strike the very clause that would say otherwise:

Since we have also introduced the case of Balaam, observe whether it can also be said about him that he did not speak from God, but from an angel … Notice that it is the angel who says, “Only the word that I speak to you, that you shall speak.” … As for the phrase, “And the spirit of God came upon him,” we have marked it with an obelus, not finding this or anything similar in the remaining versions.

— Origen, Commentary on the Gospel of John 28 (on John 11:49–52), rendered from Migne’s Patrologia Graeca.2 The textual note is telling: rather than explain how the “spirit of God” could rest on a diviner, Origen the critic athetizes the clause as absent from the other Greek columns of his Hexapla. And where the true word is not from an angel, it is squeezed out by the sheer occasion: Origen groups Balaam with Caiaphas, with Saul among the prophets, and with the Python-spirit girl of Acts 16 as cases where “circumstances cause prophecy” —

Similarly, Balaam would not have taken up his oracle and said, “From Aram Balaam has brought me,” and following words, had he not seen Israel encamped; and always, seeing another part of the camp he was moved to speak about Israel.

— Origen, Commentary on the Gospel of John 28 (on John 11:49–52), rendered from Migne’s Patrologia Graeca.3 This is the sharpest patristic answer to the “true prophecy from a false prophet” problem, and it cuts against the grain of the Jewish reworkings on this timeline. Where Philo had the prophetic spirit enter Balaam and drive his magic out, and Josephus called him outright “the greatest of the Prophets at that time,” Origen refuses him the office altogether: Balaam remains a diviner throughout, and God speaks a true word past him — through an angel, or through the force of the moment — without ever making him holy or a prophet.

The one thing Origen admires: Balaam’s speaking ass

If Origen withholds honour from the man, he lavishes it on the beast. The dumb ass that saw the angel and rebuked its master is, for Origen, the wonder of the episode — and a standing rebuke to Balaam himself:

I marvel at Balaam’s ass and heap blessings on it, because it was worthy not only to see the angel of God but even to have its mouth opened and break into human speech.

— Origen, Homilies on the Gospel of Luke 14.9.4 Elsewhere he keeps the biblical framing of the miracle intact even while rejecting a misuse of it: in On First Principles, refuting those who read the passage as proof that souls transmigrate into animals, he still speaks of “the speaking of Balaam’s ass, when God opened its mouth, and the dumb beast of burden, answering with human voice, reproved the madness of the prophet” — echoing Peter’s “the dumb ass… forbad the madness of the prophet” (2 Peter 2:16) and, with it, the New Testament’s dark verdict on Balaam.5

The star, and the magi as Balaam’s heirs (Against Celsus 1.59–60; Homilies on Numbers 13, 15)

The one Balaam oracle Origen embraces without reserve is the star. Answering Celsus in his old age, he notes that the nativity star is the single feature of the Gospel infancy for which a standing prophecy existed — and it is Balaam’s:

… but with respect to the appearance of a star at the birth of Jesus there is a prophecy of Balaam recorded by Moses to this effect: “There shall arise a star out of Jacob, and a man shall rise up out of Israel.”

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.59 (c. 248).6 He then makes the magi Balaam’s custodians — the Eastern seers who possessed and preserved his writings, and so recognized the star when it rose:

I am therefore of opinion that, possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam, which Moses also records, inasmuch as Balaam was celebrated for such predictions, and finding among them the prophecy about the star … they conjectured that the man whose appearance had been foretold along with that of the star, had actually come into the world.

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.60 (c. 248).7 In the Homilies on Numbers he states the transmission most fully — Balaam’s “race and school” of magi in the East, keeping his star-prophecy in writing until Jesus was born — and elsewhere calls the magi Balaam’s “seed, whether by physical descent or traditional instruction.”8 This is the seed of the medieval legend that made Balaam the magi’s own ancestor. Whether the historical magi really held and preserved his prophecy is weighed on the magi timeline’s “were the magi heirs of Balaam?” argument (plausible for the literary allusion behind Matthew, weak for the historical transmission); Origen’s own handling of the star and the magi is treated in full on his magi detail page.

Notes

  • A diviner, not a “false prophet.” Origen’s point is narrower than the New Testament’s moral verdict and sharper than it. He does not merely say Balaam was wicked (that he takes for granted); he says Balaam was not a prophet — the prophetic act is real, but the standing is not there. The true word is God’s, spoken through an unworthy and unwilling instrument the way it was through Caiaphas, Saul, and even confessing demons — not a gift Balaam possessed. This directly answers the puzzle the whole tradition circles: how a corrupt diviner could utter the messianic star oracle. It is load-bearing for the forthcoming “true prophet or diviner?” question this timeline will weigh on its own; Origen supplies the classic Christian answer — both, and the two are not in tension, because prophesying is not being a prophet.
  • The obelus. Origen’s note that he “marked with an obelus” the clause “the spirit of God came upon him” is a glimpse of him at work as a textual critic on his Hexapla: the phrase (Numbers 24:2 LXX) he judged intrusive because it was absent from “the remaining versions,” and its removal conveniently spares him from having to lodge the Spirit of God in a diviner. Whether the critical judgment drove the theology or the theology the judgment, the two run together here.
  • Against the Jewish reworkings on this timeline. Origen’s refusal of the prophet-title is the mirror image of Josephus (“the greatest of the Prophets”) and a hardening of Philo (in whom the prophetic spirit displaces the magic). It converges, oddly, with the later rabbinic instinct to damn the man while conceding the gift — though the tannaitic Sifrei went further than Origen ever would, ranking Balaam’s prophecy in some respects above Moses’. For Origen there is no such ranking: a diviner who truly prophesied is still only a diviner.
