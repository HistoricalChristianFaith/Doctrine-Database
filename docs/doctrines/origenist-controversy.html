The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus)

This is the story of a friendship that turned into one of the fiercest quarrels of the early Church. Jerome (c. 347–420) and Rufinus of Aquileia (c. 344/345–411) were close friends in their youth — fellow ascetics who prayed, studied, and dreamed of the monastic life together — and both were, at first, admirers of the third-century theologian Origen. Twenty years later they were public enemies, trading apologies and invectives over whether Origen was a saint or a heretic and whose translations had betrayed the truth. The rupture drew in bishops, popes, and emperors, and it outlived both men.

Unlike the doctrine pages elsewhere on this site, this timeline is a relationship told through its dated moments: the events, letters, and documents of the friendship and its breakdown, set down earliest first, each footnoted to its source. It opens with the origins and the long friendship, from Rufinus's birth to the two men living as quiet neighbours in the Holy Land in 386; then the first open breach — the Origenist quarrel that erupted at Jerusalem in the 390s and the tense reconciliation of Easter 397, as Rufinus left for Italy; and then the spark that reignited everything — Rufinus's Latin Origen and its preface pointing back at Jerome's own earlier versions of Origen, and Jerome's answer (397–399); then the open literary war — the death of the friendly Pope Siricius, Rome's condemnation of Origen, the far larger campaign Theophilus of Alexandria was waging at the same moment (which swept away a patriarch of Constantinople and made Jerome its translator into Latin), and the volley of apologies the two men fired at each other across the Mediterranean until a bishop's plea and the deaths that followed left the quarrel unhealed (399–405); and last the aftermath — Rufinus's remaining years translating the Greek fathers into Latin, his flight before Alaric and death in Sicily, the epithets Jerome applied to his dead rival, and the different memory Palladius, a contemporary who took Rufinus's part, set down of them both (401–420).

Timeline

Rufinus is born near Aquileia (c. 344/345)

Rufinus was born about 344 or 345 at Concordia, in the territory between Aquileia and Altinum in north-eastern Italy, and was baptized about his twenty-eighth year in the monastic community at Aquileia.1

The Aquileia ascetic circle (c. 370–373)

At Aquileia a brotherhood of young ascetics formed around Jerome and Rufinus — with Bonosus, Hylas, and Heliodorus — the high point of the friendship.2 Its sudden breakup scattered the friends; Jerome, driven east, later wrote to Rufinus of “that sudden whirlwind” that “dragged me from your side, severing with its impious wrench the bonds of affection in which we were knit together.”3

The friends part for the East; Rufinus goes with Melania (c. 372)

When the company was scattered, Rufinus “joined the noble Roman lady, Melania, in her pilgrimage to the East,” while Jerome made his own way to Syria and the desert of Chalcis.4

Jerome's letters from Syria: come to me (374)

From Antioch and the desert, Jerome wrote aching letters urging Rufinus — then in Egypt — to join him, longing to see his friend “more than the storm-tossed mariner looks for his haven,” and praising the newly-baptized Rufinus, to their mutual friend Florentius, as one made “white as snow.”5

Rufinus in Egypt, under Didymus the Blind (c. 373–380)

For some six years Rufinus studied at Alexandria under Didymus the Blind and lived among the desert hermits, imbibing a devotion to Origen; he suffered imprisonment and banishment under Lucius, the Arian bishop of Alexandria.6

The Mount of Olives; Rufinus ordained at Jerusalem (c. 379–386)

About 379–380 Melania the Elder settled on the Mount of Olives, where Rufinus gathered and led a community of monks for some eighteen years, by W. H. Fremantle's reckoning; he was ordained priest at Jerusalem about 386, around the time Bishop John succeeded Cyril there.7

Jerome settles at Bethlehem: neighbours again (386)

In 386 Jerome settled at Bethlehem with Paula, a few miles from Rufinus's monastery on the Mount of Olives. The old friendship was renewed — “though not apparently with all its former warmth” — and the two lived as friendly neighbours for some seven years.8

Atarbius comes accusing; Rufinus rebuffs him (c. 392)

About 392 the monk Atarbius, a hunter of Origen's errors, came to Jerusalem pressing both Jerome and Rufinus to subscribe a repudiation of Origen. Jerome readily gave satisfaction; Rufinus refused him even an interview and, in Fremantle's words, “treated him with merited scorn.”9

Epiphanius preaches against Origen; the camps form (393–394)

Visiting Jerusalem, Epiphanius of Salamis — who had already catalogued Origen as a heresy in his own right some twenty years earlier — ignited the quarrel: in the Church of the Resurrection, in the presence of the city's bishop John, he preached a sermon against Origen's errors which, by Jerome's account, John took as aimed at himself, sending his archdeacon to remonstrate. The camps hardened — Jerome with Epiphanius, Rufinus with John — and Rufinus stood, in Fremantle's account, “as the chief of a chorus of presbyters who applauded their own bishop and derided Epiphanius as a ‘silly old man.’”10 Leaving the city, Epiphanius wrote John a letter — which Jerome himself rendered into Latin, and which survives as his Letter 51 — pleading:

But may God free you, my brother, and the holy people of Christ which is intrusted to you, and all the brothers who are with you, and especially the presbyter Rufinus, from the heresy of Origen, and other heresies, and from the perdition to which they lead.

— Epiphanius to John, in Jerome, Letter 51, §6.11

Paulinian ordained; Jerome breaks with his bishop (394)

To free the Bethlehem monastery from John's jurisdiction, Epiphanius in 394 ordained Jerome's brother Paulinian a priest within John's own diocese. John was enraged; the ordination, and Jerome's defence of it, opened the breach between Jerome and his bishop.12

The stolen translation, and Jerome's manifesto on translating (395)

Epiphanius's letter was so admired that “all Palestine fought for copies of it,” and Eusebius of Cremona — a monk of Jerome's Bethlehem house who had no Greek — begged him for a Latin version for his private use. Eighteen months later it was taken from his desk and carried to Jerusalem, where Jerome's opponents used it to charge him with falsifying his original:

For a pretended monk — either bribed as there is much reason to believe or actuated by malice of his own as his tempter vainly tries to convince us — shewed himself a second Judas by robbing Eusebius of his literary property and gave to the adversary an occasion of railing against me. … What place of safety will be left us if we cannot conceal our secrets even within our own walls and our own writing-desks?

— Jerome, Letter 57 (to Pammachius), §§2–3. Jerome believed the accuser was put up to it by Rufinus. His answer, On the Best Method of Translating, defended rendering “sense for sense and not word for word” in everything but Scripture.13 The charge in this episode was one of mistranslation, brought against Jerome; the NPNF editor records that Rufinus later appealed to this letter as warrant for his own version of Origen.14

Jerome's Against John of Jerusalem (c. 396)

About 396 Jerome wrote a polemical tract, Against John of Jerusalem (addressed to Pammachius), answering John's complaints to Rome and heaping scorn on the bishop and, obliquely, on Rufinus — “your friends, who grin like dogs and turn up their noses.” Jerome broke it off and left it unpublished once the situation changed.15

The reconciliation at the Holy Sepulchre (Easter 397)

At Easter 397, before Rufinus set out for Italy with Melania, the breach was patched over — a gesture brokered amid the strife, with the influence of Theophilus of Alexandria and of Melania. As Fremantle tells it, “the two old companions who had been so long parted by ecclesiastical strife met together at the Church of the Resurrection at a solemn eucharistic feast, and joined hands in token of reconciliation, and … Jerome accompanied his friend some way on his journey before their final parting.”16 What kind of peace was made is disputed; see the disputed reading on the Easter reconciliation. Within about a year the two were in open conflict.

Rufinus reaches Italy; Pinetum and Macarius (397)

Late in 397 Rufinus returned to Italy with Melania and was welcomed by Paulinus of Nola. He settled for a time at Pinetum, near Terracina, where the monk Macarius pressed him — with a “persistence” that “even want of ability cannot resist” — to put Origen into Latin.17

Rufinus's Origen translations at Pinetum; the treatise On the Falsification (397)

At Pinetum Rufinus translated the Apology for Origen written by the martyr Pamphilus, and “supplemented” the work “by a short treatise of my own” — his On the Falsification of the Works of Origen (the De Adulteratione) — arguing that heretics had corrupted Origen's genuine text, so that a translator was entitled to prune the corruptions away. Whether the passages charged against Origen were insertions or his own teaching is disputed; see the disputed reading on the De Adulteratione.18

The preface that pointed to Jerome (397/398)

Then, at Macarius's urging, Rufinus translated Origen's Peri Archon (On First Principles) — smoothing away, as his preface states, whatever he judged heretical interpolation. To his translation he prefixed a preface to Macarius that, without naming him, cast Jerome as his precedent: the translator who had already Latinized more than seventy of Origen's homilies and had done the very same softening:

He has rendered into Latin more than seventy of Origen's homiletical treatises and a few also of his commentaries upon the apostle; and in these wherever the Greek text presents a stumbling block, he has smoothed it down in his version and has so emended the language used that a Latin writer can find no word that is at variance with our faith.

— Rufinus, Preface to the Translation of Origen's Peri Archon (Jerome, Letter 80).19 Rufinus was recalling Jerome's own earlier praise — the preface to Origen's homilies on the Song of Songs, in which Jerome had said that, “while in his other books Origen surpasses all former writers, in dealing with the Song of Songs he surpasses himself.” Jerome's answer followed in 398.20

The uncorrected papers: Eusebius of Cremona carries the charge through Italy (398)

How the Peri Archon reached Jerome's Roman friends is itself disputed, and each side told it as a theft. By Rufinus's account the translation was still unfinished and uncirculated when Eusebius of Cremona — the same monk whose desk had been robbed three years before, now “sent from their monastery to Rome” — obtained the draft and altered a sentence on the Trinity, so that where Rufinus had written “I will at once give you my reason for it” the copy read “as the Son does not see the Father, so the Holy Spirit also does not see the Son.” Eusebius recited the doctored line at Milan and, challenged, said a woman named Marcella had given him the copy. Rufinus's complaint was less that he had been contradicted than that he had never been asked — the two were living as neighbours in Rome, saw each other often and “joined together in prayer” — and that instead of coming to him,

he carried them round to private houses, to ladies, to monasteries, to Christian men one by one, wherever he might make trouble by his ex parte statements. … he went about all through Italy, accusing me, stirring up the people, throwing confusion into the churches, poisoning even the minds of the bishops.

— Rufinus, Apology against Jerome, I.21. Rufinus added the charge that Jerome's friends had bought the uncorrected papers from his secretary.21 Jerome answered on three fronts: he denied the bribe outright; he distanced himself from the accusing — “I did not approve his accusing you, nor of any Christian accusing another Christian” — while defending Eusebius as incapable of the forgery, since “a man who only knows Latin” could not “change anything in a translation from the Greek”; and he threw the theft back: “Who then was it who stole that letter in which you were so highly praised, from the cell of our brother Eusebius?” Rufinus described the copy as an uncorrected draft; Jerome answered with a dilemma built on that description. Whether the draft was altered or only unrevised is disputed; see the disputed reading on the draft.22

Jerome's answer: a literal Peri Archon, and a withheld olive branch (398)

To show what Origen's Greek said where, in his view, Rufinus had softened it, Jerome made his own strictly literal Latin Peri Archon and sent it to Rome with an open letter to Pammachius and Oceanus (Letter 84), insisting that his old praise of Origen had been for the exegete only, never the theologian: “I have praised the commentator but not the theologian, the man of intellect but not the believer, the philosopher but not the apostle.”23 He also wrote Rufinus a private, conciliatory letter (Letter 81), protesting that when reconciled to a man he did not “while offering him bread with one hand, hold a stone in the other” — but he sent it by way of Pammachius, who, in the NPNF editor's words, “treacherously suppressed it,” so the letter never reached Rufinus and the breach went public.24 Rufinus withdrew from Rome to Aquileia, where he was welcomed by its bishop, Chromatius.25

Anastasius succeeds Siricius; Rome moves against Rufinus (399)

In November 399 Pope Siricius, who had befriended Rufinus, died, and Anastasius I succeeded him. The new pope was closer to Jerome's Roman friends — the widow Marcella and the senator Pammachius — who now pressed the charge that Rufinus had smuggled Origen's heresy into the Roman church through his Latin Peri Archon. Marcella, Jerome wrote, “had the volumes of the Peri Archon brought in, and pointed out the alterations” the translator had made, until “letters were written … summoning the heretics to come and defend themselves, but they did not dare to come.”26

Theophilus purges Nitria; the Tall Brothers sail for Constantinople (400)

The quarrel of two Latin monks now became an empire-wide campaign, and it was driven from Alexandria. Theophilus, who had once sheltered the Origenist monks, turned on them: in 400 he expelled the Origenists from the monasteries of Nitria and wrote to Jerome at Bethlehem to say so — “we have cut down with the prophet's sickle certain wicked fanatics who were eager to sow broadcast in the monasteries of Nitria the heresy of Origen” — and to enlist his pen.27 Jerome answered, reporting that the campaign had already told in the West: “The old serpent hisses no longer, but, writhing and disembowelled, lurks in dark caverns unable to bear the shining of the sun” — and that a priest newly come from Rome said “again and again” that “Rome and almost the whole of Italy owe their deliverance after Christ to your letters.”28 Jerome became the campaign's Latin voice, translating Theophilus's synodical letter and, in the years following, his paschal letters of 401, 402, and 404, each of them an attack on Origen.29 The expelled monks did not submit. Writing to Epiphanius to ask for a condemning council on Cyprus, Theophilus reported where they had gone:

I have learned that certain calumniators of the true faith, named Ammonius, Eusebius, and Euthymius, filled with a fresh access of enthusiasm in behalf of the heresy, have taken ship for Constantinople, to ensnare with their deceits as many new converts as they can.

— Theophilus to Epiphanius, in Jerome, Letter 90. The three were the “Tall Brothers,” and at Constantinople they were received by its bishop, John Chrysostom.30 That Eastern campaign — Nitria, the Tall Brothers, the Synod of the Oak, and Chrysostom's exile and death — is told in full on its own timeline.

Rome condemns Origen (c. 400–401)

At the “earnest request” of Theophilus of Alexandria — who had expelled the Origenist monks from Nitria in 400 — a synod at Rome under Anastasius formally condemned Origen's errors, echoing the condemnations pronounced at Alexandria and, in 401, on Cyprus under Epiphanius. In 402/403 Jerome wrote that Origen was “condemned by the whole world.”31

Rufinus's Apology to Anastasius (400)

Rather than travel to Rome to answer — “It was impossible for me to do this in person,” he pleaded, newly returned to his family after thirty years abroad — Rufinus sent Anastasius a written profession of faith. He confessed the resurrection of “this very flesh in which we now live,” a judgment to come in which even the devil would “receive as their portion the eternal fire,” and — on the origin of the soul, the point where he was most suspected — declared himself, after reading every view, “unable to hold any of them as certain and absolute.” As for Origen, he disclaimed the role of champion, resting on his work as a mere translator:

I am not a defender or a champion of Origen; nor am I the first who has translated his works. Others before me had done the very same thing, and I did it, the last of many, at the request of my brethren.

— Rufinus, Apology to Anastasius, §7. His faith, he closed, was that “held by the Church of Rome, by that of Alexandria, and by my own church of Aquileia; and which is also preached at Jerusalem” — the see where he had been ordained.32

Rufinus's Apology against Jerome (400/401)

From Aquileia, at the end of 400, Rufinus struck back with two books of an Apology addressed to his patron Apronianus. He turned the heresy-charge on his accuser: from Jerome's own Commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians — the very work Jerome had held up as proof of his soundness against Origen — Rufinus argued that Jerome too had taught Origen's doctrines of the pre-existence of souls and the universal restoration:

You made an exception in favour of the Commentaries on the Ephesians, in which you imagined that you had written more correctly. But even you must have seen … how like they are all through to Origen's views; and, indeed, how they contain something more extreme than the views of which you demand the condemnation.

— Rufinus, Apology against Jerome, II.28. Whether the commentary taught those doctrines or reported them as an exegete is disputed; see the disputed reading on the Ephesians commentary.33

Anastasius's letter to John of Jerusalem (401)

When John of Jerusalem wrote to Rome on Rufinus's behalf, Anastasius answered (401) by condemning the translation while leaving the translator to his conscience — “he must bring his conscience to the bar of the divine majesty” — and disowning any knowledge of him:

I beg you to be assured of this, that he is so completely separate from all part or lot with us, that I neither know nor wish to know either what he is doing or where he is living.

— Anastasius to John of Jerusalem, §6.34 Whether Rome summoned Rufinus to appear, as Jerome's Letter 127 and the 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica describe, or condemned the book and left the man to his conscience, as W. H. Fremantle reads the letter, is disputed; see the disputed reading on Anastasius.34

Jerome's Apology against Rufinus, Books I–II (402)

At the beginning of Spring 402, Pammachius and Marcella sent Jerome the chief heads of Rufinus's Apology — they could not obtain the full text — and pressed him to reply. From Bethlehem he answered with two books of his own Apology against Rufinus, addressed to those same friends; Book II took up and rebutted the Apology to Anastasius point by point. Jerome mocked Rufinus's translator's-plea and his professions of faith as evasions, and threw the charge of Origenism back where it began.35

Book III; Chromatius's plea; no reconciliation (402/403)

A third book followed in 402/403, answering a now-lost private letter Rufinus had sent Jerome — one, by Jerome's account, that renewed the accusations and hinted darkly that its writer could, if he chose, produce facts to destroy his adversary. Bishop Chromatius of Aquileia begged both men to let the strife die; Rufinus, heeding him, made no public reply, but Jerome replied, closing with a call to condemn Origen together:

We once were zealous in our praise of Origen; let us be equally zealous in condemning him now that he is condemned by the whole world.

— Jerome, Apology against Rufinus, III.9. Augustine also wrote urging Jerome to peace; the two old friends never reconciled.36

The Synod of the Oak; Jerome Latinizes the case against Chrysostom (403–405)

The Alexandrian campaign reached John Chrysostom. Summoned to Constantinople to answer for his treatment of the Tall Brothers, Theophilus instead turned the proceedings against his host: at a synod held in 403 at a suburban estate called the Oak, John Chrysostom was condemned in his absence and deposed, and after a brief recall was exiled for good in 404. He died in exile in 407.37 Theophilus then composed a violent invective against him and sent it to Jerome to be put into Latin for the West, resting the charge on Origenism — a charge his own synod had by then dropped:

But, not to speak of his other misdeeds, he has taken the Origenists into his confidence, has advanced many of them to the priesthood, and by committing this crime has saddened with no slight grief that man of God, Epiphanius of blessed memory.

— Theophilus to Jerome, in Jerome, Letter 113. Jerome did the work, apologizing only for the delay — an Isaurian raid, a hard winter, famine, and an illness that brought him “to the threshold of death.”38 His covering note described the method he had used: “I have not indeed given a word-for-word rendering, as skilled translators do … Some words may be missing but none of the sense is lost.”39

Rufinus's Greek library goes into Latin (c. 401–406)

The quarrel did not stop Rufinus's pen. In these years at Aquileia he translated widely from Greek into Latin: he rendered Eusebius's Church History for Bishop Chromatius (401, as a consolation amid the Gothic terror), adding two books of his own to carry the story on; Origen's Commentary on Romans (c. 405–406) for Heraclius; Origen's homilies on the Pentateuch and Joshua, again for Chromatius; works of Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus; and, for Gaudentius of Brescia, the Clementine Recognitions.40

Rufinus leaves Aquileia; Rome, and Melania's household (c. 408)

After some nine years at Aquileia — and after the death of his patron Chromatius (c. 405) — Rufinus went to Rome as Alaric's Goths pressed into northern Italy, and attached himself to the religious household of Melania the Younger (granddaughter of the Melania who had brought him east long before), then preparing to leave Italy for the safety of the East.41

Jerome's literal version lost but for fragments (Letter 124 to Avitus, c. 409/410)

Jerome's own literal translation of the Peri Archon does not survive intact; it is known only through the passages he later quoted, a decade on, in his Letter 124 to Avitus (c. 409/410), where he recalled that “about ten years ago that saintly man Pammachius sent me a copy of a certain person's rendering, or rather misrendering, of Origen's First Principles” and asked for the true sense of the Greek.42

The sack of Rome; Rufinus flees to Sicily (410)

When Alaric sacked Rome in August 410, Rufinus fled south with Melania's party toward Sicily, watching from across the strait as the Goths burned Rhegium. In flight he continued translating — he finished Origen's homilies on Numbers on the island.43

Rufinus dies in Sicily (c. 411)

Rufinus died in Sicily soon after, about 411 (some accounts round to c. 410). He and Jerome, the friends of Aquileia, were never reconciled.44

Jerome on Rufinus's death: the scorpion and Grunnius (c. 411–414)

In the preface to his Commentary on Ezekiel — begun amid the news of Rome's fall and the deaths of Pammachius and Marcella — he described the death of his old friend in the image of a crushed beast, Rufinus the “scorpion” now lying dead beneath the soil of Sicily (Trinacria), pinned like the buried giants of myth between Enceladus and Porphyrion:

… a scar gradually covers a great wound: and Scorpius is pressed between Enceladus and Porphyrion and crushed by the Trinacrian soil, and the Hydra with many heads has at last ceased to hiss against us …

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel, preface (book I).45 He had long mocked Rufinus as “Grunnius” — the grunting pig of a popular burlesque, the Testament of the Piglet — and did so again in Letter 125 to the monk Rusticus (411), describing his dead adversary as one who “used to come forward at a snail's pace and to utter a word now and again with such long pauses between that he seemed less making a speech than gasping for breath … At home he was a monster like Nero, abroad a paragon like Cato.”46

The other portrait: Palladius remembers Rufinus (c. 419–420)

Much of the record above comes from Jerome's own writings. Palladius of Galatia, who had lived among the Egyptian and Palestinian monks and knew both households, wrote his Lausiac History about 419–420; his translator W. K. Lowther Clarke notes that he “takes Rufinus' part unhesitatingly” in the quarrel with Rufinus's old friend. He gives twenty-seven years to Melania's and Rufinus's household on the Mount of Olives, and records:

With her lived also the most noble Rufinus, from Italy, of the city of Aquileia, a man similar to her in character and very stedfast, who was afterwards judged worthy of the priesthood. A more learned man or a kinder than he was not to be found among men.

— Palladius, Lausiac History XLVI.5 (on Melania the Elder).47 Of Jerome he reports a monk's judgement that Jerome, “distinguished Latin writer and cultivated scholar as he was, showed qualities of temper so disastrous that they threw into the shade his splendid achievements,” and a prophecy that Paula would die first “and be freed from his bad temper,” and that “because of this man no holy man will dwell in these parts, but his envy will include even his own brother”; and of Paula herself, that “she was hindered by a certain Jerome from Dalmatia … he hindered her by his jealousy, having induced her to serve his own plan.”48 Palladius was a friend of Chrysostom and of the Origenist monks Theophilus had driven out; his portrait of the two men is that of the other side of the quarrel from Jerome's.49

Disputed readings

Points in this history on which the record has been read in more than one way:

Rufinus's translation of Origen

Questions about the Latin Peri Archon of 397–398 and the text that reached Rome.

  • Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching? (Rufinus's De Adulteratione) — on this turns whether Rufinus's pruning restored Origen's text or changed it
  • Was Rufinus's draft Peri Archon altered before it was read against him in Italy? — the sentence on the Trinity recited at Milan

Other questions:

  • What kind of peace did Jerome and Rufinus make at the Holy Sepulchre at Easter 397?
  • Does Jerome's Commentary on Ephesians (c. 386) teach Origen's doctrines of pre-existent souls and universal restoration, or report them?
  • Did Pope Anastasius I summon Rufinus to Rome and condemn him, or condemn the translation and leave Rufinus to his conscience?
