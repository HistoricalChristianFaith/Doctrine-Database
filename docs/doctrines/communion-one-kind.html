Communion under one kind (the chalice for the laity)

The Gospels record that at the Last Supper Jesus gave both the bread and the cup — "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) — and the earliest liturgical witnesses, East and West, describe the faithful receiving under both kinds, the consecrated bread and the consecrated wine alike. Across the medieval Latin West a different practice became the norm: the laity received the host only, while the chalice — the Precious Blood — was received by the celebrating priest. This is communion "sub una specie," communion under one kind. This page records, earliest extant attestation first, how the practice arose, how it was explained, defended and defined, how it was opposed, and how the cup was again offered to the Latin laity.

The record falls into these stages:

  • Both kinds as the received norm — instituted and administered under bread and wine in the New Testament and the early Church, East and West alike.
  • Practical exceptions — reception apart from the full two-kind synaxis is attested in particular settings: the reserved sacrament carried to the absent, the communion of the sick and of infants, and the host kept at home.
  • The rationale of concomitance — the scholastic teaching, given classic form by Thomas Aquinas, that the whole living Christ — body, blood, soul, and divinity — is present under each species, so that one who receives the host alone receives Christ entire. Constance and Trent later taught that the communicant under one kind is deprived of no grace necessary to salvation.
  • The medieval Western withdrawal of the cup — over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries lay reception of the chalice fell away across the Latin Church until communion of the host alone became the ordinary practice.
  • The Hussite / Utraquist demand — in fifteenth-century Bohemia the followers of Jan Hus made the lay chalice their central demand (Utraquism, from sub utraque specie, "under both kinds"), the cup becoming the emblem on their banners.
  • The councils respond — the Council of Constance (1415), granting that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds, decreed the custom of lay communion under one kind to be held as law; the Council of Basel and the Compactata (1430s) granted the cup to the Bohemian Utraquists on conditions.
  • The Reformation churches — Luther, the Lutheran confessions, Calvin and the English reformers gave the cup to the laity, holding "Drink ye all of it" to be Christ's command to all.
  • Trent's definition — the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562) defined that Christ is received whole and entire under either kind, that the Church had authority to regulate the manner of reception, and anathematized the claim that reception under both kinds is required of all by divine command.
  • Vatican II — the Second Vatican Council (Sacrosanctum Concilium, 1963) reopened communion under both kinds to the laity on stated occasions, a discipline since widened in much of the Latin Church.

The Eastern churches have continued to communicate the laity under both kinds; in the Byzantine rite the two species are given together, the consecrated bread placed in the chalice and administered by intinction with the communion-spoon.

Timeline

The Didache (c. 100)

The oldest surviving Christian liturgical text celebrates the assembly's thanksgiving under both kinds: it gives a blessing "concerning the cup" and then one "concerning the broken bread," and closes by admitting only the baptized — "let no one eat or drink of your Thanksgiving (Eucharist), but they who have been baptized into the name of the Lord" (Didache 9).1 Whatever the precise character of the meal, these table-prayers bless a cup and a bread, and only the baptized may eat or drink.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Explaining Christian worship to the emperor, Justin gives the earliest extant detailed account of a eucharist, and it is under both kinds. Once the president has given thanks over "bread and a cup of wine mixed with water," the deacons "give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water … and to those who are absent they carry away a portion" (First Apology 65).2 His weekly Sunday account repeats it: "bread and wine and water are brought … and there is a distribution to each … and to those who are absent a portion is sent by the deacons" (First Apology 67).3 The portion carried to the absent is the earliest attested reserved sacrament; Justin names bread and wine as what is distributed and calls what is sent out "a portion."

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251–253)

Cyprian is the fullest early witness on both sides of this timeline's opening. Writing to Caecilius against those who used water alone in the cup, he takes for granted that the laity drink it: "how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ?" — for to teach "the brotherhood" to shrink from the cup is to unfit them for martyrdom (Epistle 62.15).4 He treats both-kinds reception as the norm, and in On the Lapsed 25 a deacon offers the cup to an infant "as the rest received it." Yet On the Lapsed also preserves the earliest concrete one-kind exception: the consecrated bread reserved at home in a private box (arca), "in which was the holy (body) of the Lord" — the host kept privately, under the species of bread alone, so a believer could commune apart from the public assembly (On the Lapsed 26).5 Cyprian records the custom of home reservation without objection.

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

The fullest early Eastern account of lay reception comes from Jerusalem, where Cyril instructs the newly baptized step by step, and the rite is under both kinds: having made "thy left hand a throne for the right," the communicant receives the Body in the hollowed palm, and "then after thou hast partaken of the Body of Christ, draw near also to the Cup of His Blood … hallow thyself by partaking also of the Blood of Christ" (Mystagogic Catechesis 5).6 Of the consecrated Body received in the palm he urges care, "giving heed lest thou lose any portion thereof." The Eastern churches have continued to give the laity both kinds; on the later Byzantine manner of giving them together, see the Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon below.

The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380)

The fullest early order of service for communion comes from Syria, in the great compilation known as the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) — and it distributes the sacrament to the whole assembly under both kinds. Book VIII directs that after the clergy, "the women," "the children," and "then all the people in order" come forward: the bishop gives each the Bread, "saying, The body of Christ," and the communicant answers "Amen"; then "the deacon" gives the Cup, "saying, The blood of Christ, the cup of life," and "let him that drinketh say, Amen" (Constitutions VIII.13); the older Book II says the same in passing — "let every rank by itself partake of the Lord's body and precious blood in order" (II.57).7 The rubric gives the words said over each species to every communicant, and names the deacon as the cup's minister, as Cyprian's account of Carthage had a century earlier (On the Lapsed 25).

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

The Latin West of the same century assumed the cup no less than the East. Instructing the newly baptized at Milan, Ambrose walks them through the eucharist they have just received, under both kinds: "for you Blood flowed from Christ … the Blood satiates you for eternity," he tells them, and "you after drinking will be beyond the power of thirsting"; after the consecration the cup "is called Blood," and the communicant answers "Amen"; and the Church "exhorts her sons and her friends to come together to the sacraments, saying: 'Eat … and drink'" (On the Mysteries §§48–58).8 Ambrose is describing the rite he administers to the faithful, not arguing about the cup.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 410)

A half-century on, Augustine assumes the same at Hippo. Preaching on the forgiveness of even Christ's killers, he reaches for the eucharist as an illustration: the converts of Pentecost "came to the Lord's table, and in faith drank that Blood, which in their fury they had shed" (Sermon 27 on the New Testament).9 The figure takes for granted that the converted laity drank the cup at "the Lord's table."

Leo the Great (c. 445)

At Rome in the mid-fifth century refusing the cup was named as a sign by which Manichaeans could be detected. Preaching against the Manichaeans hiding among the faithful, Leo tells his people how to spot them: because the sect held all matter — wine included — to be evil, at "the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption" — and so are to be "driven from the society of the saints by priestly authority" (Sermon 42.5).10 How Leo's sign bears on lay reception of the cup at Rome is read differently; see the disputed reading.11

Pope Gelasius I (c. 494)

Half a century after Leo, Gelasius ruled on communicants who took the Body and abstained from the cup. In the decretal Comperimus — later gathered into Gratian's Decretum under the heading that "the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood" — he rules that those who "having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood" must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege" (De consecratione D.2 c.12).12 The decree does not name the persons; John F. X. Murphy (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) identifies them as Manichaeans who held wine impure. Aquinas read it of priests; Jakoubek of Stříbro cited it for the lay chalice in 1414, as the Augsburg Confession did in 1530. What the decree forbids is disputed; see What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?11

The Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon (692 onward)

In the East the laity continued to receive the cup, and the manner of its delivery changed. The Council in Trullo (692), canon 101, assumes the laity receive the Body in the hand — "let him draw near, arranging his hands in the form of a cross, and so let him receive the communion of grace" — forbidding only the use of gold or other "vessels" in place of the cupped hands.13 Over the seventh to eleventh centuries the Byzantine rite then merged the two species: the consecrated Bread was placed into the chalice and the sops administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis) — "the consecrated bread is immersed in the consecrated wine, then the sops are served by means of a spoon," so that the laity receive "both species together via intinction," the practice standard in Constantinople by the eleventh century and to this day.14

The Latin fistula & the West's rejected intinction (7th–13th c.)

In the Latin West the laity's reception of the cup was served for centuries by the fistula (also calamus, pugillaris, siphon) — a silver drinking-reed or tube through which the faithful sipped the consecrated wine without touching the chalice's rim, "an ordinary accompaniment of the chalice when the laity received Communion under both species," in general Western use until about the twelfth century and retained in the papal Mass long after (a golden reed) as a relic of the older practice.15 Intinction, dipping the host in the wine to give both kinds together, which the Byzantine rite adopted with the spoon, was repeatedly forbidden by Latin synods: proscribed by the Council of Braga (675), condemned again at the Council of Clermont (1095) under Urban II save "by necessity and as a precaution," restricted by Paschal II to "small children and the wholly infirm who cannot swallow the bread," and prohibited once more at a Council of London or Westminster (1175). Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) writes that "the final suppression of intinctio was followed in the thirteenth century by the gradual abolition for the laity of Communion under the species of wine," until the Council of Lambeth (1281) could direct "that wine is to be received by the priest alone, and non-consecrated wine is to be received by the faithful."16

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Writing while lay reception of the cup was ceasing in many Latin churches, Aquinas gives the doctrine of concomitance its classic scholastic form. It holds that because the risen Christ's body, blood, soul, and divinity are inseparably joined, the whole living Christ is present under each species: "the whole Christ is under each sacramental species yet not alike in each… because now Christ's blood is not separated from His body, as it was at the time of His Passion and death" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 76, a. 2).17 One who receives the host alone therefore receives Christ entire. On that basis Aquinas defends withholding the chalice from the laity as a "prudent custom" — the Blood, "if incautiously handled, it might easily be spilt," and so among "old, young, and children" it is fitting that "the blood not to be offered to the reception of the people, but to be received by the priest alone," who "both offers and consumes the blood on behalf of all" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12).18 Faced with Gelasius's canon against dividing the mystery, he reads it as speaking of priests: "Pope Gelasius is speaking of priests, who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament."19

Jakoubek of Stříbro (c. 1414)

In 1414 Jakoubek of Stříbro (Latin Jacobellus de Misa), a master of the University of Prague and soon successor to Jan Hus at the Bethlehem Chapel, began administering the consecrated wine as well as the bread to the laity, and other Prague parishes quickly followed — the founding act of Utraquism (from sub utraque specie, "under both kinds"), whence the movement's alternate name Calixtines, from calix, cup.20 He argued the cup from Christ's institution — "Drink ye all of it," and the warning of John 6:53, "except ye … drink his blood, ye have no life in you" — and from the ancient church's centuries of both-kinds reception; Hieromonk Patapios (2002) reports that he pressed it further than Hus had, as a dominical command rather than a liberty. A fifteenth-century tradition credits Master Peter of Dresden, a German schoolmaster refugee at Prague, with drawing Jakoubek to the cup; Helena Krmíčková (2000) sets that tradition aside and traces Jakoubek's chalice to the frequent-communion teaching of Matthias of Janov, on whom she argues Jakoubek drew.21 The chalice became the emblem on the Hussite banners, and communion under both kinds the first of the Four Articles of Prague (1420).

The Council of Constance (1415)

The thirteenth session of the Council of Constance (15 June 1415) issued the decree Cum in nonnullis on lay communion under one kind, printed in Tanner's edition under the heading "Condemnation of communion under both kinds, recently revived among the Bohemians by Jakoubek of Stříbro." It grants "that Christ instituted this venerable sacrament … under the forms of both bread and wine," and that "this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church" — then rules "notwithstanding this" that the later custom of giving the laity the host alone, "introduced for good reasons by the church and holy fathers … should be held as a law," that calling it "sacrilegious or illicit must be regarded as erroneous," and that those who "stubbornly assert the opposite … are to be confined as heretics."22 The decree grounds the custom in "the praiseworthy authority of the sacred canons and the approved custom of the church," and in the belief "that the whole body and blood of Christ are truly contained under both the form of bread and the form of wine." Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reads Constance and Trent as introducing no new legislation, "content with declaring that the existing custom had already acquired the force of law."23

Jan Hus (1415)

Hus himself had not begun Utraquism — he was already imprisoned at Constance when Jakoubek began giving the cup to the laity — and at first held only that Scripture and "the custom of the primitive church appear favourable" to it. But six days after the decree, and fifteen before he was burned, Hus wrote from his cell to a Prague priest who had opposed the practice, urging him: "Do not oppose the sacrament of the chalice of the Lord which Christ established … for no word of Scripture is opposed to it, only custom which, I ween, sprang from negligence … Already has the council, alleging custom, condemned the use of the chalice … as a heresy. What wickedness! Behold, they condemn Christ's enactment as heresy!"24 He was burned on 6 July 1415, and his name became joined to the chalice; Patapios (2002) reports that the claim that both kinds are of divine necessity was developed chiefly by Jakoubek after 1415.

The Council of Basel and the Compactata (c. 1436)

The Hussite wars ended in a negotiated settlement that granted the cup. Negotiating with the moderate Utraquists, the Council of Basel agreed the Compactata — the Compacts of Basel, or of Prague, accepted at Prague in 1433 and solemnly promulgated at Jihlava (Iglau) on 5 July 1436 — whose first article granted "that in Bohemia and Moravia, communion under both kinds is to be given to all adults who desire it"; the council later acknowledged that communion under both kinds was not heretical. Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) records that the concession "had never been approved by any pope."25 The grant carried the proviso that the whole Christ is present under each kind; the New Schaff–Herzog Encyclopedia reports that communion in both kinds was granted "with the understanding that Christ was entirely present in each kind."26 Its "subtle clauses" were read one way by the Czechs (a permanent charter) and another by Pope Pius II (a temporary indult for those then living), who on that reading "declared the Compactata null and void" on 31 March 1462.27 (The concession question was raised again after Trent; see the Trent entry below.)

Martin Luther (1520)

In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (October 1520) Luther named communion under one kind the first of the sacrament's three bondages under Rome: "The first captivity of this sacrament … concerns its substance or completeness, of which we have been deprived by the despotism of Rome."28 His argument turned on the words of institution as the evangelists report them — the note of universality is fastened to the cup, not the bread: "Both Matthew and Mark attach the note of universality to the cup, not to the bread … which Christ desired should be common to all," so that Christ's "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) is a command, not a permission.29 From this he concluded, against Constance, that the manner of the sacrament is not the church's to alter against Christ: "it is wicked and despotic to deny both kinds to the laity, and … this is not in the power of any angel, much less of any pope or council."30 The point was written into the Lutheran confessions — the Augsburg Confession (1530), Art. XXII, argues from "Drink ye all of it";31 Calvin wrote that the withholding of the cup "snatched or robbed a half of the Supper from the greater part of the people of God" (Institutes IV.17.47); and in England the Sacrament Act 1547 and the 1549 Book of Common Prayer prescribed both-kinds reception by statute and rubric.32

The Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562)

The Council of Trent answered the Reformers in its twenty-first session (16 July 1562), which issued the Doctrine on Communion under Both Kinds and on the Communion of Little Children, each chapter with canons of anathema. Against Luther's reading of "Drink ye all of it," it defined that "laymen, and clerics when not consecrating, are not obliged, by any divine precept, to receive the sacrament of the Eucharist under both species" (ch. I); that the church "hath ever had … this power, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient" (ch. II); and, ratifying the concomitance doctrine, that "Christ whole and entire and a true sacrament are received under either species alone … [so that] they, who receive one species alone, are not defrauded of any grace necessary to salvation" (ch. III), with a fourth chapter releasing infants from any necessity of communion.33 Trent thus defined, as Catholic teaching, concomitance, the absence of a divine precept binding the laity to both kinds, and the church's authority to regulate the manner of reception. Session 21 remitted to the pope the emperor's petition for the cup, and by a brief of 16 April 1564 Pius IV granted it, with restrictions, to subjects of the emperor and of Duke Albrecht of Bavaria. Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) records the concession as "withdrawn in the following year"; the historian James MacCaffrey writes that it "remained practically a dead-letter, and later on was withdrawn."34

The Second Vatican Council (1963)

In 1963 the cup was again offered to the Latin-rite laity. The Second Vatican Council's Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, Sacrosanctum Concilium (4 December 1963), commends the faithful's receiving "the Lord's body from the same sacrifice" and rules that, "the dogmatic principles which were laid down by the Council of Trent remaining intact, communion under both kinds may be granted when the bishops think fit … also to the laity, in cases to be determined by the Apostolic See," instancing the newly ordained, the newly professed, and the newly baptized (§55).35 The Council presents the change as one of discipline, with Trent's doctrine kept, and the short list of §55 was progressively widened in later liturgical law until both-kinds reception became a broadly available option in much of the Latin Church.36
