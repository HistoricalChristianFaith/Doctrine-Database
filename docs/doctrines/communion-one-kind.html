Communion under one kind (the chalice for the laity)

At the Last Supper the Lord gave both the bread and the cup — "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) — and for its first millennium the Church communicated the faithful under both kinds, the consecrated bread and the consecrated wine alike. Yet across the medieval West a different practice took hold: the laity received the host only, while the chalice — the Precious Blood — was reserved to the celebrating priest. This is communion "sub una specie," communion under one kind. This page traces how the withdrawal of the cup arose, how it was defended and defined, how it was fought, and how it was in part restored.

The arc has a recognizable shape:

  • Both kinds as the received norm — instituted and administered under bread and wine in the New Testament and the early Church, East and West alike.
  • Practical narrowing — long before any doctrine of withholding, communion under one kind appeared at the edges: reverent fear of spilling the Blood, the communion of the sick and of infants, the reserved sacrament carried to the absent, and reception of the host alone in the home or the desert.
  • The rationale of concomitance — the scholastic teaching, given classic form by Thomas Aquinas, that because the whole living Christ — body, blood, soul, and divinity — is present under each species, one who receives the host alone receives Christ entire and lacks nothing of the sacrament's grace. This is the theological hinge on which withholding the cup could be judged no deprivation.
  • The medieval Western withdrawal of the cup — over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries lay reception of the chalice fell away across the Latin Church until communion of the host alone became the ordinary practice.
  • The Hussite / Utraquist demand — in fifteenth-century Bohemia the followers of Jan Hus made restoration of the lay chalice a rallying cry (Utraquism, from sub utraque specie, "under both kinds"), the cup becoming the emblem on their banners.
  • The councils respond — the Council of Constance (1415) decreed communion under one kind for the laity to be lawful and binding despite the institution under both; the Council of Basel and the Compactata (1430s) conceded the cup to the Bohemians as a disciplinary concession.
  • The Reformers' restoration — Luther and the other Reformers restored the cup to the laity as a matter of Gospel obedience to "Drink ye all of it."
  • Trent's definition — the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562) defined that Christ is received whole and entire under either kind, that the Church had authority to regulate the manner of reception, and anathematized the claim that reception under both kinds is required of all by divine command.
  • Vatican II's partial restoration — the Second Vatican Council (Sacrosanctum Concilium, 1963) reopened communion under both kinds to the laity on stated occasions, a discipline since widened in much of the Latin Church.

Throughout, the East never withdrew the cup: the Eastern churches communicate the laity under both kinds to this day, having instead merged the species — the consecrated bread placed in the chalice and administered together by intinction and the communion-spoon.

Timeline

The Didache (c. 100)

The oldest surviving Christian liturgical text celebrates the assembly's thanksgiving under both kinds: it gives a blessing "concerning the cup" and then one "concerning the broken bread," and closes by admitting only the baptized — "let no one eat or drink of your Thanksgiving (Eucharist), but they who have been baptized into the name of the Lord" (Didache 9).1 Whatever the precise character of the meal, the church's earliest table-prayers already know a cup and a bread given to the faithful; nothing in the record of this period contemplates one alone.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Explaining Christian worship to the emperor, Justin gives the earliest detailed account of a eucharist — and it is emphatically under both kinds. Once the president has given thanks over "bread and a cup of wine mixed with water," the deacons "give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water … and to those who are absent they carry away a portion" (First Apology 65).2 His weekly Sunday account repeats it: "bread and wine and water are brought … and there is a distribution to each … and to those who are absent a portion is sent by the deacons" (First Apology 67).3 The portion carried to the absent is the earliest attested reserved sacrament — and in Justin it is still the whole rite, bread and cup, not the host alone.

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251–253)

Cyprian is the fullest early witness on both sides of this timeline's opening. Writing to Caecilius against those who used water alone in the cup, he takes for granted that the laity drink it: "how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ?" — for to teach "the brotherhood" to shrink from the cup is to unfit them for martyrdom (Epistle 62.15).4 Both-kinds reception is his unquestioned norm — extended even to infants, whom the deacon gives the cup "as the rest received it." Yet On the Lapsed also preserves the earliest concrete one-kind exception: the consecrated bread reserved at home in a private box (arca), "in which was the holy (body) of the Lord" — the host kept privately, under the species of bread alone, so a believer could commune apart from the public assembly (On the Lapsed 26).5 That practical accommodation — the bread that keeps and travels as the cup does not — is one of the roots from which the medieval West's withdrawal of the chalice would later grow.

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Meanwhile in the East the cup was never in question — and would never be withdrawn. The fullest early Eastern account of lay reception comes from Jerusalem, where Cyril instructs the newly baptized step by step, and the rite is plainly under both kinds: having made "thy left hand a throne for the right," the communicant receives the Body in the hollowed palm, and "then after thou hast partaken of the Body of Christ, draw near also to the Cup of His Blood … hallow thyself by partaking also of the Blood of Christ" (Mystagogic Catechesis 5).6 Tellingly, Cyril meets the very anxiety the West would later cite to reserve the chalice — the fear of losing a drop of the Precious Blood — by urging the laity to receive with the greater care, "giving heed lest thou lose any portion thereof," not by withholding the cup from them. This heads the Eastern counter-case that runs through this timeline: the East kept both kinds for the laity throughout, and its later solution to the fear of spilling was to merge the species, not to take one away (see the Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon below).

The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380)

The fullest early order of service for communion comes from Syria, in the great compilation known as the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) — and it distributes the sacrament to the whole assembly under both kinds. Book VIII directs that after the clergy, "the women," "the children," and "then all the people in order" come forward: the bishop gives each the Bread, "saying, The body of Christ," and the communicant answers "Amen"; then "the deacon" gives the Cup, "saying, The blood of Christ, the cup of life," and "let him that drinketh say, Amen" (Constitutions VIII.13); the older Book II says the same in passing — "let every rank by itself partake of the Lord's body and precious blood in order" (II.57).7 This is direct rubrical evidence — the very words said over each species — that the fourth-century Eastern laity drank the chalice, and the earliest surviving text to name the deacon as the cup's minister. With Cyril before it and the Council in Trullo after, it anchors the Eastern both-kinds norm the East would never abandon.

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

The Latin West of the same century assumed the cup no less than the East. Instructing the newly baptized at Milan, Ambrose walks them through the eucharist they have just received — and it is plainly under both kinds: "for you Blood flowed from Christ … the Blood satiates you for eternity," he tells them, and "you after drinking will be beyond the power of thirsting"; after the consecration the cup "is called Blood," and the communicant answers "Amen"; and the Church "exhorts her sons and her friends to come together to the sacraments, saying: 'Eat … and drink'" (On the Mysteries §§48–58).8 Ambrose is not defending the lay chalice but simply describing the rite he administers to the faithful — the earliest full Latin witness that both-kinds reception was the norm, the practice the medieval West would later reverse.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 410)

A half-century on, Augustine assumes the same at Hippo. Preaching on the forgiveness of even Christ's killers, he reaches for the eucharist as an illustration: the converts of Pentecost "came to the Lord's table, and in faith drank that Blood, which in their fury they had shed" (Sermon 27 on the New Testament).9 The figure works only because the ordinary converted layman drinks the cup; reception of the consecrated Blood is simply what coming "to the Lord's table" meant. With Ambrose, and with Leo and Gelasius at Rome, he fills the Western pre-withdrawal spine the whole controversy leans on.

Leo the Great (c. 445)

By the mid-fifth century the cup was so plainly the laity's due at Rome that refusing it marked a man as a heretic. Preaching against the Manichaeans hiding among the faithful, Leo tells his people how to spot them: because the sect held all matter — wine included — to be evil, at "the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption" — and so are to be "driven from the society of the saints by priestly authority" (Sermon 42.5).10 The detection test only works if every genuine communicant drinks the cup: here withholding it is not a Catholic option but the tell-tale of unbelief — the mirror image of the medieval discipline to come.

Pope Gelasius I (c. 494)

A half-century later Gelasius turned the same judgement into a canon. In the decretal Comperimus — later gathered into Gratian's Decretum under the heading that "the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood" — he rules that those who "having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood" must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege" (De consecratione D.2 c.12).11 As with Leo, the immediate target is the Manichaeans, who shunned the wine; but the principle is stated broadly — the mystery is not to be divided. Both texts would later be pressed by the Hussites and Reformers as showing that Rome once required the cup, and answered at Constance and Trent by distinguishing the heretical context from the disciplinary question (a dispute weighed among this timeline's arguments).

The Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon (692 onward)

As the Latin West began, over these same centuries, to drift toward the host alone, the East kept the cup and re-engineered its delivery. The Council in Trullo (692), canon 101, still assumes the laity receive the Body in the hand — "let him draw near, arranging his hands in the form of a cross, and so let him receive the communion of grace" — forbidding only the use of gold or other "vessels" in place of the cupped hands.12 Over the seventh to eleventh centuries the Byzantine rite then merged the two species: the consecrated Bread was placed into the chalice and the sops administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis) — "the consecrated bread is immersed in the consecrated wine, then the sops are served by means of a spoon," so that the laity receive "both species together via intinction," the practice standard in Constantinople by the eleventh century and to this day.13 The East thus met the same practical fear of spilling the Blood that the West cited for withdrawing the chalice — but by combining the kinds rather than dropping one. It is the standing counter-case that the Western withdrawal was a Latin peculiarity, not the church's universal mind, and (against the inference from concomitance below) a live proof that holding the whole Christ present under each species need not entail withholding the cup.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

By the high Middle Ages the Latin withdrawal of the cup, spreading through the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, needed a theology; Aquinas supplied it. His doctrine of concomitance holds that because the risen Christ's body, blood, soul, and divinity are inseparably joined, the whole living Christ is present under each species: "the whole Christ is under each sacramental species yet not alike in each… because now Christ's blood is not separated from His body, as it was at the time of His Passion and death" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 76, a. 2).14 One who receives the host alone therefore receives Christ entire. On that basis Aquinas defends withholding the chalice from the laity as a "prudent custom" — the Blood, "if incautiously handled, it might easily be spilt," and so among "old, young, and children" it is fitting that "the blood not to be offered to the reception of the people, but to be received by the priest alone," who "both offers and consumes the blood on behalf of all" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12).15 Faced with Gelasius's canon against dividing the mystery, he restricts it to the clergy: "Pope Gelasius is speaking of priests, who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament."16 This priest/layman distinction is the hinge on which withholding the cup could be judged no deprivation — the reasoning the councils of Constance and Trent would ratify, and whose adequacy this timeline's arguments weigh.

Jakoubek of Stříbro (c. 1414)

For a thousand years no one had deliberately set out to give the withdrawn cup back. In 1414 Jakoubek of Stříbro (Latin Jacobellus de Misa), a master of the University of Prague and soon successor to Jan Hus at the Bethlehem Chapel, began administering the consecrated wine as well as the bread to the laity, and other Prague parishes quickly followed — the founding act of Utraquism (from sub utraque specie, "under both kinds"), whence the movement's alternate name Calixtines, from calix, cup.17 He argued the cup from Christ's institution — "Drink ye all of it," and the warning of John 6:53, "except ye … drink his blood, ye have no life in you" — and from the ancient church's centuries of both-kinds reception, pressing it further than Hus had: not a mere liberty but a dominical command, so that withholding the cup is a sacrilege depriving the faithful. The impulse was long credited to Master Peter of Dresden, a German schoolmaster refugee at Prague whom a fifteenth-century tradition names as drawing Jakoubek to the cup; but modern scholarship treats that story as later embellishment, tracing the intellectual root instead to the frequent-communion teaching of Matthias of Janov — on whom Jakoubek verifiably drew — and crediting Jakoubek himself as the actual restorer.18 From his initiative the chalice became the emblem on the Hussite banners and the first of the Four Articles of Prague (1420).

The Council of Constance (1415)

The church answered at the Council of Constance, whose thirteenth session (15 June 1415) issued the decree Cum in nonnullis — the first conciliar definition of communion under one kind for the laity, aimed by name at the practice "recently revived among the Bohemians by Jakoubek of Stříbro." Its candour is the crux of every later dispute: it grants "that Christ instituted this venerable sacrament … under the forms of both bread and wine," and that "this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church" — then rules "notwithstanding this" that the later custom of giving the laity the host alone, "introduced for good reasons by the church and holy fathers … should be held as a law," that calling it "sacrilegious or illicit must be regarded as erroneous," and that those who "stubbornly assert the opposite … are to be confined as heretics."19 The whole question is thus fixed not on Scripture's plain sense — which the decree concedes — but on the church's authority to change a dominical practice; the concomitance doctrine of Aquinas supplies the warrant that the laity, receiving the host alone, lack nothing of Christ. These admissions would be pressed by the Reformers and answered again at Trent (a dispute weighed among this timeline's arguments).

Jan Hus (1415)

Hus himself had not begun Utraquism — he was already imprisoned at Constance when Jakoubek restored the cup — and at first held only that Scripture and "the custom of the primitive church appear favourable" to it. But six days after the decree, and fifteen before he was burned, Hus wrote from his cell to a Prague priest who had opposed the practice, urging him: "Do not oppose the sacrament of the chalice of the Lord which Christ established … for no word of Scripture is opposed to it, only custom which, I ween, sprang from negligence … Already has the council, alleging custom, condemned the use of the chalice … as a heresy. What wickedness! Behold, they condemn Christ's enactment as heresy!"20 His martyrdom on 6 July 1415 fused his name to the chalice, though the strong claim that both kinds are of divine necessity remained Jakoubek's, not his.

The Council of Basel and the Compactata (c. 1436)

Twenty years of Hussite war ended not by crushing the demand for the cup but by conceding it. Negotiating with the moderate Utraquists, the Council of Basel agreed the Compactata — the Compacts of Basel, or of Prague, accepted at Prague in 1433 and solemnly promulgated at Jihlava (Iglau) on 5 July 1436 — whose first article granted "that in Bohemia and Moravia, communion under both kinds is to be given to all adults who desire it," and whose framers even acknowledged that communion under both kinds is not heretical.21 This is the mirror image of Constance: the cup the one council forbade, the other restored. Yet Basel conceded nothing of the theology — the grant was hedged with the proviso that the whole Christ is present under each kind, so the chalice was given because the faithful wished it, not because the host alone had left them lacking.22 Its "subtle clauses" were read one way by the Czechs (a permanent charter) and another by Rome (a temporary indult for those then living), and on that reading Pope Pius II "declared the Compactata null and void" on 31 March 1462.23 That the same authority could both withhold the cup and grant it back is the episode's whole point: the practice was disciplinary and negotiable, not — even for Rome — beyond the church's power to change. (The concession question would reopen at Trent, where the cup was granted to parts of Germany under Pius IV in 1564 and then withdrawn — treated with Trent below.)

Martin Luther (1520)

What the councils had defended, the Reformation set out to reverse — and it began with the cup. In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (October 1520) Luther named communion under one kind the first of the sacrament's three bondages under Rome: "The first captivity of this sacrament … concerns its substance or completeness, of which we have been deprived by the despotism of Rome."24 His argument turned on the words of institution as the evangelists report them — the note of universality is fastened to the cup, not the bread: "Both Matthew and Mark attach the note of universality to the cup, not to the bread … which Christ desired should be common to all," so that Christ's "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) is a command, not a permission.25 From this he drew the conclusion that reversed Constance's whole premise — that the manner of the sacrament is not the church's to alter against Christ: "it is wicked and despotic to deny both kinds to the laity, and … this is not in the power of any angel, much less of any pope or council."26 The point was promptly written into the Reformation's standards — the Augsburg Confession (1530), Art. XXII, makes Luther's exact case from "Drink ye all of it";27 Calvin treated the withheld cup as a corruption to be undone; and in England the Sacrament Act 1547 and the 1549 Book of Common Prayer restored both-kinds reception by statute and rubric.28 Whether "Drink ye all of it" binds the laity to the cup by divine command, and whether the church may nonetheless regulate the manner of reception, are the two questions Trent would answer against Luther by distinguishing what Christ instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive (a dispute weighed among this timeline's arguments).

The Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562)

The Counter-Reformation answer came at the Council of Trent, whose twenty-first session (16 July 1562) issued the Doctrine on Communion under Both Kinds and on the Communion of Little Children — the sharpest statement of the Catholic side of this whole controversy, each point secured by a canon of anathema. Against Luther's "Drink ye all of it," it defined that "laymen, and clerics when not consecrating, are not obliged, by any divine precept, to receive the sacrament of the Eucharist under both species" (ch. I); that the church "hath ever had … this power, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient" (ch. II); and, ratifying the concomitance doctrine, that "Christ whole and entire and a true sacrament are received under either species alone … [so that] they, who receive one species alone, are not defrauded of any grace necessary to salvation" (ch. III), with a fourth chapter releasing infants from any necessity of communion.29 Trent thus fixes the three arguments this timeline turns on — concomitance, the no-divine-precept reading of "Drink ye all of it," and the church's authority to regulate the manner of reception — as the settled Catholic position (a dispute weighed among this timeline's arguments). Session 21 remitted to the pope the emperor's plea for the cup, and on 16 April 1564 Pius IV empowered the bishops of Austria, Bohemia, and much of the Empire to grant it — an indult that, like the Compactata before it, was later withdrawn diocese by diocese and finally suppressed in Bohemia in 1621.30

The Second Vatican Council (1963)

Four centuries later the cup was reopened within Roman Catholicism itself. The Second Vatican Council's Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, Sacrosanctum Concilium (4 December 1963), commends the fuller sign and rules — carefully leaving Trent's doctrine untouched — that "the dogmatic principles which were laid down by the Council of Trent remaining intact, communion under both kinds may be granted when the bishops think fit … also to the laity, in cases to be determined by the Apostolic See," instancing the newly ordained, the newly professed, and the newly baptized (§55).31 The change is disciplinary, not doctrinal — the very church-authority Trent had claimed, now exercised in the opposite direction — and the short list of §55 was progressively widened in later liturgical law until both-kinds reception became a broadly available option in much of the Latin Church.32 The cup withdrawn across the medieval West, defended at Constance and Trent, is thus once more offered to the Roman-rite laity — as a fuller sign freely given, not as a debt of divine precept.

Arguments

The chronological witnesses above record what was practised and decreed; the questions on which the whole controversy turns are weighed here, off the timeline.

Related arguments — the dominical command

Two proof-texts are pressed for the lay chalice not as history but as command — words of Christ said to bind the cup on every communicant. The institution's "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) supplies the imperative; the Bread-of-Life discourse's "except ye … drink his blood, ye have no life in you" (John 6:53) supplies, in its strong Utraquist form, the note of necessity. Each is weighed adversarially on its own page.

Does "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) bind every communicant to the cup, or only the apostles then present? — contested

The central proof-text of the both-kinds side: at the institution Christ said of the cup "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27; cf. Mark 14:23, "they all drank of it"), and Paul hands the cup to a whole congregation (1 Corinthians 11:26–28). The Utraquists and Reformers read this as a dominical command binding every communicant to the cup; Constance and Trent read the "all" as addressed to the apostles then present, whom the church holds Christ made priests, so that it binds celebrants, not the laity. On the bare text the both-kinds reading is the stronger — the note of universality attaches uniquely to the cup, the parallel imperative "eat" cannot be restricted without restricting "drink," and the councils concede the plain sense and shift to the church's authority to change the custom; but the strong claim that the command is a divine precept whose breach defrauds or damns the laity outruns what the words establish and is met by concomitance and the regulative-authority argument — hence contested. It shares the apostles-only audience-question with the ministerial-priesthood dispute over "do this in remembrance of me."

Does "except ye … drink his blood" (John 6:53) make the cup necessary for the laity? — weak

The Utraquists' second proof-text, standing behind "Drink ye all of it": in the Bread-of-Life discourse Christ says, "except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you" (John 6:53), which Jakoubek pressed — beyond Hus — as making reception of the cup necessary to the laity's salvation. As that strong necessity claim the reading is weak, defeated from inside the discourse: the same passage promises eternal life to eating "this bread" alone (vv. 51, 57–58), so the eating and the drinking are not two independently required conditions but one act of feeding on Christ named from two sides. Layered on that, it is disputed whether John 6 legislates the eucharistic rite at all (see the Real Presence timeline), and, taken strictly, the reading proves too much — the same "no life in you" would compel communicating infants, which Trent, reading the very verse, declined to require. The cup as the fuller sign of the blood is a milder and stronger claim, but a different one (weighed under concomitance); the necessity thesis this page tests outruns the text.

Is communion under both kinds necessary for salvation, so that withholding the cup imperils the laity's souls? — weak

The sharpest form the both-kinds case ever took, and the proponents' overreach: the strong Hussite thesis — pressed by Jakoubek beyond Hus, and hardest of all by the radical Taborite wing — that reception sub utraque specie is de necessitate salutis, so that the Latin withdrawal of the cup does not merely give a lesser sign but cuts the faithful off from life. As that necessity claim the reading is weak: its two scriptural props reach at most a command and a weak John-6:53 necessity reading (life is promised to the bread alone, vv. 51, 57–58); concomitance severs the inference (the one-kind communicant lacks no grace necessary to salvation); the "universal ancient practice" was never exceptionless (the host was reserved alone for the sick and the absent as viaticum, and infants communicated in a single species, without anyone alleging damnation); taken strictly it would condemn every uncommunicated infant, which Trent, reading the very verse, declined; and it did not even command its own camp, the moderate Calixtines demanding the cup as institution and right rather than on pain of perdition. The milder thesis — both kinds as the dominical, apostolic, and fuller form that ought to be restored — is far stronger, but a different claim (weighed under "Drink ye all of it" and concomitance); the necessity form is a conjecture beyond the evidence, the cup's dignity pressed into a proof of its necessity.

By concomitance, is the whole Christ received under one species alone? — sound (as a reception claim)

The load-bearing defense of one-kind communion: because the risen Christ is indivisible — body, blood, soul, and divinity inseparably joined — the communicant who receives the host alone receives the whole and entire Christ and is deprived of no grace (Aquinas, Summa III q. 76; Trent, Sess. 21). As a claim about what is received this is sound and near-consensus — the Orthodox East affirms it no less than the Catholic West. But its soundness is narrowly scoped and does not carry the disciplinary conclusion. Concomitance settles the grace worry only; it is silent on the sacramental sign (the twofold species signifying the blood poured out — a fuller sign the church itself now commends), it does not by itself answer the dominical command to drink, and it does not entail withdrawing the cup — the East holds the very same doctrine yet never withdrew the chalice, merging the species instead (see Cyril and the communion-spoon).

Is reception under both kinds a fuller sacramental sign that communion under one kind lacks? — sound (as a sign claim)

The quiet, sturdy core of the both-kinds case — milder than the command or the necessity claims, and admitted on both sides of the dispute. The species are consecrated separately, the body "given" under bread and the blood "shed" under wine, and the two apart set forth Christ's blood poured out — the covenant sealed in his blood — so reception under both kinds is the fuller sacramental sign, a real good the one-kind communicant forgoes. As a claim about the sign this is sound and effectively uncontested: it holds even granting concomitance (which answers what is received and is silent on what is signified), and the very church that withdrew the cup now urges it back for precisely this reason — the Second Vatican Council commending "the fuller sign" (Sacrosanctum Concilium §55; General Instruction §281). Two precisions keep it honest: the fuller sign lay reception adds is most exactly the sign of the eucharistic banquet and the covenant blood (the sacrificial sign of the separated species is already set forth in the priest's double consecration at every Mass); and a fuller sign is a real good, not an obligation — it does not by itself make the cup commanded or necessary (those are the command and necessity claims, weighed apart).

May the church withdraw the cup — alter a manner of reception Christ instituted under both kinds? — contested

The ecclesiological hinge of the Catholic case, and its most candid concession. Constance and Trent grant that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds and that the primitive church received under both — yet hold the church competent to change the manner of administration for grave and reasonable causes, distinguishing what is of divine precept from what is disciplinary: "this power has ever been in the Church, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient" (Trent, Sess. 21, ch. II). The steelman is strong — the church demonstrably reshaped other dominical and apostolic forms (the evening Supper moved to a morning fast, the agape detached, the kiss of peace repositioned) without any charge of disobedience, and concomitance secures that no grace is withheld. But the candour of the concession is itself the Reformers' sharpest lever — an admitted dominical-and-apostolic practice reversed by positive law, with no clear line between reformable discipline and a form Christ fixed (Luther: "not in the power of any angel, much less of any pope or council") — sharpened by Rome's own earlier Gelasian ruling that dividing the mystery is "grave sacrilege." Strong by ecclesiology's lights, weak by the lights of an instituted form — a genuine authority question with no clear winner, hence contested.

Did the medieval withdrawal of the cup reverse ancient Roman discipline? — contested

The historical prong of the authority question, pressed by the Utraquists and Reformers (the Augsburg Confession, Art. XXII, "Pope Gelasius commands that the Sacrament be not divided"): Rome once enforced the cup on the laity — Leo made refusing it the tell-tale of a heretic, and Gelasius called dividing "one and the same mystery" a "grave sacrilege" — so the later Latin one-kind norm reverses a discipline a fifth-century pope laid down, and cannot be the church's constant mind. As bare fact the reversal is real and even conceded by Constance (both kinds was instituted and primitive); what is disputed is its force. The Catholic reply limits Gelasius to the crypto-Manichaeans who spurned wine as evil — a heretical refusal, not a reverent reservation — and casts the change as reformable discipline, not doctrine; but the decretal grounds its "sacrilege" in the act of dividing (not the motive) and struck laymen, straining Aquinas's confinement of it to priests. Strong as the sharpest historical lever against a "constant mind," weaker once it rests on the church's authority to alter an instituted form — hence contested, and the historical companion to the authority argument above.

Do the ancient one-kind exceptions (viaticum, the sick, infants, home-reservation) warrant withdrawing the cup from the laity? — weak

The Catholic counter-history to the "exceptionless ancient practice" premise: long before any doctrine of withholding, the church already communicated in a single species — the host reserved at home in Cyprian's arca, the portion carried to the absent and the dying as viaticum, infants communicated under one kind — so one-kind reception was never foreign to the church, and the medieval withdrawal is the generalization of an accepted principle rather than a naked innovation. As a defeater to the strong Utraquist thesis that both kinds is exceptionless and de necessitate the exceptions succeed (see the necessity argument); but as a positive precedent licensing the routine withdrawal of an available cup from healthy laity at the assembly they are weak, disanalogous twice over — the reserved-host and viaticum cases are emergency-and-absence accommodations turning on the plain fact that wine will not keep or travel, and the one settled non-emergency one-kind custom, infant communion, was under the consecrated wine (Cyprian's deacon "forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup"), the reverse of host-only. The withdrawal's real warrant lies in concomitance and the church's authority, where the councils actually argued it — not in these precedents; the exceptions clear away an objection to the rule without supplying its foundation.
