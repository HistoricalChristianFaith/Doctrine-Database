Communion under one kind (the chalice for the laity)

At the Last Supper the Lord gave both the bread and the cup — "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) — and for its first millennium the Church communicated the faithful under both kinds, the consecrated bread and the consecrated wine alike. Yet across the medieval West a different practice took hold: the laity received the host only, while the chalice — the Precious Blood — was reserved to the celebrating priest. This is communion "sub una specie," communion under one kind. This page traces how the withdrawal of the cup arose, how it was defended and defined, how it was fought, and how it was in part restored.

The arc has a recognizable shape:

  • Both kinds as the received norm — instituted and administered under bread and wine in the New Testament and the early Church, East and West alike.
  • Practical narrowing — long before any doctrine of withholding, communion under one kind appeared at the edges: reverent fear of spilling the Blood, the communion of the sick and of infants, the reserved sacrament carried to the absent, and reception of the host alone in the home or the desert.
  • The rationale of concomitance — the scholastic teaching, given classic form by Thomas Aquinas, that because the whole living Christ — body, blood, soul, and divinity — is present under each species, one who receives the host alone receives Christ entire and lacks nothing of the sacrament's grace. This is the theological hinge on which withholding the cup could be judged no deprivation.
  • The medieval Western withdrawal of the cup — over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries lay reception of the chalice fell away across the Latin Church until communion of the host alone became the ordinary practice.
  • The Hussite / Utraquist demand — in fifteenth-century Bohemia the followers of Jan Hus made restoration of the lay chalice a rallying cry (Utraquism, from sub utraque specie, "under both kinds"), the cup becoming the emblem on their banners.
  • The councils respond — the Council of Constance (1415) decreed communion under one kind for the laity to be lawful and binding despite the institution under both; the Council of Basel and the Compactata (1430s) conceded the cup to the Bohemians as a disciplinary concession.
  • The Reformers' restoration — Luther and the other Reformers restored the cup to the laity as a matter of Gospel obedience to "Drink ye all of it."
  • Trent's definition — the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562) defined that Christ is received whole and entire under either kind, that the Church had authority to regulate the manner of reception, and anathematized the claim that reception under both kinds is required of all by divine command.
  • Vatican II's partial restoration — the Second Vatican Council (Sacrosanctum Concilium, 1963) reopened communion under both kinds to the laity on stated occasions, a discipline since widened in much of the Latin Church.

Throughout, the East never withdrew the cup: the Eastern churches communicate the laity under both kinds to this day, having instead merged the species — the consecrated bread placed in the chalice and administered together by intinction and the communion-spoon.

Timeline

The Didache (c. 100)

The oldest surviving Christian liturgical text celebrates the assembly's thanksgiving under both kinds: it gives a blessing "concerning the cup" and then one "concerning the broken bread," and closes by admitting only the baptized — "let no one eat or drink of your Thanksgiving (Eucharist), but they who have been baptized into the name of the Lord" (Didache 9).1 Whatever the precise character of the meal, the church's earliest table-prayers already know a cup and a bread given to the faithful; nothing in the record of this period contemplates one alone.

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Explaining Christian worship to the emperor, Justin gives the earliest detailed account of a eucharist — and it is emphatically under both kinds. Once the president has given thanks over "bread and a cup of wine mixed with water," the deacons "give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water … and to those who are absent they carry away a portion" (First Apology 65).2 His weekly Sunday account repeats it: "bread and wine and water are brought … and there is a distribution to each … and to those who are absent a portion is sent by the deacons" (First Apology 67).3 The portion carried to the absent is the earliest attested reserved sacrament — and in Justin it is still the whole rite, bread and cup, not the host alone.

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251–253)

Cyprian is the fullest early witness on both sides of this timeline's opening. Writing to Caecilius against those who used water alone in the cup, he takes for granted that the laity drink it: "how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ?" — for to teach "the brotherhood" to shrink from the cup is to unfit them for martyrdom (Epistle 62.15).4 Both-kinds reception is his unquestioned norm — extended even to infants, whom the deacon gives the cup "as the rest received it." Yet On the Lapsed also preserves the earliest concrete one-kind exception: the consecrated bread reserved at home in a private box (arca), "in which was the holy (body) of the Lord" — the host kept privately, under the species of bread alone, so a believer could commune apart from the public assembly (On the Lapsed 26).5 That practical accommodation — the bread that keeps and travels as the cup does not — is one of the roots from which the medieval West's withdrawal of the chalice would later grow.

Leo the Great (c. 445)

By the mid-fifth century the cup was so plainly the laity's due at Rome that refusing it marked a man as a heretic. Preaching against the Manichaeans hiding among the faithful, Leo tells his people how to spot them: because the sect held all matter — wine included — to be evil, at "the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption" — and so are to be "driven from the society of the saints by priestly authority" (Sermon 42.5).6 The detection test only works if every genuine communicant drinks the cup: here withholding it is not a Catholic option but the tell-tale of unbelief — the mirror image of the medieval discipline to come.

Pope Gelasius I (c. 494)

A half-century later Gelasius turned the same judgement into a canon. In the decretal Comperimus — later gathered into Gratian's Decretum under the heading that "the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood" — he rules that those who "having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood" must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege" (De consecratione D.2 c.12).7 As with Leo, the immediate target is the Manichaeans, who shunned the wine; but the principle is stated broadly — the mystery is not to be divided. Both texts would later be pressed by the Hussites and Reformers as showing that Rome once required the cup, and answered at Constance and Trent by distinguishing the heretical context from the disciplinary question (a dispute weighed among this timeline's arguments).
