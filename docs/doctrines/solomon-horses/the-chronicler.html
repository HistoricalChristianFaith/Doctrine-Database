The Chronicler on Solomon's Stalls

Dates: c. 400 BC (the composition of 1–2 Chronicles) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest witness to the smaller figure. Retelling Solomon's reign from the older book of Kings, the Chronicler writes that the king had four thousand stalls — one tenth of the "forty thousand" that stands in the Masoretic text of 1 Kings 4:26 — and the four thousand is the number that fits Solomon's 1,400 chariots.

Claims & evidence

Solomon had four thousand stalls, not forty thousand

"And Solomon had four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen; whom he bestowed in the chariot cities, and with the king at Jerusalem."

— 2 Chronicles 9:25 (KJV), source. The verse is the Chronicler's parallel to 1 Kings 4:26, which in the received Hebrew reads "forty thousand stalls of horses for his chariots." Both notices end with the same twelve thousand horsemen; only the stall-count differs, and by exactly a factor of ten.

Notes

  • A dependent, not independent, witness — but an early one. The Chronicler wrote after the exile (conventionally c. 400 BC) and used Samuel–Kings as a written source, so his "four thousand" is not a second sighting of Solomon's stables but a reading of his copy of Kings. That is precisely why it matters: on the mainstream view his copy still read "four" before a later scribe of the Kings tradition inflated it to "forty," so Chronicles transmits the older number. See the summary timeline.
  • The minority reading reverses the arrow. Because the Chronicler is known elsewhere to enlarge Solomon's glory, a few readers suppose he (or his tradition) lowered an original "forty thousand" to something he found credible. The proportionality of the chariots tells against that: it is the four thousand, not the forty, that matches the 1,400 chariots the Chronicler himself records at 2 Chronicles 1:14. The direction of the change is weighed on the argument page.
  • Same pattern as the Goliath crux. A number that differs between the Samuel–Kings tradition and Chronicles, with the fix sought in textual transmission rather than in real history, is the recurring shape of these hard passages — compare who killed Goliath, where Chronicles again carries the reading many judge original.
