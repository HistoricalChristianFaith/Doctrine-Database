How has the "forty thousand" of 1 Kings 4:26 been read beside the "four thousand" of 2 Chronicles 9:25?

Disputed reading · on Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · witness: the Chronicler

The passage

"And Solomon had forty thousand stalls of horses for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen"

— 1 Kings 4:26 (KJV, rendering the Masoretic Hebrew; 5:6 in the Hebrew versification), source.1

"And Solomon had four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen; whom he bestowed in the chariot cities, and with the king at Jerusalem."

— 2 Chronicles 9:25 (KJV), source.2

The question

The two notices give the same twelve thousand horsemen and differ in the stall-count by a factor of ten; both books elsewhere give Solomon 1,400 chariots (1 Kings 10:26 = 2 Chronicles 1:14). The Greek of 3 Kingdoms carries "forty thousand" in its supplement after 2:46 and "four thousand" at 10:26, and Josephus (c. 93) gives "forty thousand."3 Cantrell and Finkelstein reconstruct the largest of the stable-complexes excavated at Megiddo at about 450 horses and date the standing buildings to the eighth century BC.4 What is disputed is whether the two figures count different things, so that both stand as written, or whether one of them is a copyist's error, and if so what the original figure was.

Kings and Chronicles count different things

In brief: the two books number different objects, such as stalls and stables or horses and stables, so both figures stand as written.

Held by: Gersonides (Jewish, 14th c.) ⚠ unverified; the Geneva Bible notes (English Protestant); Matthew Poole (English Protestant, Annotations upon the Holy Bible, 1685); John Gill (Exposition of the Entire Bible).5

The case starts from the Hebrew. 1 Kings 4:26 has ʾurwoth (אֻרְוֺת) where 2 Chronicles 9:25 has ʾuriyyoth (אֻֽרְיוֹת). Poole: "It is not exactly the same Hebrew word which is here and there, though we translate both stalls; and therefore there may well be allowed some difference in the signification, the one signifying properly stables, of which there were 4000, the other stalls or partitions for each horse, which were 40,000."6 The Geneva note on the Chronicles verse gives the same count: "ten horses in every stable, which in all amounts to 40,000 as in 1Ki 4:26."7

Gill, "as Ben Gersom notes," has Kings give "the number of the horses that were in the stables, which were forty thousand, there the stables themselves, which were four thousand, ten horses in a stable; or here he numbers the stalls, which were forty thousand, and there the stables, which were four thousand, there being ten stalls in each." The Chronicles word, he adds, "has the letter 'yod' in it more than here, which is the numerical letter for 'ten', and may point thereunto." He also allows that Kings counts "all the stalls for horses Solomon had throughout the kingdom, there of those only he had in Jerusalem."8

On the size of the establishment, Poole reads "for his chariots" as "both for his military chariots, which seem to be those 1400, 1 Kings 10:26, and for divers other uses, as about his great and various buildings, and merchandises, and other occasions, which might require some thousands of other chariots," and he allows that "the 40,000 horses were in part appointed for his chariots, and in part for his 12,000 horsemen."9 On this reading the two books report one royal establishment counted in two different units, and neither text needs emending.

A copyist enlarged the numeral in Kings

In brief: the text of Kings once agreed with Chronicles, and in copying its "four" became "forty."

Held by: Matthew Poole (Annotations, 1685); Keil & Delitzsch (German Lutheran, Biblical Commentary on the Old Testament, 1872); Gleason Archer (conservative evangelical, Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties, 1982); Raymond Dillard (2 Chronicles, Word Biblical Commentary, 1987) ⚠ unverified.10

The case starts from the chariots both books record. Keil & Delitzsch: "4000 horses are a very suitable number for 1400 chariots, though not 40,000, since two draught horses were required for every war chariot, and one horse may have been kept as a reserve." Barnes' Notes reckons that "4,000 horses would supply the full team of three to 1,200, and the smaller team of two to 200 chariots," and holds the figure "in due proportion to the 12,000 horses for cavalry, and … in accordance with all that we know of the military establishments of the time and country."11

Its holders name the slip. Poole's first answer reports: "Some acknowledge an error of the transcriber, writing arbahim, forty, for arbah, four, which was an easy mistake." Keil & Delitzsch: "ארבּעים (40) is an old copyist's error for ארבּעה (4), which we find in the parallel passage 2 Chronicles 9:25." Archer frames it as a figure that "has undoubtedly undergone multiplication by ten because of an obscurity in or misunderstanding of the Vorlage."12 Keil & Delitzsch take the noun as "a rack or box in a stable," citing Vegetius by way of Bochart that "every horse had it own crib in the stable," one horse to a stall.13

Dillard, as reported, holds that "the ratios work better in the Chronicles passage (4000/1400)" and places the difficulty "in the history of transmission," while holding that "it is not possible to speak with confidence regarding the original text."14 The NIV prints "four thousand" at 1 Kings 4:26, with the note "Some Septuagint manuscripts (see also 2 Chron. 9:25); Hebrew forty."15 On this reading the two books agree on Solomon's establishment, and the difference lies in one copied numeral in the text of Kings.
