Is the "forty thousand" of 1 Kings 4:26 a scribal inflation of an original "four thousand"?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound that the "forty thousand" is inflated and 2 Chronicles 9:25 the better witness — the "stables-vs.-stalls" harmonization does not hold, though the exact original wording stays uncertain · advanced by Keil & Delitzsch, Gleason Archer, and most modern commentators.

Related arguments: reconciling the count — see also Do Kings and Chronicles count different things (40,000 stalls vs. 4,000 stables)?

The claim

"ארבּעים (40) is an old copyist's error for ארבּעה (4), which we find in the parallel passage 2 Chronicles 9:25."

— Keil & Delitzsch, on 1 Kings 4:26, link. The interpretation: the "forty thousand stalls" of 1 Kings 4:26 is not a real count but a transcription error that inflated an original "four thousand" — the figure the Chronicler still read, and the figure that fits Solomon's chariots — so the two biblical books do not actually disagree about history, only about a corrupted numeral in the text of Kings.1

Who advances it

It is the mainstream verdict. Keil & Delitzsch (1872) state it as a word-slip; Gleason Archer (1982) as a "multiplication by ten"; Barnes, conservative and critical commentators alike carry it, and modern translations enact it — the NIV printing "four thousand" in the text of 1 Kings 4:26 and footnoting the Hebrew "forty."2 It stands against the older harmonization — associated with Matthew Poole — that keeps both numbers by making them count different things.

The evidence for

The arithmetic. Solomon's chariot-force is fixed independently at 1,400 chariots (1 Kings 10:26 = 2 Chronicles 1:14). Four thousand stalls supplies a full three-horse team to roughly twelve hundred chariots with reserves; forty thousand horses for 1,400 chariots is a stable nearly thirty times the fleet it serves. As Barnes puts it, "4,000 horses would supply the full team of three to 1,200, and the smaller team of two to 200 chariots" — the number in Kings "is probably a corruption."3

A surviving un-inflated witness. 2 Chronicles 9:25 — the Chronicler retelling the same reign — reads "four thousand," with the identical "twelve thousand horsemen" that Kings gives. The smaller number is not a conjecture but an attested biblical reading, and the one that matches the chariots.4

A plausible mechanism. In unpointed Hebrew "four" (ʾarbāʿā) and "forty" (ʾarbāʿîm) differ only by the plural ending; a scribe could read or write the one for the other, an error framed alternatively as a straight "multiplication by ten" of the numeral. Either way the corruption runs in the direction of enlargement, the common drift of copied numbers.5

The material scale. The largest Iron-Age chariot-city stabling excavated in Israel, the Megiddo complexes, held only a few hundred horses; a kingdom-wide total of four thousand is credible for the period, forty thousand far beyond its means. (Whether those complexes are stables, and whether they are Solomon's, is weighed on the Megiddo-stables page; the scale point holds either way, since a premier chariot-city's capacity marks the ceiling whatever the buildings' function or exact date.)6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That "forty thousand" is disproportionate and best explained as an inflated corruption is as close to consensus as this kind of crux gets: the arithmetic, the parallel with Chronicles, the numeral mechanism, and the archaeological scale all pull the same way, and the translations follow. The two biblical books are not in real historical conflict; the difficulty lies in the transmitted text of one of them.

The standing rival is weaker. The chief alternative — that Kings counts forty thousand individual stalls and Chronicles four thousand larger stables (ten horses each), so both numbers stand as written — is genuinely advocated (Poole, Benson, Gill, after Gersonides) but does not survive the text: the two verses are the same sentence, each closing with the identical twelve thousand horsemen; 2 Chronicles 9:25 calls its four thousand "stalls for horses and chariots," not stable-buildings; and the harmonization leaves the very disproportion at issue unrelieved. It is weighed on its own page and rated weak.7

What is overstated. The evidence establishes that the forty thousand is inflated and that four thousand is the better-attested and better-proportioned figure. It does not establish that "four thousand" is the demonstrable original wording of Kings — a stronger claim that Archer's "undoubtedly" and Keil & Delitzsch's flat emendation press too far. The oldest witnesses are divided: the Greek carries both numbers, and Josephus already knew the forty thousand at the end of the first century — so, unlike the shorter Goliath, there is no early external line uniting on "four." Dillard's caution is the right register: the variants are "surrounded by a thicket of textual difficulties," and "we do not know the exact number."8

Assessment

Sound. On its load-bearing claim — that the "forty thousand" of 1 Kings 4:26 is an inflated corruption and 2 Chronicles 9:25 preserves the better figure — the reading is well-grounded and near-consensus: the chariot-arithmetic, the surviving Chronicles reading, the numeral mechanism, and the material scale converge, and the modern translations enact it. The competing "stables-vs.-stalls" harmonization does not hold. Two honest limits keep this short of certainty about the autograph rather than about the inflation: the earliest external witnesses (the split Greek, Josephus) do not converge on "four," so the case rests on the internal parallel and the arithmetic; and "the forty thousand is too large" is a firmer claim than "four thousand is the exact original," which the tangle of variants leaves genuinely open. Rate the inflation-reading sound; leave the precise original figure uncertain.
