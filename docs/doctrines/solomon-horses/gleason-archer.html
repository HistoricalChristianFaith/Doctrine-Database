Gleason Archer on Solomon's Stalls

Dates: c. 1982 (the Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties) · Wikipedia
Summary: A conservative evangelical apologist who nonetheless adopts the textual explanation: the "forty thousand" of Kings is a numerical copying-error, ten times too large, and the "four thousand" of Chronicles the credible original.

Claims & evidence

The Kings figure was "multiplied by ten" in copying

"We have a probable example of this as we compare 2 Chronicles 9:25 (which gives four thousand as the number of stalls built for Solomon's chariot horse) and 1 Kings 4:26 (which gives the figure as forty thousand). The latter citation has undoubtedly undergone multiplication by ten because of an obscurity in or misunderstanding of the Vorlage."

— Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties (Zondervan, 1982), borrowable at the Internet Archive. Archer elsewhere names it "a good example of a more modest (and credible) figure… four thousand… whereas 1 Kings 4:26 puts the figure at forty thousand." For him the four thousand is the reliable number and the forty thousand a scribe's inflation of it.

Notes

  • An inerrantist conceding a copyist's error. Archer's whole project defends the reliability of Scripture, yet on this verse he locates the difficulty not in the autograph but in its transmission — the ordinary hazard of hand-copied numerals, recoverable precisely because a second, un-inflated witness survives in Chronicles. He does not reach for the "stables vs. stalls" harmonization.
  • "Multiplication by ten" vs. word-confusion. Archer's decimal-slip mechanism and Keil & Delitzsch's ʾarbāʿîm/ʾarbāʿā word-confusion are two proposals for the same corruption; the argument page weighs them and the harmonization together.
  • "Undoubtedly" overstates the certainty of the original wording. That the forty thousand is inflated is well-grounded; that we can be sure four thousand is the exact original is the point where Dillard and the text-critics urge more caution, given the tangle of variants across Kings, Chronicles, and the Greek. Flagged and rated on the argument page.
