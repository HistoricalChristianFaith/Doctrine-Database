Matthew Poole on Solomon's Stalls

Dates: c. 1683 (the Annotations upon the Holy Bible, published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: The classic early-modern harmonizer. Poole reconciles the two numbers first by distinguishing the units — that Kings and Chronicles count different things — and secondarily by allowing that "forty thousand" may be a copyist's slip, laying out both routes that later interpreters would take.

Claims & evidence

The "stables vs. stalls" distinction: 4,000 stables, 40,000 stalls

"It is not exactly the same Hebrew word which is here and there, though we translate both stalls… the one signifying properly stables, of which there were 4000, the other stalls or partitions for each horse, which were 40,000."

— Annotations upon the Holy Bible, on 1 Kings 4:26, source. Poole's leading solution keeps both numbers true by making them count different objects: Chronicles' four thousand are stable-buildings, Kings' forty thousand the individual stalls or partitions within them — roughly ten horses to a stable.

The copyist-error alternative

Poole also allows the second route, that the figures should agree and the larger is a transcription error — the option his near-contemporary John Gill states plainly on the same verse: "some think that here is a mistake of the copier, of arbaim, forty, for arbah, four." The two routes are the fork the whole later discussion runs along: harmonize by units, or emend the text.

Notes

  • Why the units-distinction is doubted. The two verses are manifestly the same sentence — each pairs the stall-count with the identical "twelve thousand horsemen" — and 2 Chronicles 9:25 calls its four thousand "stalls for horses and chariots," not larger stable-blocks. Reading one verse as buildings and the other as partitions is a distinction the Hebrew does not clearly carry. The critique is set out on the argument page for the units-distinction, which rates the harmonization weak; the rival textual explanation is rated sound.
  • Poole preserves the honest ambiguity. By offering both a harmonization and a textual option he marks the exact place where interpreters divide — and his willingness to entertain a copyist's slip anticipates the verdict of Keil & Delitzsch and Archer two centuries later.
