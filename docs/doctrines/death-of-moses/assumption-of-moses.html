The Assumption of Moses — the lost apocryphon linked to Jude 9

Dates: c. AD 7–30 (a Palestinian Jewish work of the early first century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The book that Clement of Alexandria, Origen and modern editors connect with the notice in Jude 9 of Michael and the devil disputing over the body of Moses. It survives only in a single mutilated Latin manuscript — which breaks off before the disputed scene — and in a handful of quotations preserved by the Fathers. Its title, Assumption, bears on the question whether Moses was buried or taken up.

What the book is, and what survives of it

The Assumption of Moses (Greek Analēpsis Mōuseōs) is a Jewish pseudepigraphon cast as Moses’ farewell testament to Joshua on the eve of his death: he foretells Israel’s history down to the author’s own day, and exhorts Joshua; on Bauckham’s reconstruction, the lost close told of his death. It is preserved in one witness: a sixth-century Latin palimpsest in the Ambrosian Library at Milan, discovered and first published by Antonio Maria Ceriani in 1861.1 That Latin text is a fragment — perhaps a third to a half of the work is gone — and it breaks off before the end: it contains no account of Moses’ death, no burial, and no dispute between Michael and Satan over the body. Bauckham places the scene Jude alludes to in the now-missing conclusion.2

“Assumption” or “Testament”? — the title problem

The surviving Latin is cast as a deathbed discourse, the form of a Testament, and the extant part contains no taking-up or ascent. Yet the ancient book-lists name two Moses-apocrypha — a Testament of Moses (Diathēkē Mōuseōs) and an Assumption / Ascension of Moses (Analēpsis) — and the Stichometry of Nicephorus assigns them different lengths (1,100 and 1,400 lines).3 R. H. Charles (1897) argued that these were “two originally independent works subsequently put together,” the extant Latin being the Testament. Richard Bauckham treats the extant text as the Testament of Moses, while Johannes Tromp’s critical edition, following Ceriani, keeps the title Assumption of Moses and identifies the manuscript with it through a match between its 1:14 and a quotation in Gelasius of Cyzicus. Bauckham holds that the Assumption was a later rewritten version of it, which on his account explains why the ancient lists carry both titles for what is one tradition.4 On both accounts the disputed scene belongs to the lost ending, and the surviving pages do not preserve the manner of Moses’ passing that the title Assumption names.

The dispute over the body, reconstructed from the fragments

Because the Latin text breaks off, the content of the scene is pieced together from the Fathers who read the whole book. Bauckham distinguishes two strands. In the murder-and-burial strand — the one he identifies as the older and as Jude’s source — the archangel Michael is sent to bury Moses, and Satan opposes the honourable burial by slandering Moses as a murderer (he had killed the Egyptian, Exodus 2:12); Michael, refusing to trade insult for insult, answers only “The Lord rebuke thee” — the words spoken to Satan in Zechariah 3:2. A scholion on Jude gives this form:

For the devil also slandered Moses, calling him a murderer because he smote the Egyptian. Michael the archangel, not tolerating his slander, said to him, “May the Lord God rebuke thee, devil!”

— scholion on Jude 9, as gathered and translated in Bauckham, Jude, 2 Peter.5 A second, later strand recasts the quarrel in anti-Gnostic terms: the devil claims the corpse as his own “because I am the master of matter,” and Michael rebukes him as one who would deny that God made the material world. Bauckham judges this the Assumption’s second-century Christian reworking, not the original.6 Ryan Stokes (2017) reconstructs the dispute differently, as a quarrel over Moses’ bodily ascent into God’s presence rather than over the burial of his body.7

Jude’s use of the book and the canon question

Jude 9 does not quote a sentence of the book; it alludes to the scene, making Michael’s restraint a model against the “railing accusations” of the teachers the letter opposes. The same letter quotes, five verses later, the prophecy of 1 Enoch 1:9 (Jude 14–15). Whether drawing on such books bears on the canon, including Bauckham’s answer (that valuing a tradition as true need not canonize the book it comes from, as Qumran valued Enoch without placing it in a canon), is set out at “What source lies behind Jude 9?”8 2 Peter 2:11, which parallels this part of Jude’s letter, keeps the moral but has no identifiable Moses-dispute.9

Reception and debate

  • Charles dates the book c. AD 7–30: it looks back on Herod the Great’s death and the war of Varus (4 BC) but still expects the Temple to stand, and he reads its prediction about Herod’s sons as pointing to composition before about AD 30.10
  • The Fathers who connect the book with Jude 9 — Clement of Alexandria (“here he confirms the assumption of Moses”) and Origen (who calls it The Ascension of Moses) — are treated on their own pages; the later Greek and Latin commentators who retell the dispute are gathered at “The Fathers retell the dispute.”
  • Whether Moses died, as the death-notice of Deuteronomy 34 records, or was taken up, as the title Assumption names, is answered differently by the sources on the timeline: see Josephus, Ambrose (on “The Fathers on the death”), and Stokes (on “Modern scholarship”).
