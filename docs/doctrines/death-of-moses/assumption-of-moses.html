The Assumption of Moses — the lost apocryphon behind Jude 9

Dates: c. AD 7–30 (a Palestinian Jewish work of the early first century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The book the New Testament book of Jude drew on for its notice of Michael and the devil disputing over the body of Moses. It survives only in a single mutilated Latin manuscript — which breaks off before the disputed scene — and in a handful of quotations preserved by the Fathers. Its very title, Assumption, is the seed of the question whether Moses was buried or taken up.

What the book is, and what survives of it

The Assumption of Moses (Greek Analēpsis Mōuseōs) is a Jewish pseudepigraphon cast as Moses’ farewell testament to Joshua on the eve of his death: he foretells Israel’s history down to the author’s own day, exhorts Joshua, and (in the lost close) dies. It is preserved in one witness: a sixth-century Latin palimpsest in the Ambrosian Library at Milan, discovered and first published by Antonio Maria Ceriani in 1861.1 That Latin text is a fragment — perhaps a third to a half of the work is gone — and, crucially, it breaks off before the end: it contains no account of Moses’ death, no burial, and no dispute between Michael and Satan over the body. The scene the Apostle Jude alludes to stood in the now-missing conclusion.2

“Assumption” or “Testament”? — the title problem

The surviving Latin reads like a Testament (a deathbed discourse), not an Assumption (a taking-up narrative): there is no ascent in the extant part at all. Yet the ancient book-lists name two Moses-apocrypha — a Testament of Moses (Diathēkē Mōuseōs) and an Assumption / Ascension of Moses (Analēpsis) — and the Stichometry of Nicephorus assigns them different lengths (1,100 and 1,400 lines).3 R. H. Charles, whose 1897 edition remains a landmark, argued that these were “two originally independent works subsequently put together,” the extant Latin being the Testament. The now-standard view, framed by Richard Bauckham and the critical edition of Johannes Tromp, is subtler: the extant text is the Testament of Moses, and the Assumption was most likely a later rewritten version of it — which would explain why the ancient lists carry both titles for what is essentially one tradition.4 Either way, the disputed scene belongs to the lost ending, and the manner of Moses’ passing that the title Assumption advertises is precisely what the surviving pages no longer preserve.

The dispute over the body, reconstructed from the fragments

Because the Latin fails us, the content of the scene must be pieced together from the Fathers who read the whole book. Two strands emerge. In the older, murder-and-burial strand — the one Bauckham identifies as Jude’s actual source — the archangel Michael is sent to bury Moses, and Satan opposes the honourable burial by slandering Moses as a murderer (he had killed the Egyptian, Exodus 2:12); Michael, refusing to trade insult for insult, answers only “The Lord rebuke thee” — the very words the angel of the Lord speaks to Satan in Zechariah 3:2. A scholion on Jude preserves the fullest form:

For the devil also slandered Moses, calling him a murderer because he smote the Egyptian. Michael the archangel, not tolerating his slander, said to him, “May the Lord God rebuke thee, devil!”

— scholion on Jude 9, as gathered and translated in Bauckham, Jude, 2 Peter.5 A second, later strand recasts the quarrel in anti-Gnostic terms: the devil claims the corpse as his own “because I am the master of matter,” and Michael rebukes him as one who would deny that God made the material world. Bauckham judges this the Assumption’s second-century Christian reworking, not the original.6 The point of the older story is Moses’ supreme worthiness: despite the blot on his record, he is vindicated as worthy of burial by God’s own archangel.

How Jude uses it — and the canon question

Jude 9 does not quote the book; it alludes to its climactic scene as a known and trustworthy tradition, making Michael’s restraint a model against the “railing accusations” of the false teachers he opposes. This is exactly parallel to Jude’s other use of a non-canonical Jewish work — his direct quotation, five verses later, of the prophecy of 1 Enoch 1:9 (Jude 14–15). Whether drawing on such books implies that Jude regarded them as Scripture is the question weighed on the argument page “What did Jude 9 quote?” Bauckham’s answer — that valuing a tradition as true need not canonize the book it comes from, as Qumran valued Enoch without placing it in a canon — is set out there.7 A telling reception-history footnote: 2 Peter 2:11, which reworks Jude’s letter, keeps the moral but strips out the identifiable Moses-dispute — often read as an early unease with Jude’s apocryphal source.8

Notes

  • The book’s date (c. AD 7–30) makes it a near-contemporary of the New Testament: it looks back on Herod the Great’s death and the war of Varus (4 BC) but still expects the Temple to stand, and its failed prediction about Herod’s sons points to composition before about AD 30.9
  • The Fathers who name the book as Jude’s source — Clement of Alexandria (“here he confirms the assumption of Moses”) and Origen (who calls it The Ascension of Moses) — are treated on their own pages; the later Greek and Latin commentators who retell the dispute are gathered at “The Fathers retell the dispute.”
  • The bodily-assumption reading the title suggests, weighed against the plain death-notice of Deuteronomy 34, is adjudicated at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”
