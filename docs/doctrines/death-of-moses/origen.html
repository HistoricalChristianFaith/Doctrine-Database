Origen on the death of Moses and the Assumption of Moses

Dates: c. 185–253 (On First Principles c. 229–230) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Father who states outright that Jude 9 is drawn from a book called The Ascension of Moses, and who elsewhere reads a “little book” about “two Moseses” — one dead in the body, one living in the spirit — as a figure of the letter and spirit of the Law.

Claims & evidence

Jude’s Michael-and-devil notice comes from “The Ascension of Moses”

…in the work entitled The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle), the archangel Michael, when disputing with the devil regarding the body of Moses, says that the serpent, being inspired by the devil, was the cause of Adam and Eve’s transgression.

— Origen, On First Principles (De Principiis) III.2.1, read the work. This is the earliest surviving statement that names the apocryphon behind Jude 9. Origen treats the identification as settled and uses the book (without vouching for its authority) to fill in what Michael and the devil said.1

The “little book” of the two Moseses — one dead in the body, one living in the spirit

And indeed, in a certain little book — which, though it is not held in the canon, nevertheless portrays a figure of this mystery — it is related that two Moseses were seen: one alive in the spirit, and the other dead in the body.

— Origen, Homilies on Joshua 2.1, read the work. Preaching on “Moses my servant is dead” (Joshua 1:2), Origen invokes an uncanonical “little book” — which patristic scholarship identifies with the same Assumption of Moses — for its image of a doubled Moses, and allegorizes it: the Moses “dead in the body” is the letter of the Law read as empty, the Moses “living in the spirit” is the Law understood spiritually.2

Notes

  • The Latin of On First Principles (Rufinus’ translation) preserves only that Michael, in the book, blamed the devil-inspired serpent for the Fall; the further detail that the devil claimed the body or charged Moses with the Egyptian’s murder survives in the Greek catena fragments, not here — those are gathered on the Assumption of Moses page.
  • Origen “the ‘two Moseses’ image” leaves the door open both ways: the man dead in the body concedes a real death, while the survival of the book’s title (Ascension) keeps the assumption tradition alive — the crux weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”
  • That the book is “not held in the canon” is Origen’s own qualification — the same measured distance he keeps from 1 Enoch; the canon bearing of Jude’s two apocryphal sources is weighed at “What did Jude 9 quote?”
