What did Jude 9 quote — and does citing the Assumption of Moses bear on the canon?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound that Jude 9 draws on the lost Assumption / Testament of Moses (the ancient and modern near-consensus) — and sound that this no more canonizes that book than Jude’s Enoch quotation canonizes 1 Enoch: Jude uses a Jewish tradition he takes to be true without thereby placing the book in Scripture · advanced by Origen and Clement of Alexandria, and by modern scholarship (Charles, Bauckham, Tromp)

The claim

…in the work entitled The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle), the archangel Michael, when disputing with the devil regarding the body of Moses…

— Origen, On First Principles III.2.1, link. The claim has two parts: (1) that the Michael-and-devil dispute of Jude 9 — a scene found nowhere in the Old Testament — is drawn from the lost Jewish apocryphon the Assumption / Testament of Moses; and (2) that this bears on the canon exactly as Jude’s other non-canonical citation does, his quotation of 1 Enoch 1:9 at Jude 14–15.

Who advances it

The source-identification is old and stable. Clement of Alexandria reads Jude 9 as confirming “the assumption of Moses”; Origen names the book outright as The Ascension of Moses; Didymus the Blind reports that some rejected Jude precisely because it leaned on the Assumptio Moysi. Modern scholarship converges on the same result — R. H. Charles assembled the fragments, and Richard Bauckham and Johannes Tromp made the “lost ending of the Testament of Moses” the standard identification.1 The canon corollary is drawn by the same voices, and by the Fathers who set the boundary: Origen already notes the book is “not held in the canon,” and Bede, commenting on Jude’s Enoch citation, records that Jude was “rejected by many” for leaning on apocrypha yet was kept in Scripture on its own authority.2

The evidence for

The identification. Jude 9 reports a specific narrative — Michael contending with the devil over Moses’ body, refusing a “railing accusation,” and saying only “The Lord rebuke thee” — that has no source in Deuteronomy 34 or anywhere in the Hebrew Bible. Three second- and third-century readers who still had the book (Clement, Origen, Didymus) name it as Jude’s source; the surviving Latin Testament of Moses, though it breaks off before the scene, is a deathbed testament that “must have ended with a story of Moses’ death and burial” (Bauckham), and the dispute survives in catena fragments that dovetail with Jude’s wording (the “Lord rebuke thee” of Zechariah 3:2, the devil’s charge of the Egyptian’s murder). Convergent internal and external evidence make this as secure as a lost-source identification gets.3

The canon corollary. That Jude drew on the book does not entail that he — or the church — held it to be Scripture. Jude’s parallel use of 1 Enoch shows the pattern plainly: he says Enoch “prophesied,” valuing the oracle as a true word, yet, as Bauckham notes, this “need not imply that he regarded the book as canonical Scripture… At Qumran the Enoch literature… [was] valued without being included in the canon.” A first-century Jewish writer could treat a well-known haggadic tradition as true — a real event of Moses’ burial, a real lesson about reviling — without treating its book as inspired. The reception history confirms the instinct ran the other way: 2 Peter 2:11, reworking Jude, keeps the moral but strips the identifiable Moses-dispute; and the canon-defining Fathers who admitted 1 Enoch’s inspiration-language (Tertullian) were overruled by those (Athanasius, Jerome, Augustine) who kept the apocrypha out.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The identification is about as well-founded as the identification of a lost book can be: independent ancient witnesses who had the text, a surviving torso of the very work, and dovetailing fragments. The canon corollary is the mainstream reading of how Jude uses Jewish tradition — citing a true tradition is not the same as canonizing its book, and Jude’s own church-reception (the epistle nearly excluded for its apocryphal leanings) shows the distinction was felt from early on.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two cautions. First, “Assumption of Moses” is a loose label: the extant text is a Testament, the assumption-narrative is lost, and the ancient lists carry two titles — so the precise book Jude used is best called the (lost ending of the) Testament, not flatly “the Assumption of Moses” as later writers did. Second, the canon corollary cuts only against the maximal inference (“Jude quotes it, therefore it is Scripture”); it does not by itself decide the positive status of either book — that Jude’s letter is canonical while its sources are not is a judgement the church made on other grounds, weighed for 1 Enoch at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (rated weak).5

Assessment

Sound. That Jude 9 draws on the lost Testament / Assumption of Moses is the near-consensus of both the Fathers who still read the book and modern critical scholarship, resting on convergent internal and external evidence; the reading is secure, with only the book’s title needing care. And it is equally sound that this citation does not canonize the apocryphon: Jude uses a Jewish tradition he takes to be true, exactly as he uses the prophecy of Enoch, without placing either book in the canon — a distinction the early church itself drew when it kept Jude while setting its sources aside. The maximal apologetic inference (“an inspired author quoted it, so the book is inspired”) does not follow, and was not drawn.
