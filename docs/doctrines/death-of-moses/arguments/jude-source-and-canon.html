What source lies behind Jude 9, and does Jude’s use of it bear on the canon?

Disputed reading · on The Death and Assumption of Moses · witness: Origen

The passage

…in the work entitled The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle), the archangel Michael, when disputing with the devil regarding the body of Moses…

— Origen, On First Principles III.2.1, source.1 The verse Origen comments on reads: “Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee” (Jude 9). Five verses later the same letter quotes a prophecy of Enoch found in 1 Enoch 1:9 (Jude 14–15).

The question

Jude 9 refers to a dispute over the body of Moses that the Hebrew Bible does not narrate. Disputed are which writing the scene came from and what that writing told, and whether Jude’s use of a book outside the canon bears on the standing of that book or of Jude’s own letter.

Jude drew on the lost ending of the Assumption (or Testament) of Moses

On this reading the scene comes from a Jewish book about Moses’ end, whose closing section, now lost, told of his burial and the devil’s challenge to it.

Held by: Clement of Alexandria (Adumbrationes on Jude, c. 200); Origen (On First Principles, c. 229–230); R. H. Charles (The Assumption of Moses, 1897); Richard Bauckham (Jude, 2 Peter, 1983)2

The early witnesses tie the verse to a named book. Clement remarks on Jude 9, “Here he confirms the assumption of Moses”; Origen calls it “The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle).” Charles prints these, with Didymus the Blind, among the patristic witnesses to the book.2 The book itself survives in one sixth-century Latin palimpsest, published by Ceriani in 1861, which breaks off before Moses’ death. Bauckham writes that “there is widespread agreement that Jude’s source in v 9 was the lost ending of the work… more appropriately known as the T. Mos.,” and that, as a testament, it “must have ended with a story of Moses’ death and burial.” From the catena fragments and scholia he reconstructs the scene: Michael is sent to bury Moses; the devil slanders him as a murderer, for the Egyptian he killed, to deny him honourable burial; and Michael answers only “The Lord rebuke thee,” the words of Zechariah 3:2. A scholion on Jude 9 gives it: “For the devil also slandered Moses, calling him a murderer because he smote the Egyptian.”3 Bauckham separates this murder-and-burial strand from a “master of matter” form, which he takes for a second-century Christian reworking. He treats the Assumption as a rewritten Testament, which accounts for the two titles in the ancient book-lists.4 On this reading the identification rests on witnesses who still had the book, a surviving portion of it, and fragments that match Jude’s wording.

Jude used a tradition he held true without making its book Scripture

On this reading Jude could take a story from a book outside the canon as true, and the letter could be received, without the book being received.

Held by: Origen (Homilies on Joshua); Bede (Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, before 735); Richard Bauckham (Jude, 2 Peter, 1983)5

Origen calls Jude “the Apostle” while citing the book he names, and in the Homilies on Joshua he draws on “a certain little book” which, “though it is not held in the canon, nevertheless portrays a figure of this mystery”: the “two Moseses… one alive in the spirit, and the other dead in the body.” Charles and Bauckham identify that little book with the Assumption.5 Bede states the principle for Jude’s other citation: the Book of Enoch “is classified among the apocryphal Scriptures by the Church, not because the sayings of such a great patriarch can be in any way dismissed or should be considered false, but because the book offered under his name does not seem to have been truly written by him.” Of the letter he writes that “due to its authority, antiquity, and use, it has merited to be counted among the holy Scriptures, especially because Jude took such testimony from an apocryphal book which is conspicuous for the clear truth of its true light.”6 Bauckham reads Jude’s use of 1 Enoch the same way: the word “prophesied” “indicates that Jude regarded the prophecies in 1 Enoch as inspired by God,” but “need not imply that he regarded the book as canonical Scripture. At Qumran… the Enoch literature and other apocryphal works were evidently valued without being included in the canon of Scripture.”7 On this reading a first-century Jewish writer could cite a known tradition about Moses’ burial as a true lesson against reviling, and the letter’s reception and the book’s were separate matters.

Jude’s use of the apocryphon counted against the letter

On this reading a letter that drew on books outside the canon was itself open to doubt on that account.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The view is known through those who report it. Didymus the Blind, commenting on Jude 9, writes of opponents who raise an objection against both the letter and the book on account of this verse: “Adversarii hujus contemplationis praescribunt praesenti epistolae et Moyseos Assumptioni propter eum locum ubi significatur verbum Archangeli de corpore Moyseos ad diabolum factum.”8 Bede, on Jude’s Enoch citation, reports that “this same Epistle of Jude, because it bears testimony from an apocryphal book, was rejected by many in the early times.”6 On this reading the link between the letter and the apocryphon was close enough that one judgement carried over to the other: the passage about the archangel’s word over Moses’ body was the point at which the objection fastened, and the citation of Enoch was the ground on which “many” set the letter aside.

The source is uncertain, and the scene can be read through Zechariah 3

On this reading Jude’s source cannot be fixed, and the scene is best understood from the contest between the angel and Satan in Zechariah.

Held by: Bede (Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, before 735)9

Bede writes: “From which Scriptures Jude took this testimony is not easily clear. But it should be known that we find something similar to these things in the prophet Zechariah,” where Satan stands to oppose the high priest and “the Lord said to Satan: The Lord rebuke you, Satan.” Rufinus had already set the rebuke beside Zechariah: “Even in Zechariah, we read something similar to this.”10 Bede reads that scene as a contest over the people of Israel returning from captivity, Satan “not wanting the people of God to be liberated.” Of Jude’s dispute itself he writes: “we have no certainty. However, there are those who say that the same people of God were called the body of Moses, because Moses himself was part of that people; and so Jude, having read what was done about the people, could rightly say it was done about the body of Moses.” On this reading the verse’s lesson stands whatever its source: “wherever and whenever this dispute between the angel and the devil took place,” if Michael restrained himself with “modest speech,” “how much more should all blasphemy be avoided by men.”9

The dispute was over Moses’ bodily ascent, not his burial

On this reading the scene Jude alludes to concerned Moses being taken up bodily into God’s presence, and the devil’s objection was to that ascent.

Held by: Ryan E. Stokes (“Not over Moses’ Dead Body,” Journal for the Study of the New Testament, 2017)11

Stokes reads Jude 9, together with Jude 22–24, against its early Jewish context and argues that the Michael–devil dispute concerned not the burial of a dead body but Moses’ bodily ascent, the devil objecting that Moses in the flesh was unfit for heaven.11 The book was known by titles that name such an event: Analēpsis Mōuseōs in the Greek lists, Origen’s “The Ascension of Moses,” and the Assumptio Moysi of Didymus. The surviving Latin breaks off before Moses’ end, so the content of the lost close is known only through later quotation.12 On this reading the title under which Origen and Didymus knew the book, and the scene Jude cites, describe the same event: Moses taken up, and an archangel answering the accuser with “The Lord rebuke thee.”
