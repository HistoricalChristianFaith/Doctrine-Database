Josephus on the death of Moses

Dates: c. AD 93–94 (the Antiquities) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable interpreter of Moses’ end after the New Testament. Josephus narrates that a cloud came over Moses and he “disappeared” — yet notes that Moses himself wrote that he died, and did so deliberately, to forestall the claim that he had been taken up to God. Josephus thus preserves, and pointedly checks, the first-century tradition of a translated Moses.

Claims & evidence

A cloud comes over Moses and he “disappears” — but he wrote that he died

…and as he was going to embrace Eleazar and Joshua, and was still discoursing with them, a cloud stood over him on the sudden, and he disappeared in a certain valley, although he wrote in the holy books that he died, which was done out of fear, lest they should venture to say that, because of his extraordinary virtue, he went to God.

— Flavius Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews IV.8.48 (4.326), trans. William Whiston, source. Josephus keeps the marvel — the sudden cloud, the vanishing — but immediately subordinates it to Scripture’s own word: Moses “wrote… that he died.”

The disappearance is framed as a guard against deification

The striking move is the motive Josephus supplies. Moses recorded his own death, he says, “out of fear, lest they should venture to say that… he went to God” — that is, to stop his followers from concluding, from so extraordinary a departure, that he had escaped death altogether and been assumed, as a god or demigod. The scene reads against the grain of the Greco-Roman apotheosis stories (Aeneas, Romulus vanishing into a cloud) even as it borrows their imagery: Josephus grants the wonder its dramatic force and then denies it its pagan conclusion. The authoritative datum, on his own telling, is the written record that Moses died.1

Notes

  • Josephus is the hinge witness for the death-versus-assumption question: he attests that a “translated Moses” reading was live in the first century (why else forestall it?), yet rules for the death-notice of Deuteronomy 34:5. The tension is weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”
  • His “lest they should say he went to God” anticipates the rabbinic minority of b. Sotah 13b that Moses “stands and serves” above, and the very title of the Assumption of Moses — the traditions Josephus is writing to restrain.
