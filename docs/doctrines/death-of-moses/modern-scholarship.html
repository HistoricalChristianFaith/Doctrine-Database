Modern scholarship on the death and assumption of Moses

Dates: 1861–2017 (a consolidated survey block; last on the timeline) · Wikipedia
Summary: Modern scholars published the surviving manuscript of the lost apocryphon (Ceriani, 1861), assembled the patristic fragments and dated the book to c. AD 7–30 (Charles, 1897), reconstructed the dispute-scene identified as Jude 9’s source and separated it from a later Christian reworking (Bauckham, 1983), produced a critical edition (Tromp, 1993), and read the dispute as concerning Moses’ ascent rather than his burial (Stokes, 2017).

Claims & evidence

Ceriani recovers the only manuscript (1861)

The text existed, for the modern era, only as a set of patristic quotations until Antonio Maria Ceriani found a sixth-century Latin palimpsest in the Ambrosian Library at Milan and published it as “Fragmenta Assumptionis Mosis.” It is incomplete and breaks off before Moses’ death — so the scene Jude alludes to is not in the recovered text.1

Charles assembles the fragments and dates the book (1897)

R. H. Charles’s edition gathered the scattered patristic witnesses to the lost ending (Clement, Origen, Didymus, Gelasius, the scholia), argued that Testament and Assumption were two works later joined, and dated the composition to roughly AD 7–30 — a Palestinian Jewish work contemporary with the New Testament.2

Bauckham reconstructs the lost ending and distinguishes two traditions (1983)

Richard Bauckham’s commentary on Jude reconstructs the lost ending: Michael, sent to bury Moses, is opposed by Satan, who slanders Moses as a murderer (the Egyptian) to deny him honourable burial; Michael answers only “The Lord rebuke thee,” and buries the body, “so that no one saw the burial of Moses.” He distinguishes this murder-and-burial tradition, which he takes as the older and as Jude’s source (which he identifies with the Testament), from a later, anti-Gnostic “master of matter” version (a second-century Christian reworking, the Assumption proper) — and, unlike Charles, treats the two not as separate compositions but as a work and its later rewrite.3

Tromp’s critical edition (1993)

Johannes Tromp produced a critical edition and commentary under the title The Assumption of Moses. Following Ceriani, he identifies the surviving manuscript with the Assumption, on the strength of a match between its 1:14 and a quotation in Gelasius of Cyzicus; he describes the work as a first-century Palestinian Jewish pseudepigraphon.4

Stokes: a dispute over Moses’ ascent (2017)

Ryan E. Stokes, in contrast to Bauckham’s burial reconstruction, argues that the Michael–devil dispute concerned not the burial of Moses’ dead body but his bodily ascent into God’s presence — the devil objecting that fleshly Moses was unfit for heaven.5

Reception and debate

  • Bauckham reports “widespread agreement that Jude’s source in v 9 was the lost ending” of the Testament of Moses; this and the other readings of the verse are set out at “What source lies behind Jude 9?”6
  • On whether Moses died or was taken up, Stokes’s reconstruction may be read beside Josephus and Ambrose (on “The Fathers on the death”).
