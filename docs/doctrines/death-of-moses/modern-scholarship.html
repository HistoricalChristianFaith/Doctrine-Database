Modern scholarship on the death and assumption of Moses

Dates: 1861–2017 (a consolidated survey block; last on the timeline) · Wikipedia
Summary: Critical study recovered the physical remains of the lost apocryphon (Ceriani, 1861), assembled the patristic fragments and dated the book to the age of Jesus (Charles, 1897), reconstructed the dispute-scene that Jude 9 drew on and separated it from a later Christian reworking (Bauckham, 1983), produced the standard critical edition (Tromp, 1993), and reopened the death-versus-ascent question (Stokes, 2017).

Claims & evidence

Ceriani recovers the only manuscript (1861)

The text existed, for the modern era, only as a set of patristic quotations until Antonio Maria Ceriani found a sixth-century Latin palimpsest in the Ambrosian Library at Milan and published it as “Fragmenta Assumptionis Mosis.” It is incomplete and breaks off before Moses’ death — so the very scene Jude alludes to is not in the recovered text.1

Charles assembles the fragments and dates the book (1897)

R. H. Charles’s edition gathered the scattered patristic witnesses to the lost ending (Clement, Origen, Didymus, Gelasius, the scholia), argued that Testament and Assumption were two works later joined, and dated the composition to roughly AD 7–30 — a Palestinian Jewish work contemporary with the New Testament. His collection of the fragments remains the starting point for the reconstruction.2

Bauckham reconstructs Jude’s source and splits two traditions (1983)

Richard Bauckham’s commentary is the standard treatment of Jude 9. He reconstructs the lost ending: Michael, sent to bury Moses, is opposed by Satan, who slanders Moses as a murderer (the Egyptian) to deny him honourable burial; Michael answers only “The Lord rebuke thee,” and buries the body, “so that no one saw the burial of Moses.” He distinguishes this older murder-and-burial tradition (Jude’s actual source, likely the Testament) from a later, anti-Gnostic “master of matter” version (a second-century Christian reworking, the Assumption proper) — and reframes Charles: the two are not separate compositions but a work and its later rewrite.3

Tromp’s critical edition (1993)

Johannes Tromp produced the current standard critical edition and commentary, editing the surviving text as the Testament of Moses and questioning the traditional title “Assumption” altogether — the extant work being a first-century Palestinian Jewish pseudepigraphon with no assumption-narrative in what survives.4

Stokes reopens death-versus-ascent (2017)

Against the dominant “burial of a corpse” reconstruction, Ryan E. Stokes argues that the Michael–devil dispute concerned not the burial of Moses’ dead body but his bodily ascent into God’s presence — the devil objecting that fleshly Moses was unfit for heaven. The reading keeps the minority “assumption” tradition scholarly-live and bears directly on the crux weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”5

Notes

  • The consensus that Jude 9’s source is the lost ending of the Testament / Assumption of Moses (Charles, Bauckham, Tromp, following Origen and Clement) is the basis of the argument “What did Jude 9 quote?”
  • Stokes’s minority reconstruction is the one scholarly prop, alongside Josephus and a rabbinic minority, for the bodily-assumption reading — a genuinely-advocated but outweighed option, not a consensus.
