The Fathers retell the dispute over the body of Moses

Dates: c. 386–1090 (a consolidated block; head-dated to its earliest voice, Jerome) · Wikipedia
Summary: How the Greek and Latin commentators unpacked Jude 9. Two things recur: a moral reading — even Michael would not hurl a “railing accusation” at the devil, but left judgment to God, as the angel does in Zechariah 3 — and a narrative reading drawn from the apocryphon: the devil tried to block Moses’ honourable burial by charging him with the murder of the Egyptian.

Claims & evidence

Michael’s restraint: judgment left to God (Jerome and Rufinus), with the Zechariah 3 parallel

For when Michael the Archangel disputed with the devil over the body of Moses, he did not dare to bring a judgment of blasphemy, but said, “May God rebuke you”… The devil deserved a curse: but it should not have come out through the mouth of an archangel.

— Jerome, Commentary on Titus (on 3:1–2), read the work. Jerome makes Jude 9 a lesson in the restraint of speech: if even Michael would not revile the devil, how much more must Christians keep “pure from every curse.”1 Rufinus draws the same lesson and supplies the Old Testament parallel outright:

Thus also Michael, when he was arguing with the devil over the body of Moses, did not dare to bring an accusation of blasphemy against him… but said, “May the Lord rebuke you.” Even in Zechariah, we read something similar to this. “The Lord rebuke you, O Satan, and the Lord that chose Jerusalem rebuke you.”

— Rufinus of Aquileia, Apology, quoted in Jerome, Against Rufinus 2.18, source. The rebuke Michael speaks is verbatim the angel’s word to Satan in Zechariah 3:2 — the scriptural key several Fathers used to license Jude’s otherwise-unknown scene.2

The narrative: the devil charges Moses with the Egyptian’s murder to block the burial (Oecumenius)

It is said that Michael the archangel provided ministry in burying Moses: the Devil could not bear this but brought an accusation because of the killing of the Egyptian, as if it were Moses’ own, and therefore did not allow him to obtain an honorable burial.

— Oecumenius, Commentary on Jude, read the work. Oecumenius reports the apocryphal story in full and reads it doctrinally: it shows “that the same God is of the Old and New Testament,” and that after death the devil “rises against our souls… but the good angels of God are there to assist them: as blessed Anthony also saw” — while Michael still leaves the verdict to God, “May God rebuke you, O Devil.”3

“In the apocryphal writings it is said” (Theophylact of Ohrid)

In the apocryphal writings it is said: the Archangel Michael ministered at the burial of the body of Moses; the devil would not allow this, but charged Moses with the guilt of killing the Egyptian, and on account of such guilt declared Moses unworthy of burial.

— Theophylact of Ohrid, Commentary on Jude, read the work. Theophylact is the commentator who states plainly that Jude’s source is “the apocryphal writings” — naming the genre Clement and Origen had identified as the Assumption of Moses.4

The dispute as angelic warfare over the soul’s ascent (Andreas of Caesarea)

Michael is said to have taken care of the burial of Moses, and the devil is supposed to have objected to this. God then came to the rescue and wanted to show those who at that time saw only a very little that eventually our souls would be changed and we would all ascend into heaven. But the devil and the evil spirits with him wanted to cut off the way to heaven… This is what the blessed Antony saw in his vision.

— Andreas of Caesarea, Commentary on Jude (catena), on Jude 9. Andreas reads the quarrel over the corpse as a figure of the greater contest over every departing soul — the devil trying to “cut off the way to heaven,” the angels defending it, as in Antony’s vision.5

The Western hesitation: an uncertain source, and “the body of Moses” as Israel (Bede)

From which Scriptures Jude took this testimony is not easily clear… But when Michael contended with the devil about the body of Moses, we have no certainty. However, there are those who say that the same people of God were called the body of Moses, because Moses himself was part of that people.

— Bede, Commentary on the Catholic Epistles (on Jude 9), read the work. Bede, wary of the apocryphon, offers the Zechariah 3 parallel and floats an allegorical escape — “the body of Moses” as the people of Israel, over whom Satan and the angel contend as in Zechariah. In his note on Jude 15 he registers the parallel canon worry: because Jude also cites the apocryphal Book of Enoch, the epistle itself “was rejected by many.”6

Notes

  • The story every one of these commentators retells — Michael burying Moses, the devil objecting — presupposes a corpse: it is a reading built on Moses’ real death, and it is the weightiest patristic mark against a bodily assumption (weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”).
  • Bede’s and Theophylact’s awareness that Jude leans on non-canonical books is the seam the canon argument works: “What did Jude 9 quote?”
