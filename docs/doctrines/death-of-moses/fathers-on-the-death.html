The Fathers on the death of Moses and the hidden grave

Dates: c. 340–440 (a consolidated block; head-dated to its earliest voice, Aphrahat) · Wikipedia
Summary: Fourth- and fifth-century readings of Deuteronomy 34:5–6. Aphrahat, Gregory of Nyssa, Paulinus of Nola and Peter Chrysologus write of Moses’ death and of a grave no one can find, reading the hidden sepulchre as a safeguard against making his tomb a place of worship, a singular honour, and a death “more sublime than his life”; Ambrose reads it to mean “that he was taken up into heaven rather than buried.”

Claims & evidence

The grave was hidden lest it become a place of worship (Aphrahat)

Two godly benefits did his Lord accomplish for Moses in not making known his tomb to the children of Israel. He rejoiced that his adversaries should not know it and cast forth his bones from his tomb; and in the second place, that the children of his people should not know it and make his tomb a place of worship, for he was accounted as God in the eyes of the children of his people.

— Aphrahat the Persian Sage, Demonstration 8.9, on Deuteronomy 34:6. For Aphrahat the hidden grave is a mercy: it keeps Moses’ bones from desecration and keeps a people who “accounted” him “as God” from turning his tomb into a shrine (compare the motive Josephus gives for Moses’ record of his death).1

“Taken up into heaven rather than buried” (Ambrose of Milan)

We read that he died by the word of God—for a God does not grow weak or undergo diminution or addition. Hence Scripture added: No man hath known of his sepulture until this present day—by which we are to understand that he was taken up into heaven rather than buried, for death may be called a separation of the soul from the body. He died, therefore, as the Scripture states: by the word of God—not “in accordance with the word”—so as to make known that this was not an announcement of his death, but was more in the nature of a gracious gift to one who was translated rather than left here, and whose sepulture was known to no man.

— Ambrose of Milan, On Cain and Abel I.2.8, read the work, trans. John J. Savage. Ambrose writes that Moses “died by the word of God,” and reads the unknown sepulchre to mean “that he was taken up into heaven rather than buried” — one “translated rather than left here.”2

A death “more sublime than his life” (Gregory of Nyssa)

Moses’ death is recorded to have been even more sublime than his life. He died on a mountain peak and left behind neither trace nor memorial of his earthly burden in life. The impress of beauty was not altered by time but remained unchangeable in the changeable nature.

— Gregory of Nyssa, On the Inscriptions of the Psalms I.56, on Deuteronomy 34:5. Gregory writes of a death on a mountain peak that left “neither trace nor memorial,” and of a beauty that “remained unchangeable in the changeable nature.”3

The secret grave as a unique divine honour (Paulinus of Nola)

God had granted the gift of being buried in secret ground to only one of his friends, for it was right that so great a distinction should be appropriate only for that body which had shone from the close presence and conversation of God. Thus when Moses had performed his duties as man, he could take joy in having God alone as witness of his grave.

— Paulinus of Nola, Poems 15.213, on Deuteronomy 34:6. For Paulinus the secret burial is a distinction reserved for the one body that had “shone from the close presence… of God” — God himself, “alone as witness of his grave.”4

Angels present, God himself the undertaker (Peter Chrysologus)

The angels were present at the death of Moses, and God himself took care of his burial.

— Peter Chrysologus, Sermons 83, on Deuteronomy 34:6. Chrysologus writes that Moses died, angels attended, and God buried him.5

Reception and debate

  • A burial by the archangel Michael, opposed by the devil, stands in Bauckham’s reconstruction of the lost ending of the Assumption of Moses; the Greek commentators’ retellings are gathered at “The Fathers retell the dispute.”
  • Whether Moses died or was taken up is answered differently by the sources on the timeline; besides Ambrose, see Josephus and the modern scholarship entry.
