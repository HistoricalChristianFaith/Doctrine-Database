The Fathers on the death of Moses and the hidden grave

Dates: c. 340–440 (a consolidated block; head-dated to its earliest voice, Aphrahat) · Wikipedia
Summary: The mainstream patristic reading of Deuteronomy 34:5–6 — Moses died and God buried him in a grave no one can find. The Fathers turn the hidden sepulchre in three directions: a safeguard against making his tomb an idol, a singular honour, and a death “more sublime than his life” — and one, Ambrose, presses it toward a “translation rather than a demise.”

Claims & evidence

The grave was hidden lest it become a place of worship (Aphrahat)

Two godly benefits did his Lord accomplish for Moses in not making known his tomb to the children of Israel. He rejoiced that his adversaries should not know it and cast forth his bones from his tomb; and in the second place, that the children of his people should not know it and make his tomb a place of worship, for he was accounted as God in the eyes of the children of his people.

— Aphrahat the Persian Sage, Demonstration 8.9, on Deuteronomy 34:6. The hidden grave is a mercy: it keeps Moses’ bones from desecration and, more tellingly, keeps a people who “accounted” him “as God” from turning his tomb into a shrine — the same anti-deification instinct Josephus voiced.1

“A translation rather than his demise” (Ambrose of Milan)

…he died through the word of God. For God neither experiences defection or diminution, nor does he undergo addition. Hence, the Scripture also adds: “For no one knows his burial place until this day,” so that you understand it as a translation rather than his demise. For death is a certain separation of the soul and body. Therefore, he was dead through the word of God, as Scripture says, not according to the flesh… who was transferred rather than abandoned, of whom no one knows the burial.

— Ambrose of Milan, On Cain and Abel I.2.8, read the work. Ambrose is the one Latin Father who leans the hidden-grave datum toward the assumption reading: he grants that Moses “died,” but reads “by the word of God” and the unknown sepulchre as marking “a translation rather than a demise” — Moses “transferred rather than abandoned.” He is the patristic anchor for the minority weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”2

A death “more sublime than his life” (Gregory of Nyssa)

Moses’ death is recorded to have been even more sublime than his life. He died on a mountain peak and left behind neither trace nor memorial of his earthly burden in life. The impress of beauty was not altered by time but remained unchangeable in the changeable nature.

— Gregory of Nyssa, On the Inscriptions of the Psalms I.56, on Deuteronomy 34:5. Gregory keeps the death but empties it of everything ordinary — a passing on a summit that leaves “neither trace nor memorial,” the very absence of a grave becoming a sign of the man’s incorrupt beauty.3

The secret grave as a unique divine honour (Paulinus of Nola)

God had granted the gift of being buried in secret ground to only one of his friends, for it was right that so great a distinction should be appropriate only for that body which had shone from the close presence and conversation of God. Thus when Moses had performed his duties as man, he could take joy in having God alone as witness of his grave.

— Paulinus of Nola, Poems 15.213, on Deuteronomy 34:6. The secret burial is not a loss but a prize reserved for the one body that had “shone from the close presence… of God” — God himself its “only witness.”4

Angels present, God himself the undertaker (Peter Chrysologus)

The angels were present at the death of Moses, and God himself took care of his burial.

— Peter Chrysologus, Sermons 83, on Deuteronomy 34:6. Chrysologus states the plain majority position with no assumption at all: Moses died, angels attended, and God buried him — the reading that the dispute of Jude 9 presupposes (there is a body to bury).5

Notes

  • The angelic burial these Fathers describe is exactly what the Assumption of Moses dramatized: Michael sent to bury Moses, the devil contesting it — the scene retold in full by the later Greek commentators at “The Fathers retell the dispute.”
  • Ambrose excepted, the block reads for a real death; the minority pull he represents (and Josephus before him) is weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”
