Synesius of Cyrene on the discernment of dreams

Dates: c. 405 · Wikipedia
Summary: In On Dreams (De insomniis) — a Neoplatonic treatise the philosopher-bishop of Ptolemais sent to Hypatia of Alexandria — Synesius shifts the discernment question from the dream's source to the dreamer's instrument. Dreams work through the "imaginative spirit" (the phantastikon pneuma), the soul's first vehicle; kept pure it "receives true impressions of things," but defiled by a life sunk "in matter" it passes its life "in error." The discriminator, then, is the moral state of the seer — and, because every sleeper carries this instrument, dream-divination is a possession "common to all," repudiating "neither race, nor age, nor condition, nor calling."

Claims & evidence

Dreams work through the imaginative spirit — an instrument kept ready by a pure life

Whoever keeps this purified by a life in accordance with nature has an instrument ready to his hand, and one that is thus again common to all, for this pneuma comprehends our spiritual disposition…

— On Dreams §4, trans. A. Fitzgerald. The imaginative spirit (phantastikon pneuma) is the soul's first "vehicle," the faculty through which images reach us in sleep; keeping it clean by a right life makes it a reliable organ of true dreaming.

A pure spirit yields true dreams; a matter-sunk spirit yields error

[If] a man's imaginative pneuma is pure and well-defined, and whether he is waking or sleeping receives true impressions of things, it promises him a better lot, so far as the soul's formation is concerned.

— On Dreams §6. The converse is the source of false and confused dreams: "Error in predictions of the future is a never-ending experience; passion and ambition belong to those who wallow in matter" (§6), and, of the soul's conduct, "the best conduct in the case of souls lightens it, whereas the worst imparts a stain to them" (§5). The dream's reliability tracks not its subject-matter but the purity of the receiving spirit — Synesius's answer to the same problem the Fathers meet by sorting God from demon from body.

The spirit's own state can make it "god, demon, and phantom"

This envelope of soul-matter which the happy have called the enveloping soul, is in turn god, demon of every sort, and phantom, and in it the soul pays its penalties…

— On Dreams §5. Where the Fathers locate three external sources (God, demons, the body), Synesius internalizes the trichotomy: the one imaginative spirit becomes divine, demonic, or illusory according to its own condition — "there is a penalty for the godless who have defiled the divine part in them" (§5). Discernment is therefore inseparable from self-purification.

Dream-divination belongs to everyone alike — the "democratic" oracle

… the dream is visible to the man who is worth five hundred medimni, and equally to the possessor of three hundred, to the teamster no less than to the peasant who tills the boundary land for a livelihood, to the galleyslave and the common laborer alike, to the exempted and to the payer of taxes.

— On Dreams §2. Unlike the external divinations that need shrine, water, chasm, or travel, "each one of us is perforce his own instrument, so much so that it is not possible to desert our oracle… even if we so desired" (§7); and so "this accessibility to all makes divination very humane… its freedom from violence gives it sanctity" (§3). The oracle of dreams "repudiates neither race, nor age, nor condition, nor calling. She is present to every one, everywhere" (§8).

Notes

  • Synesius stands apart from the Christian Fathers on either side of him: his frame is Neoplatonic (the pneumatic vehicle of the soul, its ascent and descent through matter), and he weighs dreams less as messages to be attributed than as a mirror of the dreamer's own state. Yet he serves the same doctrine — that a dream's truth is not read off its content but discerned — and adds the mechanism the Fathers leave unspecified.
  • The contrast with Origen is instructive. Both make the receiving faculty decisive, but Origen restricts true discernment to "the blessed man… alone," while Synesius makes the capacity for divinatory dreaming a possession "common to all" — purity determines the quality of one's dreams, not whether one has access to them at all.
  • He shades toward the wider late-antique world of divination by the heavens, which he treats as a lower, external art next to the interior oracle of dreams.
