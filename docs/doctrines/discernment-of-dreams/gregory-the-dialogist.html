Gregory the Great on the discernment of dreams

Dates: c. 593 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Dialogues IV.48 Gregory gives a classification of six kinds of dream and holds that the evil source mixes truth into falsehood to win credit, so that because dreams "grow from such divers roots" they are the harder to trust; holy men, he writes, discern true revelation from illusion "by a certain inward spiritual taste."

Claims & evidence

Six kinds of dream

Concerning this point, Peter, you must understand that there are six kind of dreams. For sometime they proceed of too much fulness or emptiness of the stomach: sometime by illusion: sometime both by thought and illusion: sometime by revelation: and sometime both by thought and revelation. The two first all by experience know to be true: and the four latter we find mentioned in holy scripture.

— Dialogues IV.48, tr. P.W. 1608 (ed. Gardner, 1911) (also historicalchristian.faith). Gregory's six kinds draw on four causes: the stomach (too full or too empty), illusion (which he ascribes in the next passage to "the wicked spirit"), revelation, and the dreamer's own thought, which may join either of the last two. He anchors the revelation-dreams in Scripture — Joseph's dream of exaltation (Gen. 37), the angel warning Joseph to flee to Egypt (Matt. 2:13), Nebuchadnezzar (Dan. 2) — and the thought-mixed dreams in "Dreams follow many cares" (Eccles. 5:2).

The wicked spirit foretells true things — to ensnare the soul with one lie

But seeing dreams do grow from such divers roots, with so much the more difficulty ought we to believe them: because it doth not easily appear unto us, from what cause they do proceed. Holy men, indeed, by a certain inward spiritual taste, do discern betwixt illusions and true revelations… so that they know what they receive from the good spirit, and what they suffer by illusion from the wicked: and therefore, if our mind be not herein very attentive and vigilant, it falleth into many vanities, through the deceit of the wicked spirit: who sometime useth to foretell many true things, that, in the end, he may by some falsehood ensnare our soul.

— Dialogues IV.48. Here Gregory gives his reason why a true-seeming dream is not to be trusted on its content: in his account the deceiver salts his illusions with truths to earn belief, then springs "some falsehood." The safeguard he names is the discernment of holy men, "by a certain inward spiritual taste."

Gregory's scriptural warnings: Sirach and the Law

For if dreams did not sometime proceed by illusion from our secret enemy, never would the wise man have said: Dreams have made many to err, and hoping in them have they been deceived: and again: [Ye] shall not be soothsayers, nor observe dreams: by which words we see how they are to be detested, that are compared with sooth-sayings.

— Dialogues IV.48, quoting Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) 34:7 and the Law's prohibition of observing dreams (cf. Lev. 19:26). Gregory sets Sirach's warning beside the Law's ban on observing dreams, and compares dream-observance with soothsaying, alongside the revelation-dreams he had just cited from Scripture.

Notes

  • Gregory cites Sirach 34:7 as the words of "the wise man," beside the Law. The standing of Sirach in the canon is traced on the Old Testament canon timeline.
  • In the same book of the Dialogues Gregory takes the departed to appear as themselves and solicit the living's prayers; see his detail page on dream-apparitions of the dead. In the Dialogues the two treatments sit together: dreams as a real channel of revelation, and dreams as needing to be sifted from the illusory.
