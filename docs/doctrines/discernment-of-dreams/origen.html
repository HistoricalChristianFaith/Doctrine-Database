Origen on the discernment of dreams

Dates: c. 248 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Against Celsus (Contra Celsum) I.46–48, 66, Origen gives the Greek East's early epistemology of the God-sent dream: a dream carries impressions "some relating to divine things, and others to future events of this life," and it does so through "the mind alone," exactly as a waking vision does — so the divine dream is grasped not by the bodily senses but by "a kind of general divine perception which the blessed man alone knows how to discover." He also concedes the plurality of sources at the root of the discernment problem: the impression may be produced "either by an angel or by some other thing."

Claims & evidence

Dreams carry two sorts of impression — of divine things, and of future events

… as it is a matter of belief that in a dream impressions have been brought before the minds of many, some relating to divine things, and others to future events of this life, and this either with clearness or in an enigmatic manner…

— Against Celsus I.48, Book 1 (also newadvent.org). Origen is defending, against Celsus, the credibility of the vision at Jesus' baptism; his argument turns on a taxonomy of what a dream can convey — divine matters or ordinary futures, plainly or enigmatically — a distinction the whole discernment tradition presupposes.

The dream and the waking vision work by the same faculty — the mind, not the bodily organs

… how is it absurd to say that the mind which could receive impressions in a dream should be impressed also in a waking vision… And as in a dream we fancy that we hear, and that the organs of hearing are actually impressed, and that we see with our eyes—although neither the bodily organs of sight nor hearing are affected, but it is the mind alone which has these sensations—so there is no absurdity in believing that similar things occurred to the prophets.

— Against Celsus I.48. This is the seed of Augustine's later "spiritual" vision (images seen with the senses at rest): the dream is a real perception, but an interior one, so its truth cannot be tested by the bodily eye.

The God-sent dream is perceived by a "divine sense" the holy man alone discovers

… there being, as the Scripture calls it, a kind of general divine perception which the blessed man alone knows how to discover… the blessed prophets having discovered this divine perception, and seeing and hearing in this divine manner… saw in this manner what they record as having seen, and heard what they say they heard.

— Against Celsus I.48. Origen posits a full set of "spiritual senses" — a divine sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch — paralleling the bodily five. The discernment of a divine dream is therefore not a technique open to all but the exercise of a purified faculty: only "the blessed man" perceives rightly. (The contrast with Synesius is exact: Synesius makes dream-divination a possession "common to all," where Origen restricts true discernment to the sanctified.)

Post-apostolic dreams and waking visions still convert and guide — attested firsthand

… many have been converted to Christianity as if against their will, some sort of spirit having suddenly transformed their minds from a hatred of the doctrine to a readiness to die in its defence, and having appeared to them either in a waking vision or a dream of the night. Many such instances have we known…

— Against Celsus I.46. Origen reports God-sent dreams as a live, contemporary phenomenon he has himself witnessed — weighing against the later cessationist claim that revelatory dreams lapsed with the apostolic age.

The impression may come "either by an angel or by some other thing"

… in either passage of Scripture it is stated that it was in a dream that the angel spoke these words; and that in a dream certain persons may have certain things pointed out to them to do, is an event of frequent occurrence to many individuals,—the impression on the mind being produced either by an angel or by some other thing.

— Against Celsus I.66 (on the angel warning Joseph to flee to Egypt). The guarded phrase "or by some other thing" concedes that the same interior mechanism can be worked by more than one agent — which is precisely why a dream's content, even a true and useful one, does not by itself certify its source.

Notes

  • Origen supplies what the earlier Tertullian fourfold division leaves implicit: an account of how a divine dream is received (the interior "mind," the "divine sense") and thus how it is, in principle, told from the merely bodily or the deceptive. The taxonomy of sources and the epistemology of the faculty are the two halves of the discernment problem.
  • Origen is a witness across this cluster: on dream-apparitions of the dead and the wider question of the soul's perception in sleep, the same doctrine of the spiritual senses is at work.
