John Chrysostom on the discernment of dreams

Dates: c. 390 · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching on Matthew's infancy narrative, Chrysostom treats the God-sent dream as a real vehicle of revelation and holds that God fits the mode of revelation to the recipient — a dream for Joseph, "exceedingly full of faith"; a "marvellous vision" for the Virgin, whose tidings were greater; an angel for the shepherds, "as being by disposition rather dull and clownish."

Claims & evidence

God guides Joseph by a dream because his faith needed no more

And why not openly, as to the shepherds, and to Zacharias, and to the Virgin? The man was exceedingly full of faith, and needed not this vision. Whereas the Virgin, as having declared to her very exceeding good tidings, greater than to Zacharias, and this before the event, needed also a marvellous vision; and the shepherds, as being by disposition rather dull and clownish.

— Homilies on the Gospel of St. Matthew, Homily IV.10 (on Matt. 1:20–21), NPNF1 10. Chrysostom takes the dream as a channel fitted to a strong faith: because Joseph "was… full of faith," a quiet dream sufficed where Zacharias, the Virgin, and the "dull and clownish" shepherds were met with open angelic appearances. In his reading, revelation is graded to its hearer.

The dream comes upon Joseph mid-thought

While he thought on these things, the angel appears in a dream.

— Homilies on Matthew, Homily IV.10. Chrysostom stresses the timing: the angel comes as Joseph is turning the matter over, so that in his reading the dream answers a real deliberation. Matthew's Joseph is guided by dreams again at 2:13 and 2:19–22, which Chrysostom also treats as divine communications (see the Notes).

Notes

  • In the homilies on the flight into Egypt (Hom. VIII, on Matt. 2:11–16) Chrysostom's stated reason for God working "hiddenly" — by dreams and quiet providences rather than open miracle — is to safeguard belief in Christ's true humanity: "For if from His earliest infancy He had shown forth wonders, He would not have been accounted a Man." In this reading the dream belongs to the economy of the hidden Incarnation.
  • These homilies do not give an explicit caution to test every dream; their subject is the God-sent dream, apportioned by God to the measure of the recipient. The discernment of demonic and bodily dreams is treated at length on this timeline by Augustine, Cassian, and Gregory.
  • A caution from Chrysostom comes on the sibling question of dream-apparitions of the dead, where (preaching on the rich man and Lazarus) he warns that "the forms of the departed… have appeared in dreams, and have deceived and ruined many." Read together, the two passages treat a dream as able to carry a divine message, and a dream presenting a dead person as not to be trusted as that person.
