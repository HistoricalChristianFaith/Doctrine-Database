Gregory of Nyssa on the discernment of dreams

Dates: c. 379 · Wikipedia
Summary: In On the Making of Man (De opificio hominis) 13 Gregory gives the tradition's fullest physiology of the natural dream — sleep as the by-product of digestion's rising vapours, most dreams mere "shadows and echoes" of the day "pictured as chance will have it," sorted by the state of the body and the character. Yet he expressly carves off a divine class: "some, not all, share by means of their dreams in some more Divine manifestation," and Daniel and Joseph, "instructed by Divine power," are the exception "not [to] class… with common dreams."

Claims & evidence

Most dreams are the soul's own memory-echoes, moulded by chance while reason sleeps

… some shadows and echoes of those things which happen in our waking moments—of the operations both of sense and of intellect—which are impressed upon it by that part of the soul which is capable of memory, that these, I say, are pictured as chance will have it, some echo of memory still lingering in this division of the soul.

— On the Making of Man 13.6, De opificio hominis (also newadvent.org). Because in sleep "the senses are at rest" the mind is inactive, so "the fantastic nonsense which occurs to us in sleep" is not the mind's work but "some appearances of the operations of the mind… accidentally moulded in the less rational part of the soul" (13.5) — which is why the dreamer "often seems to be in absurd and impossible situations."

Dreams are shaped by the body's condition and the dreamer's character

… the thirsty man seems to be among springs, the man who is in need of food to be at a feast, and the young man in the heat of youthful vigour is beset by fancies corresponding to his passion.

— On the Making of Man 13.14. Gregory presses the point with medical detail — "the visions of those of weak stomach are of one kind, those of persons suffering from injury to the cerebral membrane of another, those of persons in fevers of yet another" (13.16) — and with moral detail: "most men's dreams are conformed to the state of their character: the brave man's fancies are of one kind, the coward's of another; the wanton man's dreams of one kind, the continent man's of another" (13.17). This grounds the "bodily" source that Gregory the Great would later split into stomach-full and stomach-empty dreams and Aquinas fold under the "inward… disposition of the body."

A natural "prophecy of the mind" can foretell — without any divine act

… the images of their customary occupations imprinted on the prescient element of their soul, gave them for a time the power of foretelling, by this sort of prophecy on the part of the mind, what should come to pass.

— On the Making of Man 13.11 (Pharaoh's butler and baker, Genesis 40). Gregory allows that even a true foretelling dream may be wholly natural — a "prophecy on the part of the mind" — so a dream's coming true is, for him too, no proof of divine origin. This is the physiological form of the argument the summary rates weak.

Yet a few dreams are truly divine — the exception not to be classed with common dreams

… so, while the imagination of sleep naturally occurs in a like and equivalent manner for all, some, not all, share by means of their dreams in some more Divine manifestation.

— On the Making of Man 13.12. The divine dreams of Daniel and Joseph were given "by Divine power, without any confusion of perception" and belong to a different order: "we must reckon these cases as exceptional, and not class them with common dreams" (13.13). Gregory thus draws the discernment line as a two-source one — the natural (bodily/character/memory) against the divine — with the divine narrowed sharply to "some, not all."

Notes

  • Gregory extends Athanasius's "the soul ranges in sleep" from a proof of immortality into a working physiology of the ordinary dream — and, unlike Athanasius, he explicitly reserves a divine remainder, so his page supplies both the naturalist grounding and the guarded exception the later schemes assume.
  • Notably absent from Gregory's chapter is the demonic source that Tertullian, Augustine, and Cassian make central. His frame here is soul-and-body, not the three-source (God / demon / self) scheme; the discernment he draws is between the natural many and the divine few.
