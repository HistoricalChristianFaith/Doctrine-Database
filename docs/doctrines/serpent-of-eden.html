The Serpent of Eden and the Fall of the Nachash

Genesis 3 opens with a speaking serpent: "now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made" (Genesis 3:1). Revelation calls the dragon "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan" (Revelation 12:9). This timeline follows, earliest extant attestation first, how writers have identified the serpent and its tempter: the Life of Adam and Eve, Irenaeus, Augustine and John Chrysostom describe an animal used as the instrument or mouthpiece of a fallen angel; Justin Martyr counts "him who was called the serpent" among the fallen "princes"; and Michael Heiser and Fr. Stephen De Young read the figure of Genesis 3, the nachash, as a divine or spiritual being of God's heavenly court rather than only an animal. A second thread runs alongside: the reading of Ezekiel 28's "anointed cherub" and Isaiah 14's "Lucifer" as the devil's fall, from Tertullian and Origen to Augustine, and of the cherub as the Eden rebel in Heiser and De Young.

A related reading — that the serpent, or the fallen angel riding it, fathered Cain, Eve's firstborn (the “serpent seed”) — is traced on the Cain timeline.

Related doctrines: The unseen realm — see also the Divine Council and the Sons of God. The sons of God of Genesis 6 are followed on the Nephilim timeline.

Timeline

The Wisdom of Solomon (c. 50 BC)

The Wisdom of Solomon, a Greek-language Jewish wisdom book from Alexandria, attributes the entry of death into the world to the envy of a devil: "through envy of the devil came death into the world" (Wisdom 2:24). The verse names a devil but does not name the serpent.1

The Life of Adam and Eve / Apocalypse of Moses (dated 1st–5th c.)

The Life of Adam and Eve (and its Greek recension, the Apocalypse of Moses) names the devil as the tempter and gives a motive: the devil, cast out of his heavenly glory for refusing to worship the newly-made Adam — "I will set my seat above the stars of heaven and will be like the Highest" — approaches the serpent and makes it his instrument: "only be my vessel and I will speak through thy mouth words to deceive him." Its date is disputed: M. D. Johnson dates the original to the 1st century AD, L. Michael White reads the work as a Christian composition in Greek, and the surviving versions are dated to the 3rd–5th centuries.2 In this account the serpent is the vehicle and the tempter a fallen heavenly being moved by pride and envy.

The Revelation to John (c. 95)

Revelation twice calls the adversary by the serpent's name: "the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world" (12:9), and again "that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan" (20:2). Luke's Gospel reports Jesus saying, "I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven" (Luke 10:18). Revelation draws the dragon as "a great red dragon, having seven heads" (12:3); Psalm 74:13–14 speaks of God breaking the heads of the dragons and of leviathan, Isaiah 27:1 of his punishing leviathan and slaying the dragon. Readings of the dragon and of leviathan are followed on the mythical-beasts timeline.3

Justin Martyr (c. 160)

Justin Martyr ties the names together etymologically: the one "whom Moses calls the serpent" is "in Job and Zechariah… called the devil, and by Jesus is addressed as Satan" — Satanas a compound of "sata… apostate" and "nas… the serpent."4 Reading Psalm 82's fallen "princes," he places the serpent among them: "the fall of one of the princes, i.e., of him who was called the serpent, who fell with a great overthrow, because he deceived Eve."5

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus joins the apostate angel, envy as motive, and the serpent as carrier: man was "led astray by the angel who, for the great gifts of God which He had given to man, was envious and jealous of him"; that angel, having "apostatized and departed from God… was called Satan"; and "God cursed the serpent which carried and conveyed the Slanderer… even on Satan."6

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian roots the Fall in the devil's impatience and envy that God had "subjected… the universal works… to His own image, that is, to man," so that the woman was "through his very speech with her, breathed on by a spirit infected with impatience."7 His Against Marcion is the earliest text on this timeline to apply both prophetic fall-texts to the devil: Ezekiel 28 shows "this angel… both by creation good and by choice corrupt… the devil there in the person of the prince of Tyre," and Isaiah 14's "I will be like the Most High" "must mean the devil."8

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen argues in On First Principles that the Isaiah 14 oracle cannot concern a mere man: "Most evidently by these words is he shown to have fallen from heaven, who formerly was Lucifer, and who used to arise in the morning… at one time he was light."9 He holds likewise that the second Ezekiel 28 prophecy to the prince of Tyre ("among the delights of paradise… in the holy mount of God") "cannot be at all understood of a man, but of some superior power which had fallen away from a higher position," a power "formerly holy and happy" that fell "from the time that iniquity was found in it."10

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373)

Ephrem the Syrian, in his Commentary on Genesis, lists four ways the serpent might have spoken: "either Adam understood the serpent's own mode of communication, or Satan spoke through it, or the serpent posed the question in his mind and speech was given to it, or Satan sought from God that speech be given to the serpent for a short time."11 Each option treats the serpent as a creature; two of the four bring in Satan, as speaker or as petitioner.

John Chrysostom (c. 388)

John Chrysostom, preaching through Genesis at Antioch, distinguishes the tempter from the animal. The tempter is the fallen devil — "cast down from that pinnacle on account of his depravity of will" — and the serpent is "this wild animal," "this irrational animal," which the devil "made use of… like some instrument." To those who ask "if the wild animals also shared the power of speech" he answers "Not so—perish the thought": "the words came from the devil." He speaks of "the serpent that is visible," the beast that served "like some sword" in the devil's hand, and of "the serpent perceptible only to the mind," the devil himself.12

Augustine of Hippo (c. 415)

Augustine writes that "that proud and therefore envious angel… fell from the spiritual Paradise, and… chose the serpent as his mouthpiece… and first tried his deceit upon the woman."13 He reads Ezekiel 28 of the devil, "where it is meant that he was some time without sin" (City of God 11.15), and Isaiah 14's "Lucifer, son of the morning" of the devil, while noting that not every line of the oracle "altogether fitly" applies to the fallen spirit rather than the human king.14

Michael S. Heiser (c. 2015)

Michael S. Heiser, in The Unseen Realm, holds that "the nachash of Genesis 3 was a divine being," not a member "of the animal kingdom," because "the incident with the nachash takes place in the divine council abode." He argues that the word nachash puns on serpent, on a verb for divining/deceiving, and on the root for "shining, bronze" (a "shining one"), and he reads Ezekiel 28's guardian cherub on "the holy mountain of God" as the same Edenic rebel's fall — though he cautions the point is "not that Ezekiel 28 draws upon a tale about a being called Satan" but a divine council-member who rebelled. In his larger scheme the Eden revolt is the first of three, followed by the sons of God of Genesis 6 and by Babel (see his Divine Council page).15

Fr. Stephen De Young (c. 2018)

Fr. Stephen De Young, an Orthodox priest writing for Ancient Faith, holds that the Hebrew word "means not only a snake or serpent, but also refers to a being which is deceptive or clever," and reads Ezekiel 28's cherub "as an angelic being… coated in precious gems and dwelling in the presence of God," in garden-and-mountain imagery of "the dwelling place of the gods" merged onto "the dwelling place of the true God of Israel." He concludes: "the identification of the Devil as a serpent in Gen 3 and a cherub in Ezek 28 are actually commensurate."16
