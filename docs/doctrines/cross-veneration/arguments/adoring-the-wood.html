Does the Good Friday “we adore thy Cross” language cross into worship of the object?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the liturgy's own words (adoramus, venite adoremus, dulce lignum) name the act adoration and address the wood directly, which is a real prima-facie case that the Cross-cult crosses the line; but the defence that liturgical adoro is a broad word for reverent homage, and that the antiphon syntactically terminates the adoration on Christ, is coherent and not defeated.

Related arguments: the charge of idolatry — see also is venerating the Cross itself dulia or idolatry? This is the sharpest, liturgy-specific form of the general question weighed for images and relics.

The claim

“Crucem tuam adoramus, Domine…” — “We adore Thy Cross, O Lord, and Thy holy resurrection we praise and glorify: behold, by the wood joy has come to the whole world.”

— the Good Friday antiphon Crucem tuam adoramus, attested in the Gelasian Sacramentary (8th c.). The objector's claim: the Cross is the one venerated object whose liturgy calls the act adoration in so many words — “we adore thy Cross,” “come, let us adore” (Ecce lignum crucis… venite adoremus), the wood itself hailed as “sweet” (dulce lignum) and addressed in the second person. If the tradition's own words name the act worship and direct love and homage to the timber, the dulia-not-latria defence is contradicted by the liturgy it is meant to protect.

Who advances it

The texts are the developed Latin cult's own: Venantius Fortunatus's Crux fidelis… dulce lignum (569), the Ecce lignum crucis / venite adoremus and Crucem tuam adoramus chants of the Good Friday Adoration of the Cross. The objection that this language is worship is the Reformed case, most sharply Calvin's — that calling homage to an object “veneration” rather than “worship” is an empty relabelling, and here the tradition does not even relabel: it says adoro.1

The evidence for

The prima-facie case is strong. Unlike the icon-decree, which is careful to say proskynēsis and not latreia, the Good Friday liturgy uses the unhedged verb adorare (“adore”) of the Cross, and the imperative venite adoremus — the very formula used of adoring God (“O come, let us adore Him”). Fortunatus's hymn goes further and speaks to the wood, calling the timber, the nails, and the weight it bore “sweet” — the affectionate, personal address of devotion, not the language of a mere teaching-aid. A plain reading of the rite is that the object of the “adoration” is, grammatically, the Cross.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The objection correctly reports the words: the Cross-cult does use adoro, does say venite adoremus, and does apostrophize the wood. It is not inventing a worry; the language is stronger here than anywhere else in the veneration family, and a critic who took only the surface of the rite would reasonably call it object-worship.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two things blunt it. First, Latin adoro is genuinely broader than English “worship” or theological latria: in classical and liturgical usage it covers reverent homage — the Magi “adore” (adoraverunt, Mt 2:11) the infant, subjects “adore” a king — so the verb does not by itself decide that the act is the worship due God alone; the theological tradition that uses adoro of the Cross is the same one (Nicaea II, John of Damascus) that expressly denies the Cross latreia. Second, the Crucem tuam adoramus antiphon does not stop at the wood: it runs “we adore thy Cross… and thy holy resurrection we praise and glorify: for by the wood joy came to the whole world” — the wood is honoured because salvation came through it, and the sentence terminates in Christ's saving work, not in timber. On the defenders' reading the apostrophe to the “sweet wood” is the recognized figure of poetic address (as one might say “O blessed day”), and the “adoration” is the relative honour that passes to the Crucified. That reading is coherent — but, as with the general question, it rests on the worshipper's intention, which the words adoro and dulce lignum, taken alone, do not secure.3

Assessment

Contested. The objection lands a real point the general dulia defence has to answer: the Cross-liturgy alone among the venerations says adore, and addresses the object directly — so if any cult crosses into worship-of-the-thing by its own words, it is this one. But the case is not decisive: liturgical adoro is a broad word that the same tradition uses while explicitly withholding latreia, and the central antiphon terminates its adoration in Christ's resurrection and the salvation “by the wood,” not in the wood as such. Genuinely two-sided — the words read as worship, the theology reads them as relative — so contested, and inseparable from the general Cross question it sharpens.
