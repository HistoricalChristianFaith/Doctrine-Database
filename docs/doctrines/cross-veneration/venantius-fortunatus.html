Venantius Fortunatus on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: c. 530–609 (the True-Cross hymns, 569) · Wikipedia
Summary: Italian-born Latin poet, later bishop of Poitiers, who composed Pange lingua … proelium certaminis (the Crux fidelis), sung at the Good Friday Adoration of the Cross, and Vexilla regis prodeunt, sung at Passiontide Vespers. Written in 569 for the reception of a relic of the True Cross at Poitiers, they address the wood itself in the second person, calling it “sweet” (dulce lignum).

Claims & evidence

The Cross addressed as the noblest of all trees

“Crux fidelis, inter omnes arbor una nobilis: nulla silva talem profert, fronde, flore, germine. Dulce lignum, dulces clavos, dulce pondus sustinet.”

“Faithful Cross! above all other, one and only noble Tree! None in foliage, none in blossom, none in fruit thy peers may be; sweetest wood and sweetest iron! Sweetest Weight is hung on thee!”

— Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis (569), the Crux fidelis refrain; Latin with J. M. Neale's translation, overview. The hymn speaks to the Cross in the second person and calls the wood “sweet” (dulce lignum). It is chanted during the Adoration of the Cross on Good Friday (“Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis”).

The Cross as the standard of the King

“Vexilla regis prodeunt, fulget crucis mysterium…” — “The royal banners forward go, the Cross shines forth in mystic glow…”

— Vexilla regis prodeunt (569), overview. The hymn was first sung in the procession that carried the relic of the Cross to Queen Radegund's monastery of the Holy Cross at Poitiers. ⚠ unverified: that Fortunatus modelled the hymn on the imperial adventus, the ceremonial welcome given a sovereign entering a city.

Reception and debate

  • The occasion: in 569 the Byzantine emperor Justin II sent a fragment of the True Cross, at Radegund's request, to her convent at Poitiers; Fortunatus wrote both hymns for its solemn reception. The hymns entered the Roman liturgy — Vexilla regis at Passiontide Vespers, Pange lingua/Crux fidelis to accompany the Good Friday Adoration of the Cross.
  • John Calvin's objection to honouring objects, the Cross included, is recorded on his page; the account of the honour as conferred on Christ is recorded on the pages of John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea. Thomas Aquinas cites the Vexilla regis (“Dear Cross, best hope o'er all beside”) — the stanza O crux ave, spes unica, which, with a new doxology, gradually replaced the hymn's original stanzas 7 and 8 in the tenth century (“Vexilla regis prodeunt”) — in arguing that the Cross is to be worshipped with latria, “for the Church sings” it (Summa Theologiae III, q. 25, a. 4).
  • On Pange lingua: “The concluding stanza was not written by Fortunatus, but was added later” (Preces Latinae, “Pange Lingua (Fortunatus)”; similarly “Pange lingua”). Neither names who added it or when.
