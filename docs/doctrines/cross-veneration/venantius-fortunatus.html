Venantius Fortunatus on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: c. 530–609 (the True-Cross hymns, 569) · Wikipedia
Summary: Italian-born Latin poet, later bishop of Poitiers, who composed the two hymns still sung at the Good Friday veneration of the Cross — Vexilla regis prodeunt and Pange lingua … proelium certaminis (the Crux fidelis). Written in 569 for the reception of a relic of the True Cross at Poitiers, they turn the veneration into song — and address the wood itself with terms of homage and love, which is exactly what the Reformed critics would later fasten on.

Claims & evidence

The Cross is addressed directly, and hailed as the noblest of all trees

“Crux fidelis, inter omnes arbor una nobilis: nulla silva talem profert, fronde, flore, germine. Dulce lignum, dulces clavos, dulce pondus sustinet.”

“Faithful Cross! above all other, one and only noble Tree! None in foliage, none in blossom, none in fruit thy peers may be; sweetest wood and sweetest iron! Sweetest Weight is hung on thee!”

— Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis (569), the Crux fidelis refrain; Latin with J. M. Neale's translation, overview. The hymn speaks to the Cross in the second person and calls the wood “sweet” (dulce lignum) — the affectionate, quasi-personal language of the developed cult, sung at the Good Friday veneration ever since.

The Cross is the standard of the King — a royal welcome for the wood

“Vexilla regis prodeunt, fulget crucis mysterium…” — “The royal banners forward go, the Cross shines forth in mystic glow…”

— Vexilla regis prodeunt (569), overview. Fortunatus adapted the form of the imperial adventus — the ceremonial welcome given a sovereign entering a city — to greet the relic of the Cross carried in procession to Queen Radegund's monastery of the Holy Cross at Poitiers. Honour paid an emperor is transferred, in the hymn's own logic, to the wood of the Cross.

Notes

  • The occasion: in 569 the Byzantine emperor Justin II sent a fragment of the True Cross, at Radegund's request, to her convent at Poitiers; Fortunatus wrote both hymns for its solemn reception. The hymns entered the Roman liturgy — Vexilla regis at Passiontide Vespers, Pange lingua/Crux fidelis to accompany the Good Friday Adoration of the Cross.
  • The dulce lignum language is the sharpest exhibit for the objection that the Cross-cult addresses homage and love to a physical object: it is weighed at “Does the Good Friday ‘we adore thy Cross’ language cross into worship of the object?” The defenders answer that the apostrophe is a poetic figure and that the honour terminates on Christ, whose instrument the wood is.
  • The closing stanza now sung with the Crux fidelis is a later addition, not Fortunatus's own.
