John of Damascus on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: c. 675–749 (the Exposition, c. 743) · Wikipedia
Summary: The great defender of the holy images gives the classic theology of venerating the Cross itself. In the Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.11 he holds that the very wood on which Christ died “is to be worshipped,” and that the cross-image is worshipped relatively — “not honouring the tree… but the image as a symbol of Christ.” He supplies the distinction on which the whole later defence rests.

Claims & evidence

The Cross is the most marvellous of Christ's works — the instrument of salvation

“Every action, therefore, and performance of miracles by Christ are most great and divine and marvellous: but the most marvellous of all is His precious Cross. For no other thing has subdued death, expiated the sin of the first parent, despoiled Hades, bestowed the resurrection… save the Cross of our Lord Jesus Christ.”

— An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.11 (NPNF2 vol. 9). This is the doctrine's own reason for singling out the Cross: it is venerated not as one holy object among the saints' relics but as the instrument of salvation itself — the ground of its distinct rite and hymns.

The wood of the Cross is to be worshipped — as sanctified by contact with Christ

“So, then, this same truly precious and august tree, on which Christ has offered Himself as a sacrifice for our sakes, is to be worshipped as sanctified by contact with His holy body and blood; likewise the nails, the spear, the clothes… are to be worshipped.”

— Exposition IV.11. The Greek proskynein (rendered “worship” here) is the relative veneration, not latreia: John's whole point is that the honour is due the wood because it touched Christ, terminating in Christ. Note the same warrant extends to the other Passion instruments — the seed of the wider cult.

The cross-image is venerated relatively — not the tree, but Christ it signifies

“Moreover we worship even the image of the precious and life-giving Cross, although made of another tree, not honouring the tree (God forbid) but the image as a symbol of Christ… It behoves us, then, to worship the sign of Christ… But it does not behove us to worship the material of which the image of the Cross is composed, even though it be gold or precious stones, after it is destroyed, if that should happen. Everything, therefore, that is dedicated to God we worship, conferring the adoration on Him.”

— Exposition IV.11. This is the exact distinction the defenders need: the veneration attaches to the Cross as sign, is withdrawn once the sign is destroyed, and is “conferred on Him” — the honour passing to the prototype, applied to the Cross. It is the same machinery John frames for the icons at Exposition IV.16.

Notes

  • John also appears on this wiki as the classic defender of image-veneration (the incarnational defence and “the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype”) — see the veneration of images and icons. The Cross-passage is the same doctrine applied to the instrument of the Passion.
  • His careful guard — the wood is honoured only for its contact with Christ, and the honour is “conferred on Him” — is what the defenders press against the idolatry charge (see “Is venerating the Cross itself dulia or idolatry?”). The critics answer that in lived practice the wood is kissed and censed as if it were itself holy.
