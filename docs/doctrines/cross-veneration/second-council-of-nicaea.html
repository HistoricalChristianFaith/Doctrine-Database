The Second Council of Nicaea on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: 787 · Wikipedia
Summary: The council of 787, which restored the images (the seventh in the NPNF collection The Seven Ecumenical Councils), names the Cross in the clause of its Definition on the images: the images are to be set forth “just as the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross,” and given proskynēsis, not latreia.

Claims & evidence

The Cross and the images in the Definition

“…just as the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross, so also the venerable and holy images… should be set forth… To these should be given due salutation and honourable reverence (ἀσπασμὸν καὶ τιμητικὴν προσκύνησιν), not indeed that true worship of faith (λατρείαν) which pertains alone to the divine nature; but… to the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross… incense and lights may be offered according to ancient pious custom.”

— Second Council of Nicaea (787), Definition, in The Seven Ecumenical Councils (NPNF2 vol. 14). The Definition names the figure of the Cross first and the images by comparison with it, and allows incense and lights to the images “as to the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross and to the Book of the Gospels and to the other holy objects.” In the extracts from its fourth session printed in the same edition, the text reads: “we salute the image of the honourable and life-giving Cross, and the holy relics of the Saints.”

Reception and debate

  • The Definition treats the Cross and the images in one clause. Calvin (1559) rejected the distinction between the honour the council allows and latreia (see his page). Fernand Cabrol (Catholic, 1908) reports that Albert the Great, Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure and Thomas Aquinas taught that the Cross receives latria given relatively, and that Bellarmine, Bossuet and Petavius taught, with this council, a relative veneration that is not latria (“The True Cross”).
  • The same council legislated the placing of relics in every altar (its seventh canon) — see the relic-veneration treatment. Its Definition speaks to all three practices recorded in this family: images, relics, and the Cross.
  • The fourth-session text quoted above salutes the image of the Cross “according to the ancient traditions of the holy Catholic Church of God.” The Wikipedia article “Byzantine Iconoclasm” reports that Leo III “apparently forbade the veneration of religious images in a 730 edict, which did not apply to other forms of art, including the image of the emperor, or religious symbols such as the cross,” and that in “the classic account” Leo III had an image of Christ over the Chalke Gate of the Great Palace replaced with a cross; it reports that Leo V later replaced the Chalke icon with a cross, describing the earlier removal by Leo III as “fictitiously claimed.”
