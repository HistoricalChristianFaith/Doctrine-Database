The Second Council of Nicaea on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: 787 · Wikipedia
Summary: The seventh ecumenical council, which restored the images, defines the veneration of the Cross in the very same clause — the “figure of the precious and life-giving Cross” is the benchmark of lawful proskynēsis against which the images are measured. The Cross is not an afterthought to the icon decree; it is its yardstick.

Claims & evidence

The Cross is the benchmark of honourable reverence, not true worship

“…just as the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross, so also the venerable and holy images… should be set forth… To these should be given due salutation and honourable reverence (ἀσπασμὸν καὶ τιμητικὴν προσκύνησιν), not indeed that true worship of faith (λατρείαν) which pertains alone to the divine nature; but… to the figure of the precious and life-giving Cross… incense and lights may be offered according to ancient pious custom.”

— Second Council of Nicaea (787), Definition, in The Seven Ecumenical Councils (NPNF2 vol. 14). The council does not argue the Cross's veneration — it takes it as the settled, uncontested case, and licenses the images by analogy to it. The Cross therefore carries the same proskynēsis-not-latreia distinction, with incense and lights, “according to ancient pious custom.”

Notes

  • The council thereby welds the veneration of the Cross to the veneration of the images: whatever verdict is reached on the one bears on the other. The general distinction is weighed at “Is the veneration–worship (dulia/latria) distinction a real difference?” and, in its Cross-specific form, at “Is venerating the Cross itself dulia or idolatry?”
  • The same council legislated the placing of relics in every altar (its seventh canon) — see the relic-veneration treatment. Nicaea II is the common charter behind all three practices in this family: images, relics, and the Cross.
  • That the council names the Cross as the already-fixed case is itself evidence for the antiquity Egeria attests: by 787 no one on either side of the icon war disputed that the Cross was venerated.
