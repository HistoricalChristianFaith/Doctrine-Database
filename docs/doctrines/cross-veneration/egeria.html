Egeria on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: pilgrimage c. 381–384 · Wikipedia
Summary: A Western pilgrim (probably a Gallaecian nun) whose travel diary gives the earliest eyewitness account of the Good Friday Adoratio Crucis at Jerusalem — the faithful bowing to, touching, and kissing the wood of the Cross, guarded lest anyone bite off a fragment. The practice is already fixed liturgy in her day, decades before any theology of it was written.

Claims & evidence

The faithful bow to and kiss the wood of the Cross on Good Friday

“…the bishop, as he sits, holds the extremities of the sacred wood firmly in his hands, while the deacons who stand around guard it. It is guarded thus because the custom is that the people, both faithful and catechumens, come one by one and, bowing down at the table, kiss the sacred wood and pass through.”

— The Pilgrimage of Etheria §37 (McClure & Feltoe trans., SPCK, 1919), full text. On Good Friday morning at Golgotha a silver-gilt casket holding the wood of the Cross is set on a linen-covered table; the bishop grips the ends of the wood while deacons ring the table, and the whole people file past to venerate it. This is the ancestor of the Roman rite's Good Friday Adoration of the Cross — and it is practice, not yet a defended doctrine.

They touch it with forehead and eyes, then kiss it — and it is guarded against theft-by-biting

“And because someone is said to have once bitten off and stolen a portion of the sacred wood, it is guarded by the deacons, lest anyone approaching should venture to do so again. And as all the people pass by one by one, all bowing themselves, they touch the cross and the titulus, first with their foreheads and then with their eyes; then they kiss the cross and pass through, but none lays his hand upon it to touch it.”

— The Pilgrimage of Etheria §37, full text. The detail that a pilgrim once bit off a fragment — the reason for the guard — is incidental evidence of how intense the devotion already was: the wood is treated as intrinsically precious, and the bodily gestures (prostration, touching with the forehead and eyes, kissing) are exactly the proskynēsis the later councils would call veneration.

Notes

  • Egeria describes the rite; she does not theorize it. The vocabulary of latria vs. dulia, and the “honour passes to the prototype” defence, are three-and-a-half centuries later (John of Damascus, the Second Council of Nicaea). Her value is that she shows the practice was already customary and public in the 380s — so the debate the Reformers open is over a rite far older than its formal defence.
  • The wood she venerates is the relic of the True Cross reportedly recovered at Jerusalem in the 320s. This page is about the practice and its warrant (may Christians bow to and kiss the Cross?), not the authenticity of the physical fragments; the crowded medieval market in True-Cross wood is Calvin's target and belongs to the relic question.
  • Egeria also records the faithful venerating the titulus (the inscription over Christ's head) and other Passion objects at the same station — the seed of the whole cult of the instruments of the Passion.
