Egeria on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: pilgrimage c. 381–384 · Wikipedia
Summary: A Western pilgrim whose travel letter describes the Good Friday Adoratio Crucis at Jerusalem: the faithful bow to, touch and kiss the wood of the Cross, which deacons guard lest anyone bite off a fragment. Herbert Thurston (1908) traces the Western Good Friday veneration of the Cross back to this rite.

Claims & evidence

The faithful bow to and kiss the wood of the Cross on Good Friday

“…the bishop, as he sits, holds the extremities of the sacred wood firmly in his hands, while the deacons who stand around guard it. It is guarded thus because the custom is that the people, both faithful and catechumens, come one by one and, bowing down at the table, kiss the sacred wood and pass through.”

— The Pilgrimage of Etheria §37 (McClure & Feltoe trans., SPCK, 1919), full text. On Good Friday morning at Golgotha a silver-gilt casket holding the wood of the Cross is set on a linen-covered table; the bishop grips the ends of the wood while deacons ring the table, and the whole people file past to venerate it. Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) writes that the Good Friday veneration of the Cross “must no doubt be traced back, as Amalarius already in the ninth century correctly divined, to the practice of honouring the relic of the True Cross at Jerusalem which is described in detail in the ‘Pilgrimage of Etheria’” (“The Cross and Crucifix in Liturgy”).

They touch it with forehead and eyes, then kiss it, under guard

“And because, I know not when, some one is said to have bitten off and stolen a portion of the sacred wood, it is thus guarded by the deacons who stand around, lest any one approaching should venture to do so again. And as all the people pass by one by one, all bowing themselves, they touch the Cross and the title, first with their foreheads and then with their eyes; then they kiss the Cross and pass through, but none lays his hand upon it to touch it.”

— The Pilgrimage of Etheria §37, full text. Egeria gives the reported theft as the reason the deacons guard the wood. The gestures she records are bowing, touching the Cross and the title (titulus) with forehead and eyes, and kissing the Cross, without laying a hand on it.

Reception and debate

  • Egeria describes the rite without discussing the nature of the honour paid. The terms in which John of Damascus (c. 743) and the Second Council of Nicaea (787) describe that honour are recorded on their pages.
  • Her identity and date are discussed: Marius Férotin (1903) identified her with the Egeria of Valerio of Bierzo's letter and dated the pilgrimage c. 381–384; Karl Meister (1909) placed her in Gaul and her journey in the sixth century. The account is reported in McClure & Feltoe's introduction and summarized in the Wikipedia article linked above.
  • Cyril of Jerusalem (Catechetical Lectures 10) also describes the wood of the Cross at Jerusalem: “The holy wood of the Cross bears witness, seen among us to this day, and from this place now almost filling the whole world, by means of those who in faith take portions from it.” This page is about the practice (bowing to and kissing the Cross), not the authenticity of the physical fragments; Calvin's Treatise on Relics (1543) on the number of True-Cross fragments is recorded on his page and belongs to the relic question.
  • Egeria also records the faithful venerating the titulus (the inscription over Christ's head) at the same station, and, after the Cross, kissing “the horn from which the kings were anointed” and gazing at “the ring of Solomon” held by a deacon (§37).
