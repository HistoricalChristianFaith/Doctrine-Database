John Calvin on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: 1509–1564 (Treatise on Relics 1543; Institutes final ed. 1559) · Wikipedia
Summary: The sharpest Reformed voice against the whole cult — and it makes the Cross no exception. Calvin holds that erected crosses are useless where Christ is truly preached, that the dulia/latria distinction is an evasion invented to license idolatry, and (in the Treatise on Relics) that the True-Cross wood venerated across Europe is a fraud multiplied past all possibility.

Claims & evidence

Erected crosses are useless where Christ is rightly preached

“Of what use, then, were the erection in churches of so many crosses of wood and stone, silver and gold, if this doctrine were faithfully and honestly preached…?”

— Institutes of the Christian Religion I.11.7 (Beveridge trans.). For Calvin the crucifix and the Cross-image belong to the same “idol factory” as the saints' images: what teaches the crucified Christ is the preached Word, not a venerated object, so the material Cross is at best superfluous and at worst an occasion of idolatry.

The dulia/latria distinction is an evasion invented to license idolatry

“The distinction of what is called δυλια and λατρια was invented for the very purpose of permitting divine honours to be paid to angels and dead men with apparent impunity… It is, therefore, an unjust division to assign the greater to the saints and leave the less to God.”

— Institutes I.12.2. This is the machinery that answers the Cross-defence directly: when the defenders say they only venerate the Cross and do not worship it, “what they say is just the same as if they were to confess that they worship their images without worshipping them” (I.11.11). Bowing, kissing, and censing the wood is worship in fact, whatever it is labelled.

The venerated True-Cross wood is a fraud multiplied past possibility

Calvin's factual charge is that the relic at the heart of the cult is so multiplied among the churches that it convicts the whole trade of imposture: the fragments of “true Cross” scattered across Europe, gathered together, would make a “whole ship's cargo” — though the Gospel says one man carried the Cross (A Treatise on Relics, 1543, on the wood of the Cross, Krasinski trans., 1854). This is the relic-fraud strand of his attack; he presses it at length against the whole cult of relics (see his relic-veneration page). It bears on the authenticity of the wood, not the lawfulness of the practice — so it belongs to the relic question, and is noted here only because the Cross furnished his most notorious example.

Notes

  • Calvin's two lines of attack are distinct and both bear on the Cross: (1) the principled charge that venerating any object — Cross included — is idolatry the dulia label cannot excuse (weighed at “Is venerating the Cross itself dulia or idolatry?”), and (2) the factual charge of relic-fraud (which belongs to the relic question, since it concerns the authenticity of the wood, not the lawfulness of the practice).
  • The Reformed objection bites hardest on the Good Friday liturgy's own words — “we adore thy Cross” (Crucem tuam adoramus) and Fortunatus's “sweet wood” (dulce lignum) — which name the act adoration and address the wood directly: weighed at “Does the Good Friday ‘we adore thy Cross’ language cross into worship of the object?”
  • Calvin appears on this wiki also as a witness against image-veneration (the images timeline) and against relic-veneration (the relics timeline) — the same Reformed doctrine of worship applied across all three.
