John Calvin on the Veneration of the Cross

Dates: 1509–1564 (Treatise on Relics 1543; Institutes final ed. 1559) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Genevan reformer applies his rejection of image-veneration to the Cross. Calvin asks what use the crosses erected in churches serve where Christ is faithfully preached, holds that the dulia/latria distinction was devised to permit divine honours to be paid to angels and the dead, and (in the Treatise on Relics) argues that the True-Cross fragments shown across Europe are too many to be genuine.

Claims & evidence

The crosses erected in churches and the preaching of Christ

“Of what use, then, were the erection in churches of so many crosses of wood and stone, silver and gold, if this doctrine were faithfully and honestly preached…?”

— Institutes of the Christian Religion I.11.7 (Beveridge trans.). Calvin treats the crosses in the same chapter as the images of the saints. The doctrine he means is that “Christ died that he might bear our curse upon the tree, that he might expiate our sins by the sacrifice of his body, wash them in his blood, and, in short, reconcile us to God the Father,” and he adds: “From this one doctrine the people would learn more than from a thousand crosses of wood and stone.” Just before, he cites Paul's declaration “that by the true preaching of the gospel Christ is portrayed and in a manner crucified before our eyes” (Gal. 3:1), and holds that God would have the people instructed in churches “by the preaching of the Word, and the administration of the sacraments.”

Calvin on the dulia/latria distinction

“The distinction of what is called δυλια and λατρια was invented for the very purpose of permitting divine honours to be paid to angels and dead men with apparent impunity… It is, therefore, an unjust division to assign the greater to the saints and leave the less to God.”

— Institutes I.12.2. Of those who call the honour they pay images dulia and deny that it is latria, Calvin writes that “what they say is just the same as if they were to confess that they worship their images without worshipping them” (I.11.11). For Calvin the honour paid to images is worship whatever name is given it.

The number of True-Cross relics

Calvin argues that the fragments of the true Cross are so many that they cannot be genuine: “if we were to collect all these pieces of the true cross exhibited in various parts, they would form a whole ship’s cargo,” although “the Gospel testifies that the cross could be borne by one single individual” (A Treatise on Relics, 1543, on the wood of the Cross, Krasinski trans., 1854). He presses the same charge at length against the cult of relics generally (see his relic-veneration page). Calvin sets the scope of the Treatise himself: “It is not my intention now to discuss the abominable abuse of the relics of our Lord, as well as of the saints, at this present time, in the most part of Christendom. This subject alone would require a separate volume” (p. 222).

Reception and debate

  • Calvin's two lines of argument bear on the Cross in different ways: (1) in the Institutes, the charge that venerating any object, the Cross included, is idolatry that the dulia label does not excuse; and (2) in the Treatise on Relics, the charge about the number of True-Cross fragments.
  • The defence Calvin rejects is set out on the pages of John of Damascus (the Cross worshipped relatively, “conferring the adoration on Him”) and the Second Council of Nicaea (proskynēsis, not latreia).
  • Calvin appears also as a witness against image-veneration (the images timeline) and against relic-veneration (the relics timeline), applying the same doctrine of worship to all three.
