Hippolytus of Rome on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 215 AD (the Apostolic Tradition) · Hippolytus, c. 170 – c. 235; presbyter (and rival bishop) at Rome · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Tradition preserves the earliest surviving Christian ordination prayers. The bishop is consecrated in high-priestly terms — to "serve as thy high priest," to "offer thee the gifts of thy holy church," and by "the Spirit of high-priesthood to have authority to remit sins." The deacon, by contrast, "is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop," and receives none of "that Spirit that is possessed by the presbytery." The document thus grades the orders by their relation to the offering: bishop and presbyter share the priestly Spirit; the deacon does not.

Note on attribution: this church-order, long ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome and dated c. 215, survives only in later Latin, Coptic, and other versions. Its authorship, date and Roman provenance are debated (see Notes). It is cited here from Burton Scott Easton's 1934 English edition.

Claims & evidence

The prayer for a bishop: "high priest" and the Church's gifts (Apostolic Tradition 3)

At the consecration of a bishop, one of the assembled bishops lays on hands and prays that God pour out the "royal Spirit," in language saturated with the offering and the high priesthood:

Thou who knowest the hearts of all, grant to this thy servant, whom thou hast chosen to be bishop, [to feed thy holy flock] and to serve as thy high priest without blame, ministering night and day, to propitiate thy countenance without ceasing and to offer thee the gifts of thy holy church. And by the Spirit of high-priesthood to have authority to remit sins according to thy commandment…

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. 3, source (Burton Scott Easton, The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus, Cambridge, 1934).1 In the prayer the bishop is to "serve as thy high priest" and "to offer thee the gifts of thy holy church," and the same "Spirit of high-priesthood" carries the authority to remit sins. Ignatius (c. 108) had written that the proper Eucharist is the bishop's or his delegate's; here an ordination prayer asks for the bishop the office of offering, in sacerdotal words.

The deacon "is not ordained to the priesthood" (Apostolic Tradition 9)

The rite distinguishes the orders by whether they share the Spirit of the presbytery. Presbyters join in laying hands on a new presbyter "because of the common and like Spirit of the clergy," but the deacon is set apart:

When the deacon is ordained, this is the reason why the bishop alone shall lay his hands upon him: he is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop and to carry out the bishop's commands. … He does not receive that Spirit that is possessed by the presbytery, in which the presbyters share; he receives only what is confided in him under the bishop's authority. For this cause the bishop alone shall make a deacon.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. 9, source (Easton translation).2 In the document bishop and presbyter share "the common and like Spirit of the clergy" — hence the presbyters lay on hands at a presbyter's ordination — whereas the deacon "is not ordained to the priesthood," but to service, and so is ordained by the bishop alone. The document also limits the presbyter: he "has only the power to receive; but he has no power to give," so "a presbyter does not ordain the clergy."

Notes

  • The prayers together. In the Apostolic Tradition the bishop is consecrated "high priest" to offer the Church's gifts, the presbyter shares "the common and like Spirit of the clergy," and the deacon is "not ordained to the priesthood."
  • Authorship and date. The ascription to Hippolytus and a date before 235 have been defended by Allen Brent (1995) and Alistair Stewart (2015); Paul Bradshaw, Maxwell Johnson and L. Edward Phillips (2002) hold that its liturgical sections combine material of different origins, from the middle of the second century to the fourth, compiled c. 375–400, probably in Egypt or Syria.3 Elsewhere on the site Hippolytus is dated c. 204–205 for his Commentary on Daniel; the date here is the traditional one for the church-order.
  • Placement on the timeline. At its traditional date the church-order stands between Tertullian (c. 208) and Cyprian (c. 253).
