Clement of Rome on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 96 AD (1 Clement, written c. 95–96; Clement floruit as bishop of Rome, d. c. 99) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing to quell a revolt at Corinth that had deposed its presbyters, Clement argues that the church's ministry is a divinely ordered office, not an open function. God himself, he says, fixed "where and by whom" the offerings are to be presented and at what times; just as the old covenant assigned distinct ministrations to high priest, priests, and Levites — while "the layman is bound by the laws that pertain to laymen" — so each Christian must keep "the rule of the ministry prescribed to him." The apostles, in turn, appointed bishops and deacons and arranged that "when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry." It is the earliest extant text to set the Levitical order of offerings beside the church's appointed, succeeding ministry.

Claims & evidence

"Where and by whom" the offerings are presented (1 Clement 40)

These things therefore being manifest to us… it behoves us to do all things in [their proper] order, which the Lord has commanded us to perform at stated times. He has enjoined offerings [to be presented] and service to be performed [to Him], and that not thoughtlessly or irregularly, but at the appointed times and hours. Where and by whom He desires these things to be done, He Himself has fixed by His own supreme will… For his own peculiar services are assigned to the high priest, and their own proper place is prescribed to the priests, and their own special ministrations devolve on the Levites. The layman is bound by the laws that pertain to laymen.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 40, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 Clement's premise is that the presenting of "offerings" (Greek prosphorai) is not left to every worshipper's discretion: God has determined both the persons and the occasions. He sets out the Levitical order — high priest, priests, Levites, and laymen, each in a distinct station — as the pattern of how God orders worship; the "layman" (laikos, its earliest extant Christian use) is named as a separate station from the priestly ones. The Eucharist is not named here; the argument is applied to the deposed Corinthian ministers.

Each must keep "the rule of the ministry prescribed to him" (1 Clement 41)

Let every one of you, brethren, give thanks to God in his own order, living in all good conscience, with becoming gravity, and not going beyond the rule of the ministry prescribed to him. Not in every place, brethren, are the daily sacrifices offered, or the peace-offerings, or the sin-offerings and the trespass-offerings, but in Jerusalem only. And even there they are not offered in any place, but only at the altar before the temple, that which is offered being first carefully examined by the high priest and the ministers already mentioned.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 41, source.2 The phrase "in his own order" (en tō idiō tagmati) governs the whole appeal: worship has assigned stations, and to overstep one's is to "go beyond the rule of the ministry prescribed." Clement cites the Jerusalem-only restriction of sacrifice as an instance of the principle: God limits not only the act of offering but its place and its ministers.

The apostles appointed bishops and deacons as a deliberate order of ministers (1 Clement 42)

Christ therefore was sent forth by God, and the apostles by Christ. Both these appointments, then, were made in an orderly way, according to the will of God… And thus preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first-fruits [of their labours], having first proved them by the Spirit, to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 42, source.3 Clement traces a line of authorization — God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons — so that the ministry derives, on his account, from the divine sending rather than from self-appointment.

The apostles arranged for ministers to succeed one another in office (1 Clement 44)

Our apostles also knew, through our Lord Jesus Christ, that there would be strife on account of the office of the episcopate. For this reason, therefore, inasmuch as they had obtained a perfect fore-knowledge of this, they appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry. We are of opinion, therefore, that those appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole Church… cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry. For our sin will not be small, if we eject from the episcopate those who have blamelessly and holily fulfilled its duties.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 44, source.4 In this chapter the apostles, foreseeing conflict, both appoint ministers and "gave instructions" for an ongoing succession — "when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them." Clement concludes that the deposed Corinthian presbyters, appointed in that way "with the consent of the whole Church," cannot be justly removed, and that to eject them is sin. The chapter is a principal text in later discussions of apostolic succession. Where the Roberts–Donaldson translation quoted above reads "those who have blamelessly and holily fulfilled its duties," J. B. Lightfoot renders the clause "those who have offered the gifts of the bishop's office unblamably and holily" (44.3).5

Notes

  • Scope of the letter. Clement describes an ordered, appointed, succeeding ministry charged with presenting the church's offerings, and names the layman as a separate station. He does not set out a theology of the Eucharist, nor use the word "priest" (hiereus) of the Christian minister; the Levitical priesthood is his analogy, not his title for the bishop. Of later witnesses on this timeline, Ignatius ties the proper Eucharist to the bishop and Justin describes a presiding “president”; Cyprian calls the celebrant sacerdos.
  • Bishop and presbyter in chs. 42 and 44. Clement appoints "bishops and deacons" (ch. 42) yet speaks of the deposed officers as "presbyters" and of ejecting men "from the episcopate" (ch. 44) without distinguishing the two; both titles also occur in the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7). The succession he describes is that of the deposed Corinthian presbyters.
  • Use in the succession debate. Ch. 44, with its apostolically arranged succession, is the earliest text cited for apostolic succession; see also Irenaeus on this timeline.
  • Same author, other doctrines. Clement also figures as an early witness on the Old Testament canon (his use of Wisdom and Judith).
