Justin Martyr on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 155 (the First Apology addressed to Antoninus Pius, c. 155; the Dialogue with Trypho c. 155–160) · Wikipedia
Summary: Justin gives the earliest surviving description of the Christian Sunday liturgy, and in it a single minister — "the president of the brethren" — leads the prayers and "offers" the thanksgiving over the bread and cup, while "those who are called by us deacons" distribute the elements and carry a portion to the absent. The eucharistic food, he insists, is received "not as common bread and common drink" but "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh," and admission to it is reserved to the baptized believer. And in the Dialogue with Trypho he reads Malachi's prophecy of a "pure offering… in every place" as fulfilled "in the Eucharist of the bread and the cup… presented by Christians in all places throughout the world." His texts speak of a presiding minister who gives thanks over the gifts, of the elements as Christ's flesh and blood, and of the Eucharist as the Malachi offering.

Note: this page treats Justin's witness to the ministerial shape of the rite; his testimony to the Real Presence and to the Eucharist as sacrifice are quoted here because they are bound up in the same passages.

Claims & evidence

The "president" and the deacons (First Apology 65, 67)

There is then brought to the president of the brethren bread and a cup of wine mixed with water; and he taking them, gives praise and glory to the Father of the universe, through the name of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, and offers thanks at considerable length for our being counted worthy to receive these things at His hands. And when he has concluded the prayers and thanksgivings, all the people present express their assent by saying Amen… And when the president has given thanks, and all the people have expressed their assent, those who are called by us deacons give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water over which the thanksgiving was pronounced, and to those who are absent they carry away a portion.

— First Apology, ch. 65, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The Sunday gathering follows the same pattern:

And on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts to the imitation of these good things. Then we all rise together and pray, and, as we before said, when our prayer is ended, bread and wine and water are brought, and the president in like manner offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability, and the people assent, saying Amen; and there is a distribution to each, and a participation of that over which thanks have been given, and to those who are absent a portion is sent by the deacons.

— First Apology, ch. 67, source (cf. New Advent).2 In Justin's description a "president" (Greek ho proestōs, "the one presiding") offers the prayers and thanksgiving, the people answer "Amen," and "those who are called by us deacons" distribute the elements. Justin does not call the president a "priest" (hiereus) or "bishop" (see Notes).

Not common bread: "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" (First Apology 66)

And this food is called among us the Eucharist, of which no one is allowed to partake but the man who believes that the things which we teach are true, and who has been washed with the washing that is for the remission of sins, and unto regeneration, and who is so living as Christ has enjoined. For not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word, and from which our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh.

— First Apology, ch. 66, source (cf. New Advent).3 The passage calls the food "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" and restricts admission to the baptized believer in right standing. The food is "blessed by the prayer of His word"; in chs. 65 and 67 that prayer is offered by the president. Justin's witness to the Real Presence is treated on the Real Presence page.

The Malachi "pure offering," fulfilled "in the Eucharist of the bread and the cup" (Dialogue 117)

Accordingly, God, anticipating all the sacrifices which we offer through this name, and which Jesus the Christ enjoined us to offer, i.e., in the Eucharist of the bread and the cup, and which are presented by Christians in all places throughout the world, bears witness that they are well-pleasing to Him. But He utterly rejects those presented by you and by those priests of yours, saying, "And I will not accept your sacrifices at your hands; for from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is glorified among the Gentiles… but ye profane it."

— Dialogue with Trypho, ch. 117, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).4 Following the Didache, Justin reads Malachi 1:10–12 as a prophecy of the Christian Eucharist: the "pure offering" foretold "in every place" is "the Eucharist of the bread and the cup," offered by Christians the world over, against the rejected sacrifices of the Levitical priests. For Justin the Eucharist is a sacrifice, offered among the Gentiles in place of the temple's.

Notes

  • The rite described. Justin gives the earliest extant detailed description of the rite. At c. 155 his Sunday Eucharist has (a) a presiding minister who "offers" the prayers and thanksgiving, (b) deacons who distribute, (c) the elements called Christ's flesh and blood, and (d) admission restricted to the baptized.
  • Terms Justin uses. Justin does not call the president a "priest" (hiereus) or "bishop," and does not describe his ordination. Maurice Hassett (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) identifies Justin's "president of the Brethren" as "the bishop, or a priest deputed in his place for the occasion."6
  • The "high priestly race." In the immediately preceding chapter Justin calls the whole believing people "the true high priestly race of God," for whom "in every place… sacrifices are presented to Him well-pleasing and pure," adding that "God receives sacrifices from no one, except through His priests" (Dialogue 116).5 Here the priestly language attaches to the body of believers. Justin's texts speak both of a priestly people offering in every place and of one who presides over the thanksgiving in each assembly.
  • Same author, the Real Presence and the sacrifice. Justin is also a primary witness that the Eucharist is the true "flesh and blood" of Christ (First Apology 66) and that it is the Malachi "pure offering" (Dialogue 117); both are quoted above and the first is also cited on the Real Presence page.
