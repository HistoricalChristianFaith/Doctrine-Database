Cyprian of Carthage on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 253 AD (the letter to Caecilius, Epistle 62; On the Unity of the Church c. 251) · Cyprian, c. 200 – 258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248 and martyr under Valerian · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian writes that the one who offers the Eucharist acts in the place of Christ: "that priest truly discharges the office of Christ, who imitates that which Christ did; and he then offers a true and full sacrifice in the Church to God the Father." The offering is bound to the priest who reproduces what Christ did at the Supper, and the rite is not to be "changed by human tradition into anything else than what was divinely appointed." In On the Unity of the Church he draws the corollary that "the flesh of Christ… cannot be sent abroad," for "there is… no other home to believers but the one Church" — one Eucharist within the one episcopal communion.

Note: this page treats Cyprian's witness to the ministerial shape of the offering; his testimony that the Eucharist is a true sacrifice is quoted here because the two are inseparable in the same passage, and the same Epistle 62 is cited on Cyprian's Real Presence page.

Claims & evidence

"That priest truly discharges the office of Christ" (Epistle 62, to Caecilius)

Writing to correct those who used water alone, without wine, in the cup, Cyprian grounds the rite's unchangeable form in the priest's reproduction of what Christ himself did and offered:

For if Jesus Christ, our Lord and God, is Himself the chief priest of God the Father, and has first offered Himself a sacrifice to the Father, and has commanded this to be done in commemoration of Himself, certainly that priest truly discharges the office of Christ, who imitates that which Christ did; and he then offers a true and full sacrifice in the Church to God the Father, when he proceeds to offer it according to what he sees Christ Himself to have offered.

— Epistle 62 (to Caecilius, "On the Sacrament of the Cup"), § 14, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent; some editions number this letter 63).1 In Cyprian's argument the priest "discharges the office of Christ" by "imitating that which Christ did," and offers "a true and full sacrifice" when he offers "according to what he sees Christ Himself to have offered." Ignatius (c. 108) had tied the proper Eucharist to the bishop's authority; Cyprian speaks of the celebrant as imitating Christ, "the chief priest of God the Father."

"Not… changed by human tradition into anything else than what was divinely appointed"

In the same letter Cyprian frames the offering as a divine institution no human custom may alter, appealing to Christ's warning against breaking "the least of the Lord's commandments":

But if we may not break even the least of the Lord's commandments, how much rather is it forbidden to infringe such important ones, so great, so pertaining to the very sacrament of our Lord's passion and our own redemption, or to change it by human tradition into anything else than what was divinely appointed!

— Epistle 62, § 14, source (cf. New Advent).2 For Cyprian the Eucharist has a form "divinely appointed" by Christ, which the priest is bound to reproduce, not to alter by "human tradition."

One Eucharist within the one Church (On the Unity of the Church 8)

In On the Unity of the Church, expounding the Passover lamb that "shall not be sent abroad from the house," Cyprian applies it to the Church: the Lord's body is not to be carried outside the one Church.

The flesh of Christ, and the holy of the Lord, cannot be sent abroad, nor is there any other home to believers but the one Church.

— On the Unity of the Church (Treatise 1), § 8, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).3 In the treatise the Eucharist and the Church's unity are joined: because the "holy of the Lord" cannot be carried outside the "one house," Cyprian holds that there is no Eucharist apart from the one Church in communion with its bishops. The same treatise says that "he can no longer have God for his Father who has not the Church for his mother."

Notes

  • The passages together. Cyprian speaks of the celebrant as one who "discharges the office of Christ," imitating the act of Christ the high priest. He calls the eucharistic minister sacerdos and the Eucharist sacrificium, and ties that offering to fidelity to Christ's institution and to the unity of the Church.
  • Sacrifice and ministry together. Cyprian routinely calls the bishop/presbyter sacerdos ("priest") and the Eucharist a sacrificium offered to God the Father; the two appear together in Epistle 62. The Didache and Justin had earlier read Malachi's "pure offering" of the Eucharist.
  • The cup controversy. The immediate occasion of Epistle 62 was the "Aquarians," who offered water alone. Cyprian's statement on the priest who "discharges the office of Christ" is made in the course of arguing that the mixed cup is "divinely appointed."
