Was Ignatius of Antioch the child Jesus took in His arms (Matthew 18:2–4)?

Disputed reading · on The Ministerial Priesthood · witness: Ignatius of Antioch

The passage

Many say that this child was St. Ignatius the martyr, whose feast we celebrated yesterday and whose Office is transferred to tomorrow. Oh, how blessed was this glorious St. Ignatius, since he was taken up into Our Lord's arms and given as an example to the Apostles! How precious and sweet was that kiss!

— Francis de Sales, sermon on The Purification of the Blessed Virgin Mary (1622), source.1 The child in question is the one whom Jesus "set in the midst" of the disciples as the model of humility; Mark adds that Jesus "took a child, and set him in the midst of them: and when he had taken him in his arms, he said unto them…" (Matthew 18:2–4; Mark 9:36; Luke 9:47–48).2 De Sales records in the same sermon that "many say that this child was St. Martial, who later became Bishop of Limoges," before giving Ignatius as "the more common opinion."

The question

The Gospels do not name the child. Anastasius the Librarian (c. 878) reports that "the Greeks relate" that he was Ignatius, "and therefore they call him God-bearer," adding that "not even the apostles dared to lay their hands on him again when he was promoted to the bishopric."3 What is disputed is whether that identification preserves a memory of Ignatius's childhood or arose in the Middle Ages from a reading of his epithet Theophoros; in its earliest report it is tied to how Ignatius came to the episcopate. The present-day holders of the identification located here are Orthodox hagiographical and liturgical sources; an academic study defending it was not located.

The child of Matthew 18 was Ignatius

In brief: the child Jesus embraced and set before the apostles grew up to be Ignatius, and his surname Theophoros recalls that he was carried in Christ's arms.

Held by: the Greek tradition reported by Anastasius the Librarian (c. 878);3 Symeon Metaphrastes, Life of Ignatius (Byzantine hagiographer, c. 987), and the Orthodox synaxarion for 20 December;4 Francis de Sales (Catholic, sermon of 1622);1 Nikolai Velimirovich (Serbian Orthodox bishop of Ohrid and Žiča, 1920–1956), Prologue from Ohrid.5

On this reading the surname itself carries the memory: the Greeks, Anastasius reports, "call him God-bearer" because he was that child.3 Symeon Metaphrastes writes that "while he was still clearly a boy, they say that Christ… laid His undefiled hands upon him," and the story passed into the Byzantine office for his feast on 20 December, where it is still read.4 The Orthodox Church in America's life of the saint gives both senses of the name together: he was called God-bearer "because he bore God in his heart and prayed unceasingly to Him. He also had this name because he was held in the arms of Christ, the incarnate Son of God."4 Velimirovich tells the Gospel scene and concludes: "This child was Ignatius."5

The tradition is attested in Greek from the ninth century, received in the West by Anastasius, carried in the liturgical books of the Byzantine churches, and repeated by de Sales in 1622 as "the more common opinion." Its earliest report gives it a bearing on the episcopate: the child sanctified by Christ's own embrace was, the Greeks said, one on whom "not even the apostles dared to lay their hands on him again" when he became bishop.

A later legend grown from the epithet

In brief: no writer before the ninth century knows the identification; it arose from re-accenting Theophoros in the passive sense, and the earlier Antiochene tradition has Ignatius ordained by the apostles.

Held by: Jean Daillé (Reformed, 1666);6 John Pearson (Anglican, Vindiciae Epistolarum S. Ignatii, 1672);7 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, d. 1893), History of the Christian Church, vol. II;8 John Bonaventure O'Connor (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910).9

Pearson found no trace of the story before the ninth century: Anastasius and Metaphrastes are its first recorders. Daillé called it "unheard of among all the most ancient sources… clearly fabulous, and born of a fable," and O'Connor writes that early writers credited it "apparently without good reason."6,9 On this reading the name explains itself without the story. Pearson held that the ancients accented the word as active, Theophóros, "God-bearer," as Ignatius uses it of himself; Schaff notes that the Martyrium Ignatii has Ignatius explain it to Trajan as "one who has Christ in his breast," and that "the still later legend (in Symeon Metaphrastes and the Menaea Graeca), by changing the accent… gives the name the passive meaning, 'one carried by God.'"7,8

Pearson also set the earlier Antiochene memory beside the legend. John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch c. 386–397, says Ignatius "had not even seen" Christ, "nor enjoyed his companionship," and that "the hands of the blessed apostles touched his sacred head"; Theodoret (c. 447) has him receive "episcopal grace by the hand of the great Peter."10 For Pearson the legend's timing has its own explanation: it crystallized in the ninth century by confusion with a contemporary namesake, Ignatius, Patriarch of Constantinople (d. 877), styled the "new God-bearer."7
