Tertullian on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 208 AD (On Exhortation to Chastity; On Baptism c. 200–206) · Tertullian, c. 155 – c. 220; a Latin Christian writer of Carthage · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the earliest extant Latin writer to name the difference between the "Order" (ordo) and the laity, and in the same passage asks, "are not even we laics priests?" He ascribes the distinction to "the authority of the Church" and the "honour" of the Order, and tells the layman that where no clergy are present "you offer, and baptize, and are priest, alone for yourself." In On Baptism he ranks the right to baptize — chief priest (the bishop), then presbyters and deacons, then laymen only in necessity — and warns that a layman's "emulation of the episcopal office is the mother of schisms."

Claims & evidence

"The difference between the Order and the laity" (On Exhortation to Chastity 7)

Arguing that clergy must be men of a single marriage, Tertullian anticipates the objection that what is forbidden to priests is allowed to laymen, and answers it by drawing the line between the ordained Order and the laity, and in the same passage calls the laity priests (Revelation 1:6):

Vain shall we be if we think that what is not lawful for priests is lawful for laics. Are not even we laics priests? It is written: "A kingdom also, and priests to His God and Father, hath He made us." It is the authority of the Church, and the honour which has acquired sanctity through the joint session of the Order, which has established the difference between the Order and the laity. Accordingly, where there is no joint session of the ecclesiastical Order, you offer, and baptize, and are priest, alone for yourself. But where three are, a church is, albeit they be laics.

— On Exhortation to Chastity, ch. 7, source (Thelwall translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 This is the earliest extant Latin statement of the ordo/laity distinction. In the passage the layman offers and baptizes "where there is no joint session of the ecclesiastical Order"; it is "the authority of the Church" and the "honour" of the Order that "established" the difference, and Tertullian calls the laity "priests" on the strength of Revelation 1:6.

The right to baptize is ranked by order (On Baptism 17)

Closing his treatise on baptism, Tertullian sets out who may confer it, in a descending order that places the layman last:

Of giving it, the chief priest (who is the bishop) has the right: in the next place, the presbyters and deacons, yet not without the bishop's authority, on account of the honour of the Church, which being preserved, peace is preserved. Beside these, even laymen have the right; for what is equally received can be equally given. … But how much more is the rule of reverence and modesty incumbent on laymen—seeing that these powers belong to their superiors—lest they assume to themselves the specific function of the bishop! Emulation of the episcopal office is the mother of schisms.

— On Baptism, ch. 17, source (Thelwall translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).2 The order is bishop first, then presbyters and deacons "not without the bishop's authority," and then the layman, whose right Tertullian bounds by "necessity" and by the warning that to usurp "the specific function of the bishop" breeds schism. The sacrament in view is baptism, not the Eucharist.

Notes

  • The texts together. Tertullian uses ordo, "Order," for the clergy as distinct from the laity. In his texts the offering, the baptizing and the priestly office belong to that Order, while the layman may act where no clergy are present and must not "emulate" the bishop's office.
  • The laity as priests. In the same chapter Tertullian asks, "Are not even we laics priests?", allows the layman to offer and baptize where no clergy are present, and roots the ordo/laity difference in the Church's authority and honour.
  • Date and setting. The Ante-Nicene Fathers editor dates On Exhortation to Chastity "possibly, circa a.d. 204";3 in ch. 10 Tertullian cites "the holy prophetess Prisca."
