Ignatius of Antioch on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 108 (the seven letters written en route to martyrdom at Rome, c. 107–110) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing to the churches of Asia Minor on his way to die at Rome, Ignatius gives the earliest extant rule that a Eucharist is proper only when the bishop, or one he authorizes, presides. Clement (c. 96) had written of an ordered, succeeding ministry; Ignatius ties the ministry to the Eucharist itself: "Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is administered either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it"; "It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast." The congregation is to gather around "one altar" and "one bishop, along with the presbytery and deacons."

Claims & evidence

The bishop or his delegate and the "proper Eucharist" (Smyrnaeans 8)

See that ye all follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father, and the presbytery as ye would the apostles; and reverence the deacons, as being the institution of God. Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop. Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is [administered] either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it. Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude [of the people] also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church. It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast; but whatsoever he shall approve of, that is also pleasing to God, so that everything that is done may be secure and valid.

— Epistle to the Smyrnaeans, ch. 8, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 Ignatius makes the bishop's presidency (or his delegation of it) the condition of a proper Eucharist (the Greek is bebaia, rendered "proper" in the Roberts–Donaldson translation and "valid" in that of Lightfoot and Harmer, 1891), and extends the same rule to baptism and the love-feast: they are "not lawful" without the bishop. The phrase "or by one to whom he has entrusted it" provides for delegation; the letter does not say to whom the bishop entrusts it.

One Eucharist, one altar, one bishop with the presbytery and deacons (Philadelphians 4)

Take ye heed, then, to have but one Eucharist. For there is one flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ, and one cup to [show forth] the unity of His blood; one altar; as there is one bishop, along with the presbytery and deacons, my fellow-servants: that so, whatsoever ye do, ye may do it according to [the will of] God.

— Epistle to the Philadelphians, ch. 4, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).2 Here Ignatius pairs the unity of the Eucharist with the unity of the ministry: "one altar; as there is one bishop, along with the presbytery and deacons."

Later accounts of his ordination and succession at Antioch

Ignatius never describes his own consecration. Later writers report that he received the see of Antioch by the laying-on of apostolic hands, in succession to Peter. Three such reports follow.

For I do not wonder at the man alone that he seemed to be worthy of so great an office, but that he obtained this office from those saints, and that the hands of the blessed apostles touched his sacred head. … But since I have mentioned Peter, I have perceived a fifth crown woven from him, and this is that this man succeeded to the office after him. … [W]hen Peter was about to depart from here, the grace of the Spirit introduced another teacher equivalent to Peter, so that the building already completed should not be made more unsound by the insignificance of the successor.

— John Chrysostom, Eulogy on Saint Ignatius (delivered at Antioch, c. 386–397), §§2, 4, source (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series I, Vol. IX).3 In the encomium Ignatius, who "had not even seen" Christ in the flesh, received his office from the apostles: "the hands of the blessed apostles touched his sacred head," and he "succeeded to the office after" Peter at Antioch. A ninth-century report has the Greeks saying that "not even the apostles dared to lay their hands on him again"; see Was Ignatius the child Jesus took in His arms?

You have no doubt heard of the illustrious Ignatius, who received episcopal grace by the hand of the great Peter, and after ruling the church of Antioch, wore the crown of martyrdom.

— Theodoret of Cyrus, Eranistes, Dialogue I ("The Immutable"), c. 447, source (cf. New Advent).4 Theodoret names the ordaining apostle: episcopal grace conferred "by the hand of the great Peter." A note in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers edition records differing early accounts — the Apostolic Constitutions (vii. 46) has Ignatius ordained by Paul, while John Malalas, like Theodoret, names Peter, ordaining him on the death of Euodius.5

… qui ornarunt sedem beatae memoriae multum triumphantis Ignatii martyris, cujus tu indignus existens, nescio quomodo insiluisti inchoare novitatem in catholica Ecclesia… imo ille qui vertex est omnium pastoralium sedium… apostolorum vere optimus Petrus.

— Felix III of Rome, synodical letter deposing Peter the Fuller of Antioch, c. 485 (they "adorned the see of the blessed memory of the greatly-triumphant Ignatius the martyr, of whom, being unworthy, you have somehow leapt in to bring novelty into the Catholic Church… nay, he who is the summit of all pastoral sees… truly the best of the apostles, Peter").6 Felix roots the dignity of the Antiochene see in Ignatius the martyr who "adorned" it, and his rebuke in Peter the apostle, "the summit of all pastoral sees." The Latin text quoted here does not contain the statement that Ignatius "was ordained bishop by Peter's right hand," which John Pearson's Vindiciae attributes to Felix; the "hand of the great Peter" wording appears in Theodoret.7

Notes

  • The letters together. Ignatius is the earliest extant writer to tie the proper Eucharist to the presiding minister's office: the Eucharist is the bishop's or that of one he authorizes.
  • Bishop, presbytery and deacons. Ignatius writes of one bishop with a presbytery and deacons in the churches he addresses, and ties the Eucharist to that bishop. Whether this describes a settled order or urges a new one is set out as a disputed reading on “Do Ignatius’s letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one?”
  • Same author, the Real Presence. Ignatius is also a key witness that the Eucharist is the true "flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ" — see his detail page on the Real Presence.
  • Ordination and succession. The fourth- and fifth-century reports that Ignatius was ordained by apostolic hands and succeeded Peter at Antioch are cited in discussions of episcopal ordination and apostolic succession; see also the Baptist-successionist timeline.
  • The "child in Christ's arms" tradition. A Greek tradition, reported by Anastasius the Librarian (c. 878) and carried in the Byzantine office for his feast, identifies Ignatius as the child whom Jesus set in the disciples' midst and embraced (Matthew 18:2–4). Whether it preserves a memory of Ignatius's childhood or arose in the ninth century is set out on Was Ignatius the child Jesus took in His arms?
