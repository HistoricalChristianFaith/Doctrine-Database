Jerome on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 393 AD (the position stated in the Commentary on Titus, c. 386–387, and Letter 146 to Evangelus, date uncertain; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome writes that "a presbyter… is the same as a bishop," that earlier "the churches were governed by the common council of the presbyters," and that afterward "it was decreed throughout the whole world that one chosen from among the presbyters should be set over the rest," which in Letter 146 he says "was done to remedy schism." Bishops, he writes, are "greater than the presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's arrangement," and the one function he names as the bishop's alone is ordination. In Letter 146 he protests that a deacon should not exalt himself over the presbyters, "through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced," and closes by saying that "the traditions handed down by the apostles were taken by them from the old testament," bishops, presbyters and deacons holding in the church the positions of "Aaron, his sons, and the Levites." The rival readings of the early ministry on this point are set out under were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

Claims & evidence

"A presbyter, therefore, is the same as a bishop"

Expounding Paul's charge to Titus to "ordain presbyters in every city… for a bishop must be blameless" (Titus 1:5–7), Jerome points to the apostle's use of both titles for the same men and concludes that the New Testament uses presbyteros and episkopos for one office. His Commentary on Titus reads:1

A presbyter, therefore, is the same as a bishop; and before, at the devil's instigation, factions arose in religion, and it was said among the peoples, "I am of Paul, I of Apollos, but I of Cephas" (1 Cor. 1:12), the churches were governed by the common council of the presbyters. But after each one thought that those whom he had baptized were his own and not Christ's, it was decreed throughout the whole world that one chosen from among the presbyters should be set over the rest, to whom the whole care of the church should belong, and the seeds of schisms be removed.

He restates the whole argument, with the same proof-texts, in Letter 146 to Evangelus — written to rebuke someone who had ranked deacons above presbyters. There the lexical case is marshalled from Philippians 1:1 ("with the bishops and deacons"), Acts 20:28 (Paul calls the Ephesian presbyters "bishops"), Titus 1:5–7, and 1 Peter 5:1: "when the apostle clearly teaches that presbyters are the same as bishops."2

The single bishop as a remedy for schism

From the identity of the two titles Jerome draws a historical thesis. The single bishop — one man set over the presbyters of a city — is, on his account, an arrangement adopted everywhere to check faction. He cites a local precedent: at Alexandria the presbyters themselves made their bishop from among their own number down to the mid-third century.1

When subsequently one presbyter was chosen to preside over the rest, this was done to remedy schism and to prevent each individual from rending the church of Christ by drawing it to himself. For even at Alexandria from the time of Mark the Evangelist until the episcopates of Heraclas and Dionysius the presbyters always named as bishop one of their own number chosen by themselves and set in a more exalted position, just as an army elects a general… For what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?

In the closing question Jerome names one exclusive episcopal power — ordination; on his account the presbyter shares the bishop's other functions. Ignatius (c. 108) had tied the proper Eucharist to the bishop, and Cyprian (c. 253) wrote of bishops as successors of the apostles. Jerome describes a two-order ministry (presbyter-bishop and deacon) over which a presiding bishop was set — greater than the presbyters, he says in the Titus commentary, "more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's arrangement," closing the passage by binding both sides to that custom:3

Just as the presbyters, then, know that by the custom of the Church they are subject to him who has been set over them, so let the bishops know that they are greater than the presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's arrangement, and that they ought to rule the church in common, imitating Moses…

The presbyters "through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced"

The occasion of Letter 146 is a man who set deacons over presbyters. Jerome's protest rests on the presbyters' place at the Eucharist: it is the presbyters "through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced," and a deacon, "a mere server of tables and of widows," should not set himself over them.2

For when the apostle clearly teaches that presbyters are the same as bishops, must not a mere server of tables and of widows be insane to set himself up arrogantly over men through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced?

The NPNF translators give the Latin of that clause in a note: ad quorum preces Christi corpus sanguisque conficitur.4 In the letter the Eucharist is made through the presbyters' prayers, and Jerome's argument concerns the rank of presbyters relative to deacons and to bishops. He applies the language of "priesthood" to every bishop alike, "whether it be at Rome or at Engubium… his priesthood is one."

The close of Letter 146: the apostolic traditions and the Old Testament orders

After answering an objection drawn from Roman custom, Jerome ends the letter by setting the three orders beside the orders of the Temple:5

In fact as if to tell us that the traditions handed down by the apostles were taken by them from the old testament, bishops, presbyters and deacons occupy in the church the same positions as those which were occupied by Aaron, his sons, and the Levites in the temple.

Notes

  • Bishop, presbyter and laity. Jerome's argument concerns the rank of bishop over presbyter, which he says bishops hold "more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's arrangement," and of presbyter over deacon, which he defends from the presbyters' part in the Eucharist. He reserves ordination to bishops.
  • Aerius. Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355) had equated presbyter and bishop a generation before, from the same New-Testament interchange of titles — "a bishop is no different from a presbyter… one order, and one honor and one rank" (Epiphanius, Panarion 75.3). Aerius, on Epiphanius's report, rejected the episcopal rank and broke communion over it, and Epiphanius catalogued him among the heresies, answering that ordination is the bishop's distinguishing power. Jerome makes the same lexical case and names the same exception (ordination), describes the episcopate as a remedy for schism, and remained in communion. Aerius's list also assigns the eucharistic liturgy to bishop and presbyter alike.
  • Later use. John Calvin (Institutes IV.iv.2, 1559) quotes the Titus commentary ("A bishop is the same as a presbyter…") in support of his statement that "the ancients themselves confess that this practice was introduced by human arrangement, according to the exigency of the times."6
  • Related question. Jerome's equation of the two titles is also discussed under the government of the church: were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?
  • Texts used. The quotations are from Jerome's Commentary on Titus (on Titus 1:5–7, c. 386–387) and Letter 146 to Evangelus (date uncertain; the NPNF English of W. H. Fremantle). The timeline anchors his ecclesiological position at c. 393. Jerome also appears on the Old Testament canon, rabbinic corruption, perpetual virginity, prayer to the saints, infant baptism, and Nephilim timelines.
