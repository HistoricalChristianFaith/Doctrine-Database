The Didache on the Eucharist as the "Pure Sacrifice"

Dates: c. 100 AD (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles; compiled c. AD 100, range c. 50–150; anonymous, no single author) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Didache — the earliest surviving church-order manual — is the earliest extant post-apostolic text to call the Sunday Eucharist a sacrifice and to read it as the fulfilment of Malachi's prophecy of a "pure offering" made "in every place" among the nations. Gathering on "every Lord's day" to "break bread and give thanksgiving," the assembly is told to confess its transgressions and be reconciled first, "that your sacrifice may be pure… For this is that which was spoken by the Lord: In every place and time offer to me a pure sacrifice." The Didache does not say who may offer it; the next chapter directs the church to "appoint… bishops and deacons" who "render to you the service of prophets and teachers."

Claims & evidence

The Sunday Eucharist called a "sacrifice," with Malachi's pure offering (Didache 14)

But every Lord's day do ye gather yourselves together, and break bread, and give thanksgiving after having confessed your transgressions, that your sacrifice may be pure. But let no one that is at variance with his fellow come together with you, until they be reconciled, that your sacrifice may not be profaned. For this is that which was spoken by the Lord: In every place and time offer to me a pure sacrifice; for I am a great King, saith the Lord, and my name is wonderful among the nations.

— Didache (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles), ch. 14, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 Three features of the passage are cited in later discussion. First, the Eucharist is called a sacrifice (Greek thysia) three times in as many sentences. Second, that sacrifice must be kept "pure," so confession of sin and reconciliation with one's brother are required before it is offered. Third, the warrant cited is Malachi 1:11, "In every place… offer to me a pure sacrifice" — a text Justin, Irenaeus and later writers also read of the Eucharist.2

The church is to appoint bishops and deacons for its ministry (Didache 15)

Appoint, therefore, for yourselves, bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord, men meek, and not lovers of money, and truthful and proved; for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers. Despise them not therefore, for they are your honoured ones, together with the prophets and teachers.

— Didache, ch. 15, source (cf. New Advent).3 The instruction follows immediately on the sacrifice passage (ch. 14). The Didache does not say that the offering belongs to the bishops, nor does it name who presides at it; it sets the appointment of bishops and deacons in the chapter after the offering. Clement of Rome (c. 96) and Ignatius (c. 108) write of an appointed ministry and the offering in the same period.

Notes

  • Date. Estimates of the Didache's date run c. 50–150, with c. 100 often given; it stands with Clement of Rome (c. 96) among the earliest post-apostolic texts.
  • Scope of the text. The Didache calls the Eucharist a sacrifice and cites Malachi; it does not say who may offer it. The earliest extant text tying the proper Eucharist to the bishop is Ignatius ("Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is [administered] either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it," Smyrnaeans 8).
  • The sense of "sacrifice." The Ante-Nicene Fathers editor reads the Didache's "sacrifice" in the spiritual sense found in Romans 12:1, Philippians 2:17, Hebrews 13:15, and 1 Peter 2:5.4
