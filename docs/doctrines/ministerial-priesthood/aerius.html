Aerius of Sebaste on the Ministerial Priesthood — presbyter and bishop, one order

Dates: c. 355 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest named figure on record asserting that presbyter and bishop are one and the same order. A presbyter of Sebaste in Pontus, passed over for the bishopric that went to Eustathius, Aerius held that "one is no different from the other … one order, and one honor and one rank," reciting a list of functions the two share, among them the eucharistic liturgy ("the bishop performs the eucharistic liturgy, the presbyter likewise"). Every function on the list is assigned to presbyter and bishop; the list does not mention the laity. Epiphanius catalogued the position as the seventy-fifth heresy and answered it by making ordination the power that distinguishes the bishop. Jerome (c. 393) equated the two titles from the same texts and likewise named ordination as the one function reserved to a bishop.

Claims & evidence

"A bishop is no different from a presbyter — one order, one honor, one rank"

… his teaching was more insane than is humanly possible, and he says, "What is a bishop compared with a presbyter? The one is no different from the other. There is one order," he said, "and one honor and one rank. A bishop lays on hands," he said, "but so does a presbyter. The bishop administers baptism, and the presbyter does too. The bishop performs the eucharistic liturgy, the presbyter likewise. A bishop occupies the throne, and the presbyter also occupies one." With this he misled many, < who > regarded him as their leader.

— Aerius, as quoted by Epiphanius, Panarion 75 (Against the Aerians), §3.3 (Frank Williams trans.), archive.org. Aerius argues from the New Testament's interchange of titles — the apostle writes "to bishops and deacons" (Philippians 1:1) and tells a bishop he received his gift "at the hands of the presbytery" (1 Timothy 4:14) — "so that, as Aerius says, bishops and presbyters are the same" (§4.4). Jerome's later commentary on Titus and Letter 146 argue from the same texts; Aerius, on Epiphanius's report, drew from them the conclusion that the episcopal rank should not be distinguished from the presbyterate.1

The functions on Aerius's list

Every function Aerius recites — laying on hands, baptizing, performing the eucharistic liturgy, occupying the throne — is one he assigns to presbyter and bishop alike; the quoted list does not mention the layman. His recorded argument concerns the gradation of the clergy, the ranking of bishop over presbyter; the surviving report does not show him addressing who else may celebrate the Eucharist. Jerome likewise equated presbyter and bishop while protesting that a deacon should not set himself over those "through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced."

Epiphanius's reply: ordination is the bishop's distinguishing power

< From > his saying that a bishop and a presbyter are the same, it is plain to people with sense that he is simply foolish. How can this be? The one is an order that generates fathers. For the episcopate produces fathers for the church. But the presbyterate, which cannot produce fathers, produces children through the laver of regeneration, but surely not fathers or teachers. And since he is not ordained for the purpose of ordaining, how could a presbyter consecrate a bishop, or say that he is equal to a bishop? Aerius' quarrel and his jealousy have deceived him.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 75 §4.2–3, archive.org. Epiphanius rests the whole distinction on the power to ordain: the bishop "generates fathers" by consecrating other clergy, which the presbyter cannot do. Jerome also names ordination — "what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?" Epiphanius makes ordination the mark of a distinct, superior order; Jerome makes it the single reserved episcopal power, the other functions being shared.2

Notes

  • Aerius and Jerome. Aerius (c. 355) and Jerome (c. 393) make the same equation — presbyter = bishop — from the same New-Testament texts (Philippians 1:1; 1 Timothy 4:14; and, in Jerome, Acts 20:28; Titus 1:5–7). They differ in conclusion: Jerome describes the single bishop over the presbyters as an arrangement “decreed throughout the whole world” so that “the seeds of schisms be removed”4 (for the readings of when that took place, see “Were ‘bishop’ and ‘presbyter’ originally one office?”), remained in communion and reserved ordination to the bishop; Aerius, on Epiphanius's report, rejected the episcopal rank as such and broke communion over it, for which Epiphanius catalogued him among the heresies. Both speak to the ranking of bishop and presbyter rather than to who may offer the Eucharist.
  • Attested only through Epiphanius. No work of Aerius survives; his words reach us only as Epiphanius reproduces them in order to refute them, and Epiphanius prefaces the quotation by calling the teaching "more insane than is humanly possible." The bracketed insertions (< >) are the translator's. Epiphanius gives the words as a direct quotation, and Augustine repeats the charge (De Haeresibus 53: the Aerians held "that a presbyter ought not to be distinguished from a bishop") — though, as with the parallel charge on prayer for the dead, Augustine, in Henry Wace's words, "merely epitomises Epiphanius," so the ancient record of the position traces to this one witness.3
  • The same man, a second tenet. The Panarion 75 that records this presbyter-bishop equation is the same chapter that preserves Aerius's better-known rejection of prayer and offering for the dead — treated on his purgatory-timeline page. He is one figure carrying two distinct dissents, each catalogued by Epiphanius and each surviving only through him.
  • Placement on the timeline. Aerius falls between the First Council of Nicaea (325), whose canon 18 appeals to "canon" and "custom" on who may offer, and Ambrose (c. 390). His floruit is fixed by his break with Eustathius, made bishop of Sebaste c. 355, under whom Aerius had been a presbyter and a rival for the same see.
