Cyril of Jerusalem on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 350 (the Mystagogical Catecheses, traditionally placed near Cyril's accession; see the authorship note below, which may push the text to c. 390); Cyril of Jerusalem c. 313–386 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril's last mystagogic lecture walks the newly baptized step by step through the Jerusalem liturgy of the faithful. The celebrant is throughout "the Priest" (ho hiereus), who leads the thanksgiving; the Spirit is invoked on the gifts; and over "that sacrifice of propitiation" the "bloodless service" is offered. Around him stands a graded ministry: the deacon serves and the presbyters "stand round God's altar." Justin (c. 155) had called the celebrant "the president"; Cyril's lecture describes the rite as celebrated in Jerusalem in the fourth century.

Claims & evidence

The celebrant called "the Priest"

Ye have seen then the Deacon who gives to the Priest water to wash, and to the Presbyters who stand round God's altar… After this the Priest cries aloud, "Lift up your hearts."… Then the Priest says, "Let us give thanks unto the Lord."

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (Mystagogic 5), On the Sacred Liturgy and Communion, §§2–5, source (NPNF Series II, vol. 7; cf. New Advent). Throughout the lecture Cyril names the presider "the Priest" (ho hiereus) and sets him apart from the deacon who serves and the presbyters who "stand round God's altar." Justin, two centuries earlier, had called the celebrant "the president of the brethren"; Cyril uses hiereus, as Chrysostom does in the same generation.1

The invocation of the Spirit upon the gifts (the epiclesis)

Then having sanctified ourselves by these spiritual Hymns, we beseech the merciful God to send forth His Holy Spirit upon the gifts lying before Him; that He may make the Bread the Body of Christ, and the Wine the Blood of Christ; for whatsoever the Holy Ghost has touched, is surely sanctified and changed.

— Mystagogic Catechesis 5.7, source. Cyril locates the change of the gifts in the descent of the Holy Spirit, invoked in the anaphora — the epiclesis of the Eastern liturgies. Ambrose (c. 390) speaks instead of the priest's speaking Christ's own words of institution. The passage on the change ("sanctified and changed") is also cited on the Real Presence page.2

The "sacrifice of propitiation" at the altar

Then, after the spiritual sacrifice, the bloodless service, is completed, over that sacrifice of propitiation we entreat God for the common peace of the Churches, for the welfare of the world… and, in a word, for all who stand in need of succour we all pray and offer this sacrifice… we… offer up Christ sacrificed for our sins, propitiating our merciful God for them as well as for ourselves.

— Mystagogic Catechesis 5.8–10, source. Cyril names the Eucharist a "spiritual sacrifice," a "bloodless service," and a "sacrifice of propitiation," offered at the altar for the living and the dead alike. The Didache and Justin had earlier read Malachi's "pure offering" of the Eucharist. The text says "we all pray and offer this sacrifice," within a rite whose prayers the lecture assigns to the priest.3

The graded ministry in the lecture

The lecture opens on the deacon who "gives to the Priest water to wash, and to the Presbyters who stand round God's altar" (§2) and who "cries aloud" the ritual directions to the people (§3); the priest leads the great thanksgiving, the epiclesis, and the intercessions, and it is the priest who declares "Holy things to holy men" before communion (§19). The three ranks have distinct roles — the deacon assists, the presbyters attend the altar, and the priestly celebrant leads the prayers. Justin had distinguished the president who gives thanks from the deacons who distribute, and the Council of Nicaea (325, canon 18) forbade deacons to give the Body of Christ to those who offer.4

A note on dating and authorship

The eighteen Catechetical Lectures proper are securely Cyril's and are usually dated to around 350; the five Mystagogical Catecheses (Lectures 19–23), from which this passage comes, are textually less secure. Some manuscripts ascribe them to Cyril, others to his successor John of Jerusalem (bishop 387–417), and some scholars date them to a redaction of the 380s or 390s. Whether Cyril's own (c. 350) or John's (c. 390), the lecture describes the Jerusalem liturgy of the fourth century. The page dates it c. 350, following the traditional ascription, and records the later dating.5

Notes

  • The neighbouring entries on the timeline are the Council of Nicaea (325) before, and Ambrose and Chrysostom after.
  • The same lecture is a witness on the Real Presence (the epiclesis and the change of the elements), on prayer to the saints (the commemoration "at their prayers and intercessions"), and on purgatory and prayer for the dead (the supplication offered "for those who have fallen asleep") .
