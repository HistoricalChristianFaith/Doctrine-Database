Cyril of Jerusalem on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 350 (the Mystagogical Catecheses, traditionally placed near Cyril's accession; see the authorship note below, which may push the text to c. 390); Cyril of Jerusalem c. 313–386 · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Justin described the Sunday rite through a "president" and Ambrose would locate the offering in the priest's speaking Christ's words, Cyril's last mystagogic lecture supplies the missing middle: a step-by-step walk through the Jerusalem liturgy of the faithful in which the celebrant is throughout "the Priest" (ho hiereus) — a frankly sacerdotal title — who alone invokes the Spirit on the gifts and, over "that sacrifice of propitiation," makes the "bloodless service" the church's offering. Around him a graded ministry: deacons serve, presbyters stand at the altar, and the offering is reserved to those who confect it. It is a liturgical, not merely homiletic, witness that by the mid-fourth century the eucharistic offering was the act of an ordained priesthood.

Claims & evidence

The celebrant is "the Priest" who leads the eucharistic action

Ye have seen then the Deacon who gives to the Priest water to wash, and to the Presbyters who stand round God's altar… After this the Priest cries aloud, "Lift up your hearts."… Then the Priest says, "Let us give thanks unto the Lord."

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (Mystagogic 5), On the Sacred Liturgy and Communion, §§2–5, source (NPNF Series II, vol. 7; cf. New Advent). Throughout the lecture Cyril names the presider "the Priest" (ho hiereus) and sets him apart from the deacon who serves and the presbyters who "stand round God's altar." Where Justin, two centuries earlier, had called the celebrant only "the president of the brethren," Cyril uses the sacerdotal word without embarrassment — the same shift to a distinct hiereus-vocabulary that Chrysostom would carry further in the same generation.1

The priest invokes the Spirit upon the gifts (the epiclesis)

Then having sanctified ourselves by these spiritual Hymns, we beseech the merciful God to send forth His Holy Spirit upon the gifts lying before Him; that He may make the Bread the Body of Christ, and the Wine the Blood of Christ; for whatsoever the Holy Ghost has touched, is surely sanctified and changed.

— Mystagogic Catechesis 5.7, source. Cyril locates the consecration in the descent of the Holy Spirit, invoked by the presiding priest — the classic Eastern epiclesis. This is the counterpoint to Ambrose, who a generation later fixes the change on the priest's speaking Christ's own words of institution; the two name different moments, but both make the confecting the act of the ordained minister who prays over the gifts. The realism of the change ("sanctified and changed") is also a major witness on the Real Presence page and is treated there; it is cited here for the ministerial point — that the offering is confected through the priest's invocation, not by the congregation.2

The offering is a propitiatory sacrifice made by the priest at the altar

Then, after the spiritual sacrifice, the bloodless service, is completed, over that sacrifice of propitiation we entreat God for the common peace of the Churches, for the welfare of the world… and, in a word, for all who stand in need of succour we all pray and offer this sacrifice… we… offer up Christ sacrificed for our sins, propitiating our merciful God for them as well as for ourselves.

— Mystagogic Catechesis 5.8–10, source. Cyril names the Eucharist a "spiritual sacrifice," a "bloodless service," and a "sacrifice of propitiation," offered at the altar for the living and the dead alike — the developed form of the "pure offering" the Didache and Justin read out of Malachi. The offering is the priest's act at the altar: the "we" who "offer this sacrifice" is the worshipping church acting through the presiding priest whose whole role the lecture has just detailed, not the congregation confecting it for itself.3

A graded ministry: deacon serves, presbyters stand at the altar, the priest offers

The lecture opens on the deacon who "gives to the Priest water to wash, and to the Presbyters who stand round God's altar" (§2) and who "cries aloud" the ritual directions to the people (§3); the priest leads the great thanksgiving, the epiclesis, and the intercessions, and it is the priest who declares "Holy things to holy men" before communion (§19). The three ranks are kept distinct by their relation to the offering — the deacon assists, the presbyters attend the altar, and the priestly celebrant offers — the very line Justin drew between the president who offers and the deacons who distribute, and that the Council of Nicaea (canon 18) had just enforced by forbidding deacons to give the Body of Christ to those who offer.4

A note on dating and authorship

The eighteen Catechetical Lectures proper are securely Cyril's and are usually dated to around 350; the five Mystagogical Catecheses (Lectures 19–23), from which this passage comes, are textually less secure. Some manuscripts ascribe them to Cyril, others to his successor John of Jerusalem (bishop 387–417), and several scholars judge them a redaction of the 380s or 390s. The question matters here only at the margin: whether Cyril's own (c. 350) or John's (c. 390), the lecture testifies to the eucharistic offering holding a fixed place, and a sacerdotal celebrant, in the Jerusalem liturgy of the later fourth century. The page dates it c. 350 (the traditional ascription) while flagging that a later date is defensible.5

Notes

  • The nearest companions on the timeline are the institution-narrative and Malachi strands the offering-language rests on, the Council of Nicaea (325) just before, and Ambrose and Chrysostom just after — the Greek and Latin developments of the same conviction that the confecting belongs to the ordained.
  • The same lecture is a witness on the Real Presence (the epiclesis and the change of the elements), on prayer to the saints (the commemoration "at their prayers and intercessions"), and on purgatory and prayer for the dead (the supplication offered "for those who have fallen asleep") — the four doctrines the single Jerusalem anaphora braids together.
