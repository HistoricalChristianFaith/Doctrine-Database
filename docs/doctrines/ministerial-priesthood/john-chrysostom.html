John Chrysostom on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: c. 392 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom's Treatise on the Priesthood (De Sacerdotio, c. 386–392) sets out the dignity of the ordained priest's office. Book 3 argues that the priesthood is instituted not by any created power "but the Paraclete Himself"; that the priest at the altar "makes prolonged supplication, not that some flame … may consume the offerings" but that the Holy Spirit descend on the sacrifice; and that priests have "received an authority which God has not given to angels or archangels" — to bind and loose and to forgive sins, so that "what priests do here below God ratifies above." The same treatise is cited on the Real Presence page; here the passages concern the priest: regeneration and the eucharistic gift, he says, are "accomplished only by means of those holy hands, I mean the hands of the priest."

Claims & evidence

"Amongst heavenly ordinances" (On the Priesthood 3.4)

For the priestly office is indeed discharged on earth, but it ranks amongst heavenly ordinances; and very naturally so: for neither man, nor angel, nor archangel, nor any other created power, but the Paraclete Himself, instituted this vocation, and persuaded men while still abiding in the flesh to represent the ministry of angels. Wherefore the consecrated priest ought to be as pure as if he were standing in the heavens themselves in the midst of those powers.

— Treatise on the Priesthood (De Sacerdotio) 3.4, New Advent (NPNF Series I, vol. 9; also at this edition).1 For Chrysostom the office is instituted by "the Paraclete Himself," and the priest is to be "as pure as if he were standing in the heavens."

The priest at the altar and the Holy Spirit (On the Priesthood 3.4)

Picture Elijah and the vast multitude standing around him, and the sacrifice laid upon the altar of stones … then the sudden rush of fire from Heaven upon the sacrifice … Now then pass from this scene to the rites which are celebrated in the present day … There stands the priest, not bringing down fire from Heaven, but the Holy Spirit: and he makes prolonged supplication, not that some flame sent down from on high may consume the offerings, but that grace descending on the sacrifice may thereby enlighten the souls of all, and render them more refulgent than silver purified by fire.

— On the Priesthood 3.4, New Advent.2 Chrysostom contrasts Elijah's fire with the Spirit for whom the priest "makes prolonged supplication" over the sacrifice. Cyril of Jerusalem's lecture (c. 350) likewise describes an invocation of the Spirit upon the gifts.

An authority "not given to angels or archangels" (On the Priesthood 3.5)

For they who inhabit the earth and make their abode there are entrusted with the administration of things which are in Heaven, and have received an authority which God has not given to angels or archangels. For it has not been said to them, "Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in Heaven, and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in Heaven." … and what priests do here below God ratifies above, and the Master confirms the sentence of his servants.

Chrysostom continues:

For if no one can enter into the kingdom of Heaven except he be regenerate through water and the Spirit, and he who does not eat the flesh of the Lord and drink His blood is excluded from eternal life, and if all these things are accomplished only by means of those holy hands, I mean the hands of the priest, how will any one, without these, be able to escape the fire of hell, or to win those crowns which are reserved for the victorious?

— On the Priesthood 3.5, New Advent.3 In this passage baptismal regeneration and the eucharistic gift of Christ's flesh and blood are dispensed "only by means of" the priest's hands, and Chrysostom grounds the office on the power to bind and loose (Matthew 18:18; John 20:23).

The Jewish priests and "our priests" (On the Priesthood 3.6)

The Jewish priests had authority to release the body from leprosy, or, rather, not to release it but only to examine those who were already released … But our priests have received authority to deal, not with bodily leprosy, but spiritual uncleanness—not to pronounce it removed after examination, but actually and absolutely to take it away. … For not only at the time of regeneration, but afterwards also, they have authority to forgive sins.

— On the Priesthood 3.6, New Advent.4 Chrysostom compares the Levitical priesthood with the Christian: the Aaronic priest examined the healed leper, while "our priests" take spiritual uncleanness away "actually and absolutely." Clement of Rome (c. 96) had used the Levitical orders as an analogy without naming the Christian minister a priest; Chrysostom calls the Christian minister hiereus ("priest").

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Chrysostom wrote the Treatise c. 386–392, before he became bishop of Constantinople (398). The timeline anchors it at c. 392, consistent with the dating on his Real Presence page.
  • Two sides of one treatise. The same Book 3 is a principal witness on the Real Presence — "when thou seest the Lord sacrificed, and laid upon the altar." That page cites it for what is offered; this page for who offers it.
  • Vocabulary. Chrysostom uses the sacerdotal title for the Christian minister and ties both Eucharist and absolution to the priest's hands; Cyril of Jerusalem's lecture uses the same title for the celebrant.
