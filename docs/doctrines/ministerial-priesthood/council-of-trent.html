The Council of Trent on the Ministerial Priesthood

Dates: 15 July 1563 (Session 23 of the Council of Trent, the nineteenth ecumenical council, under Pope Pius IV; the True and Catholic Doctrine concerning the Sacrament of Order, with its canons) · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering the Reformers, Trent's twenty-third session defines the Catholic doctrine of the sacrament of order. It defines that in the New Testament there is "a new, visible, and external priesthood," instituted by Christ, to which was delivered "the power… of consecrating, offering, and administering His Body and Blood, as also of forgiving and of retaining sins"; that this order is a true sacrament that imprints an indelible character, so that "he who has once been a priest" cannot "again become a layman"; and that the church's ministry is "a hierarchy… by divine ordination instituted, consisting of bishops, priests, and ministers." Canon 1 anathematizes reducing the priesthood to "only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel." The decree states that "the tradition of the Catholic Church has always taught" this priesthood.

Claims & evidence

The priesthood of the New Law (Chapter 1)

The decree opens by tying the priesthood to the sacrifice: because the Church has received "the holy visible sacrifice of the Eucharist" (defined at Session 22), there must be a priesthood to offer it. It then attributes that priesthood to Christ's institution, with the powers of offering and absolving:

Sacrifice and priesthood are, by the ordinance of God, in such wise conjoined, as that both have existed in every law. Whereas, therefore, in the New Testament, the Catholic Church has received, from the institution of Christ, the holy visible sacrifice of the Eucharist; it must needs also be confessed, that there is, in that Church, a new, visible, and external priesthood, into which the old has been translated. And the sacred Scriptures show, and the tradition of the Catholic Church has always taught, that this priesthood was instituted by the same Lord our Saviour, and that to the apostles, and their successors in the priesthood, was the power delivered of consecrating, offering, and administering His Body and Blood, as also of forgiving and of retaining sins.

— Council of Trent, Session 23 (1563), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Order, ch. 1, trans. J. Waterworth, source.1 The chapter presents the power of "consecrating, offering, and administering" as delivered by Christ "to the apostles, and their successors in the priesthood," and appeals both to "the sacred Scriptures" and to "the tradition of the Catholic Church."

The preaching office and the priesthood (Canon 1)

The attached canons anathematize the view that the priesthood is a ministry of preaching only:

If any one saith, that there is not in the New Testament a visible and external priesthood; or that there is not any power of consecrating and offering the true body and blood of the Lord, and of forgiving and retaining sins; but only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel, or, that those who do not preach are not priests at all; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 23 (1563), Canon 1, trans. Waterworth, source.2 The canon addresses the question at issue between the council and the Reformers — whether the New Testament institutes a distinct priesthood with a power of "consecrating and offering," or only "an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel."

Order as a sacrament; the character of ordination (Canons 3–4)

Trent defines ordination as a true sacrament instituted by Christ, through which the Holy Spirit is given and a character imprinted. Augustine had argued from the baptism/orders parallel that the ordained are not re-ordained:

If any one saith, that order, or sacred ordination, is not truly and properly a sacrament instituted by Christ the Lord… let him be anathema.

If any one saith, that, by sacred ordination, the Holy Ghost is not given; and that vainly therefore do the bishops say, Receive ye the Holy Ghost; or, that a character is not imprinted by that ordination; or, that he who has once been a priest, can again become a layman; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 23 (1563), Canons 3 and 4, trans. Waterworth, source.3

A hierarchy of divine ordination: bishops, priests, and ministers (Canons 6–7)

Finally Trent defines the graded ministry and the bishop's superiority, with the power of ordination assigned to the bishop:

If any one saith, that, in the Catholic Church there is not a hierarchy by divine ordination instituted, consisting of bishops, priests, and ministers; let him be anathema.

If any one saith, that bishops are not superior to priests; or, that they have not the power of confirming and ordaining; or, that the power which they possess is common to them and to priests… let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 23 (1563), Canons 6 and 7, trans. Waterworth, source.4 The question Aerius (c. 355) and Jerome (c. 393) had raised — whether the bishop outranks the presbyter — is here defined for the Catholic Church in favour of gradation; ordination, which Jerome had named as the one function reserved to a bishop, is assigned to bishops.

Notes

  • Contents of the session. Trent 23 defines, under anathema: a visible priesthood with the power to consecrate, offer and absolve (Chapter 1, Canon 1), order as a sacrament (Canon 3), the character imprinted by ordination (Canon 4), and a hierarchy of bishops, priests and ministers (Canons 6–7).
  • Priesthood joined to sacrifice. The decree's opening move — that sacrifice and priesthood are "conjoined… in every law" — ties it to the prior Session 22 definition of the Mass as a true sacrifice (1562).
  • The council's own account. The council presents the priesthood it defines as what "the sacred Scriptures show, and the tradition of the Catholic Church has always taught," and issues its definitions and anathemas in answer to the Reformers.
  • Translation provenance. The English is J. Waterworth's, The Canons and Decrees of the Sacred and Oecumenical Council of Trent (London: Dolman, 1848), a nineteenth-century English edition of the conciliar acts.
