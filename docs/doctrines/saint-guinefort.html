Saint Guinefort, the holy greyhound

In the hill-country of the Dombes north of Lyon, in the thirteenth century and again in records down to the twentieth, peasant mothers carried their sick and sickly infants to a wood and a well to be healed by a saint named Guinefort. In Stephen of Bourbon’s report, Guinefort was a greyhound. On the story the country people told, the dog had guarded its master’s baby, killed a serpent that came at the cradle, and been rashly slain by the knight who mistook the blood on its muzzle for the child’s — then, the baby found alive, was buried in a well, and venerated by the peasants as a martyr. The whole of it is known to us because a Dominican inquisitor, Stephen of Bourbon, came upon the cult while hearing confessions and recorded it in a section on superstitions, together with his own suppression of it — disinterring the dog, felling and burning the sacred grove, and banning the practice on pain of forfeiture. His hostile report is the only known medieval source for the holy greyhound; the later threads — the fable behind the story, the healing rite, the human saint from whom, on Jean-Claude Schmitt’s reconstruction, the dog took its name, and the later traces of the cult — are studied around that single page.

This page is not a life of a dog; it is a history of a legend and of how a folk cult formed, was condemned, and appears again in later records, set out earliest source first: the Indian fable of the faithful animal wrongly killed and its long migration west, the human Saint Guinefort of Pavia whose cult had spread into the region, the inquisitor’s thirteenth-century exemplum that alone records the dog-saint, the later traces of the cult from 1632 to the twentieth century, and the modern scholarship, above all Schmitt’s Holy Greyhound. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate. Like that of Barlaam and Josaphat, Guinefort’s story belongs to a tale-type with Indian versions; like Saint George’s condemned Passion, his cult is known chiefly from the words of the churchman who tried to destroy it.

Timeline

The Indian fable of the faithful animal wrongly slain (c. 200 BC – 5th c.)

The earliest attested form of the tale is a fable, not a cult. The Sanskrit Panchatantra (compiled somewhere between the third century BC and the fifth century AD) tells of a Brahmin’s loyal mongoose left to guard an infant: a black snake approaches the cradle, the mongoose kills it, and the returning mother, seeing the animal’s blood-smeared mouth, strikes it dead — only to find her baby safe beside the dead serpent, and to be undone by remorse. This is the story-skeleton the folklorists label Aarne–Thompson–Uther type 178A, “the faithful animal rashly killed.” Its plot is the plot of Stephen of Bourbon’s Guinefort story. Whether the Guinefort story descends from it by the literary road west, or reached the Dombes as an oral folk tale, is disputed; see the interpretation page.1

The fable migrates west: Kalīla wa-Dimna, the Seven Sages, the Gesta Romanorum (8th–13th c.)

From India the fable rode the same great current of story that carried Barlaam and Josaphat. It passed into the Arabic Kalīla wa-Dimna (eighth century), and through that and its Persian and Hebrew kin into the Latin West — John of Capua’s Directorium vitae humanae (c. 1263–1278), and the widely-copied Seven Sages of Rome, where it circulated as the tale of “Canis,” and the Gesta Romanorum. Along the way the rescuer shifts with its setting — mongoose to greyhound, weasel, or falcon; the menace from snake to wolf — and the hero is upgraded from a Brahmin’s pet to a nobleman’s hound. The Welsh Gelert, Llywelyn the Great’s wolfhound, belongs to the same tale-type (its localization at “Beddgelert” dates from the eighteenth century). Lester K. Little, reviewing Schmitt, reports versions “in Old French of 1155 and one in Latin of 1184”; Rebecca Rist (2019), citing Schmitt, notes that the first Latin translation of the Arabic line appeared after Stephen of Bourbon’s death.2

A human Saint Guinefort of Pavia (attested 11th c.)

A man was also venerated as Saint Guinefort. A martyr Guinefortus (Gunifortus) was venerated at Pavia, said to have died under Diocletian; his Acts tell that arrows were discharged against his body, a legend compared with Saint Sebastian’s (shot full of arrows “like a hedgehog”) ⚠ unverified. The Roman Martyrology commemorates him on 22 August. From the end of the eleventh century his cult spread west of the Alps into France, carried, as Rist (2019) puts it, “probably” by Cluniac monks; Schmitt mapped fifty-nine places where a variant of the cult was observed, eight in Italy and the rest in France. Schmitt reconstructs the dog’s name and its office as protector of children as taken from this human saint; Rist also lists other regional Guinefort cults and child-healer saints of similar names. Where the name came from is disputed; see the interpretation page. The greyhound is now, by convention, given the human saint’s 22 August feast.3

Stephen of Bourbon records the dog-cult — and suppresses it (c. 1250–1261)

The only known medieval source for the holy greyhound is the Dominican Stephen of Bourbon (d. c. 1261), who set the story down in the De supersticione section of his unfinished preaching-manual, the Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus. He learned of the cult in the diocese of Lyon while hearing confessions — “numerous women confessed that they had taken their children to Saint Guinefort. As I thought that this was some holy person, I continued with my enquiry and finally learned that this was actually a greyhound.” He reports the whole legend (the guarding dog, the serpent, the rash killing, the burial in a well ringed with planted trees), and, in detail, the healing ritual conducted at the grove by an old woman: offerings of salt, swaddling-clothes hung on the bushes, the naked child passed nine times between two tree-trunks with an invocation to the fauns to take the sick changeling and return the healthy child, then left alone between two lit candles. Stephen judged it an offering to demons and reports that the candles “burnt and killed several babies”; he “had the dead dog disinterred, and the sacred wood cut down and burnt,” and had the lords of the estate decree forfeiture against anyone who returned. Like the George Passion, known in part through the Gelasian Decree’s condemnation, the dog-cult is known from the source written to suppress it.4

Later traces of the cult (1632 → 1940s)

Later sources record a cult of Saint Guinefort at the site. A document of 1632 refers to a chapel of “St. Guy le Fort” where Schmitt places the original cult. In 1826 a survey by the local bishop produced a letter about “the spurious cult of St. Guinefort to whom mothers brought sickly children” (its ascription to the curé of Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne, and its report of tree-branches knotted to cure fever, ⚠ unverified). After Stephen’s exemplum was published in 1877, the folklorist A. Vayssière found that everyone he spoke with “avowed that Guinefort was a dog,” placing its death in a woodsman’s cabin rather than a knight’s chateau (the date 1879, and his report of a wood hung with thousands of knotted branches, ⚠ unverified). The Abbé Delaigne replied in 1886, “Is Guinefort a saint or a dog? That is the question,” and argued that he was a saint; the Baron Raverat answered both with “Guinefort was first a dog, then a saint.” An old woman still active in the 1930s was remembered as the cult’s last guardian; the last known visit to cure a sick child fell around the 1940s, and community memory of the practices was still present in the 1970s. Whether these traces record one continuous cult is disputed; see the interpretation page.5

Modern scholarship reconstructs the cult (1979 → today)

Jean-Claude Schmitt’s Le Saint Lévrier (1979; English The Holy Greyhound, 1983) built a book-length study on Stephen’s single exemplum, as a study of how a historian recovers medieval popular culture through the hostile sources that record it. Schmitt argued that the dog-saint arose from the confluence of three folk strands — the international faithful-hound fable, a pre-existing magical rite for “changeling” children, and the name and child-patronage of the human Saint Guinefort — and he mapped some fifty-nine Guinefort cult-centres and traced the greyhound’s worship down to the twentieth century. Rebecca Rist (2019) writes that Stephen’s censure “was not just a ban on a non-orthodox cult”: “It was also a condemnation by the papal inquisition of a healing cult which had got out of hand and had led to the death of infants.” That he “chose to shut the rite down, rather than imposing more severe penalties, suggests,” in her reading, “that he almost certainly did not regard the cult as ritual infanticide.” How the strands combined is set out under Disputed readings.6

Disputed readings

How the legend’s origin, its name, its healing rite and its later history have been read.

  • Does the Guinefort story descend from the Indian “faithful animal wrongly slain” fable by the literary road west? On whether the peasants had the tale from the written versions or as an oral folk tale.
  • Where did the greyhound’s name and child-patronage come from? The martyr of Pavia, the name’s own etymology, or a regional family of child-healer saints.
  • What was the faun-invocation rite at Guinefort’s grove? An invocation of demons, as Stephen judged it, or an older folk rite for changeling children.
  • Do the later traces of Saint Guinefort’s cult record one continuous cult from the thirteenth century to the twentieth?
