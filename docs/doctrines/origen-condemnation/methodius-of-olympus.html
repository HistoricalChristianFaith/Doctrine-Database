Methodius of Olympus on the condemnation of Origen

Dates: d. c. 311, martyred · Wikipedia
Summary: Methodius's are the earliest extant works written at length against Origen's teaching. In three works — a dialogue on the resurrection (the Aglaophon), a treatise on created things, and a lost book on the witch of Endor — he attacked the pre-existence of souls, the eternity of the world, and above all Origen's account of the risen body. He wrote in the form of a philosophical dialogue; the surviving texts do not call Origen a heretic, and in the course of the argument he cites him with approval on a point of exegesis. This page covers his place on the timeline of judgements passed on Origen: what he charged, on what grounds, how Caesarea answered him, and how his dialogue was copied, some seventy years later, into Epiphanius's heresy-entry.

The man, the see, and Eusebius's omission

What Jerome records

The earliest account of him is Jerome's, in the catalogue of 392:

Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia and afterwards of Tyre, composed books Against Porphyry written in polished and logical style also a Banquet of the ten virgins, an excellent work On the resurrection, against Origen and On the Pythonissa and On free will, also against Origen. He also wrote commentaries On Genesis and On the Song of Songs and many others which are widely read. At the end of the recent persecution or, as others affirm, in the reign of Decius and Valerianus, he was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece.1

Three of the six titles Jerome lists are anti-Origen: On the Resurrection, On the Pythonissa (against Origen's reading of the witch of Endor in 1 Samuel 28), and On Free Will. Jerome gives two dates for the martyrdom. Porphyry wrote in the 270s, so that the alternative Jerome reports, under Decius and Valerian in the 250s, would place Methodius's death before the work he answered; the date conventionally given is the one Jerome puts first, the end of the last persecution, about 311.

Olympus, Patara, Tyre, Philippi

His see is variously reported. Jerome's transfer to Tyre is otherwise unattested, and the episcopal list for Tyre in those years is occupied; some manuscripts and later writers call him bishop of Patara, the dramatic setting of the Aglaophon; Franz Diekamp (1928) proposed Philippi in Macedonia, which would fit Jerome's placing of the martyrdom in Greece. The Catholic Encyclopedia article (1911) settles on Olympus and treats the rest as later confusion.2 His own writing supplies one small confirmation of the Lycian setting: describing a marvel he says he witnessed personally, he places it “on Olympus, which is a mountain of Lycia.”3

Eusebius's Church History

The Church History, the fullest surviving record of the generation in which Methodius lived and died, has no entry for him at all — no notice of his works, and none of his martyrdom, though Eusebius catalogues the martyrs of that persecution at length. The Catholic Encyclopedia accounts for the omission “probably because he opposed various theories of Origen,” Eusebius being Origen's biographer and defender.4

The charges

That the body is not a fetter, and was not put on after a fall

The Aglaophon is a dialogue: Methodius argues against a named interlocutor, Aglaophon, who holds the Origenist position, and the tone throughout is that of an argument among Christians. The ninth-century epitome of it opens by naming the target:

Read the Book on the Resurrection by St. Methodius, Bishop and Martyr, of which that which follows is a selection, that the body is not the fetter of the soul, as Origen thought, nor are souls called by the prophet Jeremiah “fettered” on account of their being within bodies… That Origen said that the body was given to the soul as a fetter after the fall, and that previously it lived without a body; but that this body which we wear is the cause of our sins; wherefore also he called it a fetter, as it can hinder the soul from good works.5

His counter-argument appeals to the history of sin after the fall:

But surely we see that, at the beginning, Cain, clad in this body, committed murder; and it is evident into what wickedness those who succeeded him ran. The body is not, then, a fetter upon evil, nor indeed a fetter at all; nor was the soul clothed in it for the first time after the fall.6

Behind this stands a definition of man he thinks Origen has taken from Plato rather than from Scripture: “man, with respect to his nature, is most truly said to be neither soul without body, nor, on the other hand, body without soul; but a being composed out of the union of soul and body into one form of the beautiful. But Origen said that the soul alone is man, as did Plato.”7 This is the earliest extant form of the pre-existence charge. Whether the pre-existence of souls was an open question when Origen taught it is a disputed reading.

That the flesh itself rises, not merely its form

On the resurrection Methodius states his opponent's position before answering it. Origen, he says, holds that the material of the body is in constant flux and so cannot be what rises; what persists and is raised is the form — the individuating shape — stamped now on a spiritual body:

Origen therefore thinks that the same flesh will not be restored to the soul, but that the form of each, according to the appearance by which the flesh is now distinguished, shall arise stamped upon another spiritual body; so that every one will again appear the same form; and that this is the resurrection which is promised.8

His reply is that a form has no existence apart from the matter it shapes, and perishes with it: melt a bronze statue and the shape does not survive somewhere, waiting to be re-imposed, for “the shape which disappears around the brass is separated from the melted statue, and has no longer a substantial existence.” Hence “it is, therefore, untenable to say, that the form shall arise in nowise corrupted, but that the body in which the form was stamped shall be destroyed.”9 And he presses a scriptural difficulty: if the resurrection is the appearing of the form only, then Moses and Elijah at the Transfiguration had already risen, and Christ is not “the first begotten of the dead.”10 How Origen's teaching on the risen body relates to Methodius's account of it, and to the later charge of a spherical risen body, is a disputed reading.

In the surviving Greek of the dialogue itself he puts the same point without the technical apparatus, and objects to the method of allegorizing:

For I cannot endure the trifling of some who shamelessly do violence to Scripture, in order that their opinion, that the resurrection is without flesh, may find support; supposing rational bones and flesh, and in different ways changing it backwards and forwards by allegorizing.11

He reads the “coats of skins” of Genesis 3:21 not as bodies but as mortality, which on his reading is God's remedy rather than God's sentence: death was appointed “in order that, by the dissolution of the body, sin might be altogether destroyed from the very roots”, as a fig-tree rooted in a temple wall must be torn out before the stones can be reset.12 And he refuses the inference from “they are as the angels in heaven” to a fleshless resurrection: Christ “did not say ‘they shall be angels,’ but like angels,” in respect of not marrying — as one may say the moon shines like the sun without saying that the moon is the sun.13

That the world is not co-eternal with God

The second front is the treatise On Things Created, of which a synopsis survives. Here Methodius takes on the argument that God cannot have become a Creator, since a maker with nothing made is no maker — so the world must always have existed:

For he says, since there is no workman without some work, or maker without something made, so neither is there an Almighty without an object of His power… And so it must be, that these things were made by God from the beginning, and that there was no time in which they did not exist… And it will result that the unchangeable and unaltered God has altered and changed.14

Methodius answers it twice over. First, that it makes God's perfection depend on the world: if God is Almighty and Creator on account of what He rules and makes, then “He would by Himself be imperfect and have need of these things” — whereas “He is His own riches, and is by Himself rich and powerful.” Second, that the change-objection cuts both ways, since God rested from making: “If, then, the act of making, after not making, makes an alteration in God, does not His ceasing to make after making the same?” The interlocutor arguing the Origenist side is addressed in the dialogue as “O Centaur.”15

The manner of the argument

The surviving texts do not call for Origen's condemnation or invoke ecclesiastical authority. Origen is addressed as an opponent in argument, in the second person — “If then, O Origen, you maintain…” — and is cited with approval in the course of the argument, on the etymology of Hades: by hell is meant “the air, derived from aeïdēs, because it is invisible, as was said by Origen.”16 Socrates, writing in Origen's defence in the fifth century, observes that his critics attacked different doctrines and none of them touched him on the Trinity, so that “those who have sought out whatever they deemed worthy of reprobation in him, and yet have never charged him with holding unsound views respecting the holy Trinity, are in this way most distinctly shown to bear witness to his orthodox piety.”17

The answer from Caesarea

A reply came from Caesarea. In the sixth and last book of the Apology for Origen — the book Eusebius added after Pamphilus's execution — Eusebius answered Methodius. Jerome, who had the work about a century later, reports it in his quarrel with Rufinus:

Eusebius the Bishop of Cæsarea, of whom I have made mention above, in the sixth book of his Apology for Origen makes the same complaint against Methodius the bishop and martyr, which you make against me in your praises of me.18

The complaint, as Jerome reports it, was one of inconsistency: how could Methodius write against Origen after having spoken as he had of his teaching?

Transmission and later reception

Two later readers preserved the text. Epiphanius of Salamis copied the Aglaophon into Panarion 64, his catalogue entry on Origen: the long stretch of the dialogue that survives entire in Greek — Methodius's own voice, not an epitome — survives precisely because Epiphanius copied it into a heresy-catalogue. The remainder comes through the ninth-century summaries of Photius, who read both the resurrection dialogue and On Things Created and abstracted them.19

Socrates, writing about 440 and defending Origen, put Methodius at the head of a list of four:

And first Methodius, bishop of a city in Lycia named Olympus, labored under this malady; next Eustathius, who for a short time presided over the church at Antioch; after him Apollinaris; and lastly Theophilus. This quaternion of revilers has traduced Origen, but not on the same grounds…20

He adds a report for which he is the only witness: “Methodius indeed, when he had in various places railed against Origen, afterwards as if retracting all he had previously said, expresses his admiration of the man, in a dialogue which he entitled Xenon.”21 The Xenon is lost; Socrates's own wording is “as if retracting.”

Reception and debate

  • Two of the three positions Methodius attacked — the pre-existence of souls, and a risen body that is not this flesh — are also the subjects of the first and the tenth of the fifteen anathemas of the sixth century.
  • Methodius read Origen's works in Greek about a century before Rufinus's Latin translations, and his reports are among the earliest extant evidence of what Origen was taken to hold. Rufinus's argument that passages charged against Origen were interpolated by others is set out at its disputed-reading page.
  • Origen's own account of the soul between death and resurrection is traced on the intermediate state; the pre-existence of souls on the origin of the soul.
