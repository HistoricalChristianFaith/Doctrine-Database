Justinian and the edict to Menas (543)

Dates: c. 482 – 14 November 565; emperor from 527; the edict to Menas issued c. 543 · Wikipedia
Summary: In or about 543 the emperor Justinian addressed to Menas, patriarch of Constantinople, a long treatise against Origen with a set of extracts from Peri Archōn and nine anathemas attached, and instructed him to have the bishops resident in the capital subscribe it. The edict sets out the charges against Origen as numbered anathemas issued by imperial authority; its occasion was a dispute between parties of monks in the Judaean desert. This page covers the document's occasion, its text, its authority, and its later use as a witness to Origen's own words. It is one block of the timeline of judgements passed on Origen; the question of what the council of 553 did with it is treated there.

The occasion: a quarrel in the Judaean desert

The revival of the Origenist question in the sixth century arose among the monks of Palestine; whether the condemnation that followed was driven by ecclesiastical politics or by doctrine is a disputed reading. The narrative source is Cyril of Scythopolis, a monk of the Judaean desert writing in the 550s, who tells the story in the closing chapters of his Life of Sabas. Cyril wrote from the anti-Origenist side and was himself among the monks who took over the New Laura after its Origenists were expelled; Daniel Hombergen (2001) has examined the limits of his testimony as a source for sixth-century Origenism.1

An Origenist current had taken root in the Palestinian lauras (how far it drew on Evagrius of Pontus rather than on Origen is part of a disputed reading), and its centre was the New Laura near Tekoa, where Nonnus and Leontius of Byzantium had settled around 520. The aged Sabas, founder of the Great Laura, travelled to Constantinople in 531 and asked Justinian, among other things, for imperial help against them; he was honoured and his requests were granted, but no measure against the Origenists followed. He died the following year. In 537 the new abbot of the Great Laura, Gelasius, had an anti-Origenist book read out to his monks; the Origenists among them were expelled and joined Nonnus at the New Laura, and in Cyril's account the quarrel turned violent. Meanwhile two monks from the Origenist side, Domitian and Theodore Askidas, had come to a synod in the capital and been made bishops there — Domitian of Ancyra, Theodore of Caesarea in Cappadocia — and neither went to reside in his see. Evagrius Scholasticus, writing a generation later, describes Theodore's position at court: he was “constantly about the person of Justinian, as being trusty and highly serviceable to him.”2 On the anti-Origenist side, Gelasius and Sophronius drew up a memorandum against Origen for the patriarch of Jerusalem; it was put into the hands of the papal representative at Constantinople, the deacon Pelagius, who brought it before the emperor. The edict followed.

The document

What Justinian issued is a treatise, not a canon: a Liber adversus Origenem setting out the grounds of condemnation, followed by a series of excerpts from Peri Archōn — some two dozen of them — and then the anathemas. It was addressed to Menas as patriarch of the capital, with the instruction that the bishops then resident in Constantinople (the synodos endēmousa, the “home synod”) subscribe it; copies went to the other patriarchs, Pope Vigilius at Rome among them, and all gave their adhesion, Prat citing Liberatus and Cassiodorus for Vigilius's.3

The nine anathemas

In Richard Price's translation they run:

1. If anyone says or holds that the souls of human beings pre-exist, as previously minds and holy powers, but that they reached satiety with divine contemplation and turned to what is worse and for this reason grew old in the love of God and are therefore called souls, and were made to descend into bodies as a punishment, let him be anathema.

2. If anyone says or holds that the Lord's soul pre-existed and came into being united to God the word before the incarnation and birth from a virgin, let him be anathema.

3. If anyone holds or says that the body of our Lord Jesus Christ was first formed in the womb of the holy Virgin and that afterwards both God the word and the soul, being pre-existent, were united to it, let him be anathema.

4. If anyone says or holds that the Word of God became like all the heavenly orders, becoming cherubim for the cherubim, seraphim for the seraphim, and becoming (in a word) like all the powers above, let him be anathema.

5. If anyone says or holds that at the resurrection the bodies of human beings will be raised spherical and does not profess that we shall be raised upright, let him be anathema.

6. If anyone says or holds that heaven, sun, moon, stars, and the waters above the heavens are ensouled and rational powers, let him be anathema.

7. If anyone says or holds that in the age to come Christ the Master will be crucified on behalf of demons as well as on behalf of human beings, let him be anathema.

8. If anyone says or holds that God's power is finite and that he created [only] what he could grasp and comprehend, or that creation is coeternal with God, let him be anathema.

9. If anyone says or holds that the punishment of demons and impious human beings is temporary and that it will have an end at some time, and that there will be a restoration of demons and impious human beings, let him be anathema.4

They are sometimes counted as ten rather than nine; the older Latin handbooks describe the edict as appending “twenty-four censurable texts” from Peri Archōn and “ten propositions to be anathematized.”5 The difference lies in how the propositions are divided.

Its authority

The edict is often cited as “the synod of Constantinople of 543”, and Cyril reports that Menas and the bishops signed. Richard Price, who edited and translated the acts of the council that followed, describes the status of the canons: they were issued as an imperial decree and sent to the patriarchs “not for their confirmation but for their circulation. Their authority was imperial rather than synodal.”6 No acts of a synod survive; what survives is the emperor's document and the report of its subscription, among the signatories being Theodore Askidas, of the Origenist party, who remained at court afterwards.

The earlier judgements on this timeline — Theophilus's eight articles of 400, Epiphanius's catalogue entry, Augustine's single doctrine — were lists of errors in a letter, a handbook, or a synodical epitome. The edict of 543 sets the charges out as numbered anathemas issued under imperial authority; the fifteen anathemas of the 550s take the same form.

The anathemas and Origen's writings

How far each of the nine corresponds to what Origen wrote is disputed anathema by anathema. The first (the fall of pre-existent minds cooled from contemplation) and the ninth (the end of punishment and the restoration of demons) concern charges brought against Origen since Methodius and among the accusations Pamphilus answered; see whether pre-existence was an open question when Origen taught it and whether he taught universal restoration as doctrine. On the sixth, Origen discusses the heavenly bodies as living and rational at Peri Archōn I.7 and Against Celsus V; whether he taught what the anathema states is a disputed reading, as is whether his remarks were offered as inquiry or assertion. On the eighth, Origen argues an eternal, immutable Creator at I.2.10 and a determinate, finite number of creatures at II.9.1; how these relate to the charge that “God's power is finite” and creation coeternal is set out at its disputed-reading page. The fifth (the spherical risen body) is treated at whether Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh, and the seventh at whether he taught a second crucifixion for the demons. The second and third (the pre-existent soul of Christ united to the Word before the incarnation) and the fourth (the Word becoming cherubim for the cherubim) bear on the question whether the sixth-century anathemas describe Origen's teaching or a later Palestinian Origenism; see that disputed reading.

The acts of 553 preserve, in the libels the anti-Origenist monks submitted, statements attributed to the sixth-century party. Evagrius Scholasticus quotes from the fifth head of the schedule appended to the synod's address to Justinian, giving “the blasphemous expressions uttered by private individuals belonging to what is called the New Laura”: Theodore Askidas, he reports, said “If the Apostles and Martyrs at the present time work miracles, and are already so highly honoured, unless they shall be equal with Christ in the restitution of things, in what respect is there a restitution for them?”7 The saying is attributed to a sixth-century monk and concerns the eventual equality of the saints with Christ; it is not presented as a quotation from Peri Archōn. Whether the anathemas of 543 and the fifteen of 553 describe Origen's teaching or that of his sixth-century followers is a disputed reading.

The edict as evidence for Origen's text

Peri Archōn survives whole only in Rufinus's Latin of 397, which Rufinus said he had revised in places; the substantial Greek of the work that survives is largely what its opponents quoted in order to condemn it, and Justinian's edict is the largest such body of extracts. Paul Koetschau's critical edition of 1913 — the one G. W. Butterworth translated in 1936, the standard English version for some eighty years — treated Rufinus as systematically untrustworthy and Justinian, along with Jerome, as a reliable witness, and accordingly inserted the Greek fragments into the Latin where he judged Rufinus had suppressed something. Butterworth's English thus printed extracts from Justinian and Jerome within the text of Origen.8

Later editors have taken a different course. Crouzel and Simonetti's edition argued that several of the passages Koetschau imported are not exact quotations of Peri Archōn at all, and that Justinian probably never read the book, working instead from the dossier the Palestinian monks supplied. John Behr's Oxford edition of 2017 prints Rufinus's text and places the Justinianic material in an appendix, with an introduction arguing against Koetschau's use of it.9 How far Origen's teaching can be recovered from this transmission is a disputed reading.

Reception and debate

  • The edict of 543 was issued as imperial legislation and received by subscription. Evagrius Scholasticus reports that Justinian later “proceeded to compel the bishops in all quarters to give their assent” to another edict of his own, the aphthartodocetist edict of his last years.10
  • The relation of these nine to the fifteen anathemas usually printed as the council's is disputed. Franz Diekamp held that the fifteen were the work of bishops already gathered at Constantinople in the weeks before the council opened on 5 May 553, restating the same targets in more explicitly Christological terms; Hefele assigned the fifteen to the synod of 543. Both sets are treated on the summary timeline, and whether the council itself passed the fifteen is a disputed reading.
  • Two ancient narratives of the affair survive and do not agree in their mechanics: Cyril of Scythopolis's Life of Sabas and Liberatus of Carthage's Breviarium. Where they diverge, the account above draws on both.
