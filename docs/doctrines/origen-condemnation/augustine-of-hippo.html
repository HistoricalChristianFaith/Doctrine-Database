Augustine of Hippo on the condemnation of Origen

Dates: 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine wrote on Origen between c. 415 and c. 428. He came to the case from outside the Jerome–Rufinus quarrel, at the prompting of a Spanish priest who reported Origenist books circulating in Spain as an answer to Priscillianism. He rejected two doctrines — the pre-existent fall of souls into bodies as punishment, and the eventual release of the damned and of the devil — and in his catalogue of heresies he reported the other charges he had received from Epiphanius together with the reply of Origen's defenders. On the origin of the soul he declined throughout his life to decide. This page covers his place on the timeline of judgements passed on Origen; his writing on the soul's origin is traced on the origin of the soul, where he has a page of his own.

How the question reached him: Orosius, 414

In 414 the Spanish priest Paulus Orosius arrived at Hippo with a memorandum on the state of his home province. Its burden was that Priscillianism had been answered in Spain with books of Origen's, and that the readers of those books had taken up errors of their own from them. Orosius sets out what he had heard taught, in six articles — that nothing God made began to be made (“Deus enim quaecumque fecit faciendo non coepit”); that angels, principalities, powers, souls and demons are of one origin and one substance, each placed according to the quality of its deserts (“Maiorem locum minor culpa promeruit” — a lesser fault earned a higher place); that the world was made last of all so that souls which had sinned beforehand might be purged in it; that the eternal fire is neither real fire nor eternal; that after the purging of conscience all the souls of sinners will return into the unity of the body of Christ; and — this one, Orosius says, they wanted to assert and could not carry — that since the good substance made in the devil cannot perish, his malice being wholly burnt away, that substance too must one day be saved.1

Augustine's reply, Ad Orosium contra Priscillianistas et Origenistas, is his earliest extant writing on the subject. He grants the grief Orosius feels — error ought to have been driven out by truth, not by another falsehood, “for this is to change an evil, not to avoid it” — and then says that the province gained from those books on the doctrine of the Trinity, the point on which he judged Priscillian's error most serious:

Verumtamen quia de ipso Deo creaturarum omnium conditore, hoc est de ipsa coaeterna et incommutabili trinitate ab eis, qui ad vos libros Origenis attulerunt, id traditum esse commemoras quod veritas habet… non parum boni ex libris illis provincia vestra percepit, in ea re maxime, in qua capitaliter erratur.2

— since what those who brought Origen's books taught your people about God the maker of all things, that is about the co-eternal and unchangeable Trinity itself, is what the truth holds, your province got no small good from those books, in the very matter where the error is mortal. (No published English version of this treatise is available online; the renderings here are from the Latin.) What he forbids is the last article on Orosius's list, on the ground that nothing may be added to the Judge's final sentence: “do not dare to think anything about the correction of the devil and his angels and their restoration to their former state—not because we begrudge the devil and the demons… but because we ought to add nothing of our own presumption to the final sentence of the highest and most truthful Judge.”3 The argument he then makes against a temporary hell, which he made again in City of God XXI.23 (below), rests on the symmetry of the two eternities in Matthew 25: if mercy persuades us that the punishment of the wicked will not be endless, what shall we believe about the reward of the just, “when in both parts, in the same place, in the same sentence, in the same word, eternity is pronounced?” Shall we say the just too will one day slide back out of that sanctification into uncleanness and death? “Absit hoc a christianae fidei sanitate.”4

The City of God: two chapters on Origen

Book XI.23 — the fall of souls into bodies

Writing the eleventh book about 417, Augustine reached the goodness of creation and stopped to name a Christian account of the world's origin he rejected. He attributes it to a named book of Origen's, the Peri Archōn:

But they say that souls, though not, indeed, parts of God, but created by Him, sinned by abandoning God; that, in proportion to their various sins, they merited different degrees of debasement from heaven to earth, and diverse bodies as prison-houses; and that this is the world, and this the cause of its creation, not the production of good things, but the restraining of evil. Origen is justly blamed for holding this opinion. For in the books which he entitles peri archōn, that is, Of Origins, this is his sentiment, this his utterance. And I cannot sufficiently express my astonishment, that a man so erudite and well versed in ecclesiastical literature, should not have observed, in the first place, how opposed this is to the meaning of this authoritative Scripture, which, in recounting all the works of God, regularly adds, “And God saw that it was good.”5

Two objections follow. The first is exegetical: Genesis gives the world's creation one cause — that a good God should make good creatures — and a cosmos built as a house of correction is not what “behold, it was very good” describes. The second is a reduction: if bodies are graded penalties, then the devils, who are deepest in wickedness, ought to have the grossest bodies of all, whereas, he says, “the wickedest devil possesses an ethereal body, while man, wicked, it is true, but with a wickedness small and venial in comparison with his, received even before his sin a body of clay.” And if the sun exists because exactly one soul sinned to exactly that degree, then a hundred souls sinning alike would have given us a hundred suns. He ends by saying that men who hold such opinions “should aim at confining, not souls of which they know not what they say, but themselves.”6

Book XXI.17 — the “tender-hearted” and Origen

Ten books and about ten years later, arguing against the various parties who expected the damned to be released, Augustine gives Origen a paragraph that states the doctrine, reports that the Church had condemned him for it, and names the error he singles out (“especially”): the alternation of happiness and misery.

I must now, I see, enter the lists of amicable controversy with those tender-hearted Christians who decline to believe that any, or that all of those whom the infallibly just Judge may pronounce worthy of the punishment of hell, shall suffer eternally… In respect of this matter, Origen was even more indulgent; for he believed that even the devil himself and his angels, after suffering those more severe and prolonged pains which their sins deserved, should be delivered from their torments, and associated with the holy angels. But the Church, not without reason, condemned him for this and other errors, especially for his theory of the ceaseless alternation of happiness and misery, and the interminable transitions from the one state to the other at fixed periods of ages; for in this theory he lost even the credit of being merciful, by allotting to the saints real miseries for the expiation of their sins, and false happiness, which brought them no true and secure joy, that is, no fearless assurance of eternal blessedness.7

The sentence “the Church, not without reason, condemned him” was written in the 420s, before the imperial and conciliar acts of 543 and 553, by a bishop who had taken no part in the Egyptian and Palestinian quarrels. It speaks of Origen's condemnation as a past act and does not name the proceedings meant; the earlier proceedings recorded on this timeline are the synods and papal letters of 400–401. In the same passage he separates the “tender-hearted,” with whom he enters “amicable controversy,” from Origen, and his stated objection to the alternation is that it allots the saints “false happiness” without “fearless assurance of eternal blessedness.” His argument against a temporary hell follows in XXI.23, resting on the same reading of Matthew 25 he had given Orosius: “as the eternal life of the saints shall be endless, so too the eternal punishment of those who are doomed to it shall have no end.”8

De haeresibus 43 (c. 428): the charges reported and the charge endorsed

At the end of his life Augustine compiled, for the deacon Quodvultdeus, a catalogue of eighty-eight heresies. Origen has an entry — number 43 — in which Augustine reports a list of charges he took from an earlier catalogue and distinguishes those he reports from the one he condemns in his own voice. (Number 42 is a different Origen, of a libertine sect Epiphanius names; the Alexandrian is 43.)

He opens by quoting Epiphanius verbatim — the four charges from the Panarion's index: that the Origenists reject the resurrection of the dead, make Christ and the Holy Spirit creatures, and allegorize paradise, the heavens and all the rest. Then he reports what Origen's defenders say on three of the four:

Haec quidem de Origene Epiphanius. Sed qui eum defendunt unius eiusdemque substantiae esse dicunt docuisse Patrem et Filium et Spiritum Sanctum, neque resurrectionem reppulisse mortuorum; quamvis et in istis eum convincere studeant qui eius plura legerunt.9

— This much about Origen from Epiphanius. But those who defend him say that he taught the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit to be of one and the same substance, and did not reject the resurrection of the dead; though those who have read more of him try to convict him on these points too. Augustine thus records the Trinitarian and resurrection charges together with the defenders' denial and with the note that readers of more of Origen's works pressed them, without deciding between the two.

The doctrine he condemns in his own voice has two parts — the purging and release of all the damned including the devil and his angels, and then, after immense ages, the relapse of all the liberated back into the same evils, the alternation of blessednesses and miseries running on for ever in both directions:

Quis enim catholicus christianus vel doctus vel indoctus non vehementer exhorreat eam quam dicit purgationem malorum… ipsum etiam postremo diabolum atque angelos eius, quamvis post longissima tempora, purgatos atque liberatos regno Dei lucique restitui, et rursus post longissima tempora omnes qui liberati sunt ad haec mala denuo relabi et reverti, et has vices alternantes beatitudinum et miseriarum rationalis creaturae semper fuisse, semper fore?10

— For what catholic Christian, learned or unlearned, would not violently shudder at that purgation of the wicked which he speaks of… that even the devil himself at the last, and his angels, though after the longest ages, are purged and freed and restored to the kingdom of God and the light; and that again after the longest ages all who were freed slide back and return to these same evils; and that these alternating turns of blessedness and misery of the rational creation always have been and always will be? He closes by referring the reader to the City of God for his answer, and by naming as the source of the doctrine “the philosophers from whom Origen learnt these things.” Whether Peri Archōn asserts such a cycle or raises it as an inquiry is itself disputed: see whether Origen taught an endless succession of worlds and whether his condemned opinions were offered as inquiry or as assertion.

The origin of the soul: a question he left open

The pre-existence of souls Augustine had rejected in the City of God in its Origenist form, as a fall and a punishment. But the underlying question of where any soul comes from he declined all his life to settle, and he declined it in writing, to Jerome, in the middle of the controversy. Asked to adjudicate in 415, he answered that he did not know, listed the opinions he could not choose between, and said what was at stake for him: “where can the soul, even of an infant snatched away by death, have contracted the guilt which, unless the grace of Christ has come to the rescue by that sacrament of baptism which is administered even to infants, involves it in condemnation?”11 Five years later he was still saying “I have no certain knowledge how it came into my body”, and in the Retractations at the end: “as to its origin… I did not know then, and I still do not know.” The full record, with his reasons for setting aside the Origenist answer, is on his page on the origin of the soul.12

Placement on the timeline

  • Where Epiphanius listed four charges and Theophilus's synod eight, Augustine's De haeresibus entry condemns one in his own voice — universal restoration and the cycle that follows it — and reports the Trinitarian and resurrection charges with the defence. The passages quoted on this page do not take up the propositions of 543 and 553 about the original equality of minds or the spherical risen body.
  • His route into the Origen question was through Orosius and Priscillianism in Spain, not through the Palestinian quarrel between Jerome and Rufinus. His correspondence with Jerome, including his letter of 404 grieving over that quarrel, is its own timeline.
  • “The Church, not without reason, condemned him” (City of God XXI.17) was written more than a century before the acts of 543 and 553. Whether the papal sentence of 400–401 condemned the man or his books is a disputed reading.
  • Augustine appears on this wiki also on the origin of the soul, the Jerome–Augustine correspondence, and a number of the belief timelines.
