The Second Council of Constantinople (553) on Origen

Dates: 5 May – 2 June 553, eight sessions, at Constantinople · Wikipedia
Summary: Three bodies of evidence bear on the statement that Origen was condemned by the Fifth Ecumenical Council: the fifteen anathemas against Origen and the Origenists, which a manuscript heading attaches to the council, which Franz Diekamp, Richard Price and Georges Florovsky date to the bishops gathered before the opening, which the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya places at a sitting whose record is lost, and which Norman Tanner's edition leaves out as not the council's; the council's own eleventh canon, which names Origen last in a roll of those it anathematizes, a name Ramelli holds to be interpolated and the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya and the nineteenth-century editors of the acts hold authentic; and the reception, in which the councils of 680–681 and 787 refer to the fifth synod as having acted against Origen. This page sets out what the documents say. It is one block of the timeline of judgements passed on Origen; the imperial edict of ten years earlier is on Justinian's page.

What the council was called for

Justinian convoked the council. Its business was the Three Chapters — the writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and Ibas of Edessa — which the emperor had already condemned by edict in 543 and 551 and now wanted a council to endorse. Pope Vigilius had been brought from Rome to Constantinople by force in 546 and declined to take part; he declined even to open the council, so Justinian did. Eutychius, patriarch of Constantinople, presided, with about 150 bishops in the hall attached to Hagia Sophia; the Italian bishops stayed away with Vigilius. On 14 May the pope issued his first Constitutum, condemning sixty passages from Theodore but refusing to anathematize a dead man, and forbidding the council to proceed without him. The council struck his name from the diptychs at its seventh session, and Justinian had him detained. At the eighth and last session, on 2 June, the bishops published fourteen anathemas against the Three Chapters. On 8 December Vigilius assented to the condemnation of the Three Chapters, and in 554 issued a second Constitutum, which does not mention the council.1

Origen, who had died three hundred years before, was not among the Three Chapters, and he is not mentioned in the summons or in the letters surrounding the council. The nineteenth-century English editors of the acts raise the point:

To my mind the chief difficulty in supposing these anathematisms to have been adopted by the Fifth Ecumenical is that nothing whatever is said about Origen in the call of the council, nor in any of the letters written in connexion with it.2

The fifteen anathemas

The text most often quoted as “the condemnation of Origen” is a set of fifteen numbered anathemas, fuller and more explicitly Christological than the nine Justinian had appended to his edict to Menas in 543. They open on pre-existence and restoration, and close on the same pair restated as a single thesis about time:

I. If anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema.

XV. If anyone shall say that the life of the spirits shall be like to the life which was in the beginning while as yet the spirits had not come down or fallen, so that the end and the beginning shall be alike, and that the end shall be the true measure of the beginning: let him be anathema.3

Between those two the list describes a system. The second anathematizes the doctrine that all reasonable creatures were originally naked intelligences, alike in substance and undifferentiated, who “no longer desiring the sight of God… gave themselves over to worse things, each one following his own inclinations, and that they have taken bodies more or less subtile, and have received names” — cherubim, seraphim, thrones, angels, the celestial ranks being so many grades of a single fall. The third condemns the view “that the sun, the moon and the stars are also reasonable beings, and that they have only become what they are because they turned towards evil.” The fourth reaches the bottom of the same ladder:

IV. If anyone shall say that the reasonable creatures in whom the divine love had grown cold have been hidden in gross bodies such as ours, and have been called men, while those who have attained the lowest degree of wickedness have shared cold and obscure bodies and are become and called demons and evil spirits: let him be anathema.4

Anathemas VI–IX turn Christological, condemning the notion that Christ is a single unfallen intelligence united to the Word rather than the Word himself, and that he passed through the ranks of beings assuming the form of each. The tenth rejects a risen body that is “ethereal” and “having the form of a sphere.” The twelfth and the fourteenth take the end of the story:

XII. If anyone shall say that the heavenly Powers and all men and the Devil and evil spirits are united with the Word of God in all respects, as the Nous which is by them called Christ and which is in the form of God, and which humbled itself as they say; and [if anyone shall say] that the Kingdom of Christ shall have an end: let him be anathema.

XIV. If anyone shall say that all reasonable beings will one day be united in one, when the hypostases as well as the numbers and the bodies shall have disappeared… moreover, that in this pretended apocatastasis, spirits only will continue to exist, as it was in the feigned pre-existence: let him be anathema.5

The fifteen condemn propositions; Origen's name does not occur in the text of any of them. Whether the system they describe is Origen's own teaching or a sixth-century Origenism is a disputed reading: Franz Diekamp, Antoine Guillaumont and Richard Price relate the spherical resurrection body, the nous-Christ, and the equality of all spirits with Christ at the end to the Palestinian monks called Isochristoi, “those equal to Christ,” and to Evagrius, while Cyril of Scythopolis, Hefele and Georges Florovsky read the list as Origen's own scheme. On the third anathema, Origen discusses the stars as living and rational beings at Peri Archōn I.7; whether he taught what the anathema states is a disputed reading.

Whose anathemas are they?

The text as we have it was found by Peter Lambeck, the imperial librarian at Vienna, in the seventeenth century, and carries in his manuscript the heading “Canons, of the 165 holy Fathers of the holy fifth Synod, held in Constantinople” — 165 being the number of bishops who subscribed the acts of 553.6 This heading is the manuscript basis for attributing them to the council, and editors have doubted it since Walch and Döllinger. Three alternatives have been proposed: that the fifteen were passed by the home synod of 543 alongside Justinian's nine (Hefele's view, of which he wrote — “We certainly possess no strong and decisive proof that the fifteen anathematisms belong to the Constantinopolitan synod of the year 543”); that they were the work of the bishops already gathered at Constantinople in the weeks before the council opened on 5 May 553, which is Franz Diekamp's reconstruction of 1899, followed by Richard Price and by Georges Florovsky, who holds that Origen's condemnation “was proclaimed by the fathers who had convened earlier than the official opening of the Council”; or that the council itself, or a narrower group of its leading delegates, passed them at a sitting whose record has not survived, which is the view of the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya.7 Ilaria Ramelli holds that the fifteen “were already prepared by Justinian, or his counselors, before the opening of the council, and were appended to his letter to the conciliar fathers,” where “Origen is considered to be the inspirer of the so-called Isochristoi.”8 Norman Tanner's edition of the conciliar decrees prints the acts of 553 without them, on the ground that “recent studies have shown that these anathemas cannot be attributed to this council.”9

Cyril of Scythopolis, a Judaean monk writing within five years of the council, gives a different account. He closes his Life of Sabas by reporting that the assembled fathers issued a common and universal anathema against Origen, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and the teaching of Evagrius and Didymus on pre-existence and restoration, with the four patriarchs present and consenting. He is the earliest narrative source; Ramelli describes his account as “the position of the Sabaite opponents of Origen.”10 Florovsky reads Cyril as one who “directly inculcates it in the Ecumenical Council,” and holds the condemnation the council's own, noting that it is included in the eleventh anathema and that Theodore Askidas mentions it during the council; the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya follows Evagrius Scholasticus's order of events, in which the monks' petition against Origen's teaching reached Justinian after the sessions on the Three Chapters, and holds that the fifteen were adopted at a single sitting whose text is lost.7 The nineteenth-century editors of the acts declined to decide: “It would seem that with the evidence now in our possession it would be the height of rashness to give a dogmatic answer to this question… After all is said the matter remains involved in the greatest obscurity.”11

The eleventh canon

The council's own fourteen anathemas against the Three Chapters do contain Origen's name, once, in the eleventh:

If anyone does not anathematize Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen, as well as their heretical books, and also all other heretics who have already been condemned and anathematized by the holy, catholic and apostolic church and by the four holy synods which have already been mentioned, and also all those who have thought or now think in the same way as the aforesaid heretics and who persist in their error even to death: let him be anathema.12

The clause requires assent to condemnations it describes as already made, and names Origen in a list without stating charges. Ramelli points to two features of the list. He comes last, out of the chronological order the other six observe — Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinaris, Nestorius, Eutyches run from the 320s to the 440s, and the man from the 230s is put after all of them. And the parallel roll in Justinian's Homonoia, the draft of the anathemas issued from the imperial chancery, does not have him. Ramelli takes both together, with the fact that Vigilius's own documents do not name Origen, as evidence that the name was inserted afterwards: “This suggests an interpolation. Indeed, several anathemas, including those which mention Origen, did not belong to the official acts of the council, but were interpolated later.”13 The minutes of the sessions survive in a Latin version, the original Greek acts being lost; the anathemas survive also in Greek, which Tanner's edition translates as “the more authoritative version”, and the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, which judges the interpolation hypothesis unconvincing, notes that Origen's name stands in the Greek list as well.7 The nineteenth-century editors held that “That Origen was condemned by name in the Eleventh Canon of this council there seems no possible reason to doubt,” adding that this did not decide the question of the fifteen.14 Whether, taking the fifteen anathemas and the eleventh canon together, Origen himself was anathematized by an ecumenical council is a disputed reading.

The reception

The later councils refer to the fifth synod as having acted against Origen. Constantinople III (680–681) described “the fifth holy synod, the latest of them, which was gathered here against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius”; Nicaea II (787) anathematized “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus, as did the fifth synod, that assembled at Constantinople.”15 Both refer to the fifth synod's action, and both name Origen together with Didymus and Evagrius. Ramelli reports that suspicions had been raised in 680 that the original Greek acts of 553 were interpolated.16

Notes

  • The three questions are separable. The fifteen anathemas are of the 540s or 550s. Whether the council in session passed them is doubted by Tanner and Ramelli; Diekamp, Price and Florovsky assign them to the bishops gathered before the opening, Florovsky counting the condemnation the council's; the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya places them at a council sitting whose record is lost; and Cyril of Scythopolis, the earliest narrative witness, reports them as the council's act.7 The eleventh canon names Origen in the Latin acts and in the Greek text of the anathemas; Ramelli disputes whether the canon did so in 553, citing the Homonoia and Vigilius's silence. From 680 the councils refer to Origen as condemned by the fifth synod.
  • Henri Crouzel (Jesuit), in articles of 1986 and 1999 summarized by Ramelli, concluded that Origen was never officially condemned by the church.17
  • The same council, at its eighth session, gave the term aeiparthenos — ever-virgin — its conciliar standing, which is treated on the perpetual virginity timeline.18
