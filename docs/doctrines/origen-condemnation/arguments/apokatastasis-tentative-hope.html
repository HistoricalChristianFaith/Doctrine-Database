Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn I.6 and III.6; the belief across time is traced on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Related readings: Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds, and does it entail a second fall? · Did the charge that Origen taught the devil’s salvation represent his teaching?

The passage

We think, indeed, that the goodness of God, through His Christ, may recall all His creatures to one end, even His enemies being conquered and subdued… for Christ must reign until He has put all enemies under His feet.

— Origen, De Principiis I.6.1 (c. 229), source.1

If anyone says or holds that the punishment of demons and impious human beings is temporary and that it will have an end at some time, and that there will be a restoration of demons and impious human beings, let him be anathema.

— Justinian, edict to Menas (543), anathema 9, source.2

The question

Origen’s Peri Archōn describes an end in which all rational creatures are brought back to God, reading 1 Corinthians 15:28 (“that God may be all in all”) with Acts 3:21 and Colossians 1:20; the first of the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemns “the fabulous pre-existence of souls” and “the monstrous restoration which follows from it.”3 Two questions are bundled: whether Origen taught the restoration as a firm doctrine or proposed it as an inquiry and a hope, and whether it was a permitted opinion in his day. Both bear on how the sixth-century sentences relate to what Origen wrote.

Origen taught the restoration as doctrine, and Augustine and the sixth-century edicts rejected it

On this reading Origen argued the restoration of all as the outcome of Scripture, and Augustine, before and apart from any council, and Justinian’s edicts rejected it on its merits.

Held by: Augustine (City of God XXI.17, c. 426); Justinian (edict to Menas, 543); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox); Michael McClymond (2015, 2018).4

Origen’s book states the outcome as a consummation: “all things shall be re-established in a state of unity, and… God shall be all in all,” a result “brought about, not suddenly, but slowly and gradually… during the lapse of countless and unmeasured ages.”5 Augustine reports the “tender-hearted” Christians who expected the damned to be released, and continues: “Origen was even more indulgent; for he believed that even the devil himself and his angels, after suffering those more severe and prolonged pains which their sins deserved, should be delivered from their torments, and associated with the holy angels. But the Church, not without reason, condemned him for this and other errors, especially for his theory of the ceaseless alternation of happiness and misery… for in this theory he lost even the credit of being merciful.”6 Justinian’s ninth anathema names the doctrine in the terms quoted above. Florovsky reads the sixth-century documents as a sketch of a single system ending “with a universal restoration and disincarnation of everything that exists. This is the scheme of Origen himself.”7 McClymond, against the view that the doctrine was inherited, writes that “there are no unambiguous cases of universalist teaching prior to Origen,” and that “Origen’s apokatastasis had no sequel or successor; the teaching began and ended with Origen.”8 On this reading the restoration was Origen’s own thesis, argued as the meaning of Scripture, and its rejection by Augustine and by the sixth-century edicts answered the doctrine he taught.

Origen offered the restoration as a hope, under the caution of inquiry

On this reading the restoration belongs to the class of questions Origen treated as open, and he proposed it as a scriptural hope rather than laying it down as dogma.

Held by: F. Prat (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Henri Crouzel (Catholic, 1988); John R. Sachs (Catholic, 1993); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 1998).9

Origen’s preface assigns to later inquiry the questions the restoration answers, and when he reaches them he says they are “treated by us with great solicitude and caution, in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than in that of fixed and certain decision.”10 The statement of the hope opens “We think, indeed,” and the subjection of all things to Christ is “this very subjection by which we also wish to be subject to Him,” a saving submission.1 Prat defends Origen by the distinction he finds in Athanasius and Jerome, “that it is one thing to dogmatize and another to enunciate hypothetical opinions which will be cleared up by discussion.” Crouzel describes the purpose of Peri Archōn as “to offer a theology in research, not a complete system.”11 Sachs: “While it cannot be said that Origen presents a coherent, systematic doctrine of apocatastasis… his work does represent the first major ‘theology of hope’ for the salvation of all.”12 Ware stresses Origen’s reserve on the Last Things, quoting him: “We need to speak about them with great fear and caution, discussing and investigating rather than laying down fixed and certain conclusions,” and concludes for his own part that “our faith in God’s love makes us dare to hope that all will be saved.”13 On this reading the restoration in Origen is a hope for the final reconciliation of all, argued from Scripture and offered under an announced reserve.

The restoration was a biblically grounded doctrine widely held among the Fathers

On this reading the restoration was a coherent doctrine with a continuous patristic line, which Origen set out heuristically, and which teachers of unquestioned standing held after him.

Held by: Ilaria Ramelli (The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; reply to McClymond, 2015); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, “Saint Origen,” 2015, on the sixth-century anathemas).14

Ramelli’s monograph sets out “how the apokatastasis doctrine is biblically, philosophically, and especially christologically grounded in its patristic supporters,” from the New Testament to Eriugena. She holds at the same time that “Origen in Peri Archōn is much more zetetic/heuristic than (as Jerome and Comestor would have it) ‘dogmatizing,’” a method Gregory of Nyssa and Gregory of Nazianzus “deemed… the only one admissible in matters left unclarified by Scripture and tradition.”15 Its scriptural core is older than Origen: Clement of Alexandria held that “God’s punishments are saving and disciplinary, leading to conversion,” and that the wicked are chastised “till they become ashamed and repent.”16 After Origen, Gregory of Nyssa read 1 Corinthians 15:28 as teaching “the complete annihilation of evil”: “when evil shall have been some day annihilated in the long revolutions of the ages, nothing shall be left outside the world of goodness, but that even from those evil spirits shall rise in harmony the confession of Christ’s Lordship.”17 Ramelli writes that Gregory “upheld this doctrine in an even stronger form than Origen did, including the salvation of the devil.”15 Hart holds that the fifteen anathemas “do not even really condemn universalism as such at all”: the first speaks of a “monstrous restoration” only in “the version of that idea that logically follows from a particular ‘fabulous’ account of the soul’s preexistence.”18 On this reading the general hope, that all rational creation is at last reconciled in a final and stable consummation, was a permitted reading held by teachers of the first rank, and the sixth-century sentence reached a particular system built on pre-existence.
