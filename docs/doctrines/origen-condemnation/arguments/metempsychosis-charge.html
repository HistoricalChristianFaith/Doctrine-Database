Did Origen teach reincarnation (the transmigration of souls)?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Commentary on Matthew XIII.1–2 and Peri Archōn I.8.4, as charged by Jerome (Letter 124) and in the list Pamphilus answered

Related readings: Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds, and does it entail a second fall? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? · Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching?

The passage

In this place it does not appear to me that by Elijah the soul is spoken of, lest I should fall into the dogma of transmigration, which is foreign to the church of God, and not handed down by the Apostles, nor anywhere set forth in the Scriptures.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew XIII.1 (on Matthew 17:10–13), source.1

It is an evidence of great negligence and sloth, that each one should fall down to such (a pitch of degradation), and be so emptied, as that, in coming to evil, he may be fastened to the gross body of irrational beasts of burden.

— Origen, Peri Archōn I.8.4, in the fragment translated by Jerome in his letter to Avitus, source.2

The question

From the early fourth century Origen was charged with teaching the transmigration of souls (metempsychōsis, metensōmatōsis): Pamphilus answered it as the last of the charges he listed, and Jerome and Justinian’s edict of 543 repeated it.3 Origen’s extant works reject transmigration by name, while the fragments of Peri Archōn that Jerome translated speak of souls bound to the bodies of beasts, and Rufinus’s Latin of the same chapter rejects that view. The question is whether Origen taught, or his system carried, a transmigration of souls, or a single entry of the pre-existent soul into a body that he himself distinguished from transmigration. It bears on what the charge-lists, and the popular claim that the council of 553 removed reincarnation from Christian teaching, attribute to him.

The soul’s entry into a body, which Origen distinguished from transmigration

On this reading Origen held that the pre-existent soul enters a body, and rejected its passage from body to body, into animals or through a chain of earthly lives.

Held by: Pamphilus (Apology for Origen, c. 307–310); E. V. Tkachev (Orthodox, Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, “Origen”); Henri Crouzel (Origen, 1985) ⚠ unverified; Jonathan H. Young (Studia Patristica, 2021).4

Origen rejects “the dogma of transmigration” at the Elijah text and argues that it is “in opposition to the saying… that this age shall have a consummation”; John came “in the spirit and power of Elijah” (Luke 1:17): “he did not say in the soul of Elijah, in which case the doctrine of transmigration might have some ground”, and “Elijah, therefore, was not called John because of the soul, but because of the spirit and the power.”5 In the Commentary on John he sets out the transcorporationist case at length and then gives the answer of “a churchman, who repudiates the doctrine of transcorporation as a false one, and does not admit that the soul of John ever was Elijah.”6 Against Celsus he calls “the transmigration of souls”, in which “the rational nature descends sometimes into all kinds of irrational animals”, a “folly”; places the soul’s entrance into the body “not according to the metempsychosis of Plato, but agreeably to another and higher view of things”; and guards “the doctrine of the entrance of souls into bodies (not, however, that of the transmigration from one body into another).”7 Rufinus’s Latin of Peri Archōn I.8.4 says of the descent of souls into “the condition of irrational animals”: “we not only do not receive, but, as being contrary to our belief, we refute and reject.”8 The anonymous five-book Defence of Origen Photius read listed as slanders “that rational natures enter into the bodies of irrational beings; that there are migrations from one body into another.”9 Tkachev writes that Origen distinguishes the soul’s embodiment (ensōmatōsis) from metempsychosis and “Метемпсихоз О. решительно отвергает” (“Origen decisively rejects metempsychosis”), the body being the principle of the soul’s individuation; a change of state to angel or demon can happen only to a rational being “и только по завершении текущего эона” (“and only at the completion of the present age”).10 Young finds that Origen “insists that the Christian teaching of the resurrection should contrast with metensomatosis” and “transforms the paradigm of metensomatosis, limiting future resurrection to rational souls in heaven.”11 Justinian’s first canon of 543 condemns souls that “pre-exist, as previously minds and holy powers” and “were made to descend into bodies as a punishment”, and the first of the fifteen of 553 “the fabulous pre-existence of souls”; Gudel, Bowman and Schlesinger hold that the council of 553 “had nothing whatever to do with reincarnation.”12 On this reading Origen taught one descent of a pre-existent soul into a body within each age, a doctrine he set apart from transmigration in his own words.

A transmigration of souls through successive bodies, set out in Peri Archōn

On this reading Origen’s account of souls falling into bodies matched to their negligence, and changing from one order of being to another, is a transmigration of souls.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 124, c. 409–410); Justinian’s edict against Origen (543); Michael Maher (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Geddes MacGregor (Reincarnation in Christianity, 1978) ⚠ unverified.13

Jerome, translating Peri Archōn from the Greek, has Origen write that “it argues great negligence and sloth for a soul so far to empty itself as to fall into sin and allow itself to be tied to the material body of an unreasoning brute”, and draws the conclusion “that we men may change into any other reasonable beings, and that not once only or on emergency but time after time.”14 Jerome reports that an angel, a human soul and a demon “may in punishment for great negligence or folly be transformed into brutes”, and that Origen then, “lest he should be held guilty of maintaining with Pythagoras the transmigration of souls”, added: “I must not be taken to make dogmas of these things; they are only thrown out as conjectures…”15 Of a passage on passing into another world he writes: “In speaking thus he clearly pleads for the transmigration of souls as taught by Pythagoras and Plato”, and of another, that it “is a convincing proof that he holds the transmigration of souls.”16 Jerome set out to “give the true sense of the Greek” against what he called Rufinus’s “misrendering”;17 which version of I.8.4 keeps the Greek is treated on its own page. Florovsky counts “the doctrine of the preexistence and transmigration of souls” among the doctrines the edict of 543 condemned, opinions that “do actually flow from” Origen’s premises.18 Maher writes that metempsychosis “was held, however, in a Platonic form by the Gnostics, and was so taught by Origen in his great work, Peri archon”: the soul restored to God declines again “through satiety of the good”, “and so on through endless cycles of apostasy, banishment, and return.”19 MacGregor, as Schlesinger reports him, holds that “while Origen rejected certain forms of reincarnation, his system depends in part on reincarnation”, citing Peri Archōn I.4.1 and I.8.4.20 A popular literature extends the reading to the council of 553: Leslie Weatherhead held that reincarnation “was accepted by the early church for the first five hundred years of its existence. Only in AD 553 did the Second Council of Constantinople reject it and only then by a narrow majority.”21 On this reading a soul that passes, age after age, into whatever body its negligence deserves is undergoing transmigration, whatever name Origen gave the process.
