Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh, and did he teach that risen bodies are spherical?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Commentary on Psalm 1 (fragment) and De Principiis II.10 and III.6, as charged by Methodius of Olympus, Jerome and the anathemas of 543 and 553

Related readings: Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? · Did Origen teach that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled rational beings? · Did Origen teach that the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) are the mortal human body? · Can Origen be fairly judged at all, given how his text reaches us? · Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching?; and the timeline of the resurrection of the body, where both readings of Paul’s “sown a natural body… raised a spiritual body” (1 Corinthians 15:44) are traced.

The passage

Origen therefore thinks that the same flesh will not be restored to the soul, but that the form of each, according to the appearance by which the flesh is now distinguished, shall arise stamped upon another spiritual body; so that every one will again appear the same form; and that this is the resurrection which is promised.

— Methodius of Olympus, On the Resurrection III, reporting Origen’s lost Commentary on Psalm 1, source.1

our flesh is supposed by ignorant men and unbelievers to be destroyed after death, in such a degree that it retains no relic at all of its former substance. We, however, who believe in its resurrection, understand that a change only has been produced by death, but that its substance certainly remains

— Origen, De Principiis III.6.5, in Rufinus’s Latin, source.2

If anyone shall say that after the resurrection the body of the Lord was ethereal, having the form of a sphere, and that such shall be the bodies of all after the resurrection… let him be anathema.

— the tenth of the fifteen anathemas against Origen (553), source.3

The question

The charge that Origen denied the resurrection stands in the list of accusations Pamphilus answered around 307, and fifth in Jerome’s indictment of 397: Origen “most openly denies the resurrection of the flesh and the bodily structure, and the distinction of senses”; Justinian’s edict of 543 and the anathemas of 553 added the spherical risen body.4 The question is whether Origen’s account of the risen body, in which a persisting bodily form (eidos) carries identity into a spiritual body, is a denial of the resurrection of the flesh or a doctrine of the resurrection of the same body, and whether the spherical body is his. It bears on what the charges of 400 and the anathemas of 543 and 553 reached in his writings.

A denial of the resurrection of the flesh

On this reading Origen kept the language of a resurrection of the body but denied that this flesh, with its matter, members and sex, rises, and the spherical body belongs to the same teaching.

Held by: Methodius of Olympus (On the Resurrection, c. 300); Jerome (Against John of Jerusalem, c. 397; Letter 84, 400); Justinian (Letter to Menas, 543); Norman Geisler (evangelical Protestant, 1991).5

Methodius answers Origen’s fragment on Psalm 1 that the form cannot outlive its matter: “the shape which disappears around the brass is separated from the melted statue, and has not longer a substantial existence.”1 Jerome, setting out “Origen’s views respecting the resurrection”, observes that “though he nine times speaks of the resurrection of the body, he has not once introduced the resurrection of the flesh”, and quotes him: the flesh the seeds reconstitute “will not be the same flesh, nor will it be in the old forms”; “a different body, spiritual and ethereal, is promised to us, which is neither tangible, nor perceptible to the eye, nor ponderable”; for if the flesh were the same, “there will again be males and females, there will again be marriage.”6 In Letter 84 he writes that the Origenists “use the word body instead of the word flesh”.7 Eustathius of Antioch held that Methodius had shown that Origen “inconsiderately gave the heretics an opening by defining the resurrection in reference to form (eidous), but not in reference to body.”8 On this reading the sphere follows from the same premises, a risen body modelled on Paul’s “celestial” bodies (“A celestial body is that of the sun, moon, stars”) and on ensouled luminaries; Jerome answers that the transfigured Lord did not “roll along in a round shape like that of the sun or a ball”, and the edict of 543 condemned the teaching that “the bodies of human beings will be raised spherical.”9 Geisler writes that “he does not really believe in a physical resurrection body but in a transformation of the physical body into a non-physical body”, one without limbs that “would possibly be spherical in shape.”10 The article measured is the one Cyril of Jerusalem taught around 350, this body “raised the very same body, though by putting on incorruption it shall be fashioned anew”.11 On this reading a resurrection that keeps the form and gives up the flesh is the denial Methodius named.

A resurrection of the same body, its identity carried by the bodily form

On this reading Origen taught a real resurrection of the body as part of the rule of faith, the same body transformed, with personal identity carried by its bodily form; the spherical body is not found in his writings.

Held by: Pamphilus of Caesarea (Apology for Origen, c. 307–310); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, Creation and Redemption, 1976); Henri Crouzel (Catholic, Jesuit, 1988); E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, vol. 53).12

Origen writes that “the same body… shall be transmuted into a condition of glory”; that the apostle teaches the risen “receive those identical ones which they had possessed when living, transformed from an inferior into a better condition”; and that “It is of the body, then, that there will be a resurrection”, a germ “always safe in the substance of the body” raising it as the grain raises the ear.13 Against Celsus: “a certain power is implanted in the body, which is not destroyed, and from which the body is raised up in incorruption.”14 Jerome reports him: “Paul’s body will be that of Paul, Peter’s that of Peter, and each will have his own.”6 Tkachev sets out that Origen counted belief in the resurrection of the body in incorruption part of the rule of faith, held a measure of identity between the earthly and the risen body, and located individuality in an immaterial bodily form.15 Florovsky: “The same individuals will rise, and the individual identity of the bodies will be preserved”; Methodius “does not seem to have understood him well”, for to him “the ‘form’ meant rather merely the external shape of the body, and not the internal vital power, as for Origen.”16 Crouzel wrote that Methodius gave “the wrong meaning” to the bodily eidos, “which explains, according to Origen, the identity between the present and the resurrected body”, and that “This mistake takes away nearly all strength from Methodius’ criticism of Origen’s position.”17 On the sphere, Crouzel holds that after Guillaumont “it is not possible to confound late Origenism with the thought of Origen himself”, and Tadros Malaty writes that the spherical risen body of the tenth anathema has “no parallel in the known texts of Origen.”18 An anonymous five-book Defence of Origen listed “that there is neither eternal punishment nor resurrection of the flesh” among counts it called “mere slanders”, answered from his writings.19 On this reading Origen answered Celsus’s ridicule of a reassembled corpse with a resurrection of the same body, transformed, whose continuity lies in its form.
