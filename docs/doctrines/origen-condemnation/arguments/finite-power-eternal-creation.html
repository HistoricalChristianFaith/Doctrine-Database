Did Origen teach that God’s power is finite and that creation is coeternal with God?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn I.2.10 and II.9.1, as charged by Theophilus of Alexandria (402) and Justinian’s edict to Menas (543)

Related readings: Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds, and does it entail a second fall?

The passage

If anyone says or holds that God’s power is finite and that he created [only] what he could grasp and comprehend, or that creation is coeternal with God, let him be anathema.

— Justinian, edict to Menas (543), anathema 8, source.1

…it is not to be imagined, as some would have it, that creatures have not a limit, because where there is no limit there can neither be any comprehension nor any limitation. Now if this were the case, then certainly created things could neither be restrained nor administered by God. For, naturally, whatever is infinite will also be incomprehensible.

— Origen, De Principiis II.9.1, in Rufinus’s Latin, source.2

The question

Two propositions are bundled in the eighth anathema: that God’s power is finite, because he made only as many beings as he could comprehend, and that creation is coeternal with God, because God was never without creatures over whom to exercise his power. The passages behind both are Origen’s; the question is whether the conclusions the anathema states are his teaching, which bears on what the edict of 543 condemned.

The charges report his teaching

On this reading Origen bounded creation by what God could grasp and held that God must always have had creatures, so that both charges state what he taught.

Held by: Methodius of Olympus (On Things Created, c. 300); Theophilus of Alexandria (paschal letter, 402); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, “The Idea of Creation in Christian Philosophy”).3

Origen’s argument in Peri Archōn I.2.10 makes God’s lordship require subjects: “God cannot be called omnipotent unless there exist those over whom He may exercise His power,” and “if there never was a time when He was not omnipotent, of necessity those things by which He receives that title must also exist; and He must always have had those over whom He exercised power.”4 Methodius, writing against him around 300, reports the argument and its result: “Origen says that what he calls the Centaur is the universe which is co-eternal with the only wise and independent God. For he says, since there is no workman without some work, or maker without something made, so neither is there an Almighty without an object of His power.”5 On the first strand, the Greek of II.9.1 that Justinian’s letter preserves reads, in the ANF rendering: “for we must say that the power of God is finite, and not, under pretence of praising Him, take away His limitation. For if the divine power be infinite, it must of necessity be unable to understand even itself.”6 Theophilus, in 402, quotes the same thought, “God made as many things as he could grasp and hold in subjection to himself and control by his providence,” and concludes that Origen “asserts that God’s power is limited.”7 Prat writes that Origen “believes that God created from eternity… Consequently he is forced to admit a double infinite series of worlds before and after the present world.” Florovsky: “He had to come to the conclusion that some co-eternal non-ego was necessary for God, as a condition of the Divine fullness and perfection.”8 Augustine set out a different account of the same problem, the world made with time and God’s will to create eternal and unchanging.9 On this reading the anathema compresses into two clauses what Origen argued from God’s immutability and from the comprehensibility of what God governs.

The limit belongs to creation, and only the ideas are eternal

On this reading Origen’s finitude thesis is about the created order, which must be finite to be governed, and what he held eternal is the pre-existence of creatures as ideas in God’s Wisdom, not a created world coeternal with God.

Held by: Mark J. Edwards (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Origen,” 2014); Ilaria Ramelli (2014; 2022); Damian Mrugalski (Vox Patrum, 2017).10

In II.9.1 the subject of the limit is the creature: rational beings were made “according to some definite number, predetermined by Himself,” because “whatever is infinite will also be incomprehensible,” and Origen cites Wisdom 11:20, “God has arranged all things in number and measure.”2 Edwards: “Origen was later accused of slighting the omnipotence of the Creator. His meaning, however, is not that there are limits to the power of God, but that any particular exercise of it must logically have some limit.” Mrugalski holds that “in the surviving works of Origen” he “nowhere states explicitly that God’s power is finite,” and that “the controversial thesis of a finite God is found only in fragments written by ancient critics.”11 Theophilus, pressing the charge, writes that God “himself is not limited by measure and number.”7 On the second strand, the eternity in Origen’s argument is that of God’s attributes, never idle and never increased, “not then for the first time did God begin to work when He made this visible world.”12 Ramelli identifies what is eternal: “coeternal with God are only the Forms / Ideas / Logoi of all rational creatures in the Mind of God, who is Christ-Logos-Wisdom.” Edwards reads Origen as positing “a noetic realm, created but eternal… preceding ours in the ontological hierarchy rather than in the temporal continuum,” and on the succession of worlds writes that an infinite series would be needed, “and even Origen’s enemies do not say that he went so far.”13 On this reading Origen’s God is eternally almighty over creatures eternally present to his Wisdom, and each created world is finite and has a beginning.
