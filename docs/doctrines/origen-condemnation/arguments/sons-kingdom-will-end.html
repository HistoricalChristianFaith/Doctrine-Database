Did Origen teach that the Son’s kingdom will come to an end?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn I.6–7 and III.5–6, on 1 Corinthians 15:24–28; the charge as pressed by Epiphanius of Salamis and Theophilus of Alexandria

Related readings: Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds in which the restored may fall again? · Does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen?

The passage

If anyone shall say that the heavenly Powers and all men and the Devil and evil spirits are united with the Word of God in all respects, as the Nous which is by them called Christ…; and [if anyone shall say] that the Kingdom of Christ shall have an end: let him be anathema.

— the twelfth of the fifteen anathemas against Origen (553), NPNF2 XIV.1

The question

The charge is older than the anathema. Epiphanius’s Panarion of the 370s sums up the Origenist error in a sentence ending “and foolishly say that Christ’s kingdom will come to an end”;2 the synodical letter of Theophilus’s council of 400 lists as its second article “Christ’s kingdom will one day come to an end”;3 and the Greek charge-list that an anonymous Defence of Origen answered, as Photius reports it, included “that the Only Begotten has no share in the Kingdom.”4 All of them turn on Origen’s reading of 1 Corinthians 15:24–28, “then comes the end, when he delivers up the kingdom to God the Father… that God may be all in all.” The question is whether Origen’s reading of that text makes the kingdom of Christ a stage that ends, or reads the Son’s “subjection” as the completion of his work in his members. The sources opened here name no modern scholar for the first reading.

The kingdom of Christ handed over and ended

On this reading Origen makes Christ’s reign a dispensation that gives way, at the end, to the immediate rule of the Father, so that the kingdom of Christ comes to an end.

Held by: Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 64, c. 375); Theophilus of Alexandria (synodical letter, 400); the fifteen anathemas of 553.5

Origen takes the “delivering up of the kingdom” as a real term of the economy. In Peri Archōn the rational creation, made subject to Christ, is passed on with him to the Father: “When Christ shall have delivered up the kingdom to God even the Father, then also those living things, when they shall have first been made the kingdom of Christ, shall be delivered, along with the whole of that kingdom, to the rule of the Father, that when God shall be all in all, they also… may have God in themselves, as He is in all things.”6 The Son reigns over subjects until they are perfected, and then hands that kingdom to the Father, so that God is “all in all.” The consummation answers to the beginning: “the end is always like the beginning: and, therefore, as there is one end to all things, so ought we to understand that there was one beginning.”7 If the beginning was a unity of minds contemplating God, the economy between, the fall, the incarnation and the mediating reign of Christ, lapses when that unity is restored. The twelfth anathema sets “the Kingdom of Christ shall have an end” beside the doctrine that all rational beings are united with the Word “as the Nous which is by them called Christ.”1 In the fourth century Marcellus of Ancyra read the same verse to mean that at the consummation the Word’s kingdom would cease and the Word return into God, and the creed associated with the Council of Constantinople in 381 added “and of His kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:33).8 On this reading the Son’s reign is the mediating stage of an economy that returns to its first unity, and Epiphanius, Theophilus and the sixth-century anathema name its term.

The Son’s “subjection” as the salvation of his members, the kingdom perfected

On this reading Origen understood the Son’s being “subjected” to the Father as the completion of his saving work in those he gathers, so that the handing over of the kingdom is its fulfilment, not its end.

Held by: the anonymous author of the Defence of Origen read by Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 117); E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53); Ilaria Ramelli (Vigiliae Christianae, 2011).9

Origen addresses the objection directly: “I know not, indeed, how the heretics, not understanding the meaning of the apostle in these words, consider the term ‘subjection’ degrading as applied to the Son.”10 Subjection is a word for salvation: “the name ‘subjection,’ by which we are subject to Christ, indicates that the salvation which proceeds from Him belongs to His subjects,”11 and the Son’s being subjected to the Father “indicates that our happiness has attained to perfection, and that the work undertaken by Him has been brought to a victorious termination,” in which “the perfect restoration of the whole of creation is signified.”10 The Head is subjected in his body: Christ, “embracing in Himself all whom He subjects to the Father… He Himself, along with them, and in them, is said also to be subject to the Father.”10 The Homilies on Leviticus say the same: “As long as I am not subjected to the Father, neither is he said to be ‘subjected’ to the Father. Not that he himself is in need of subjection before the Father, but for me, in whom he has not yet completed his work, he is said not to be subjected.”12 The anonymous Defence listed “that the Only Begotten has no share in the Kingdom” among fifteen counts it called slanders, answering them “by quotations from his writings.”4 Tkachev reads Origen’s exegesis of the same chapter: “Подчинение врагов Божиих, о к-ром говорит ап. Павел (1 Кор 15. 25), согласно О., есть не что иное, как их спасение” (the subjection of God’s enemies of which the Apostle speaks is, according to Origen, nothing other than their salvation).13 Ramelli argues that Gregory of Nyssa’s treatise on the Son’s subjection, In illud: Tunc et ipse Filius, takes its arguments from Origen, and places that exegesis in Origen’s anti-subordinationist heritage.14 On this reading the kingdom delivered to the Father is Christ’s gathered body, presented in him, so that God is “all in all.”
