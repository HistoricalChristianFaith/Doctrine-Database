Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witnesses: Theophilus of Alexandria (400), as reported by Socrates Scholasticus; Justinian (543) and the council of 553

Related readings: Was Demetrius’s sentence (c. 231–232) disciplinary only, or did it also concern Origen’s teaching? · Did Anastasius I’s sentence of 400–401 condemn Origen’s books only, or Origen himself? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · What does the condemnation of Origen and Theodore of Mopsuestia by the same council of 553 indicate about its aims?

The passage

[Theophilus] condemned Origen, who had been dead nearly two hundred years: not having this as his first object, but rather his purpose of revenge on Dioscorus and his brethren.

— Socrates Scholasticus, Church History VI.10, on Theophilus of Alexandria’s synods of 400, source.1

The question

Each institutional sentence on Origen, at Alexandria around 231, at Theophilus’s synods of 400, in Justinian’s edict of 543 and at the council of 553, came in the course of a dispute over jurisdiction, monks or court influence. The question is whether those disputes were the cause of the condemnations, with doctrine the occasion, or the occasion of a doctrinal judgement held on its own grounds.

Politics drove the sentences

On this reading Origen’s name was taken up at each stage as a weapon in quarrels about jurisdiction, patronage and power, and the timing of the condemnations followed those quarrels.

Held by: Jerome (Lives of Illustrious Men 54, 392); Socrates Scholasticus (Church History VI, 440s); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Elizabeth A. Clark (The Origenist Controversy, 1992).2

The Alexandrian synods of c. 231–232 deposed Origen for his ordination in Palestine without his own bishop’s consent. Jerome, in 392, put the sentence down to Demetrius’s temper: Origen “offended Demetrius, who was so wildly enraged at him that he wrote everywhere to injure his reputation.” Prat, from Eusebius, has Demetrius “envious of the increasing influence of his catechist,” and notes that Jerome “declares expressly that he was not condemned on a point of doctrine.”3 In 399 Theophilus, facing monks who threatened his life, told them “In seeing you, I behold the face of God… I myself also disapprove of Origen’s works”; Socrates writes that at the synods of 400 his “first object” was revenge on Dioscorus, and that he later read Origen again, saying the books “are like a meadow enameled with flowers of every kind. If, therefore, I chance to find a beautiful one among them, I cull it: but whatever appears to me to be thorny, I step over.”4 Prat describes Theophilus seeking to “under this pretext ruin Chrysostom, whom he hated and envied.” The same years saw the quarrel of Jerome and Rufinus in the West.5 In the sixth century the edict of 543 followed a conflict between the monks of the Great Laura and the Origenist party of the New Laura, each lobbying at court. Evagrius Scholasticus describes Theodore Askidas as “constantly about the person of Justinian, as being trusty and highly serviceable to him,” and writes that he, “with a view to divert them from this point, introduces the subject of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret, and Ibas.”6 Prat holds it “certain that the fifth general council was convoked exclusively to deal with the affair of the Three Chapters.” Clark’s study, in its publisher’s description, argues that “Origen” became “a code word for nontheological complaints as well.”7 On this reading the doctrines were real, but what brought them before synods, edicts and councils, and when, was the politics of each moment.

A doctrinal judgement on its own grounds

On this reading the occasions were political, but the doctrines condemned were opposed on their merits, by the same core charges across centuries and by critics with no stake in the quarrels.

Held by: Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century); Richard Price (The Acts of the Council of Constantinople of 553, 2009, on Justinian’s motives); Marius Telea (Orthodox, “Origenism in the vision of Emperor Justinian I,” 2022).8

The core charges, the pre-existence and fall of souls and the final restoration of all rational creatures, recur from about 300 to 553 among writers who otherwise had little in common: Methodius, Pamphilus’s accusers, Epiphanius, Theophilus, Augustine, Gennadius, Justinian and the anathemas of 553.9 Methodius of Olympus wrote against the pre-existence and Origen’s account of the risen body in a philosophical dialogue, decades before any synod took up the question, and Augustine argued against the pre-cosmic fall from Genesis in the City of God.10 Price grants that “the condemnation of the Origenists in both 543 and 553 was brought about by their Palestinian opponents,” and holds that “the notion that he simply let himself be manipulated by his advisers is contrary to our whole picture of his character”: Justinian acted from “a sense of duty before God… this should not be reduced to mere ‘politics’.” He calls Liberatus’s account of the diversion “manifestly tendentious,” and writes that “modern historians, while they do not discount Ascidas’ role entirely, do not see in it the real explanation.”11 Telea: “this demonstrates the significance that the emperor-theologian attaches to the doctrinal issues involved in Origenist disputes.” Florovsky: “one could dispute the need to stir up the question of Origen at an Ecumenical Council. But Origen’s fallaciousness did not raise any doubts… The prohibitions of the Fifth Ecumenical Council signified a judgment over the mistakes of the past.”12 On this reading the quarrels governed when and how Origen was condemned, and the condemnations themselves expressed a theological judgement reached and repeated on its own grounds.
