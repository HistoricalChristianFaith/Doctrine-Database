Did Origen teach that the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) are the mortal human body?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Against Celsus IV.40, as charged by Methodius of Olympus and Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: Did Origen’s allegorical exegesis set aside the literal and historical sense of Scripture? · Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh, and did he teach that risen bodies are spherical?

The passage

In the fourth place, he interprets the coats of skins, with which Adam and Eve were clothed after their fall and ejection from Paradise, to be human bodies, and we are to suppose of course that previously, in Paradise, they had neither flesh, sinews, nor bones.

— Jerome, Against John of Jerusalem 7, the fourth of the eight Origenist errors Epiphanius had drawn up, source.1

…the expulsion of the man and woman from paradise, and their being clothed with tunics of skins (which God, because of the transgression of men, made for those who had sinned), contain a certain secret and mystical doctrine (far transcending that of Plato) of the soul losing its wings, and being borne downwards to earth, until it can lay hold of some stable resting-place.

— Origen, Against Celsus IV.40, source.2

The question

The charge concerns the origin of the body rather than of the soul: that the flesh is a garment God made for the fallen, so that in paradise the first couple had no flesh. Origen’s surviving texts touch Genesis 3:21 in a few places only, and the plainest statements of the identification come from his opponents; the question is whether he identified the skins with the body, or read them as mortality or a change in the body’s condition.

The skins are the earthly body

On this reading Origen read the garments of skins as the body into which the soul descended at the fall, a doctrine he held but kept from the multitude.

Held by: Methodius of Olympus (On the Resurrection, c. 300, of his opponents); Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 64, c. 376); Jerome (Against John of Jerusalem); Louis Ginzberg (The Legends of the Jews, 1925).3

In Against Celsus IV.40 Origen reads Adam as “man in general” and makes the tunics of skins the point at which the story figures “the soul losing its wings, and being borne downwards to earth,” the Phaedrus image of the soul’s fall into a body.2 In V.29 he declines to state the subject openly: “It is good to keep close the secret of a king — in order that the doctrine of the entrance of souls into bodies (not, however, that of the transmigration from one body into another) may not be thrown before the common understanding.”4 On this reading the reserve explains why no surviving sentence of his states the identification flatly. Methodius, within a generation of Origen’s death, answers readers who held it: “the question has already been raised, and answered, that the ‘coats of skins’ are not bodies,” against those who “defined the flesh to be the soul’s chain,” and he argues from Adam’s “bone of my bone and flesh of my flesh,” spoken before the skins were made.5 Epiphanius writes that in Origen the skin tunics “refer to the body,” and Jerome’s Latin carried the article into the charge-list.6 Ginzberg, in a note on Against Celsus IV.40: “They received garments of skin at the time of the fall; i. e., bodies, since before the fall they were spiritual beings.”7 According to Mark Edwards, Peter Martens notes that in a Genesis fragment Origen “deems it pithanon (‘persuasive’) that the skins should represent bodies.”8 On this reading the IV.40 allegory, the declared reserve of V.29 and the reading Methodius had to answer form one pattern: a doctrine of embodiment at the fall, taught guardedly.

The skins signify mortality, not the body as such

On this reading Origen read the garments as mortality, or a change in the quality of a body Adam already had, and never taught that the first couple were fleshless.

Held by: Manlio Simonetti (Aevum, 1962); Henri Crouzel (Gregorianum, 1972); Mark J. Edwards (“Origen, Plato and the Afterlife”).9

In the Homilies on Leviticus Origen names what the tunics signify: “those were tunics of skins taken from animals […], which are a symbol of the mortality which [man] received.”10 In the Genesis fragment he reports two readings, the skins as bodies and the skins as mortality, and of the first adds, as Edwards gives it, “it is not clear if it is true.”8 Against Celsus IV.40 speaks of a mystical doctrine of the soul’s descent to earth, not of the skins as flesh, and the flat equation with its corollary of a paradise couple with “neither flesh, sinews, nor bones” is stated by Methodius, Epiphanius and Jerome rather than in a sentence of Origen’s own. Alexandra Pârvan finds that in the few places Origen refers to the verse “there is no clear picture of how he interprets this verse.”11 Edwards: “Most attempts to harmonise the evidence postulate a change in the quality of Adam’s body, rather than a shift from a perfectly incorporeal to a corporeal state”; these theories “attribute a body of some texture to Adam before the fall,” and his own view is that the skins “stand for a combination of mortality and corruption.”12 Gregory of Nyssa, in the next century, read the coats of skins as the mortality and animal nature humanity took on at the fall.13 On this reading the garments mark the passage of an embodied Adam into mortality, and the charge-lists give a sharper form to a reading Origen offered tentatively.
