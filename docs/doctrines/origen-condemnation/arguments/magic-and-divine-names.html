Did Origen teach that “magic is not an evil”, and that certain names have a natural power?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Against Celsus I.24–26 and V.45, as charged by the synod of Alexandria (400) and in the list Photius transmits

Related readings: Did Origen teach that the stars cause events? · Did Origen teach that the sun, moon and stars are living, ensouled rational beings?

The passage

Magic, if real, is not evil.

— the synodical letter of the Council of Alexandria (400), among Origen’s chief errors, in Jerome’s Latin, Letter 92.4, source.1

the so-called magic is not, as the followers of Epicurus and Aristotle suppose, an altogether uncertain thing, but is, as those skilled in it prove, a consistent system, having words which are known to exceedingly few

— Origen, Against Celsus I.24, source.2

The question

Answering Celsus’s claim that it makes no difference whether God is called Zeus, Ammon or Sabaoth, Origen argued that names are given by nature, not by convention, and that the Hebrew divine names carry a power lost in translation; the charge-lists of 400 and the list Photius transmits record that he held “magic is not an evil”. The question is whether his doctrine of names belongs with the magical theory of his age, as the charge holds, or is a distinct account set against the sorcery he condemned.3

A theory of names that belongs with ancient magic

On this reading Origen’s doctrine of the natural power of names shares the premises of ancient magic, which is what the charge-lists recorded.

Held by: the synod of Alexandria under Theophilus (400); John Dillon (“The Magical Power of Names in Origen and Later Platonism,” 1985), as reported by Janowitz; Miroslav Šedina (Listy filologické, 2013).4

Origen sides with the Stoics and Epicurus against Aristotle that names were given “by nature”, and writes: “The name Sabaoth, and Adonai, and the other names treated with so much reverence among the Hebrews, are not applicable to any ordinary created things, but belong to a secret theology which refers to the Framer of all things. These names, accordingly, when pronounced with that attendant train of circumstances which is appropriate to their nature, are possessed of great power.”2 When names “which in a certain language were possessed of a natural power were translated into another, they were no longer able to accomplish what they did before”; “the God of Abraham” vanquishes demons, while the translated form is “attended with no result.”5 In the same chapter he defends “the so-called magic” as “a consistent system”, and different names are “efficacious against certain demons who can only do certain things”; answering Celsus on the Egyptian conjurers, he remarks: “You see that by these expressions he allows, as it were, the existence of magic.”6 The synod of 400 set its charge between “The stars are conscious of their own movements, and the demons know the future by their courses” and “Christ… will suffer again for the demons.”1 Dillon, as Janowitz quotes him, writes: “One of the basic presuppositions of magical practice, both in GrecoRoman antiquity and elsewhere, is that there is power in names, or to be more exact, in the magician’s knowledge of names,” and reads Origen alongside the Greek Magical Papyri, the Nag Hammadi texts and the Corpus Hermeticum.7 Šedina finds that Origen and the magicians “use an identical method to address divine or daimonic powers and the only difference consists in the extent of their effect”, and that in Origen’s account “it is rather the principles of contact magic that come into play.”8 On this reading the synod named, in moral terms, a doctrine whose working premises Origen shared with the practitioners of magic.

A theory of names set against sorcery

On this reading Origen held that the art of magic is real but forbidden and demonic, and that the power of the divine names and of the name of Jesus is of another kind.

Held by: an anonymous five-book Defence of Origen, as summarized by Photius; Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church II); Naomi Janowitz (History of Religions, 1991); Riemer Roukema (Annali di Storia dell’Esegesi, 2007).9

Answering the claim that the Jews “are addicted to sorcery, in which Moses was their instructor,” Origen cites the law: “Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them”, and lists the divinatory arts of the nations as “all which practices were forbidden to the Jews.”10 The conjurers’ feats are “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit”;6 the Magi were “on familiar terms with evil spirits”, and at Christ’s birth “the evil spirits… became feeble, and lost their strength, the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken.”11 The name of Jesus, which “has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits”, works against the demons the sorcerer invokes.2 The anonymous Defence Photius read rejected the charge “that magic is not an evil” among those it called slanders.3 Schaff writes that Origen “fixed certain criteria, such as the moral purity of the worker, and his intention to glorify God and benefit man, for distinguishing the true miracles from Satanic juggleries.” Janowitz: “Using Jesus’ names to heal is not magic but a practice attested to by Jesus himself”, and “Origen differentiates this very carefully from magic.” Roukema: “he makes a sharp distinction between the invocation of pagan powers and faith in Jesus and the supreme God”; for Origen “the man who concerns himself with the names of demons and with spells will be abandoned by God to those demons.”12 On this reading the reality of an art and its licitness were two questions for Origen, and his doctrine of names explains why the name of the true God masters the powers magic courts.13
