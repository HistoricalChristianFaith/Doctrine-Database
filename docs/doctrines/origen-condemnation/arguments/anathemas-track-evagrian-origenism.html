Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: the Second Council of Constantinople (553); see also Evagrius Ponticus and Justinian

Related readings: Was Origen himself anathematized by an ecumenical council? (whether the council passed the fifteen, and the eleventh canon) · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Can Origen’s teaching be recovered, given how the text of Peri Archōn has come down? · Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen teach universal restoration as doctrine? · Did Origen teach that the sun, moon and stars are living, ensouled rational beings?

The passage

X. If anyone shall say that after the resurrection the body of the Lord was ethereal, having the form of a sphere, and that such shall be the bodies of all after the resurrection…: let him be anathema.

XII. If anyone shall say that the heavenly Powers and all men and the Devil and evil spirits are united with the Word of God in all respects, as the Nous which is by them called Christ and which is in the form of God, and which humbled itself as they say…: let him be anathema.

XIV. If anyone shall say that all reasonable beings will one day be united in one, when the hypostases as well as the numbers and the bodies shall have disappeared… moreover, that in this pretended apocatastasis, spirits only will continue to exist…: let him be anathema.

— three of the fifteen anathemas against Origen (553), source.1

The question

The fifteen anathemas, dated by Diekamp, Price and Ramelli to the weeks before the council of 553 opened and by others to a sitting of the council itself (see the council page), name no author, and the first condemns “the fabulous pre-existence of souls” and “the monstrous restoration which follows from it.”2 In 1958 Antoine Guillaumont published an unexpurgated Syriac recension (S2) of Evagrius of Pontus’s Kephalaia Gnostica, which Guillaumont judged the unrevised text, the long-known version (S1) being in his judgement a revised copy.3 The question is whether the system the fifteen describe is Origen’s own teaching or a later Origenism of the Palestinian monasteries associated with Evagrius; it bears on what the sixth-century condemnation reached. Whether the council itself passed the fifteen is a separate question, treated on its own page.

The anathemas condemn Origen’s own system

On this reading the fifteen set out, in sixth-century terms, the scheme of Origen’s own teaching on the beginning and the end of rational creatures.

Held by: Cyril of Scythopolis (Life of Sabas, c. 557); C. J. Hefele (Catholic, History of the Councils); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”).4

Cyril of Scythopolis, writing from the Palestinian lauras shortly after the council, records it as a definitive anathema on Origen together with Theodore of Mopsuestia, Evagrius and Didymus, concerning the pre-existence of souls and the restoration.5 Hefele calls them “the fifteen celebrated anathematisms on the same number of propositions of Origen.”4 The list is framed by two doctrines Origen argues in Peri Archōn: the pre-existent rational natures and their descent into bodies, and the restoration in which God is “all in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28).6 Florovsky reads the sixth-century documents as a sketch of that scheme: everything “was created from eternity in perfect spirituality,” the corporeal world “arose through the Fall,” and the process ends “with a universal restoration and disincarnation of everything that exists. This is the scheme of Origen himself.” Condemned with Didymus and Evagrius were “certain ‘impious opinions’ expressed by Origen himself and his followers.” Evagrius, in Florovsky’s account, wrote at length on Origen’s pre-existence and restoration, and “In this Evagrius was even less cautious than Origen”: the Evagrian system is a development of Origen’s, not a graft from outside it.7 Ware reads the first anathema the same way, as “directed not simply against Origen’s teaching concerning universal reconciliation, but against his total understanding of salvation history.”8 The sixth-century campaign also had Origen’s book in hand: Justinian’s edict to Menas of 543 quotes Peri Archōn and appends nine anathemas, of which the first and ninth reach the pre-existent fall and the end of punishment.9 On this reading the fifteen of 553 carry Origen’s premises further into the monastic system of their day, and the condemnation of that system is a condemnation of the scheme whose author was Origen.

The anathemas describe a sixth-century Evagrian Origenism

On this reading the fifteen describe the doctrine of a party of Palestinian Origenist monks, drawn from Evagrius’s Kephalaia Gnostica or a later radicalization of it, and do not quote Origen.

Held by: Franz Diekamp (Catholic, Die origenistischen Streitigkeiten, 1899); Antoine Guillaumont (1962); Richard Price (The Acts of the Council of Constantinople of 553, 2009); John Behr (Orthodox, 2018).10

Diekamp argued that the anathemas, like the letter that accompanied them, combat “nicht die Lehre des Origenes, sondern die der origenistischen Mönche in Palästina” (not the teaching of Origen but that of the Origenist monks in Palestine), and observed: “Origenes selbst wird nicht genannt.”11 Guillaumont matched the fifteen, proposition by proposition, to the unexpurgated Syriac of the Kephalaia Gnostica: the spherical resurrection body, the nous “by them called Christ,” and the final equality of all spirits with Christ stand there and not in Peri Archōn.3 Cyril of Scythopolis names the two parties into which the Palestinian Origenists split, the Protoktistoi and the Isochristoi, “those equal to Christ.”12 Price, following Diekamp and Guillaumont, writes that the Protoctists “preserved the purer Origenist tradition, while the latter had come to adopt the speculations of Evagrius of Pontus,” and that “A comparison of the anathemas of 543 to those of 553 shows that the latter are directed specifically against the Evagrian Origenism of the Isochrists.”13 The impression that the anathemas quote Origen owed something to print: Paul Koetschau’s 1913 edition of Peri Archōn filled gaps in Rufinus’s Latin from Justinian’s edict and the anathemas of 553, and John Behr’s Oxford edition of 2017 moved that material to an appendix.14 Behr holds that the pre-existent intellects descending through a pre-cosmic fall, “ideas that were (rightly!) anathematized in the sixth century,” “do not actually occur within the work.”15 Ilaria Ramelli carries the point further, holding that the condemned ideas “were largely neither those of Origen nor those of Evagrius but those of later Origenists who radicalized and distorted Evagrius’s thinking.”16 On this reading the council legislated against a living sixth-century movement and its system, under the name of the third-century teacher the movement claimed.
