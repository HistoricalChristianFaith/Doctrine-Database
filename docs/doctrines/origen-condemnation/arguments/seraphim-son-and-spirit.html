Did Origen’s reading of the two seraphim of Isaiah 6 as the Son and the Holy Spirit rank them among ministering creatures?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn I.3.4 and Homilies on Isaiah; the charge as pressed by Jerome and Theophilus of Alexandria

Related readings: Does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position? · Can Origen be fairly judged at all, given how his text reaches us?

The passage

My Hebrew master also used to say that those two seraphim in Isaiah, which are described as having each six wings, and calling to one another, and saying, “Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God of hosts,” were to be understood of the only-begotten Son of God and of the Holy Spirit. And we think that that expression also which occurs in the hymn of Habakkuk, “In the midst either of the two living things, or of the two lives, Thou wilt be known,” ought to be understood of Christ and of the Holy Spirit.

— Origen, De Principiis I.3.4.1 He preached the same reading in the first of his homilies on Isaiah, which survive only in Jerome’s Latin:

Who are these two Seraphim? My Lord Jesus and the Holy Spirit. You should not suppose the nature of the Trinity to be divided, if duty-bound observances of the names are to be kept.

— Origen, Homilies on Isaiah 1.2.2

The question

That Origen identified the two seraphim with the Son and the Holy Spirit is not in dispute: the sentence of Peri Archōn I.3.4 survives in Greek as well as in Rufinus’s Latin,3 and the homilies repeat it. Rufinus states that the second sentence of the homily, against dividing the nature of the Trinity, is Jerome’s own addition as translator.4 The question is whether the identification places the Son and the Spirit in the rank of the ministering seraphim, as Jerome and later Justinian’s edict took it, or belongs to a Jewish-Christian reading of the two who reveal the Father, held within Origen’s Trinity. It bears on whether this item of the charge-lists of 403 and 543 reaches a teaching of Origen’s.

An error that sets the Son and the Spirit in the place of the attendant seraphim

On this reading the identification gives the Son and the Holy Spirit the station of the seraphim who serve before the throne, and so lowers them toward the rank of ministering creatures.

Held by: Jerome (Letters 61 and 84; Commentary on Isaiah); Theophilus of Alexandria (Letter from Constantinople, 403); A. R. Fokin (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53).5

In 381 Jerome, writing to Damasus, set the reading aside: “Some of my predecessors make ‘the Lord sitting upon a throne’ God the Father, and suppose the seraphim to represent the Son and the Holy Spirit. I do not agree with them, for John expressly tells us that it was Christ and not the Father whom the prophet saw” (John 12:41); he read the two seraphim as the two Testaments.6 Later he named the identification as one of Origen’s errors, “an error more important than these” of the resurrection and the souls,7 called it “this hateful explanation,”8 and wrote in his commentary on Isaiah: “Impiously, therefore, a certain one understands the two Seraphim as the Son and the Holy Spirit: since, according to the evangelist John and the apostle Paul, we teach that the Son of God was seen in the majesty of one who reigns, and that the Holy Spirit spoke.”9 Theophilus of Alexandria listed the reading among the propositions he drew from Origen in 403.10 The same circle of charges included Epiphanius’s report that for Origen “the Son… cannot see the Father, and the Holy Spirit cannot see the Son,”11 and Justinian’s edict of 543 quoted Peri Archōn I.3.4 among its extracts.12 Rufinus reports that the homily’s guarding clause is Jerome’s addition, and D. V. Smirnov notes that where the Greek survives, in the Homilies on Jeremiah, Jerome’s Latin adds “и одного Духа Святого, совечного с Отцом и Сыном” (and one Holy Spirit, coeternal with the Father and the Son).13 Fokin reports that these comparisons were afterwards regarded as lowering the divine dignity of the Son and Spirit “до уровня служебных творений” (to the level of ministering creatures), and counts them, with the hierarchy of Father, Son and Spirit “по божеству, силе, могуществу, благости, знанию и почитанию” (in divinity, power, might, goodness, knowledge and honour), among the features that make Origen’s Trinitarian teaching “несовершенным даже для своего времени” (imperfect even for its own time).12 On this reading the seraphim of Isaiah are attendants before an enthroned Lord whom John identifies with Christ, and to read the Son and the Spirit as those seraphim gives them the attendants’ place.

A Jewish-Christian reading of the two who reveal the Father

On this reading the identification comes from a Jewish-Christian tradition in which the Son and the Spirit are divine figures who make the Father known, and Origen uses it to set out his Trinity, not to number the two among creatures.

Held by: E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53); Antti Laato (Åbo Akademi University, Verbum Vitae, 2024).14

Origen ascribes the reading to his “Hebrew master” and draws from it that the two are the pair through whom the Father is known: “all knowledge of the Father is obtained by revelation of the Son through the Holy Spirit,” so that Son and Spirit “exist as the ground of the knowledge of God the Father.”1 The chapter in which it stands argues for the Holy Spirit’s divinity. In the fourth homily on Isaiah the veiling seraphim mark the limit of creaturely knowledge: “The Savior alone and the Holy Spirit, who have always been with God, see His ‘face’; and perhaps also the ‘angels’… see also the beginnings of His works,” and the threefold “Holy” shows that “to the holiness of the Father is joined the holiness of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.”15 Tkachev sets the identification within this teaching: only the Saviour and the Spirit see the Father’s face, while the other angelic orders receive their knowledge “по откровению от Сына Божия и Св. Духа” (by revelation from the Son of God and the Holy Spirit).16 Laato traces the identification to angelomorphic theophanies of the Old Testament and to the Ascension of Isaiah, where “both Christ (the Beloved One) and the Holy Spirit are depicted as angelomorphic figures who are divine and worthy of worship.” He holds that the teaching “cannot be taken in a simplistic way so that Christ and the Holy Spirit would have been created figures,” that Origen “uses it to explain the concept of the Holy Triad where Christ and Holy Spirit are subordinate to the Father,” and that it “represented a high Christology” and “was subordinationist in the same way as the Christology in the ante-Nicene period often was.”17 In the same homily the seraph who cleanses Isaiah’s lips is “my Lord Jesus Christ, who was sent by the Father,” with the gloss “do not think it an affront to his nature, if the Son is sent by the Father.”18 Laato notes that the New Testament reads Isaiah’s vision both of Christ (John 12:41) and of the Father (Revelation 4), and that Origen “knew these alternatives and accepted them both.”17 On this reading the two seraphim are the Son and the Spirit as revealers, who alone see the Father and through whom his holiness is proclaimed.
