Did Origen’s allegorical exegesis set aside the literal and historical sense of Scripture?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn IV, as charged by Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: Did Origen teach that the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) are the mortal human body?

The passage

Sixthly, he so allegorises Paradise as to destroy historical truth, understanding angels instead of trees, heavenly virtues instead of rivers, and he overthrows all that is contained in the history of Paradise by his figurative interpretation.

— Jerome, Against John of Jerusalem 7, relaying the charges Epiphanius pressed against Origen, source.1

And if God is said to walk in the paradise in the evening, and Adam to hide himself under a tree, I do not suppose that anyone doubts that these things figuratively indicate certain mysteries, the history having taken place in appearance, and not literally.

— Origen, De Principiis IV.1.16, source.2

The question

Origen read Scripture in three senses, answering to body, soul and spirit, and held that at certain points the narrative was not to be taken as literal history. Pamphilus, answering the charges against him from prison around 308, already lists among them that Origen “dissolves the historical narratives of Scripture.”3 The question is whether his method set the historical sense aside, or rested on it and departed from it only at points the text itself marked; it bears both on the charge-lists of the fourth century and on the later contrast between Alexandrian and Antiochene exegesis.

The spiritual sense rests on the letter

On this reading Origen’s method keeps the historical sense as the body of Scripture, affirms it in most passages, and sets it aside only where he judged the text itself to signal it.

Held by: Pamphilus (Apology for Origen, c. 308, answering the charge); Henri de Lubac (Catholic, Histoire et Esprit, 1950); Henri Crouzel (Catholic, Origen, 1985); Benedict XVI (Catholic, general audience, 2007).4

Origen’s statement of method in Peri Archōn IV makes the plain reading the base of the other two senses: “all the more simple individuals may be edified, so to speak, by the very body of Scripture; for such we term that common and historical sense.”5 He states the ratio in so many words: “the passages which hold good in their historical acceptation are much more numerous than those which contain a purely spiritual meaning,” and the command to honour father and mother “is sufficient of itself without any spiritual meaning.”6 Where he suspends the letter he names a textual trigger: divine wisdom “took care that certain stumbling-blocks, or interruptions, to the historical meaning should take place, by the introduction into the midst (of the narrative) of certain impossibilities and incongruities,” such as a God who “after the manner of a husbandman, planted a paradise in Eden.”7 Against Celsus, who took the story for “an old wife’s fable,” he defended its allegory as reasoned reading: the details of Eden “might of themselves lead a candid reader to see that all these things had not inappropriately an allegorical meaning.”8 De Lubac, in Daniel Hauser’s summary, “understands Origen to have retained the historical character of the faith.”9 Crouzel: “Normally the literal sense is the source of the spiritual sense: if that were not so there would only be an arbitrary sense.”10 Benedict XVI describes the senses Origen recalls in his homilies: “there is the ‘literal’ sense, but this conceals depths that are not immediately apparent.”11 The most specific charge, that the “coats of skins” of Genesis 3:21 are the human body, reaches us chiefly through hostile witnesses; Alexandra Pârvan finds that in the few places Origen refers to the verse “there is no clear picture of how he interprets this verse.”12 On this reading the spiritual sense is reached through the letter, and the letter yields at the points where Origen judged it to break down.

The allegory set aside or diminished the historical sense

On this reading Origen’s allegory dissolved narratives the Church took as history and reduced the significance of history itself.

Held by: Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 64, c. 376); William Tyndale (Protestant, The Obedience of a Christian Man, 1528); Martin Luther (Protestant, Table Talk); R. P. C. Hanson (Allegory and Event, 1959; he grants that Origen defended the historicity of most biblical events).13

Epiphanius, in the Panarion’s entry on the Origenists, charges that they “allegorize Paradise, the heavens and all the rest,” and his charge, as Jerome relays it, is that Origen “so allegorises Paradise as to destroy historical truth.”14 The target is Origen’s own position: of God walking in the garden he writes that “these things figuratively indicate certain mysteries, the history having taken place in appearance, and not literally,”2 and his theory holds that divine wisdom set impossibilities in the narrative on purpose. The criterion is the interpreter’s judgement of what is worthy of God or possible in nature. Eustathius of Antioch wrote a treatise On the Belly-Myth, against Origen, on the witch of Endor (1 Samuel 28), against Origen’s handling of that narrative and his allegorical technique; Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia built a hermeneutic of historia and theoria on the conviction that the history must anchor whatever higher sense is drawn from it.15 Tyndale traces the habit to Origen: “For Origen and the doctors of his time drew all the scripture unto allegories: whose ensample they that came after followed so long, till they at last forgot the order and process of the text,” and answers that “the scripture hath but one sense, which is the literal sense.” Luther: “St Jerome and Origen, God forgive them, were the cause that allegories were held in such esteem.”16 Hanson grants that Origen “defends the historicity of most of the events recorded in the Bible,” and holds that he “perilously reduces the significance of history”: in his view history “can be no more than an acted parable, a charade for showing forth eternal truths about God.” He describes Origen’s system as having “its disinclination towards taking history and event seriously and its allegorism twining like ivy round every branch of his theological structure.”17 On this reading the letter in Origen is the lowest rung, left behind by the advanced reader, and history becomes the vehicle of timeless truths rather than the place where God acts.
