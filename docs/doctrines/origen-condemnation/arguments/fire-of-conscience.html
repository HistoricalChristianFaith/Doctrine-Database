Did Origen teach that hellfire is the fire of a guilty conscience rather than a material, external fire?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn II.10.4–6 and Against Celsus V.15, as reported by Jerome (c. 409) and Orosius (414)

Related readings: Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh, and did he teach that risen bodies are spherical?

The passage

We find in the prophet Isaiah, that the fire with which each one is punished is described as his own; for he says, ‘Walk in the light of your own fire, and in the flame which ye have kindled.’ By these words it seems to be indicated that every sinner kindles for himself the flame of his own fire, and is not plunged into some fire which has been already kindled by another, or was in existence before himself. Of this fire the fuel and food are our sins, which are called by the Apostle Paul wood, and hay, and stubble.

— Origen, De Principiis II.10.4, in Rufinus’s Latin, source.1

Hellfire, moreover, and the torments with which holy scripture threatens sinners he explains not as external punishments but as the pangs of guilty consciences when by God’s power the memory of our transgressions is set before our eyes… it is the fire of conscience and the stings of remorse which torture the mind as it looks back on former self-indulgence.

— Jerome, Letter 124.7, to Avitus (c. 409), source.2

The question

Origen’s own text and his accusers agree that he read the fire of Scripture’s threatenings as a fire each sinner kindles from his own sins, felt in the conscience. The question is what that reading amounted to: a denial of the real fire and of God’s judgement, as the charge-lists put it, or an account of a real, divinely administered punishment located within the soul. It bears on what the lists of c. 310, 400 and 414 were condemning.

A real punishment, inward and healing

On this reading Origen relocated the fire into the soul without denying punishment, and held it to be applied by God to purify.

Held by: Pamphilus (Apology for Origen, c. 310); Karl Rudolf Hagenbach (Protestant, History of Doctrines); John R. Sachs (Jesuit, Theological Studies, 1993); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”, 2001).3

Pamphilus lists among the accusations of his day that “they impugn him with no trivial calumny, [that is,] that he denies that penalties will be inflicted upon sinners,” and answers it, in a chapter headed “De poenis peccatorum,” with Origen’s own passages from Peri Archōn II.4 There the conscience, “receiving by divine power into the memory all those things of which it had stamped on itself certain signs and forms at the moment of sinning, will see a kind of history… of all the foul, and shameful, and unholy deeds which it has done”, and “becomes an accuser and a witness against itself.”1 God is “our Physician”, applying “the punishment of fire to those who have lost their soundness of mind”, and the risen body even of those “destined to everlasting fire or to severe punishments” is “so incorruptible, that it cannot be corrupted and dissolved even by severe punishments.”5 Against Celsus gives “a purificatory fire… it burns indeed, but does not consume, those who are without a material body… and which burns and consumes those who by their actions, words, and thoughts have built up wood, or hay, or stubble”, the Lord coming “like a refiner’s fire and fullers’ soap.”6 Hagenbach: “The eternal fire is neither material nor kindled by another person, but the combustibles are our sins themselves”, and “Origen imagined that the design of all these punishments was to heal or to correct.” Sachs describes two perspectives, “the purifying, healing act of God… (God as the purifying fire), and… the intrinsic suffering and torment which results from sin (the self-enkindled fire of the sinner).” Ware: “There remains the reformative understanding of punishment, which Origen considered to be the only view that is morally acceptable.”7 On this reading the conscience-fire is an exegesis of Isaiah’s “flame which ye have kindled”, Paul’s wood, hay and stubble and Malachi’s refiner, in which God punishes in order to heal.

The fire of Scripture turned into conscience

On this reading the conscience-fire takes the place of the fire Scripture threatens and reduces God’s part as judge, which is what the charge-lists recorded.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 124, c. 409); Orosius (Commonitorium, 414); Graham Keith (“Patristic Views on Hell,” Evangelical Quarterly, 1999).8

Jerome, translating Peri Archōn for Avitus with the Greek before him, reports that Origen explains the torments Scripture threatens “not as external punishments but as the pangs of guilty consciences”, and that the whole crop of a man’s sins “grows up afresh from seeds which remain in the soul, and all our dishonourable and undutiful acts are again pictured before our gaze.”2 Origen’s text supports him closely: the sinner “is not plunged into some fire which has been already kindled by another, or was in existence before himself”; the soul bears “the chastisement and torture of its own dissension”; and “around the substance of the soul certain tortures are produced by the hurtful affections of sins themselves.”9 When Orosius brought a memorandum on Origenism to Augustine in 414, its fourth article was “that the eternal fire is neither real fire nor eternal”; the fire’s end belongs to Origen’s teaching on restoration.10 Augustine, in the City of God, held that the undying worm may be read of anguish of mind but that “that hell, which also is called a lake of fire and brimstone, will be material fire, and will torment the bodies of the damned, whether men or devils.”11 Keith writes that Origen modified Scripture’s position on punishment “by minimising God’s role as judge”: the soul is condemned by “the stinging lash of conscience”, and “Origen did not entirely rule out a role for God in this process, but he undoubtedly minimised it. The soul’s torment arises primarily from some natural mechanism as it reassesses the avenues which formerly led it into sin.” Since God’s judgements were, for Origen, always for the good of those who suffer, “no room was left for an ultimate judicial condemnation by God of persistent evil-doing.”12 On this reading the accusers described accurately a fire that is the sinner’s own, kindled by memory, in place of the fire prepared by God that their own reading of Scripture expected.
