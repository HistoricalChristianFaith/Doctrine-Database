Did Anastasius I’s sentence of 400–401 condemn Origen’s books only, or Origen himself?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Anastasius I of Rome, in the campaign of 400 opened by Theophilus of Alexandria

Related readings: Was Origen himself anathematized by an ecumenical council? (the same distinction between the man and his writings, asked of 553) · Did Anastasius summon and personally condemn Rufinus?

The passage

He has shown to me some blasphemous chapters which made me shudder as I passed judgement on them. If Origen has put forth any other writings, you are to know that they and their author are alike condemned by me.

— Anastasius I to Simplicianus of Milan (400) = Jerome, Letter 95.3, source.1

As for Origen, whose writings he has translated into our language, I have neither formerly known, nor do I now seek to know either who he was or what expression he may have given to his thought.

— Anastasius I to John of Jerusalem (401), §3, source.2

The question

In 400 Anastasius I, bishop of Rome, acting in the campaign that Theophilus of Alexandria had opened against Origen’s writings, read chapters of Rufinus’s Latin Peri Archōn brought to him by Eusebius of Cremona and wrote to Simplicianus of Milan and, a year later, to John of Jerusalem. In the same months the Alexandrian synodical decree, as Socrates Scholasticus describes it, “did not excommunicate Origen himself but condemned his books,” while Theophilus asked Epiphanius for a synodical letter “that by universal consent Origen himself may be expressly condemned.”3 The question is whether the Roman letters condemned a body of writing, or Origen as well; it bears on what had been decided about Origen’s person before the sixth century.

The sentence condemned Origen as well as his writings

On this reading the Roman letters passed judgement on the author together with the books.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus, 401–402).4

Jerome, who held copies of Anastasius’s letters, counts the pope among the bishops who had condemned Origen as a person. Writing against Rufinus, he asks what use are “the letters of the bishop Theophilus or of the pope Anastasius, who follow out the heretic in every part of the world,” and names “the reverend bishops Anastasius and Theophilus, Venerius and Chromatius, and the whole council of the Catholics both in the East and in the West, who publicly denounce him as a heretic.”4 The letter to Simplicianus supports this in its own words: having described the marked chapters, Anastasius writes that Origen’s other writings and “their author are alike condemned by me.” The letter to John of Jerusalem a year later attributes a purpose to Origen, that “his object was to disintegrate our faith, which is that of the Apostles, and has been confirmed by the traditions of the fathers, by leading us into tortuous paths.”5 A judgement on a writer’s object is a judgement on the writer. On this reading the pope’s disclaimer of any knowledge of Origen describes the grounds of the sentence, which rested on the chapters set before him, and leaves its object unchanged: the author of those chapters was condemned with them.

The sentence fell on the writings and the propositions drawn from them

On this reading Rome, like the Egyptian synod of the same year, condemned propositions and forbade reading, without passing sentence on the person of a presbyter dead a century and a half.

Held by: F. Prat (Catholic, “Origen and Origenism,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911).6

Prat states the outcome in these terms: “Anastasius, while declaring that Origen was entirely unknown to him, condemned the propositions extracted from his books.”6 The occasion was a book: Rufinus’s translation, with the offending chapters marked. The operative language of the letter to Simplicianus is a reading-prohibition, written “to the end that no man may contrary to the commandment read these books which we have mentioned,” and its sentence rejects “everything written in days gone by Origen that is contrary to our faith.”1 The enforcement that followed was of the same kind: Anastasius reports the imperial decree “by which every one who serves God is warned against the reading of Origen, and all who are convicted of reading his impious works are condemned by the imperial judgment.”7 No act of either year excommunicates Origen, degrades him, or removes his name from the diptychs. The pope says he had not known who Origen was; the editor of Rufinus in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers takes this as evidence that Origen’s works “had been neglected in the West for a century and to such an extent that the Pope Anastasius says… that he neither knows who he was nor what he wrote.”8 On this reading the phrase “they and their author” belongs to the vehement register of a private episcopal letter, and the juridical act was the one the documents record: a censure of propositions and a ban on reading, as at Alexandria.
