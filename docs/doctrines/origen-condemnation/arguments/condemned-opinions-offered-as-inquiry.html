Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn, preface and I.6.1, as defended by Pamphilus of Caesarea

Related readings: Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen teach the transmigration of souls?

The passage

The cause of the diversity of opinion on these subjects is this, viz., that those subjects on which we discourse are treated by us with great solicitude and caution, in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than in that of fixed and certain decision.

— Origen, De Principiis I.6.1 (c. 229), introducing the restoration of all things, source.1

The question

Peri Archōn divides the faith into points the apostles delivered plainly and points left open for inquiry, and its author repeatedly flags his treatment of the latter as investigation. The anathemas of 543 and 553 condemn propositions drawn from it. The question is whether those propositions were Origen’s teaching or hypotheses he offered for judgement, and what that difference meant for the sentences passed on him.

Inquiry offered for the Church’s judgement

On this reading Peri Archōn sets out its bold speculations as investigations of questions the Church had left open, so that condemning them as Origen’s doctrines mistakes what kind of book it is.

Held by: Pamphilus of Caesarea (Apology for Origen, c. 308); Athanasius of Alexandria (De Decretis 27); Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church); Henri Crouzel (Origen, 1985).2

The preface sets out the division. On some points “the holy apostles… delivered themselves with the utmost clearness… leaving, however, the grounds of their statements to be examined into”; into the second class Origen puts the origin of the soul, “not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church,” and the nature of the devil and his angels, which the Church “has not explained with sufficient clearness.”3 Having laid out three views of the end in Book II, he leaves the choice to the reader: “let each one of our readers determine for himself, with care and diligence, whether any one of them can be approved and adopted.”4 Pamphilus made the point the hinge of his defence: “his frequent custom is to add an admission that he is not declaring these things by a definitive pronouncement, nor is he defining them as secure dogma, but he is investigating to the best of his ability and discussing the meaning of the Scriptures.”5 Athanasius drew the same line: “what he has written as if inquiring and by way of exercise, that let no one take as expressive of his own sentiments, but of parties who are contending in investigation, but what he definitely declares, that is the sentiment of the labour-loving man.”6 Jerome, cataloguing the errors of the book for Avitus, records Origen’s own disclaimer: “I must not be taken to make dogmas of these things; they are only thrown out as conjectures.”7 Pamphilus complained that the malicious “learn with utmost zeal” the passages “that lead to calumny,” and the numbered anathemas quote propositions without the caution under which they were framed.8 Schaff: Origen “taught a final restoration, but with modesty as a speculation rather than a dogma, in his youthful work De Principiis,” and “speculations on open questions of theology are no heresies.” Crouzel describes the work as “research theology.”9 On this reading Origen broke no rule the Church had defined and disowned the dogmatic mood, and the propositions were condemned in a form more assertive than the book they came from.

Conclusions argued from one principle

On this reading the hedges are real but uneven, the speculations are argued as the necessary reading of Scripture, and the propositions were his to be judged whatever mood he put them in.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 124, to Avitus; Apology against Rufinus); Justinian (edict to Menas, 543); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911).10

Jerome, who preserved the disclaimer, set it at the close of “the wicked reasoning with which he has wounded his reader.”7 To Rufinus he answered that the book could not be repaired passage by passage: “when the whole discussion proceeds on a single principle, namely, the notion that the whole universe of reasonable creatures have fallen by their own will, and will hereafter return to a condition of unity: and that again from that starting point another fall will begin: what is there that you can amend, unless you alter the whole book?”11 The same Peri Archōn describes the restoration as the term of a process: “all things shall be re-established in a state of unity, and… God shall be all in all,” brought about “not suddenly, but slowly and gradually… during the lapse of countless and unmeasured ages.”12 The pre-existent fall of souls works elsewhere in his exegesis as an explanatory premise, in his reading of Jacob and Esau and of the disparities of birth. Rufinus stated that he translated so as to remove what he took for stumbling-blocks in the Greek, so the density of disclaimers in the Latin may in part be his.13 Prat grants that Origen “oftentimes corrects by orthodox professions of faith the heterodoxy of his speculations” and holds that he “does not deserve to be ranked among the promoters of heresy”; but “the hypotheses which he lays down here and there are none the less worthy of censure.”14 On this reading the mood in which Origen put a proposition bears on his personal culpability, and the propositions themselves, argued and copied widely, stood open to judgement on their content.
