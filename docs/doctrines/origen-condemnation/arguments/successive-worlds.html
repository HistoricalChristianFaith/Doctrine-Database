Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds in which the restored may fall again?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn II–III, as read by Jerome, Augustine and the anathemas of 543 and 553

Related readings: Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Did Origen teach that God’s power is finite and that creation is coeternal with God? · Did Origen teach that the Son’s kingdom will come to an end?

The passage

…not then for the first time did God begin to work when He made this visible world; but as, after its destruction, there will be another world, so also we believe that others existed before the present came into being… It is not, however, to be supposed that several worlds existed at once, but that, after the end of this present world, others will take their beginning.

— Origen, De Principiis III.5.3 (c. 229), link.1

The question

That Origen taught worlds before and after the present one is common ground. The fifteenth of the anathemas of 553 condemns anyone who says “that the life of the spirits shall be like to the life which was in the beginning… so that the end and the beginning shall be alike, and that the end shall be the true measure of the beginning”;2 the ninth of Justinian’s edict of 543 condemns holding that the punishment of the impious “is temporary and that it will have an end at some time.”3 The question is whether Origen’s succession of worlds is an open series in which restored beings may fall again and new worlds follow without end, or a bounded, non-identical sequence of ages that ends in a final rest. It bears on what the charge-lists of the sixth century reached in Origen’s own work.

An open series of worlds, in which the restored may fall again

On this reading Origen’s principles that the end is like the beginning, and that rational beings never lose their freedom, yield a series of worlds with no secured end, in which falls and restorations can recur.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus, 401–402; Letter 124, c. 409–410); Augustine (City of God XXI.17); F. Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, Catholic, 1911).4

Origen states the principle himself: “the end is always like the beginning,” and rational natures hold goodness “as an accidental and perishable quality.”5 The extracts Jerome translated for Avitus carry it further: bodily existence may pass away, “But if through the fall of reasonable beings it is a second time required it will reappear”; worlds “vary with the sins which cause them, and those are exploded theories which maintain that all worlds are alike”; and on Jerome’s summary “we men may change into any other reasonable beings, and that not once only or on emergency but time after time.”6 Jerome lists among Origen’s teachings “that there are innumerable worlds, succeeding one another in eternal ages,” and that after the restitution of all things “then will begin a new world from a new origin, and other bodies in which the souls who fall from heaven will be clothed.”7 Augustine writes that the Church condemned Origen “especially for his theory of the ceaseless alternation of happiness and misery, and the interminable transitions from the one state to the other at fixed periods of ages,” in which the saints have “no fearless assurance of eternal blessedness.”8 Prat reads Origen as holding that God “created from eternity,” so that “he is forced to admit a double infinite series of worlds before and after the present world,” and that the roles of rational beings “may be one day changed; for what free will has done, free will can undo, and the Trinity alone is essentially immutable in good.”9 On this reading the succession is the working of two principles Origen states, the likeness of end to beginning and freedom that is never removed, and the sixth-century formula “the end and the beginning shall be alike” repeats a principle of his.

A bounded, non-identical sequence of ages, ending in a rest held by love

On this reading Origen’s worlds are a providential sequence, each different from the last, that ends when God is “all in all,” and charity keeps the restored from falling again.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church II); Mark J. Edwards (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2014, rev. 2026); E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53).10

Origen sets his worlds against the Stoic doctrine that after the conflagration comes “an arrangement of things in which everything will be unchanged, as compared with the former arrangement,” so that “Socrates will be again the son of Sophroniscus, and a native of Athens,” and calls one feature of it “more ridiculous still.”11 Against it he holds that all things “are administered by God in proportion to the relation of the free-will of each individual, and are ever being brought into a better condition.”12 The worlds do not run side by side, and their diversity comes from “the movements and declensions of those who fell from that primeval unity.”13 The sequence ends in God being “all in all,” when “there will no longer be any diversity” and the last enemy is destroyed.14 In the Commentary on Romans he names what holds the restored: “what it is that in the ages to come will hold fast the freedom of choice, so that it does not fall again into sin, the Apostle teaches us in a brief word, saying: ‘Charity never falls.’”15 Tkachev reads Origen accordingly: Origen grants that, since virtue is mutable and the soul free, it could in theory fall again, “Тем не менее О. высказывает предположение, что такого падения фактически не произойдет” (nevertheless Origen proposes that such a fall will not in fact occur), the power of Christ’s cross and love being sufficient “для удержания их в любви к Богу” (to hold them in love for God).16 Edwards writes that the argument from God’s eternal activity would need “an infinite succession of worlds before and after the present one, and even Origen’s enemies do not say that he went so far.”17 Schaff reads Origen’s thought as “ever new metamorphoses of the original world,” each world having “a beginning, and an end which are comprehended in the divine Providence.”18 On this reading the plurality of worlds is the pedagogy that returns fallen minds to unity, and the restoration is its end.
