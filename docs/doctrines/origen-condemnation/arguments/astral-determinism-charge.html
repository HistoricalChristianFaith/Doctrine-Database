Did Origen teach that the stars cause events?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Commentary on Genesis III (Philocalia 23); see also Origen on the astrology timeline

Related readings: Did Origen teach that the sun, moon and stars are living, ensouled rational beings? · Did Origen teach that “magic is not an evil”? (the neighbouring count in the same charge-list)

The passage

He is charged with teaching that prayer should not be offered to the Son, and that He is not absolutely good; that He does not know the Father as Himself; that rational natures enter into the bodies of irrational beings; that there are migrations from one body into another; that the soul of the Saviour was the same as the soul of Adam; that there is neither eternal punishment nor resurrection of the flesh; that magic is not an evil; that astronomy is the cause of events; that the Only Begotten has no share in the Kingdom…

— Photius, Bibliotheca cod. 117, summarizing the charges answered in an anonymous five-book Apology for Origen, source.1

And if any one, noting what is said about the future, should allow that they are correct, he will say that they are right, not because the stars cause the events, but only because they indicate them.

— Origen, Commentary on Genesis III, on Genesis 1:14, in Philocalia 23, source.2

The question

“Astronomy” in the charge means judicial astrology, the reading of nativities and fortunes from the sky, and the count is that Origen made the stars the cause of what befalls human beings. Origen’s fullest treatment of the subject, the comment on “let them be for signs” (Genesis 1:14) that Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus excerpted into the Philocalia, holds that the heavens foreshow the future; the question is whether that teaching, joined to his view of the stars as living beings, amounts to the astral causation charged.

Origen’s heavens govern what comes to pass

On this reading Origen’s teaching that the heavens hold the future, read by living powers that act on it, makes the stars a cause of events, as the charge says.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.3

The charge stands in the list of counts that the anonymous Apology for Origen set out to answer, and a neighbouring count is recorded for the Alexandrian synod of 400 under Theophilus, whose list of Origen’s errors, in the summary printed with Jerome’s letters, includes: “The stars are conscious of their own movements, and the demons know the future by their courses.”3 The charge rests on three features of Origen’s own text. He holds that the heavens are a book of the future: “like a volume of prophecy, the heavens as a whole, being as it were one of God’s books, may contain the future,” and the heavenly signs “have been from everlasting to everlasting engraved in the book of the heavens.” He holds that the signs are read by agents who act on them: “I conjecture that the signs are exposed to the Powers which administer human affairs, in order that they may only know some things, and effect others.”4 And he teaches that the sun, moon and stars are themselves living, ensouled, rational beings. Joined together, a heaven in which all events are written, whose writing living powers read and carry out, and whose lights are themselves rational souls, is a heaven that governs the world beneath it. In the Platonic and Stoic cosmology of the time the living heavens rule the sublunar world, and on this reading the charge describes Origen’s cosmology in the terms of that ambient view.

The stars are signs, not causes

On this reading Origen’s comment on Genesis 1:14 is an argument against astral determinism: the stars indicate events but do not bring them about, and human free will stands.

Held by: the anonymous author of the Apology for Origen (as summarized by Photius, Bibliotheca cod. 117); Claire Hall (“Origen and Astrology,” 2019); Garry Phillipson (“Astrology as Heresy in Contemporary Belief,” 2019).5

The anonymous defender counted the charge among fifteen he declared “mere slanders,” answering them “by quotations from his writings.”1 Origen’s comment on “let them be for signs” opens by naming the danger of astral necessity. If human affairs “may be governed by necessity, and cannot possibly be otherwise than is ruled by the stars,” then:

And a consequence of this opinion is the complete destruction of Free Will; and a further result is that praise and blame are unmeaning, and the distinction between acceptable conduct and conduct deserving of blame is lost. And if this be so, there is an end of that Divine judgment which we preach; an end also of God’s threats of punishment awaiting sinners; an end, too, of a blessed future for those who have devoted themselves to the higher life.

Astral fatalism, he adds, would make God the author of every crime: its champions “may free their fellow-men from all guilt, but they make God the cause of all that is bad and blameworthy.”6 His answer is the distinction in the passage above: where predictions come true, the stars are right “not because the stars cause the events, but only because they indicate them,” and the astrologers’ error was that they “supposed that they from whom they thought they received the signs were the original causes of the events which the Word says the signs indicate.”4 Hall reads the chapter in these terms: “Origen begins from Gen. 1:14 to explore a familiar set of arguments against Greco-Roman astral determinism,” and “uses the distinction between signs and causes in order to pursue a complex argument that is fundamentally about the nature of foreknowledge through prophecy, and about human free will.” Phillipson: “In Origen’s view astrology dealt with signs rather than causal factors that would compel action.”5 On this reading the book of the heavens is a prophecy, and a prophecy foretells without compelling.
