Was Origen condemned for views that others held without condemnation?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witnesses: Gregory of Nyssa, Clement of Alexandria, Maximus the Confessor, Isaac of Nineveh and John Scotus Eriugena, compared with Origen, Didymus and Evagrius

Related readings: What does the condemnation of Origen and Theodore of Mopsuestia by the same council of 553 indicate about its aims? · Was the plea of heretical interpolation applied alike to Gregory of Nyssa and to Origen? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day?

The passage

But He that becomes “all” things will be “in all” things too; and herein it appears to me that Scripture teaches the complete annihilation of evil… even from those evil spirits shall rise in harmony the confession of Christ's Lordship.

— Gregory of Nyssa, On the Soul and the Resurrection (c. 380), link.1

The question

Gregory of Nyssa taught the final restoration of all rational creatures from 1 Corinthians 15:28; the Second Council of Nicaea (787) named him among the holy Fathers and in the same Definition anathematized “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus.”1 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox) asks why Gregory “has never been condemned as a heretic, but on the contrary he is honored as a saint,” and offers two answers: “Perhaps he escaped reprobation because he was Basil’s brother,” or because, “while retaining Origen’s hope in the eventual triumph of good over evil, he abandoned the notion of preexistence and so avoided the circularity of the Origenist scheme.”2 The question is whether Origen was condemned for teachings that others held unpunished, so that the sentence followed the person, or whether the sentence fell on a system, shared with Didymus and Evagrius, from which the uncondemned holders of the hope differed.

Origen singled out among holders of the same teaching

On this reading the condemnations fell on Origen’s name for teachings that others held, before and after him, without being condemned, and long after his death.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 33, c. 384, of the sentence of c. 231); Ilaria Ramelli (Catholic University of Milan, 2013–2014); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, “Saint Origen”, 2015).3

Jerome wrote of the first sentence that Origen was condemned “not-as the rabid hounds who now pursue him cry-because of the novelty or heterodoxy of his doctrines, but because men could not tolerate the incomparable eloquence and knowledge which, when once he opened his lips, made others seem dumb.”4 Gregory of Nyssa, on Ramelli’s reading, upheld the restoration “in an even stronger form than Origen did, including the salvation of the devil, without much hesitation,” and their sister Macrina held with him that otherworldly punishments are not eternal.5 Clement of Alexandria, Origen’s predecessor, taught that “God’s punishments are saving and disciplinary, leading to conversion”; no council condemned him, and his name was dropped from the Roman calendar only when the Martyrology was revised under Clement VIII.6 After 553, Maximus the Confessor kept the fuller doctrine as “a better and more secret explanation… we… will honour by silence”;7 Isaac of Nineveh taught a bounded Gehenna whose sufferers “are scourged by the scourge of love,” and was added to the Roman Martyrology in 2024;8 Eriugena’s Periphyseon, not the man, was condemned in 1210 and burned in 1225;9 Gregory of Nazianzus allowed that “some may prefer even in this place to take a more merciful view of this fire.”10 Hart writes that “to have declared any man a heretic three centuries after his having died in the peace of the church, in respect of doctrinal determinations not reached during his life, was a gross violation of all legitimate canonical order,” and that the consensus of Orthodox theologians he names (“Evdokimov, Bulgakov, Clément, Turincev, Ware”) is that universalism “as a permissible theologoumenon or plausible hope, has never been condemned by the Church.”11 On this reading the hope for which Origen’s name was condemned remained, in others, the teaching of venerated Fathers, and the sentence of the sixth century attached to the name that a later movement bore.

A school and its system condemned, from which the uncondemned differed

On this reading the sentence fell on a system, condemned in Didymus and Evagrius as well as in Origen, and those who held the hope without condemnation had set aside the doctrines that made up that system.

Held by: the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681); the Second Council of Nicaea (787); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century); Michael McClymond (Saint Louis University, Theological Studies, 2015).12

The councils that recalled the sentence of 553 named three men together: the fifth synod “against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius” (680–681), and “we anathematize the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus, as did the fifth synod” (787).13 The council of 553 also condemned by name Theodore of Mopsuestia, who had died in 428.14 Florovsky writes that “Didymus and Evagrius were condemned along with Origen,” that the condemnation “primarily referred to the Palestinian Origenists who had shattered tranquillity in the local monasteries,” and that it “was a condemnation of the inner temptations or the old Alexandrian theology which had not yet lost its influence.”15 McClymond holds that Gregory of Nyssa “deviated from Origen in basic ways and repudiated Origen’s teaching on preexistent (or premortal) souls,” and that “Gregory, Maximus Confessor, and Eriugena all rejected Origen’s static afterlife. In this sense, Origen’s apokatastasis had no sequel or successor”; “Moderate followers of Origen (e.g., Gregory) rejected the idea of a primal fall of souls, followed by embodiment and a final restoration to God,” which only “more radical followers (e.g., Evagrius of Pontus and Stephen bar Sudaili…)” kept.16 Isaac of Nineveh belonged, in the words of the Holy See’s 2024 notice, to “the Churches of the Assyrian-Chaldean tradition,” outside the jurisdiction that received the sentence of 553;8 the book of Eriugena was condemned in connection with Amalric of Bène’s pantheism.9 In Gregory’s own century the Shorter Rules ascribed to Basil pressed the parallel of Matthew 25:46 against a bounded punishment.17 On this reading the condemned teaching was a whole scheme of pre-existence, fall and restoration, held by Origen and his radical followers, and its condemnation reached those who held that scheme.
