Did the charge that Origen taught the devil’s salvation represent his teaching?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, letter to friends at Alexandria and Peri Archōn I.6 and III.6, as charged by Theophilus of Alexandria and Justinian

Related readings: Did Origen teach that Christ will be crucified again in the heavens to save the demons? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day?

The passage

…certain of these men who seek for matters of contention, ascribe to us and our teaching the blasphemy… namely, that the father of wickedness and perdition of those who shall be cast out of the kingdom of God can be saved; a thing which not even a madman can say.

— Origen, letter to friends at Alexandria, as translated by Jerome, Apology against Rufinus II.18, source.1

We think, indeed, that the goodness of God, through His Christ, may recall all His creatures to one end, even His enemies being conquered and subdued. For thus says holy Scripture, The Lord said to My Lord, Sit at My right hand, until I make Your enemies Your footstool.

— Origen, De Principiis I.6.1 (c. 229), source.2

The question

Theophilus of Alexandria’s synod of 400 and Justinian’s edict of 543 condemned, among Origen’s opinions, the final restoration of the demons; Origen, in a letter preserved by both Jerome and Rufinus, denied ever teaching that the devil would be saved.3 The question is whether the charge reported his teaching, or turned an open question and an argument about the devil’s nature into a doctrine he disowned.

The charge misrepresents him

On this reading Origen denied the doctrine, left the devil’s fate an open question, and taught the destruction of the hostile will rather than the salvation of the devil.

Held by: Origen (letter to friends at Alexandria); Rufinus (On the Falsification of the Books of Origen); Hans Urs von Balthasar (Catholic, Dare We Hope, 1988); John R. Sachs, S.J. (Catholic, “Apocatastasis in Patristic Theology,” 1993).4

Origen’s letter calls the teaching a blasphemy “which not even a madman can say,” and Rufinus copies the same complaint into his treatise on the falsification of Origen’s books: “they declare that I hold that the father of wickedness… that is the devil, is to be saved.”5 Jerome sets beside the letter the debate behind it: Candidus, “the defender of the heresy of Valentinian,” “asserts that the devil is of a nature wholly evil which can never be saved. Against this Origen rightly asserts that he is not of perishable substance, but that it is by his own will that he fell and can be saved. This Candidus falsely turns into a reproach against Origen, as if he had said that the diabolical nature could be saved.”6 Where Peri Archōn reaches the question, Origen refers it to the reader: “whether any of these orders who act under the government of the devil… will in a future world be converted to righteousness… or whether persistent and inveterate wickedness may be changed by the power of habit into nature, is a result which you yourself, reader, may approve of.”7 What is destroyed in “the last enemy” is “its mind and hostile will, which came not from God, but from itself”: “Its destruction, therefore, will not be its non-existence, but its ceasing to be an enemy, and (to be) death.”8 Pierre Batiffol reports that in the Commentary on Romans Origen “states that Lucifer, unlike the Jews, will not be converted, even at the end of time.”9 The forms of the charge enforced in 400 and after (“Christ suffered once for men; he will suffer again for the demons”) are, on this reading, inferences; Jerome writes of one of them that “he has not expressly said it.”10 Balthasar: Origen “heatedly denies having taught simply that the Devil himself would eventually be saved.” Sachs: in the letter “Origen denies ever having taught the final conversion and redemption of the demons… there is no conclusive evidence that Origen definitively held the radical form of apocatastasis.”11 On this reading the letter, the open question of I.6.3 and the will-not-substance language of III.6.5 are one consistent position: the devil fell by will and is not evil by nature, and the end abolishes evil.

His teaching implies the restoration of the fallen being

On this reading the architecture of Peri Archōn, one beginning and one end, carries the reconciliation of every fallen rational creature, the one called the devil included, whatever Origen said in his own defence.

Held by: Theophilus of Alexandria (synodal letter, 400); Jerome (Letter 124, to Avitus); Ilaria Ramelli (The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013); Michael McClymond (Theological Studies, 2015).12

The restoration in I.6.1 reaches “even His enemies,” and the scheme is symmetrical: “the end is always like the beginning,” and the fallen “have not been removed irrecoverably… they may recover themselves, and be restored to their condition of happiness.”13 The end is reached when “all things shall be re-established in a state of unity, and… God shall be all in all.” The destruction of the last enemy is defined as the removal of its enmity while “its substance, which was formed by God” does not perish; on this reading a rational creature whose hostile will is gone, returned to that unity, is a restored creature.14 Jerome, summarizing the book for Avitus, read it as teaching “that men, or rather their souls, may become demons; and that demons in turn may be restored to the rank of angels,” and of the further charge he wrote that “although he has not expressly said it, it is yet implied in his words.”15 The letter, on this reading, answers a different question: Candidus held the devil evil by nature, and Origen’s reply, that the devil “fell and can be saved,” concerns his nature, not his final end.6 Gregory of Nyssa, a century later, wrote of the end that “even from those evil spirits shall rise in harmony the confession of Christ’s Lordship.”16 Ramelli, as her reviewer Steven Nemes summarizes her, holds that for Origen “the devil will be saved qua archangel but not qua devil, death, enemy”; McClymond counts “the reconciliation of Satan and demons” among the speculations of Peri Archōn.17 Ferdinand Prat wrote that “the universal restoration (apokatastasis) follows necessarily from these principles.”18 On this reading the devil as enemy is destroyed and the creature that bore the name is restored, which is the outcome the charge named in other words.
