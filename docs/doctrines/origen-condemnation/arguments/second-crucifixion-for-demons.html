Did Origen teach that Christ will be crucified again in the heavens to save the demons?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn, as quoted by Jerome, Letter 124.13; the charge as pressed by Theophilus of Alexandria and Justinian

Related readings: Did the charge that Origen taught the devil’s salvation represent his teaching? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine? · Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen?

The passage

…it may indeed be overbold to suppose that He will suffer in heaven; yet if there is spiritual wickedness in heavenly places… why need we fear to imagine a like occurrence in the upper world… so that the nations of all realms shall be saved by a passion of Christ?

— Origen, as quoted by Jerome, Letter 124.13, to Avitus (c. 409–410), source.1

The question

The synodical letter of Theophilus’s council at Alexandria (400) lists among Origen’s errors “Christ suffered once for men; he will suffer again for the demons,” a charge Norman Russell traces to Theophilus’s own reading of De principiis;2 in 543 Justinian’s seventh anathema condemned anyone who holds “that in the age to come Christ the Master will be crucified on behalf of demons”.3 The question is whether Origen taught, or by his own premises implied, a further passion of Christ for the demons and the heavenly powers, or whether he held a single passion whose effect reaches all rational beings and raised a further passion only as a speculation. The passage survives only in Jerome’s Latin; it bears on what the charge-lists of 400 and 543 reached in Origen’s own work.

A further passion of Christ for the demons, stated or implied by Origen

On this reading Origen’s own words extend Christ’s saving passion to the powers in the heavens, so that the charge-lists of 400 and 543 record what he wrote or what his premises require.

Held by: Theophilus of Alexandria (synodical letter, 400; festal letter, 401); Jerome (Letter 124, c. 409–410); G. W. Butterworth (Origen on First Principles, 1936); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century).4

The words Jerome sets in Origen’s mouth carry the thought: extending the inquiry “to the passion of our Lord and Saviour,” Origen reasons that since “there is spiritual wickedness in heavenly places” and the Lord was once crucified “to destroy those thing’s which He has destroyed by His passion,” one need not “fear to imagine a like occurrence in the upper world”.1 Jerome sums it up: Origen “has asserted what it is sacrilegious even to think; that Christ will once more suffer in the sky for the salvation of demons,” and draws the corollary himself: “although he has not expressly said it, it is yet implied in his words that as for men God became man to set men free, so for the salvation of demons when He comes to deliver them He will become a demon.”1 Elsewhere he writes that Origen allows himself “to say that Christ has often suffered, and will often suffer again, it being always profitable to undertake what has once been profitable.”5 Theophilus’s festal letter of 401 denounces as “madness” the teaching that Christ “should be affixed to the cross for demons and spiritual wickednesses,” draws the same inference to Christ taking “the nature of demons,” and answers that those who believe it “crucify him again” (Hebrews 6:6).6 Butterworth, who judged that the passages in the letter to Avitus “have a genuine Origenistic ring about them and there is no evidence whatever of hardening or exaggeration,” reads the system the same way: since many spiritual beings had never come to earth, “Origen supposed that Christ would visit them, too, in their celestial abodes, would assume their nature and would even suffer for them.”7 Florovsky, listing that Christ “would be crucified for demons” among the doctrines of the edict of 543, holds that “the opinions which were condemned do actually flow from” Origen’s premises.8 A homily that owes nothing to Origen’s accusers gives the motif of a passion not yet finished: “My Saviour even now laments my sins… he cannot rejoice while I continue in iniquity,” and he “is in sorrow as long as we persist in error.”9 On this reading, a restoration that reaches every fallen rational creature and a salvation that runs through Christ’s passion together supply the premises from which a passion for the demons follows.

One passion for all rational beings, and a guarded speculation

On this reading Origen taught that Christ’s single sacrifice was offered for every rational creature, earthly and heavenly, and raised a further passion only as a tentative question, which his accusers turned into a doctrine.

Held by: Mario Baghos (St Andrew’s Greek Orthodox Theological College, Phronema, 2015); E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53).10

The passage is framed as inquiry: a heavenly passion “may indeed be overbold to suppose,” and Origen asks only why one “need… fear to imagine” it.1 In works transmitted apart from Jerome’s excerpt, the passion is once for all. In De principiis he takes Hebrews 9:26 to teach that “in that age which preceded this, Christ did not suffer,” for “He was once made a victim, and in the consummation of ages was manifested to take away sin”;11 in the Commentary on John Christ is the High Priest “having offered Himself as the sacrifice which is offered once for all, and not for men only but for every rational creature.”12 The first Homily on Leviticus makes the one offering twofold: “Jesus was offered as a victim not only for earthly things but also for heavenly ones,” a “twofold sacrifice… through which he saved both earthly things and heavenly ones.”13 Tkachev reads Origen accordingly: “единая Жертва Христа была принесена за всех людей и за всех существ, наделенных логосом” (the single sacrifice of Christ was offered for all men and all beings endowed with reason); Origen rejects recurring identical worlds as incompatible “с уникальностью и единственностью Жертвы Христа” (with the uniqueness and singularity of Christ’s sacrifice); and among the fruits of that sacrifice is “невидимое распятие диавола” (the invisible crucifixion of the devil), a defeat of the hostile power.14 Of “the last enemy” Origen writes that its destruction is “not as if its substance, which was formed by God, is to perish, but because its mind and hostile will, which came not from God, but from itself, are to be destroyed.”15 In a letter to friends in Alexandria he called the teaching that “the father of wickedness and perdition… can be saved” a thing “which not even a madman can say.”16 The sentence on a heavenly passion reaches us only in Jerome’s Latin; John Cavadini, introducing the 2013 reprint of Butterworth’s translation, notes that the 1936 edition filled Rufinus’s gaps from “sources as hostile as the anathemata of Justinian” and that “scholarly consensus regarding the reliability of Rufinus’s translation has considerably shifted.”17 Baghos, following Richard Price, places the tenets of 543, among them that Christ was “crucified on behalf of demons as well as humans,” with one sixth-century Origenist group, and writes that “the beliefs of this group seem to be anachronistically attributed to Origen.”18 On this reading Origen’s Christ suffers once, at the consummation of the ages, for every rational creature, and the heavenly powers are reached by that one sacrifice.
