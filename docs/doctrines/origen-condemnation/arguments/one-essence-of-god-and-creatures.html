Did Origen teach that God and all rational creatures are “of one and the same essence”?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, De Principiis IV.4.9, as rendered by Jerome (Letter 124) and as charged by Theophilus of Alexandria (402)

Related readings: Was Origen’s subordination of the Son a pre-Nicene idiom, so that reading him as an Arian is anachronistic? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Can Origen be fairly judged at all, given how his text reaches us?

The passage

God and His only-begotten Son and the Holy Spirit are conscious of an intellectual and reasonable nature. But so also are the angels, the powers, and the virtues, as well as the inward man who is created in the image and after the likeness of God. From which I conclude that God and they are in some sort of one essence.

— Origen, De Principiis IV.4.9, in Jerome’s Latin, Letter 124.15, to Avitus (c. 409–410), source.1

Every mind which partakes of intellectual light ought undoubtedly to be of one nature with every mind which partakes in a similar manner of intellectual light. If the heavenly virtues, then, partake of intellectual light, i.e., of divine nature, because they participate in wisdom and holiness, and if human souls, have partaken of the same light and wisdom, and thus are mutually of one nature and of one essence,—then, since the heavenly virtues are incorruptible and immortal, the essence of the human soul will also be immortal and incorruptible.

— the same passage in Rufinus’s Latin, De Principiis IV.4.9 (ANF IV, IV.1.36), source.2

The question

The closing recapitulation of Peri Archōn survives in two Latin versions and not in Greek: in Jerome’s, “God and they are in some sort of one essence”; in Rufinus’s, the heavenly powers and human souls “are mutually of one nature and of one essence.”3 The question is whether Origen taught that rational creatures share the essence of God, as Theophilus of Alexandria (402) and Jerome (c. 409–410) charged, or a kinship by participation in which the creature receives from the Trinity what the Trinity is by nature; it bears on one of the charges brought against him in the controversy of 400–410.

Origen made the rational creature of one nature with God

On this reading Origen’s argument places angels and human souls in the same nature as the Trinity, so that Creator and creature become of one substance.

Held by: Theophilus of Alexandria (Seventeenth Festal Letter, 402); Jerome (Letter 124, c. 409–410); Adolf von Harnack (Lutheran, History of Dogma), on the logic of Origen’s system only.4

Theophilus argues from Origen’s teaching that the Saviour’s soul was “in the form of God and equal to God”: since “that which is equal to God must be of the same substance, we are led by the logic of the argument to believe that the soul and God are of a single nature. When he says this, it follows that he maintains that our souls, too, are not of a different nature from God, for there is no doubt that our souls and the Saviour’s soul are of one substance, so that consequently the Creator and the creature would be of one substance. And how were all things created through Christ, if human souls are of the same substance as the Creator?”5 Jerome, digesting Peri Archōn from the Greek for Avitus, reports that Origen “ends his work by maintaining that all reasonable beings, that is, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost, angels, powers, dominations, and virtues, and even man by right of his soul’s dignity, are of one and the same essence.” He reads the qualifier as a precaution: “He adds in some sort to escape the charge of blasphemy.” And he sets the sentence beside Origen’s refusal elsewhere to make the Son and the Holy Spirit of one substance with the Father “lest by so doing he should appear to make the divine essence divisible”: “he here bestows the nature of God almighty upon angels and men.”1 On this reading Jerome, translating from the Greek, keeps the full force of a sentence that Rufinus’s version confines to creatures, and the hedge “in some sort” shows Origen aware of what the sentence asserts. Harnack describes Origen’s rational creatures as “the series of ideas and spirits that having been created by the Son, are in truth the unfolding of his fulness,” and, while he records Origen’s “proposition that the creaturely spirit is in no sense a portion of the divine (because it is changeable),” he holds that within the system “the mere communication of the divine element to the created spirit is not a mere communication.”6 On this reading the “one essence” of the recapitulation states openly the kinship of nature between God and the rational creature that Origen’s account of minds sharing the divine light implies.

A kinship by participation, with the Trinity alone divine by nature

On this reading the “one essence” names a common rational kind, received by participation and in varying degree from a Trinity that alone possesses by nature what creatures receive.

Held by: E. V. Tkachev (Russian Orthodox, Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya vol. 53); Sara Contini (Open Theology, 2021); Manlio Simonetti and Samuel Fernández, as reported by Contini ⚠ unverified.7

In Rufinus’s Latin the recapitulation argues for the immortality of the soul: “because the nature of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, whose intellectual light alone all created things have a share, is incorruptible and eternal, it is altogether consistent and necessary that every substance which partakes of that eternal nature should last for ever,” and “a diversity is to be noted in the participation of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, varying with the degree of zeal or capacity of mind.”2 Two chapters earlier Origen writes that the Spirit’s grace is present “that those beings which are not holy in their essence may be rendered holy by participating in it,” and that rational beings “derive their existence from God the Father; secondly, their rational nature from the Word; thirdly, their holiness from the Holy Spirit.”8 Tkachev states that the first minds shared attributes God has by nature, but «Эта причастность тем не менее была акцидентальной, а не субстанциальной» (“this participation was nonetheless accidental, not substantial”), and that they «причастны Логосу, созданы посредством Него, не могут без Него существовать, но они не божественны по природе» (“participate in the Logos, are created through Him, cannot exist without Him, but they are not divine by nature”).9 Contini, reporting Fernández, notes that “the use of moderate expressions such as quodammodo, ‘in some way,’ is typical of Origen’s language when discussing the natural affinity or kinship between human and divine,” as in his Greek of the soul’s “certain kinship” with God; on Fernández’s reading the sentence Jerome selected, “when read correctly, in fact confirms that Origen maintained that there is some natural affinity between the reasonable soul and God, rather than fully achieved consubstantiality.” Simonetti, as she reports him, holds that “only God possesses Being in his own substance, while the creatures only receive their being from God.” Contini adds that Jerome elsewhere (Letter 126) distinguishes those who, like Origen, hold that souls fell from heaven from the Manichees, for whom souls are an emanation of God’s substance, and that Epiphanius does not bring this charge.3 Of Origen’s reserve over “of one essence” for the Son, Georges Florovsky gives the reason Jerome names: the expression seemed to him “too specifically material” and to introduce “into the Divine Being the idea of division.”10 On this reading the recapitulation affirms one rational kind, sharing in different degrees the light that the Trinity is, and Origen draws on that kinship to show that a mind able to receive God does not perish.
