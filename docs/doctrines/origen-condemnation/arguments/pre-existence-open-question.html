Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn (De Principiis), Preface and Book I, and the first of Justinian’s anathemas of 543

Related readings: Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? · Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds, and does it entail a second fall? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Did Origen teach reincarnation (the transmigration of souls)?

The passage

…the holy apostles, in preaching the faith of Christ, delivered themselves with the utmost clearness on certain points which they believed to be necessary to every one… while on other subjects they merely stated the fact that things were so, keeping silence as to the manner or origin of their existence… But with respect to the soul, whether it is derived from the seed by a process of traducianism, so that the reason or substance of it may be considered as placed in the seminal particles of the body themselves, or whether it has any other beginning; and this beginning, itself, whether it be by birth or not, or whether bestowed upon the body from without or no, is not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church.

— Origen, De Principiis, Preface 3 and 5 (c. 229), source.1

1. If anyone says or holds that the souls of human beings pre-exist, as previously minds and holy powers, but that they reached satiety with divine contemplation and turned to what is worse and for this reason grew old in the love of God and are therefore called souls, and were made to descend into bodies as a punishment, let him be anathema.

— Justinian, edict to Menas (543), anathema 1, tr. Richard Price, source.2

The question

In the preface to Peri Archōn Origen lists the origin of the soul among matters the Church’s teaching had not settled. Three hundred and fourteen years later Justinian’s edict anathematized the soul’s pre-existence together with its satiety, its turning to the worse and its descent into a body “as a punishment”, and the first of the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemned “the fabulous pre-existence of souls” and “the monstrous restoration which follows from it.”3 The question is whether Origen’s teaching was a permitted speculation on a question left open, so that the sixth-century sentence applied a later definition, or a teaching argued against from early on whose condemnation gave form to a judgement already made against it. Whether the system named in 543 and 553 is Origen’s own is a separate question, treated on its own page.

A permitted speculation on a question the Church had left open

On this reading Origen speculated where the rule of faith was silent, others of good standing treated the question as open for two centuries after him, and the anathema of 543 judged him by a rule made after his death.

Held by: Rufinus of Aquileia (Apology to Anastasius, 400); Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church, vol. II); Henri Crouzel (Catholic, Origène, 1985); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, “Saint Origen,” 2015).4

Origen’s preface sets the soul’s origin among the matters on which the apostles “merely stated the fact that things were so.”1 When he reaches the fall of the rational natures he writes that these points are “treated by us with great solicitude and caution, in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than in that of fixed and certain decision”, and warns that a reader full of preconceptions “may judge these to be heretical and opposed to the faith of the Church, yielding in so doing not so much to the convictions of reason as to the dogmatism of prejudice.”5 In 400 Rufinus, clearing himself before Pope Anastasius I on the charge of Origenism, set out three accounts of the soul’s origin, one of them “the opinion of Origen”, and wrote: “I declare in the presence of God that, after reading each of these opinions, I am up to the present moment unable to hold any of them as certain and absolute.”6 In 409 Synesius of Cyrene, invited to the see of Ptolemais, wrote that “I can never persuade myself that the soul is of more recent origin than the body”;7 he was consecrated in 410 by Theophilus of Alexandria, who had convened the synod of 400 against Origen’s books.8 In 415 Augustine, writing to Jerome, listed among four undecided accounts of the soul’s incarnation “that souls already in existence somewhere are sent by divine act into the bodies” or “glide into them of their own accord”, and set aside as “this heretical error” only a fifth, that the soul is part of God.9 Schaff states the principle the reading applies: “Speculations on open questions of theology are no heresies”; “Origen was no heretic in his age, but was condemned long after his death.” Hart judges that to declare a man a heretic “three centuries after his having died in the peace of the church, in respect of doctrinal determinations not reached during his life, was a gross violation of all legitimate canonical order.”4 On this reading the sixth-century anathema defined a question that the third century, and the generations after it, had held open.

Not an open question: Origen taught a pre-cosmic fall of minds into bodies, opposed from Methodius onward

On this reading what Origen advanced was not bare pre-existence but a pre-cosmic fall of minds into penal bodies, a doctrine opposed by Methodius and by Augustine before the anathemas of 543 and 553 condemned it.

Held by: Methodius of Olympus (On the Resurrection, c. 300); Augustine of Hippo (City of God XI.23); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”).10

The Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya records that accusations of heresy were brought against Origen in his lifetime and that he had to defend himself.11 Pamphilus’s Apology, written in prison around 307–310, answers nine charges then pressed against Origen, among them that his account of the soul is erroneous and that he teaches transmigration into animals.12 Methodius reports that Origen “said that the body was given to the soul as a fetter after the fall, and that previously it lived without a body”, and answers that man is by nature neither soul without body nor body without soul.13 Florovsky records that Gregory of Nyssa “denies the pre-existence and transmigration of souls”, and Ware that Gregory “abandoned Origen’s speculations concerning preexistence and the precosmic fall”.10 Augustine writes that “Origen is justly blamed for holding this opinion”, that souls “merited different degrees of debasement from heaven to earth, and diverse bodies as prison-houses”, and sets against it Genesis’s refrain that God saw each work was good.14 The reading distinguishes bare pre-existence from the fall: Origen argues that Jacob was preferred to Esau in the womb “according to the deserts of his previous life”,15 and Jerome, translating the Greek in 409–410, describes Origen treating the “lapse into earthly bodies as due to their own negligence” of rational creatures and quotes the conclusion that “it was through their own fault that others fell from holiness.”16 Justinian’s anathema names that whole chain.2 Ware reads the first anathema of 553 as directed “against his theory of preexistent souls, of a precosmic fall and a final apocatastasis—seen as a single and undivided whole”, and Florovsky writes that “The prohibitions of the Fifth Ecumenical Council signified a judgment over the mistakes of the past.”10 On this reading the fall of the minds was Origen’s own teaching, distinct from bare pre-existence, and Methodius, Augustine and the sixth-century anathemas each answered that same doctrine.
