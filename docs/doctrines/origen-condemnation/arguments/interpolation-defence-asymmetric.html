Was the plea of heretical interpolation applied alike to Gregory of Nyssa and to Origen?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Rufinus, On the Adulteration of the Works of Origen (397), and Germanus of Constantinople, Antapodotikos (early eighth century), as summarized by Photius

Related readings: Were the views condemned in Origen also held, uncondemned, by others? · Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Were Origen’s heterodox passages heretical interpolations?

The passage

… those who held that the devils and lost souls would one day be set free had dared “to instill into the pure and most healthful spring of his [Gregory’s] writings the black and dangerous poison of the error of Origen, and to cunningly attribute this foolish heresy to a man famous alike for his virtue and his learning.”

— Germanus of Constantinople, Antapodotikos, as reported by Photius, Bibliotheca cod. 233, and quoted in the Catholic Encyclopedia, source.1

It must, I think, be felt to be wholly impossible that a man so learned and so wise, a man whom even his accusers may well admit to have been neither foolish nor insane, should have written what is contrary and repugnant to himself and his own opinions.

— Rufinus, On the Adulteration of the Works of Origen (397), source.2

The question

Gregory of Nyssa and Origen both taught a final restoration of all, citing 1 Corinthians 15:28; Origen was anathematized by name in 553, and Gregory was not condemned. Rufinus argued in 397 that the heterodox passages in Origen were heretics’ interpolations; Germanus argued in the eighth century that the restoration passages in Gregory were Origenists’ interpolations. The question is how the two pleas, and the different treatment of the two authors, are to be read: as one principle applied to both, or as a contrast explained by the texts or by the standing of the men.

Heretics corrupted the works of revered writers

On this reading the offending passages in both authors are interpolations, and the plea is one principle applied to any revered writer whose text contradicts his known faith.

Held by: Rufinus (on Origen, 397); Germanus of Constantinople (on Gregory, early eighth century); Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 233, ninth century); Tillemont (on Gregory, 1703), as reported by Batiffol.3

Rufinus held it “wholly impossible” that Origen wrote against his own opinions, and grounded the plea in a general practice: finding in renowned writers of old a full and faithful treatment of the things of God, heretics “have not scrupled to infuse into their writings the poisonous taint of their own false doctrines; this they have done, either by inserting things which the writers had not said or by changing by interpolation what they had said.”2 Germanus applied the same principle to Gregory. Photius, summarizing the lost treatise, gives its aim: “to demonstrate that St. Gregory of Nyssa and his writings are free of any taint of Origenism”; the Origenists “sometimes by faked additions, sometimes by their relentless efforts to pervert correct thinking… have attempted to falsify many of his works which were beyond reproach.”4 A gloss in a manuscript of Gregory’s On the Soul and the Resurrection records the method: “Blessed Germanus expunged as spurious a part of the preceding words, marking them with an obelos.”5 Jean Daniélou reports that this opinion became traditional in the Greek church and appears in Nicephorus Callistus’s notice on Gregory, and that among modern writers Vincenzi defended the interpolation thesis; Batiffol reports that Tillemont “inclines to the opinion that St. Germanus had good grounds for what he said.”6 Batiffol accounts for the suspicion from Gregory’s own text: in some places the suffering of the lost is called “eternal, aionia”, in others evil is annihilated after long periods, and “these verbal contradictions explain why the defenders of orthodoxy should have thought that St. Gregory of Nyssa’s writings had been tampered with by heretics.”1 On this reading Rufinus and Germanus made one argument, from an author’s known faith to the corruption of passages that contradict it.

Gregory’s restoration is his own, and differs from Origen’s

On this reading Gregory wrote the restoration passages, but they teach something other than the doctrine condemned in Origen.

Held by: Maximus the Confessor (Quaestiones et dubia 19, seventh century), as reported by Andreopoulos; Jean Daniélou (Jesuit, Recherches de Science Religieuse, 1940); Andreas Andreopoulos (The Oxford Handbook of Maximus the Confessor); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 2001), on the doctrinal difference.7

Maximus distinguishes several senses of apokatastasis and, Andreopoulos writes, “interprets the concept of restoration as he finds it in Gregory of Nyssa, as the restoration of human natural will.”8 Daniélou, examining the manuscript evidence for a disputed passage of the Life of Moses, concludes that it rules out “l’hypothèse facile de la falsification”, and that other passages on restoration are so woven into Gregory’s writings “que l’authenticité n’en peut pas faire question”; he holds that the accusation of Origenism is equally without foundation, and that Maximus “a vu clair dans la pensée de son maître” (saw clearly into his master’s thought). For Gregory, he writes, the restoration underlines the definitive character of the Incarnation and the social character of salvation.9 Ware writes that Gregory “abandoned Origen’s speculations concerning preexistence and the precosmic fall… while holding fast to his belief in an ultimate restoration; and, significantly, he has never been anathematized for this, either in 553 or in more recent times”, and that if he was treated differently from Origen, “perhaps it was because, while retaining Origen’s hope in the eventual triumph of good over evil, he abandoned the notion of preexistence and so avoided the circularity of the Origenist scheme.”10 On this reading the pleas were not applied alike because the cases were not alike: Gregory’s texts were genuine and read in a sense the Church could receive, while the condemned system was the one built on pre-existence and the precosmic fall.

Gregory’s restoration is authentic; he was spared for his standing

On this reading Gregory taught the restoration as Origen had, his texts are genuine, and he escaped condemnation for reasons of authority and reputation.

Held by: J. H. Srawley (Anglican, 1903); Pierre Batiffol (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907), with Bardenhewer and Petavius, on the authenticity of the texts; Ilaria Ramelli (Studia UBB Philosophia, 2022).11

Srawley: “the idea of a universal restoration occurs too frequently in Gregory’s writings to be disposed of by a theory of interpolation, which further receives no support from any change of style.” Batiffol: “We must, however, admit, with Bardenhewer… that the explanation given by St. Germanus of Constantinople cannot hold. This was, also, the opinion of Petavius.”12 Ramelli writes that Gregory’s doctrine “was explained away by Germanus I… as a series of interpolations by heretics, so as to avoid attributing this doctrine to Gregory,” partly because Germanus took Gregory’s “aeonian” to mean “eternal”; and that in the manuscripts “textual variants omitting all references to the doctrine of apokatastasis testify to the embarrassment of later readers before St Gregory’s theory.”5 Among her reasons why Gregory was never condemned are factors “of ‘political’ or ‘church-political’ nature”: his exile under the “Arian” party, his part in the Council of Constantinople (381) and his proclamation as a touchstone of orthodoxy; and that “Gregory—unlike Origen—was a saint himself… and was the brother and son of saints, and the friend of saints. This is probably another reason why Gregory was not condemned.” She reads Maximus as endorsing Gregory’s restoration: “Particularly the form of apokatastasis supported by St Gregory was overtly endorsed by St Maximus.”13 On this reading the same doctrine, from the same verse, met different verdicts because of who held it.
