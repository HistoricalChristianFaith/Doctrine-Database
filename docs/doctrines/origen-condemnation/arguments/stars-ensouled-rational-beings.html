Did Origen teach that the sun, moon and stars are rational beings whose bodies answer to a fall, as the anathemas of 543 and 553 state?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn I.7 and Against Celsus V; the charge as pressed by Justinian

Related readings: Did Origen teach that the stars cause events? · Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen?

The passage

But to arrive at a clearer understanding on these matters, we ought first to inquire after this point, whether it is allowable to suppose that they are living and rational beings; then, in the next place, whether their souls came into existence at the same time with their bodies, or seem to be anterior to them; and also whether, after the end of the world, we are to understand that they are to be released from their bodies; and whether, as we cease to live, so they also will cease from illuminating the world. Although this inquiry may seem to be somewhat bold, yet, as we are incited by the desire of ascertaining the truth as far as possible, there seems no absurdity in attempting an investigation of the subject agreeably to the grace of the Holy Spirit. We think, then, that they may be designated as living beings, for this reason, that they are said to receive commandments from God, which is ordinarily the case only with rational beings.

— Origen, De Principiis I.7.3 (Rufinus’s Latin, ANF IV).1

The question

In 543 the sixth anathema of Justinian’s edict to Menas condemned anyone who “says or holds that heaven, sun, moon, stars, and the waters above the heavens are ensouled and rational powers”;2 in 553 the third of the fifteen anathemas condemned the view “that the sun, the moon and the stars are also reasonable beings, and that they have only become what they are because they turned towards evil.”3 That Origen held the luminaries to be living and rational, and their souls older than their bodies, is reported by both readings below. What is disputed is the second clause and the standing of the teaching: whether Origen taught, as doctrine, that the luminaries’ bodies answer to a fall, or proposed their animation as an inquiry and held their embodiment to be a service laid on them. The wider history of the belief is traced on the living-heavens timeline.

Rational luminaries whose bodies answer to their merits, as Origen taught

On this reading Origen taught that the sun, moon and stars are rational souls set in bodies of greater or lesser brightness according to their deserts, as human souls are, so that the anathemas report his doctrine.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 124, c. 409–410); Justinian (edict to Menas, 543); Photius (Bibliotheca, cod. 8); F. Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, Catholic, 1911).4

Jerome, translating Peri Archōn for Avitus, sums up: “The sun also and the moon and the rest of the constellations are alive. Nay more; as we men by reason of our sins are enveloped in bodies material and sluggish; so the lights of heaven have for like reasons received bodies more or less luminous, and demons have been for more serious faults clothed with starry frames,” and quotes Origen that “the soul of the sun… already existed before it entered its shining and glowing body.”5 Origen’s own chapter ties the stars’ brightness to moral effort: Job’s “The stars also are not clean in Thy sight” shows that “not only may the stars be subject to sin, but even that they are actually not clean from the contagion of it,” for “if they are unable, through their own diligent efforts, either to acquire for themselves a body of greater brightness, or through their sloth to make the one they have less pure, how should they incur censure for being stars that are not clean”; and “if the stars are living and rational beings, there will undoubtedly appear among them both an advance and a falling back.”6 Their souls were not formed with their bodies: “I, for my part, suspect that the spirit was implanted in them from without.”7 In Against Celsus he writes that their light is derived “perhaps from the principle of free-will within them.”8 Photius, reading the first book of Peri Archōn, lists among its statements “frivolous talk about the migration of souls, the stars being alive, and the like.”9 Prat places the stars among Origen’s four classes of created intellects, all created “equal and alike,” whose “present differences arise solely from their different use of the gift of free will”: the spirits “fell, some more some less,” and “Hence the hierarchy of the angels; hence also the four categories of created intellects: angels, stars (supposing, as is probable, that they are animated…), men, and demons.”10 On this reading the luminaries take their place in Origen’s graded cosmos by the same rule as every rational being, and the anathemas state that rule.

Rational luminaries subjected to bodies for a service, in an inquiry Origen called bold

On this reading Origen proposed that the luminaries are rational, and held that they bear their bodies unwillingly, for the sake of others, until their work is done, not as a punishment for turning to evil.

Held by: E. V. Tkachev (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53); Mark J. Edwards (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2014, rev. 2026); Ilaria Ramelli (Brill Encyclopedia of Early Christianity Online).11

Origen opens the chapter as an inquiry that “may seem to be somewhat bold,” pursued “agreeably to the grace of the Holy Spirit,” and the preceding chapter’s matters were treated “more by way of intelligent inference than strict dogmatic definition.”12 Reading Romans 8:20, he says the sun, moon and stars “are said to be subject to vanity, because they are clothed with bodies, and set apart to the office of giving light to the human race”: “it did not undertake a voluntary service to vanity, but because it was the will of Him who made it subject,” with the promise “that when the ministry of their great work was performed, they were to be freed from this bondage.”13 An extract Jerome gives in Origen’s words puts the luminaries among the “ministers to ensure the salvation and correction of the fallen,” made subject “not willingly but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope.”14 In Against Celsus the luminaries are worshippers: “the sun himself, and moon, and stars pray to the Supreme God through His only-begotten Son.”8 Tkachev reads Origen as holding that some creatures entered the fallen state of the world “не в силу своей вины и не в качестве наказания, а для того, чтобы помочь другим” (not by their own fault and not as a punishment, but to help others), among them “небесные светила (планеты, звезды)” (the heavenly luminaries, planets and stars), and calls the teaching on the luminaries marked by “нек-рой неопределенностью” (a certain indeterminacy).15 Edwards writes that the souls of the elements “are here for our sake rather than to expiate our sin,” and that Job’s “the stars are not clean in his sight” means “that the sun and moon were made ‘subject to vanity’ for the sake of those who inhabit the physical cosmos.”16 Ramelli holds that Justinian “was not acquainted with Origen’s authentic thought” and that the anathemas on the souls’ “union with bodies only after their fall” “have nothing to do with Origen.”17 On this reading the luminaries are servants of the world’s light, awaiting their release with the rest of creation.
