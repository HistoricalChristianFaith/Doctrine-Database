Does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Commentary on John II, On Prayer 15, Against Celsus, Peri Archōn I

Related readings: Did Origen’s reading of the two seraphim of Isaiah 6 as the Son and the Holy Spirit rank them among ministering creatures? · Did Origen teach that the Son’s kingdom will come to an end? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime?

The passage

God on the one hand is Very God (Autotheos, God of Himself)… but… all beyond the Very God is made God by participation in His divinity, and is not to be called simply God (with the article), but rather God (without article). And thus the first-born of all creation, who is the first to be with God, and to attract to Himself divinity, is a being of more exalted rank than the other gods beside Him.

— Origen, Commentary on John II.2 (c. 231).1 In On Prayer prayer “in its full meaning” is owed to the Father alone, “not even to Christ himself”;2 in Against Celsus the Son is a “second God”;3 and Jerome’s rendering of the lost Greek of Peri Archōn reads: “The Son is inferior to the Father, inasmuch as He is second and the Father first; and the Holy Spirit which dwells in all the saints is inferior to the Son.”4

The question

That Origen ranks the Son below the Father, and the Spirit below the Son, is common ground to both readings. The question is how that ranking stands to the debates of the fourth century: whether it is the shared idiom of the theologians before Nicaea, held together with an eternal generation of the Son that Nicene writers cited, or an ontological subordination from whose premises the Arian position was drawn. Both the Arian and the Nicene parties of the fourth century appealed to Origen.5

The common pre-Nicene idiom, joined to an eternal generation of the Son

On this reading Origen’s ranking of the persons is the ordinary language of the third century about order and origin, and his teaching that the Son was eternally begotten was cited by Nicene writers against the Arians.

Held by: Athanasius (De Decretis 27, c. 350s); F. Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, Catholic, 1911); Ilaria Ramelli (Vigiliae Christianae, 2011); Mark J. Edwards (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2014, rev. 2026).6

In Peri Archōn the Son’s “generation is as eternal and everlasting as the brilliancy which is produced from the sun.”7 Athanasius, defending the Council of Nicaea, adduced Origen, setting aside “what he has written as if inquiring and by way of exercise” and quoting what he “definitely declares”: “as being the likeness of the Father, never was it not… let him understand well who dares to say, ‘Once the Son was not,’ that he is saying, ‘Once Wisdom was not.’”8 Prat holds that “the language of theology was not yet fixed” and that the subordination “so much urged against Origen, generally consists in differences of appropriation”, in “the liturgical practice of praying the Father through the Son in the Holy Ghost,” or in the theory “so widespread in the Greek Church of the first five centuries, that the Father has a pre-eminence of rank (taxis)”; “That is why St. Athanasius defends Origen’s orthodoxy concerning the Trinity and why St. Basil and St. Gregory of Nazianzus replied to the heretics who claimed the support of his authority that they misunderstood him.”9 Edwards writes that to say the Son shares the Father’s nature “does not preclude the Son’s subordination to the Father, and we have little evidence that Christians in the time of Origen shrank from this position; if anything, they were more afraid of assertions of coeternity or equality,” and that until the late fourth century “Origen’s doctrine of the Trinity was less often cited with censure than with approval by champions of the Nicene Creed.”10 Ramelli argues that “Origen was not the forerunner of ‘Arianism,’” but “the main inspirer of the Cappadocians, especially Nyssen, in what became Trinitarian orthodoxy,” and that Gregory of Nyssa’s anti-subordinationist treatise on 1 Corinthians 15:28 derives its arguments from Origen.11 The same works affirm the Son’s worship: “We worship one God, the Father and the Son,” to whom the right to be honoured was given “that all may honour the Son, even as they honour the Father.”12 On this reading Origen’s ranking and his eternal generation belong to one third-century account of the Father as source, which fourth-century parties then quoted in part.

An ontological subordination, from whose premises the Arian position was drawn

On this reading Origen ranks the Son and the Spirit below the Father in being and power, not only in order, and the Arian position took its starting points from that ranking.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus II.12; Letter 124, c. 409–410); Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church II); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Eastern Fathers of the Fourth Century); A. R. Fokin (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Russian Orthodox, vol. 53).13

Jerome lists among Origen’s teachings “that the Son of God is a creature, that the Holy Spirit is a servant.”14 The Commentary on John makes the Spirit one of the things made through the Word: “the Spirit also was made through the Word, and is seen to be one of the ‘all things’ which are inferior to their Maker. This view is too firmly settled to be disturbed.”15 Against those who “incautiously assert that the Saviour is the Most High God,” Origen writes, “we do not hold with them, but rather believe Him when He says, ‘The Father who sent Me is greater than I.’”16 Fokin calls Origen’s doctrine of the Son “одна из форм раннехрист. субординационизма” (one of the forms of early Christian subordinationism): the Son, God “по причастию” (by participation), is subordinate “в божестве, могуществе, воле, знании, благости, почитании” (in divinity, might, will, knowledge, goodness, honour); the unity of Father and Son is one of will, not of essence, and Origen never applies homoousios to them. Of two modern interpretations, ontological and relational subordinationism, he reports that “Большинство исследователей склоняются к 1-й” (most researchers incline to the first).17 Schaff writes that Origen “makes the Son decidedly inferior to the Father,” speaks “of a difference of substance,” and that “this subordination of the Son formed a stepping-stone to Arianism.”18 Florovsky holds that “the problems of Arian theology can be understood only in terms of the premises of Origen’s system”: Arius “differed from Origen in his conclusions but agreed with him in his premises,” and the Eusebians “remained firm supporters of Origen and his dogma of subordinationism.”5 On this reading the gradation of Father, Son and Spirit in being, power and knowledge is a structure of Origen’s system, and the fourth-century parties took up its premises.
