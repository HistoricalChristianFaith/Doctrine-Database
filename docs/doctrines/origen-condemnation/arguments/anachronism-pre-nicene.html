Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn, Preface

Related readings: Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it? · Was Origen’s subordination of the Son pre-Nicene idiom, or a teaching of the kind later condemned in Arius? · Did Origen teach universal restoration as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day? · Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh? · Was Demetrius’s sentence disciplinary only? · Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? · Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine? · Were the views condemned in Origen also held, uncondemned, by others?

The passage

The holy apostles, in preaching the faith of Christ, delivered themselves with the utmost clearness on certain points which they believed to be necessary to every one, even to those who seemed somewhat dull in the investigation of divine knowledge; leaving, however, the grounds of their statements to be examined into by those who should deserve the excellent gifts of the Spirit… On other subjects, however, they merely stated the fact that things were so, keeping silence as to the manner or origin of their existence; clearly in order that the more zealous of their successors… might have a subject of exercise on which to display the fruit of their talents.

— Origen, De Principiis, Preface 3 (c. 229), source.1

The question

Origen’s preface sets out what the apostolic teaching had defined and what it had left for inquiry. Among the settled points it lists that the soul will be rewarded after death with “eternal life and blessedness” or “eternal fire and punishments,” and that “there is to be a time of resurrection from the dead”; among the open ones, the origin of the soul, “not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church,” the nature of the devil and his angels, and “what existed before this world, or what will exist after it.”2 The sentences passed on Origen’s teaching in 400, 543 and 553 fall in large part on the pre-existence of souls, the resurrection body, the end of punishment and the restoration of all. The question is whether those sentences applied a faith already received in Origen’s day, or judged him by definitions reached after his death.

The condemned doctrines departed from a faith already received

On this reading Origen’s condemned opinions were novelties against the faith handed down, and the later sentences named an error that was an error when he wrote.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 84, 399); Vincent of Lérins (Commonitorium, 434); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, on the derivation of later errors from Origen’s premises).3

Jerome, who had read and translated Origen, writes to Rome in 399: “His doctrines are poisonous, they are unknown to the Holy Scriptures, nay more, they do them violence,” and asks, “Why after four hundred years do you try to teach us Romans doctrines of which until now we have known nothing? Why do you publicly proclaim opinions which Peter and Paul refused to profess?”4 The measure on this reading is the apostolic faith, not a later council. Vincent of Lérins, writing in 434, makes Origen his chief example of a teacher’s error becoming the people’s trial: Origen, “despising the traditions of the Church and the determinations of the ancients, and interpreting certain passages of Scripture in a novel way,” led many from “the old religion to a new profaneness.” Vincent allows growth in the Church’s understanding, but “real progress, not alteration of the faith,” growth “in the same doctrine, in the same sense, and in the same meaning.”5 A later definition on this account states what was held from the beginning, and the interval before it bears on a teacher’s culpability, not on the standing of his propositions. Several of the charges met argument in Origen’s own century and the next: Methodius of Olympus, around 300, wrote against his account of the risen body and the pre-existence of souls, and Augustine argued against the pre-cosmic fall from Genesis, neither waiting on a council.6 Florovsky holds that “Origen was not an Arian but it is easy to see how the Arians reached their conclusions not merely from misunderstandings of his teachings but from his actual premises.”3 On this reading the later anathemas named, in later language, consequences that were present in Origen’s own premises.

Origen inquired where the rule of faith of his day was open

On this reading Origen explored questions his age had not settled, in good faith and as inquiry, and the sentences of later centuries judged him by definitions reached after him.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church II, 1858–1890); F. Prat (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Henri Crouzel (Catholic, 1988); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, “Saint Origen,” 2015).7

Origen’s preface draws the line itself, and when he reaches the disputed matters he says he treats them “in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than in that of fixed and certain decision,” warning that a reader “full of preconceptions and prejudices” may “judge these to be heretical and opposed to the faith of the Church.”8 Schaff writes that “such a man might in such an age hold erroneous opinions without being a heretic. For Origen propounded his views always with modesty and from sincere conviction of their agreement with Scripture, and that in a time when the church doctrine was as yet very indefinite in many points.” Prat: “his disposition of mind is essentially Catholic and he does not deserve to be ranked among the promoters of heresy.”9 Crouzel describes the purpose stated in the preface as “to offer a theology in research, not a complete system,” and holds that “it is not possible to confound late Origenism with the thought of Origen himself.”10 Hart puts the canonical point: “to have declared any man a heretic three centuries after his having died in the peace of the church, in respect of doctrinal determinations not reached during his life, was a gross violation of all legitimate canonical order.”11 Holders of this reading point to teachers kept in honour who held views later set aside: Irenaeus and Papias taught an earthly millennium; Tertullian, whom Cyprian called “the master,” held the soul corporeal; Gregory of Nyssa taught the restoration of all from the same verse, “that God may be all in all.”12 Within a century of Origen’s death Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus compiled the Philocalia, an anthology of his writings.13 On this reading the tradition itself distinguished, in practice, between a teacher who inquired before the definitions and the propositions later pinned to his name.
