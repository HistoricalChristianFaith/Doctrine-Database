Was Origen himself anathematized by an ecumenical council?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: the Second Council of Constantinople (553); see also Justinian

Related readings: Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? (what the anathemas describe, rather than who passed them) · Did Anastasius I’s sentence of 400–401 condemn Origen’s books only, or Origen himself? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime?

The passage

If anyone does not anathematize Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen, as well as their heretical books, and also all other heretics who have already been condemned and anathematized by the holy, catholic and apostolic church and by the four holy synods which have already been mentioned, and also all those who have thought or now think in the same way as the aforesaid heretics and who persist in their error even to death: let him be anathema.

— the Second Council of Constantinople (553), anathema 11, approved at the eighth session (2 June 553), in Norman P. Tanner’s edition, source.1

Our edition does not include the text of the anathemas against Origen since recent studies have shown that these anathemas cannot be attributed to this council.

— Tanner’s editorial introduction to the same council, on the fifteen anathemas against Origen, source.1

The question

The council of 553 was summoned over the Three Chapters; the acts of its eight sessions contain no discussion of Origen, while its eleventh anathema names him, and a separate set of fifteen anathemas against Origenist teaching, which name no author, has come down under the council’s name.2 The question is whether the bishops of the Fifth Ecumenical Council themselves anathematized Origen by name, or whether his personal condemnation came from outside the council’s own acts and was later attached to them; it bears on what conciliar standing the sentence on Origen has.

The bishops of the council condemned Origen by name

On this reading the bishops assembled for the council anathematized Origen, both in the fifteen anathemas they approved before the formal sessions and in the council’s eleventh canon, and later councils received this as the act of the fifth synod.

Held by: Cyril of Scythopolis (Life of Sabas, c. 557); Franz Diekamp (Catholic, Die origenistischen Streitigkeiten, 1899); Adolf von Harnack (Lutheran, History of Dogma IV, Eng. tr. 1898); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century).3

Cyril of Scythopolis, a monk of the Palestinian lauras writing shortly after the council, records: “When the fifth holy ecumenical council had assembled at Constantinople a common and universal anathema was directed against Origen and Theodore of Mopsuestia and against the teaching of Evagrius and Didymus on pre-existence and a universal restoration, in the presence and with the approval of the four patriarchs.”4 Evagrius Scholasticus (593/4) reports that Justinian put the monks’ depositions to “the assembled synod”, which replied “after it had made acclamations against Origen and his companions in error.” Richard Price, the acts’ modern translator, holds that the second set of anathemas “needs a context of its own, and the council of 553 provides one”: a meeting of the bishops assembled for the council before its formal opening, the solution first proposed by Diekamp. Price adds that “Origen’s name was included among the heretics anathematized in the eleventh of the canons formally approved at the end of the council.”4 Diekamp rejected “mit aller Bestimmtheit” (with all certainty) the view that Origen’s name in that canon is a forgery: every manuscript contains it, the Lateran council of 649 cited the fourteen anathemas, and the renewed complaints against the Origenists between Justinian’s confession of 551 and the council gave the synod reason to extend its list to Origen.5 Florovsky writes that Origen’s condemnation “was proclaimed by the fathers who had convened earlier than the official opening of the Council. That is why nothing is said about it in the Council ‘acts.’ However, it is included in the Councils anathemas (Anathema 11).”6 The Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya adds that the anathemas also survive in the original Greek with Origen’s name, and that the fifth session already names him as a heretic condemned after death.7 Harnack summarizes: “It condemned Origen, as Justinian desired.”8 The next ecumenical councils received the sentence as the fifth synod’s: Constantinople III (680–681) names “the fifth holy synod… which was gathered here against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius,” and Nicaea II (787) anathematizes “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus, as did the fifth synod.”9

Origen’s personal condemnation is not an act of the council

On this reading the fifteen anathemas were an extra-conciliar sentence, and on several accounts Origen’s name in the eleventh canon is a later addition, so that the council in its own acts did not anathematize him.

Held by: Henri Crouzel (Catholic, 1986; ⚠ unverified, as reported by Ramelli); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, “Saint Origen,” 2015); Ilaria L. E. Ramelli (Brill Encyclopedia of Early Christianity, 2019).10

Prat, writing on the fifteen, holds it “certain that the fifth general council was convoked exclusively to deal with the affair of the Three Chapters,” that in its eight sessions “only the question of the Three Chapters is treated,” that only those acts were submitted to Pope Vigilius for approval, and that Popes Vigilius, Pelagius I, Pelagius II and Gregory the Great, writing of the fifth council, “make no mention of Origenism.” The bishops subscribed the fifteen before the opening, but “there is no proof that the approbation of the pope… was asked”; “It is easy to understand how this extra-conciliary sentence was mistaken at a later period for a decree of the actual ecumenical council.”11 The fifteen survive in a Vienna manuscript found by Peter Lambeck in the seventeenth century under the heading “Canons, of the 165 holy Fathers of the holy fifth Synod”, a caption that Walch, Döllinger and Hefele, as Henry Percival reports, looked upon as untrustworthy; Tanner’s edition omits them.12 On the eleventh canon, Ramelli notes that Origen stands last, “notably out of chronological order”, and that the same list in Justinian’s Homonoia “does not include the name of Origen. This suggests an interpolation.” She adds that “Vigilius’ documents… do not contain Origen’s name,” that “the original Greek text of the acts of this council is lost,” and that suspicions of interpolation in the Greek acts were raised “already in 680 CE”; she reports Henri Crouzel’s conclusion that “Origen in fact was never officially condemned by the church.”13 Victor of Tunnuna, as Price notes, attributes to Eutychius of Constantinople a condemnation of Didymus and Evagrius but not of Origen.14 Hart holds that the oldest records show the fifteen “were never even discussed by the assembled bishops, let alone ratified, published, or promulgated,” and that “it was only well after the Fifth Ecumenical Council’s close that they were attached to its canons, and Origen’s name inserted into its list of condemned heretics.”15 On this reading the sentence against Origen came from Justinian’s initiative, first in the edict and canons he issued in 543 and then in the fifteen put to the bishops before the council, and passed into the council’s record only afterwards.16
