Was Demetrius’s sentence (c. 231–232) disciplinary only, or did it also concern Origen’s teaching?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: the Alexandrian synods of c. 231–232 under Demetrius, as reported by Eusebius, by Pamphilus (through Photius) and by Jerome

Related readings: Did Origen offer incense under persecution, as Epiphanius reports? · Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine?

The passage

He stands condemned by his bishop, Demetrius, only the bishops of Palestine, Arabia, Phenicia, and Achaia dissenting. Imperial Rome consents to his condemnation, and even convenes a senate to censure him, not—as the rabid hounds who now pursue him cry—because of the novelty or heterodoxy of his doctrines, but because men could not tolerate the incomparable eloquence and knowledge which, when once he opened his lips, made others seem dumb.

— Jerome, Letter 33.4, to Paula (c. 384), source.1

According to Pamphilus, it was decided that he must not remain in Alexandria or teach there, but that he should be allowed to retain his priesthood. But Demetrius and some Egyptian bishops, with the assent of those who had formerly supported him, also deprived him of his sacred office.

— Photius, Bibliotheca cod. 118, source.2

The question

The record of the Alexandrian proceedings against Origen comes from Eusebius, from the lost Apology for Origen of Pamphilus and Eusebius as Photius summarized it, and from Jerome; Demetrius’s own circular letter is lost. The question is whether the sentence was passed on Origen’s ordination and standing alone or also on his teaching, which bears on how early his doctrine was formally judged. The written canons on ordaining another bishop’s cleric are those of Nicaea (325) and Antioch (341).3 A further account, which ties the expulsion to the lapse under persecution that Epiphanius reports, is set out on the incense page.4

A sentence on ordination and standing

On this reading the synods judged an irregular ordination and the standing of a presbyter, and the sentence named no doctrine.

Held by: Eusebius (Church History VI, early fourth century); Jerome (Letter 33, c. 384); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); E. A. Zabolotny (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Orthodox).5

Eusebius reports that Origen, travelling to Greece on church business, “went through Palestine, and was ordained as presbyter in Caesarea by the bishops of that country”, and speaks of “the matters that were agitated concerning him on this account.”6 Behind it lay an older quarrel: when Origen had preached as a layman in Palestine, Demetrius protested “that such a thing was never heard of before… that laymen should preach in the presence of bishops”, and the Palestinian bishops answered with precedents.7 The terms of the two sittings in Photius’s summary — residence, licence to teach, priestly rank — are terms of status. The one further charge Eusebius names is the youthful self-castration, which Demetrius “wrote of … as most foolish to the bishops throughout the world”, having “nothing else that he could say against him”; Jerome’s Lives says only that Origen “offended Demetrius, who was so wildly enraged at him that he wrote everywhere to injure his reputation.”8 The bishops who had ordained him kept him at work for the rest of his life, “as their only teacher”, and Firmilian of Cappadocia called him into his province “for the benefit of the churches.”9 Photius, who elsewhere calls Origen’s writings abominable, transmits this account of the synods without adding a doctrinal count.10 Prat summarizes: Demetrius “was very much offended by this ordination, which had taken place without his knowledge and, as he thought, in derogation of his rights”, and “St. Jerome declares expressly that he was not condemned on a point of doctrine.” Zabolotny writes that Eusebius ties the conflict to questions of discipline, not of doctrine, and that the sources record no canonical penalties laid on Origen for his theological views.11 On this reading the first condemnation is a judgement on a presbyter’s ordination and conduct, received in Egypt and declined in the provinces where he went on teaching.

Doctrine was among the grounds

On this reading the irregular ordination was the occasion, and accusations about Origen’s teaching also entered the sentence or the campaign that accompanied it.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church II); Adolf Harnack (Lutheran, Encyclopædia Britannica, 1911); Tadros Y. Malaty (Coptic Orthodox, The School of Alexandria, 1995); A. V. Seryogin (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Orthodox, who does not exclude doctrinal causes).12

Origen’s own Letter to Friends at Alexandria, quoted by Jerome, was written against Demetrius and “to inveigh against the bishops throughout the world, and to tell them that their excommunication of him is invalid”; in it he answers those who “ascribe to us and our teaching the blasphemy… that the father of wickedness and perdition… will be saved; a thing which not even a madman can say.”13 At the moment of the rupture, then, a doctrinal charge was circulating in Alexandria. Eusebius records that Origen later “wrote also to Fabianus, bishop of Rome, and to many other rulers of the churches concerning his orthodoxy”, and Jerome, who had seen that letter, says Origen there “expresses penitence for having made erroneous statements.”14 Eusebius declines to narrate the bishops’ decisions, which “demand a separate treatise”, and that treatise is lost.6 Schaff writes that Demetrius “charged him besides, and that not wholly without foundation, with corrupting Christianity by foreign speculations”, and that the councils enacted “that he, for his false doctrine, his self-mutilation, and his violation of the church laws, be deposed.” McGiffert: “The two grounds of the sentence passed by these synods were plainly his irregular ordination to the priesthood when constitutionally unfit for it… and his heterodoxy.” Harnack: the decision “seems to have been justified by referring to the self-mutilation of Origen and adducing objectionable doctrines which he was said to have promulgated.”15 Seryogin writes that Eusebius stresses the formal pretexts for the conflict but that causes of a doctrinal kind are not excluded, citing the Letter to Friends as evidence that charges of unorthodoxy were already raised in Origen’s lifetime.16 Malaty records that, besides the invalid ordination, the second council “considered that there were some errors in his teachings”: the creation of souls before bodies, the prior existence of Christ’s soul, and the return of all creation, “even Satan”, to God. He quotes J. Lebreton that Heraclas, Origen’s own pupil and Demetrius’s successor, “upheld the sentence of Demetrius”, so that the dismissal “was motivated not merely by the personal jealousy of Demetrius, but also by the Church’s own interests.”17 On this reading the jurisdictional quarrel and the doctrinal suspicion ran together in a single campaign, whose written record survives only from Origen’s side.
