Did Origen offer incense under persecution, as Epiphanius reports?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Epiphanius of Salamis, Panarion 64.2 (c. 375–377)

Related readings: Was Demetrius’s sentence (c. 231–232) disciplinary only, or did it also concern Origen’s teaching?

The passage

But his deeds did not remain worthy of the prize till the end… With diabolical malice the workers of iniquity thought of mistreating him sexually and making that his punishment, and they secured a black to abuse his body. But Origen could not bear even the thought of this devil’s work, and shouted that, given the choice of either, he would rather sacrifice. Certainly, as is widely reported, he did not do this willingly either. But since he had agreed to do it at all, he heaped incense on his hands and dumped it on the altar fire. Thus he was excluded from a martyr’s status at that time by the confessors and martyrs who were his judges, and expelled from the church.1

— Epiphanius, Panarion 64.2.1–5, tr. Frank Williams, source. Epiphanius goes on: asked to preach at Jerusalem, Origen “stood up and simply recited the verse of the forty-ninth Psalm… ‘But unto the ungodly saith God, Why dost thou preach my laws and takest my covenant in thy mouth?’ And he rolled the scroll up, gave it back, and sat down in floods of tears, and all wept with him.”2

The question

Epiphanius’s account of a forced offering of incense, an exclusion from the church and a departure from Alexandria is the earliest extant attestation of the story, written some 120 years after Origen’s death; Eusebius, writing earlier, does not report it. The question is whether it preserves an event of Origen’s life, which bears on the later picture of him as a lapsed confessor. No modern academic historian who credits the story was located in the sources opened; its holders below are ancient, Byzantine and early-modern.

A tradition of a lapse and its repentance

On this reading the story transmits a memory, not found in Eusebius, that Origen gave way under persecution, was put out of the church, and repented.

Held by: Epiphanius (Panarion 64, c. 375–377); the Suda (tenth century, Ω 183); Pierre-Daniel Huet (bishop of Avranches, 1630–1721, Origeniana), who holds that the lapse, not completed, was followed by penance.3

Epiphanius was a Palestinian monk before he became bishop of Salamis, and Frank Williams, his translator, judges that his life of Origen “is not based on Eusebius, but probably upon oral tradition which may, however, be influenced by Eusebius.”4 The account carries specific detail: the branches at the Serapeum, the incense heaped and spilled, judges who were themselves confessors, the wordless sermon on Psalm 49:16. Its outer frame is attested elsewhere: Origen’s Alexandrian career did end, he removed to Palestine, and a sentence from Alexandria barred him from teaching there and deprived him of his priesthood.5 A forced sacrifice, and exclusion from communion for those who complied, belonged to the persecutions of the third century. The story passed into Byzantine writing: the Suda repeats that “he agreed to make a sacrifice. Carrying frankincense in his hands unto the altar he kindled a fire. And thus, by these choices, he was rejected from martyrdom by the judges and he was forced out of the Church.”6 Huet takes the lapse as the chief cause of the deposition: “Ac illud praecipue flagitium caussae fuisse existimo, quamobrem sacerdotio et ecclesia abdicatus fuerit” (I judge this offence above all to have been the reason he was removed from the priesthood and the church), the penalty the Apostolic Canons lay down for such a fault. He reads the Jerusalem scene as penance, Origen having shown “nec Petri lapsum solummodo, sed lacrimas etiam ac dolorem” (not Peter’s fall only, but his tears and grief too); the editor’s note reports Huet as holding that Origen only hesitated and did not complete the act.7 On this reading the tale preserves, in Epiphanius’s form, a lapse that the Caesarean tradition passed over and that Origen’s tears at Jerusalem answered.

A legend without historical basis

On this reading the story is a later tale, reported only by Epiphanius and those who followed him, that conflicts with the earlier record of Origen’s life.

Held by: B. F. Westcott (Bishop of Durham, Dictionary of Christian Biography, 1887); Adolf Harnack (Lutheran, Encyclopædia Britannica, 1911); Cyril C. Richardson (Church History, 1937); A. V. Seryogin (Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya, Orthodox).8

Eusebius, writing a century before Epiphanius from the Caesarean library that held Origen’s letters, records that under Decius Origen bore “bonds and bodily tortures and torments under the iron collar and in the dungeon”, under a judge who “strove eagerly with all his might not to end his life”, and gives the departure from Alexandria as the sequel of the Caesarean ordination.9 Seryogin writes that such testimonies are usually regarded as having no serious historical value: Origen’s life was in danger under Septimius Severus, but he left Alexandria some twenty years later, in a quiet time for Christians, so there are no adequate grounds for linking the departure to a hypothetical apostasy.10 Williams notes that the central scene “appears to be a variation on the story of Origen’s pupil Potimiaena, who is threatened with rape by gladiators, answers defiantly, and is put to death”, and that Photius “inaccurately reported” Eusebius as saying a synod expelled Origen after this incident.11 Later in the chapter, of the reports of Origen’s self-mutilation and a memory drug, Epiphanius writes: “though I have no faith in the exaggerated stories about him, I have not neglected to report what is being said.”12 Westcott calls it “the monstrous story related by Epiphanius”; Harnack writes that “it is a malicious invention that he recanted during the persecution”; Richardson that it “is absolutely incredible… substantiated by no other source and is best explained by Epiphanius’ disagreeable nature and credulity.”13 On this reading the account is a moralized retelling of the Alexandrian deposition, whose recorded ground was the ordination, and Origen died a confessor in communion.
