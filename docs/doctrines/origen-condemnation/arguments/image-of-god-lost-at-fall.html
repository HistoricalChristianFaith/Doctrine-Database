Did Origen teach that the image of God was lost at the fall?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Origen, Peri Archōn III.6.1 and IV.37 and Homilies on Luke 39, as charged by Epiphanius of Salamis (394) and Jerome (c. 397)

Related readings: Did Origen teach that the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) are the mortal human body? · Did Origen’s allegorical exegesis set aside the literal and historical sense of Scripture?

The passage

The eighth is Origen's cavil that the image and likeness of God, in which man was created, was lost, and was no longer in man after he was expelled from Paradise.

— Jerome, Against John of Jerusalem 7, the eighth of the Origenist errors Epiphanius had drawn up, source.1

Now the expression, “In the image of God created He him,” without any mention of the word “likeness,” conveys no other meaning than this, that man received the dignity of God’s image at his first creation; but that the perfection of his likeness has been reserved for the consummation…

— Origen, De Principiis III.6.1, in Rufinus’s Latin, source.2

The question

Epiphanius charged in 394 that Origen taught Adam lost the image of God, and Jerome listed it as the last of eight Origenist errors; Origen’s surviving works distinguish the image, given at creation, from the likeness, and also speak of a second, “earthly” image taken on through sin. The question is whether the charge reports Origen’s teaching on what the fall did to the image, which bears on how the charge-lists of the 390s read his anthropology. No modern scholar holding that Origen taught the image lost was located in the sources opened.3

Origen taught that the image was lost

On this reading Origen held that Adam, in sinning, lost the image of God in which he had been made.

Held by: Epiphanius of Salamis (letter to John of Jerusalem, 394); Jerome (Against John of Jerusalem, c. 397).4

Epiphanius writes that Origen “has presumed to say this, too, that Adam lost the image of God, although Scripture nowhere declares that he did”, and answers from Genesis 9:6, James 3:9 and other texts that man still bears the image after the fall.4 Origen’s own preaching gives sin the power to bring on a second image. On the tribute coin he says that “man became in the beginning, as it is written in Genesis, ‘in the image of God,’ but later due to his disobedience, he also took on an ‘earthly’ image. Just as a coin bears the image of the ruling king… thus the one doing the works of the ‘ruler of the world’ bears his image”, and the Saviour “exhorts to give up and put away this image and to bear the image which was from the beginning created in the likeness of God.”5 Mark Edwards reports the same theme from the Homilies on Genesis: we become Satan’s prisoners “when we allow his image to oust that of god in our souls.”6 Origen speaks of the inner man as “renewed after ‘the image and likeness of God, who created him’”, and of the understanding as “restored to the image and likeness of God”; renewal and restoration imply a prior loss.7 Elizabeth Clark records a further interpretation along these lines: that since God is incorporeal his image must be too, and that “with the precosmic fall of the rational beings into bodies, this incorporeality was lost.”8 On this reading Epiphanius and Jerome name, in one proposition, what Origen’s language of an earthly image that displaces the divine, and of a renewal still to come, describes.

The image remains; the likeness is still to be gained

On this reading Origen held that man keeps the image given at creation and has yet to acquire the likeness.

Held by: Louis Berkhof (Reformed, Systematic Theology, 1932, rev. 1938); Tadros Y. Malaty (The School of Alexandria, published on CopticChurch.net).9

Origen reads “Let Us make man in Our own image, and after Our likeness” against “So God created man in His own image”, where “likeness” drops out, and concludes that the likeness is to be won “by the exercise of his own diligence in the imitation of God”, the perfect likeness being reached at the end when God is “all in all.”2 Against Celsus, preserved in Greek, gives the same distinction: God “made man ‘after the image’ of God, but not then also ‘after His likeness.’”10 In Peri Archōn IV.37 he writes that “if any one venture to ascribe essential corruption to Him who was made after the image and likeness of God, then… this impious charge extends even to the Son of God Himself”, and that in man “are manifestly to be discovered traces of the divine image, not by any appearance of the bodily frame… but by mental wisdom, by justice, moderation.”11 The “earthly” image of the homily is something taken on in addition (“he also took on”), to be put away. Berkhof writes that Clement and Origen held “that the word ‘image’ denoted the characteristics of man as man, and the word ‘likeness,’ qualities which are not essential to man.” Malaty: “Origen believes that these diabolical and bestial images cannot destroy the image of God. The latter endures beneath the former like the water in Abraham’s well which the Philistines filled with mire. A picture painted by the Son of God, it is indelible”; and “Origen insists that in all men some elements of the divine image remain.”12 On this reading the renewal Origen speaks of is the clearing of an image overlaid, and what fallen man lacks is the likeness he was always meant to acquire.

Origen’s statements point both ways

On this reading Origen’s remarks on the image’s fate are not consistent, and Epiphanius made one strand of them into a fixed doctrine.

Held by: Henri Crouzel (Jesuit, Théologie de l’image de Dieu chez Origène, 1956), as reported by Clark; Jon Dechow, as reported by Clark, on Epiphanius’s exaggeration; Elizabeth A. Clark (The Origenist Controversy, 1992).13

Clark writes of the charge: “Here, as Henri Crouzel and Jon Dechow have argued, Epiphanius exaggerates Origen’s view.” She reports Crouzel’s finding that “there are varying statements regarding the ‘image of God’ and its fate within Origen’s writing themselves: some passages point in the direction of affirming the loss of God’s ‘image’ through sin, while others suggest that humans retain the ‘image’ despite their waywardness”, and concludes: “Thus there is no consistent position to be found in Origen’s remarks on ‘the image’ on which to base a definitive judgment; perhaps the concept was not of central concern.”8 The homily on the tribute coin and Peri Archōn IV.37 can then stand side by side as two strands of a teacher who did not fix the point. Clark accounts for the sharpness of the charge from Epiphanius’s own setting: “on the point of humans’ loss of ‘the image,’ he interprets Origen through the teachings of more radical Origenists of his own day, such as Evagrius, who clearly stated that humans had lost the ‘image of God.’”14 Epiphanius’s letter itself, a section later, ascribes the view to a group: “For some of them say that the image of God which Adam had previously received was lost when he sinned. Others surmise that the body which the Son of God was destined to take of Mary was the image of the Creator.”15 On this reading the charge records a live question among the Origenists of the 390s, attached to Origen’s name from the side of his writings that spoke of loss.
