What does the condemnation of Origen and Theodore of Mopsuestia by the same council of 553 indicate about its aims?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: the Second Council of Constantinople (553), anathemas 11 and 12

Related readings: Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine? · Was Origen himself anathematized by an ecumenical council? · Were the views condemned in Origen also held, uncondemned, by others? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime?

The passage

If anyone does not anathematize Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen, as well as their heretical books… let him be anathema.

If anyone defends the heretical Theodore of Mopsuestia, who said that God the Word is one, while quite another is Christ… let him be anathema.

— Second Council of Constantinople (553), anathemas 11 and 12, source.1

The question

The council of 553 named Origen in its eleventh anathema and condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia, the Antiochene exegete, in its twelfth. The question is what the pairing shows about the council’s aims: a deliberate judgement on two opposed schools, two separate pieces of business that met in one assembly, or one quarrel in which the condemnation of Theodore answered the condemnation of Origen.

One quarrel: Theodore’s condemnation answered Origen’s

On this reading the Origenist party at court turned the emperor toward condemning Theodore, an old opponent of Origen, so that the two sentences belong to one feud.

Held by: Liberatus of Carthage (Breviarium 24, sixth century); Evagrius Scholasticus (Ecclesiastical History IV.38); F. J. Bacchus (Catholic Encyclopedia, “Three Chapters”); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911).2

Evagrius Scholasticus writes that when the anti-Origenist monks pressed their case, “Theodore of Cappadocia, with a view to divert them from this point, introduces the subject of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret, and Ibas.”3 Liberatus adds a motive: Askidas sought revenge on the memory of Theodore of Mopsuestia, who had written against Origen.4 Bacchus, following these sources, writes that Theodore Ascidas “raised the question of the Three Chapters to divert the attention of the emperor,” and that he “wished to take his revenge on the memory of Theodore of Mopsuestia, who had written much against Origen.”5 Richard Price finds “evidence of an Origenist campaign for Theodore’s condemnation in a less biased source than Liberatus,” a fragment of Domitian of Ancyra preserved by Facundus of Hermiane, and judges it “plausible to view Justinian’s condemnation of both figures as an offshoot of Palestinian feuding.”6 On this reading the anathemas against Origen and against Theodore sit side by side because the parties of the Palestinian lauras carried their dispute to the emperor, and each side’s champion fell in turn.

Both schools, or both extremes, condemned together

On this reading the council struck at the Alexandrian allegorist and the Antiochene literalist alike, whether as a balanced settlement or as the imperial suppression of both schools.

Held by: Adolf Harnack (History of Dogma, vol. IV); Alexis Pjawka (Orthodox priest, Orthodox Life, 2024).7

Origen’s exegesis dehistoricized Eden by a rule of divinely planted “impossibilities”; Theodore’s commentaries show “an almost exclusively grammatico-historical and realistic explanation.”8 Pjawka: “The council also condemned Origen, who was a follower of the Alexandrian, allegorical school of thinking”; “By condemning all of these heretical writings, the Fifth Ecumenical Council condemned two extremes.” Harnack: “Origen was already condemned; the condemnation of the Antiochene theology now followed on his,” and “It condemned Origen, as Justinian desired; it condemned the Three Chapters and consequently the Antiochian theology as Justinian desired.” In his note on the fifteen anathemas: “Since the ‘Three Chapters’ were condemned at the same time, Origen and Theodore were both got rid of.”9 The two were alike in their posthumous fate. Theodore died in 428, and “during his lifetime Theodore was always regarded as orthodox and as a prominent ecclesiastical author”; Cyril of Alexandria “was, however, unwilling to condemn Theodore, as he had died in peace with the Church.” Frederick McLeod, S.J.: Theodore “was acclaimed in the late-fourth and early-fifth centuries as the preeminent exegete of the School of Antioch… One hundred and twenty-five years later in A.D. 553 his christological writings and person were condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople as impious and heretical.”10 His teacher Diodore of Tarsus, who “rejected the allegorical interpretation of the Alexandrians, and adhered to the literal sense,” was named by Theodosius I a norm of communion for the East and was later charged with the same Christology.11 On this reading the council of 553 judged, after their deaths, the leading exegetes of both opposed schools.

Two separate matters, with the Three Chapters the council’s business

On this reading the council was summoned for the Three Chapters, as part of Justinian’s effort to reconcile the Miaphysites, and the Origenist question reached it as a side-matter.

Held by: Philip Schaff (History of the Christian Church III); Ferdinand Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); John Meyendorff (Greek Orthodox Theological Review, 1964–65); Richard Price (The Acts of the Council of Constantinople of 553, 2009).12

Prat: “the fifth general council was convoked exclusively to deal with the affair of the Three Chapters,” and “neither Origen nor Origenism were the cause of it.” Schaff writes that at the council “no further mention appears to have been made of Origenism,” and that “the fifteen anathemas against Origen do not belong to it.”13 Price, of the fifteen anathemas: “The acts contain no such canons and no discussion of Origenism”; “condemning Origenism was one of the activities that filled the bishops’ time.” Justinian’s letter to the council makes no reference to the Origenist controversy.14 Theodore, by contrast, was condemned by name and person in the recorded sessions; McLeod writes that the council used “71 extracts” of his as “the basis for its condemnation of his doctrine,” and that “after the council, the Emperor Justinian ordered his entire literary output to be destroyed.”15 Meyendorff places the council within “a supreme effort of reconciliation with the Monophysites” made under Justinian, ending in a council “which reaffirmed the total faithfulness of the Byzantine Church to the theology of St. Cyril of Alexandria”; Price calls Justinian’s theology “clearly neo-Chalcedonian, and neo-Chalcedonianism was endorsed by the canons of 553.”16 On this reading Theodore fell as the first of the Three Chapters for reasons of Christology and church union, and Origen’s name entered the council’s acts through a separate, monastic controversy.
