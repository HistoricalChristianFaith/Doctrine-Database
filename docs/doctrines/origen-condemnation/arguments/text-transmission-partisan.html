Can Origen’s teaching be recovered, given how the text of Peri Archōn has come down?

Disputed reading · on the condemnation of Origen · witness: Rufinus; see also Justinian

Related readings: Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching? · Was Rufinus’s draft Peri Archon altered before it was read against him in Italy? · Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen? · Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime? · Was the plea of heretical interpolation applied alike to Gregory of Nyssa and to Origen? · Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it?

The passage

…he “added or struck out whatever he chose, and changed things as he thought right, and published it abroad as if it were my work.”

— Origen, in a letter “to certain intimate friends at Alexandria” complaining of the falsification of the record of a disputation, preserved by Rufinus, On the Falsification of the Works of Origen, source.1

The question

Peri Archōn survives whole only in the Latin that Rufinus made in 397, whose preface says that he omitted or recast passages on the Trinity. Its Greek survives in the Philocalia excerpts gathered by Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus and in quotations by Origen’s opponents, among them Justinian’s edict to Menas (543); Jerome’s Letter 124 to Avitus preserves passages of his own literal Latin version. Much of Origen’s other work survives in Greek, including the Contra Celsum and parts of the Commentary on John, and the papyri found at Tura near Cairo in August 1941 added the Dialogue with Heraclides and the treatise On the Pascha.2,3 What is disputed is which of these witnesses gives Origen’s own teaching where they differ, and whether that teaching can be recovered at all; the answer bears on what the condemnations of 543 and 553 reached. No scholarly holder of the second reading after Koetschau’s edition of 1913 was located, though Mark Edwards reports, without naming them, that many hold the charges true; no named proponent of the third reading, scholarly or popular, was located.

Origen is read from his own surviving works, with Rufinus’s Latin as the text of Peri Archōn

On this reading Origen’s teaching is recovered from what survives under his own name, Greek and Latin, and the excerpts made by his opponents are kept apart from it.

Held by: Rufinus (Preface to Peri Archōn, 397); John Behr (Orthodox, ed. and tr. Origen: On First Principles, 2017); Mark J. Edwards (“Origen,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2014, rev. 2026); W. E. L. Begley (The Avignon Manuscript and the Transmission of Rufinus’ Translation, 2017).4

Rufinus describes his own method as a restoration of Origen from Origen. Where a passage on the Trinity contradicted what Origen “has in other places spoken of in a strictly orthodox sense,” he set it in agreement with that teaching, and where Origen was obscure he added “words which I had read in other books of his”: “I have put in nothing of my own; I have only given him back his own words, though taken from other passages.”5 Behr’s Oxford edition “is primarily an edition of Rufinus’ Latin translation” and keeps apart what comes from Rufinus and what from Origen’s opponents. Behr writes that the passages Paul Koetschau inserted “about eternally existing intellects and their fall into bodies” were “literally ‘made up,’ by stitching together sentences from various anti-Origenist writers.” He holds that the “alleged belief in pre-existent intellects descending into ranks of increasingly dense bodies via a pre-cosmic fall” (ideas, he says, “that were (rightly!) anathematized in the sixth century”) does not occur within the work.6 Edwards, who judges that Rufinus translated Origen “with reverence but not always with fidelity,” bases his account “primarily on the extant corpus in Greek and Latin, making use of polemical matter only with the caution that is now enjoined by the most learned scholars.” He notes that the passage known as Koetschau Fr. 15, from which textbook accounts take the fall of pure intellects through satiety, is an amalgam of Greek witnesses none of which claims to give Origen’s exact words, and that in Origen’s extant writings this doctrine “is never so clearly stated as in later indictments of his heterodoxy.”7 Begley adds that a major purpose of Jerome’s translation was “to demonstrate Origen’s heresies,” so that “Rufinus’ charitable reading of Peri archōn may provide more insight into Origen’s own theology than Jerome’s tendentious excerpts.”8 On this reading Origen’s mind is reached through the thousands of pages he left under his own name, read together, with the translator’s stated method in view.

The opponents’ literal excerpts preserve what Rufinus’s Latin softened

On this reading the Greek and literal Latin quoted by Origen’s opponents give his own words where Rufinus altered them, and his teaching, including what was condemned, is recovered by reading the two together.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 124 to Avitus, 409/410, and Apology against Rufinus); Justinian (edict to Menas, 543); Paul Koetschau (ed., De principiis, GCS 22, 1913).9

The translator of the only complete text announced his own changes: wherever he found “anything contrary to the truth concerning the Trinity,” Rufinus wrote, “I have either omitted it as a foreign and not genuine expression or set it down in terms agreeing with the rule of faith.”5 Jerome answered with a literal version. Pammachius had asked him, he writes, to give “the true sense of the Greek and… set down the writer’s words for good or for evil without bias in either direction,” and the extracts in his Letter 124 are offered as that record.10 Jerome also rejected the principle that offensive passages may be laid to a corrupter:

If, I reply, we admit that everything in a book which is offensive is corruptly inserted by others, nothing will remain belonging to the author under whose name the book passes, but everything can be assigned to those by whom it is supposed to have been corrupted.

— Jerome, Apology against Rufinus II.17.11 Justinian’s edict to Menas set some two dozen excerpts from Peri Archōn before its anathemas as the grounds of condemnation, quoting the work in Greek.12 Koetschau’s critical edition of 1913 built on these witnesses. Taking his lead from Rufinus’s admission, he held that Rufinus could not be trusted, and was “convinced that the accounts of Origen’s teaching given by his opponents, especially the letter of Jerome to Avitus, the reports of Justinian, and even the Anathemas of 553, do in fact represent Origen’s authentic teaching”; he therefore set those passages into the running text where he judged that Rufinus had left a gap, and G. W. Butterworth’s English translation of 1936 was made from that text.13 Edwards reports the view as still held: “Many hold none the less that the charges are true, and that Origen’s advocacy of these doctrines is concealed from us by the loss or mutilation of the incriminating texts.”9 On this reading the literal excerpts are the surviving controls on a translation that its maker said he had corrected, and they let the reader see the Greek Rufinus had before him.

The partisan record leaves the condemned teaching beyond check

On this reading the witnesses are shaped so strongly by partisans on both sides that Origen’s exact teaching on the condemned points cannot be confirmed, and the condemnations rest on texts that cannot be checked against his own page.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

On this reading the transmission is partisan in a specific and documented way. The only complete witness to Peri Archōn is a translator who announced in advance that he had corrected his author, and much of its Greek outside the Philocalia excerpts survives in documents drawn up to condemn it.2 Origen testified that an opponent had doctored the transcript of a debate and circulated it under his name, and he compared his fate to the forged letter that alarmed the Thessalonians and to Marcion’s mutilation of the Gospels.1 The sharpest charges of 553, the spherical resurrection body, the nous-Christ and the final equality of every spirit with Christ, are not set out in that form in Origen’s surviving text, and the case that they describe a later Evagrian system rests on Antoine Guillaumont’s comparison of the anathemas with the recovered Syriac of Evagrius, since there is no complete Greek Peri Archōn to compare.14 The modern printed record shows the two sides fused: Koetschau’s edition spliced the opponents’ fragments into Rufinus’s Latin, Butterworth carried the splice into English, and Behr’s edition of 2017 separated them again.13 Begley, who reads Origen through Rufinus, writes of the Greek original that “no matter how many excerpts, quotations, translations, and imitations one assembles, the original text stays continually out of view, almost certainly lost forever.”8 On this reading the defendant’s precise words on the condemned propositions are missing, and any judgement on them rests on what his defender and his accusers report.
