Evagrius Ponticus on the condemnation of Origen

Dates: c. 345–399 · Wikipedia
Summary: Evagrius is not one of Origen's judges. A deacon of the Cappadocian circle who became a monk in the Egyptian desert, he set out in his Kephalaia Gnostica, in numbered chapters, a doctrine of the pre-cosmic unity of minds, their fall, the graded cosmos, and the final restoration. Antoine Guillaumont (1961, 1962) argued that the fifteen anathemas of 553 correspond closely to this work, and whether those anathemas condemned an Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen's own teaching is a disputed reading. This page covers his place on the timeline of judgements passed on Origen: his life, what the unexpurgated Kephalaia Gnostica says, how scholars have related the anathemas to it, and the transmission of his treatise On Prayer under the name of Nilus.

The man

Cappadocian deacon, then desert monk

Evagrius was born about 345 in Pontus — at Ibora, the son of a country-bishop — and formed in the circle of the Cappadocian Fathers. The Philokalia's note on him gives his background:

Evagrios the Solitary, also known as Evagrios Pontikos, was born in 345 or 346, probably at Ibora in Pontus… A disciple of the Cappadocian Fathers, he was ordained reader by St Basil the Great and deacon by St Gregory the Theologian (Gregory of Nazianzos), and he accompanied the latter to the Council of Constantinople in 381 (the second Ecumenical Council).1

He stayed on in the capital under Nectarius as a preacher and controversialist; then, by his biographer Palladius's account, an entanglement with a married woman and a warning vision drove him to flee. He was received at Jerusalem by Melania the Elder and Rufinus — the Rufinus whose Latin Peri Archōn of 398 is treated on the Origenist controversy — and in 383 went down into Egypt, first to the monks of Nitria and then to the remoter settlement of the Cells (Kellia), where he spent the rest of his life under Macarius the Great and Macarius of Alexandria. He died there in 399, in his fifty-fourth year, the year in which Theophilus of Alexandria acted against the Origenist monks of Nitria.2 He was not a bishop and was not tried or condemned in his lifetime; he died in communion, honoured as a master of the ascetic life.

The system: the Kephalaia Gnostica

Evagrius's ascetic and contemplative works — the Praktikos, the Gnostikos, the treatise On Prayer — were prized and copied for centuries. He also wrote a work of speculative theology, the Kephalaia Gnostica (“Gnostic Chapters”), six “centuries” of terse sentences setting out a cosmology. It survives in two Syriac versions: a common recension (S1), and, in a single British Library manuscript, a recension (S2) published by Antoine Guillaumont in 1958, beside which Guillaumont judged S1 to be a doctrinally revised copy. The chapters quoted below are from S2, the text set beside the anathemas of 553 in the debate described further down.3

An original unity of minds, and a fall from it

The beginning is a unity (henad) of created minds (logikoi) contemplating God; evil enters not by God's making but by a “movement” away from that unity:

The first movement of the logikoi is the separation of the nous from the Unity that is in it.4

The fallen mind cools into “soul,” and by degrees into the graded bodies of the present cosmos:

The soul is the nous which, through negligence, has fallen from the Unity; and through its carelessness, has descended to the rank of the praktike.5

The descent of the soul before the present cosmos is traced as a belief in its own right on the origin of the soul; how far Evagrius's scheme reproduces Origen's is part of the disputed reading described below.

A restoration in which bodies and distinctions vanish

Because the end answers to the beginning, the whole graded order is finally dissolved and the minds return to their original equality:

Accompanying the knowledge that concerns the logikoi are the destruction of worlds, the dissolution of bodies, and the abolition of names; while [there] remains the equality of knowledge according to the equality of substances.6

The fifteenth anathema of 553 condemns the teaching that the end will be “like to the life which was in the beginning,” and the fourteenth the vanishing of “the hypostases as well as the numbers and the bodies.”

A Christ who is a mind, distinct from the Word

The Christological chapters are distinctive. For Evagrius “Christ” is not simply the eternal Word but the one created mind that did not fall, the nous that stayed united to the knowledge of God and so is called anointed:

The intelligible anointing is the spiritual knowledge of the holy Unity, and the Christ is he who is united to this knowledge.7

The twelfth anathema of 553 condemns anyone who says that the powers and all men and the devil “are united with the Word of God… as the Nous which is by them called Christ.” Guillaumont counts this among the propositions found in the Kephalaia Gnostica and not in Peri Archōn (below).

Why he stands on this timeline

Evagrius passed no judgement on Origen; he stands on this timeline because of a modern argument about what the anathemas of 553 condemned. When Guillaumont recovered the S2 text he argued, in a paper of 1961 and a book of 1962, that the fifteen anathemas of 553 track the propositions of the Kephalaia Gnostica almost point by point — the spherical resurrection body, the nous-Christ, the final equality of every spirit — and quote Peri Archōn nowhere. On that reading the sixth-century Palestinian Origenist monks, the Isochristoi (“equal to Christ”) and Protoktistoi (“first-created”), were Evagrians, and the anathemas of 553 condemned under Origen's name a system assembled about a century and a half after his death.8

Two other readings relate Evagrius differently to the anathemas. Georges Florovsky (Orthodox) reads the Evagrian system as a development of Origen's own scheme, whose framing doctrines — the pre-existent fall and the restoration of all — he finds in Peri Archōn, so that the anathemas reach Origen through Evagrius; his reading, with those of Cyril of Scythopolis, Hefele and Kallistos Ware, is set out on the disputed-reading page. Ilaria Ramelli, who published an English translation of the unreformed S2 in 2015, holds that the propositions condemned under Justinian belong not to Evagrius or Origen themselves but to later Origenists who radicalized and distorted Evagrius, and that the S2 text is closer to Origen's thought than to what 553 condemned. The readings are set out at whether the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemned sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen.9

His works under another name

The unrevised Kephalaia Gnostica survives in full only in the single Syriac manuscript of S2. Evagrius's ascetic works were transmitted more widely, some under other names. His treatise On Prayer (153 chapters) circulated for centuries, was printed in Migne's Patrologia Graeca among the works of St Nilus of Ancyra, and entered the eighteenth-century Greek Philokalia still ascribed to Nilus; his authorship was established in the twentieth century, by Irénée Hausherr. The Philokalia's modern editors state the fact directly:

Several of Evagrios' works have come down under the name of other authors. This is the case with the writing On Prayer, which in the Greek Philokalia is ascribed to Neilos; but recent research has made it plain beyond any reasonable doubt that this is a writing of Evagrios.10

Reception and debate

  • Guillaumont, Price and others read the edict of 543 and the anathemas associated with the council of 553 as answering the Evagrian Origenism of the Palestinian monks; Cyril of Scythopolis, Hefele, Florovsky and Ware read them as condemning Origen's own system. Both readings are set out on the disputed-reading page.
  • The doctrine of the soul's pre-cosmic fall is traced on the origin of the soul; whether Origen taught universal restoration as doctrine is a disputed reading.
