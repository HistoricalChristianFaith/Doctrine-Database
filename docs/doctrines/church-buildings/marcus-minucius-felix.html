Minucius Felix on the Place of Christian Worship

Dates: c. 250 (composition disputed, c. 200–250) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Latin dialogue Octavius, the pagan Caecilius throws at the Christians the standard taunt — "why have they no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images?" — and the Christian Octavius answers not by denying the fact but by owning it as a conviction: God cannot be shut "within one little building," needs no image because "man himself is the image of God," and is better "dedicated in our mind, consecrated in our inmost heart." It is the sharpest early Latin statement that the absence of Christian temples was a principle, not merely a want of means.

Claims & evidence

The pagan charge: Christians have "no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images"

Early in the dialogue the pagan speaker Caecilius lists the Christians' lack of cult-apparatus among the proofs that their religion is something shameful and hidden:

Why have they no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images? Why do they never speak openly, never congregate freely, unless for the reason that what they adore and conceal is either worthy of punishment, or something to be ashamed of?

— The Octavius of Minucius Felix, ch. 10, New Advent.1

The Christian answer: God cannot be housed in "one little building"

When Octavius replies, he concedes the fact and turns it into doctrine. The absence of temple, image, and sacrifice is not concealment but the only fitting worship of a God who fills all things:

But do you think that we conceal what we worship, if we have not temples and altars? And yet what image of God shall I make, since, if you think rightly, man himself is the image of God? What temple shall I build to Him, when this whole world fashioned by His work cannot receive Him? And when I, a man, dwell far and wide, shall I shut up the might of so great majesty within one little building? Were it not better that He should be dedicated in our mind, consecrated in our inmost heart?

— The Octavius of Minucius Felix, ch. 32, New Advent (cf. full text).2 The reasoning is the same de-localizing of worship the other apologists pressed — an echo of "the Most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands" (Acts 7:48) — and it treats the lack of Christian temples as a positive mark of a purer conception of God, not an embarrassment to be explained away by persecution.

Notes

  • On the Place of Christian Worship timeline, Minucius Felix supplies the theological-principle voice from the Latin West, as Origen does from the Greek East: the early absence of sacred buildings expressed a conviction that God is not to be housed or imaged, not merely the disability of an illegal cult. This is the strand the "necessity, not principle" argument grants did color the practice — while noting that Octavius' target is the pagan temple-and-image cult, not the existence of a room where Christians assemble.
  • The date of the Octavius is genuinely disputed. It turns on its close relationship to Tertullian's Apology (c. 197): if Minucius drew on Tertullian, the dialogue belongs to the middle third century (hence the c. 250 placement used here); if Tertullian drew on Minucius, it could fall around 200 or earlier. Either way it sits within the pre-Constantinian, still-unrecognized period the timeline traces, and its argument does not depend on the exact year.
