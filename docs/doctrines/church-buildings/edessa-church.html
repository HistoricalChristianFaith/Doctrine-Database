The "temple of the church of the Christians" at Edessa

Dates: c. 202 (the flood is dated to the year 513 of the Greeks [Seleucid era] = A.D. 202 in the Chronicle; Everett Ferguson and Eckhard Schnabel, as cited in the Wikipedia article, give November A.D. 201) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable literary notice of a building set apart for Christian worship that J. B. Lightfoot found. The Chronicle of Edessa, listing the edifices swept away when the river Daysan inundated the city in the reign of Abgar the Great, names among them "the temple of the church of the Christians" — a church building of some standing in a kingdom beyond the Roman frontier, where, J. B. Lightfoot notes, "no such restraints would be imposed upon the Christians" as inside the Empire.

Claims & evidence

The Chronicle of Edessa names a Christian church among the buildings the flood destroyed

In the year 513, in the reign of Severus, and in the reign of king Abgar … the fountains of the waters of the great river which is called Daisan were stirred up. … And the waters overthrew the great and beautiful palace of our lord the king, and they carried away everything that was found before them, the desirable and beautiful edifices of the city, and whatsoever was near the river … And they destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians. And there were killed by that occurrence more than two thousand men.

— The Chronicle of Edessa, §1, trans. B. H. Cowper, Journal of Sacred Literature, New Series vol. 5 (1864), tertullian.org.1 Two features of the notice are discussed. First, the dating: the flood entry, which the chronicler presents as taken from the Edessene royal archives, opens with a precise Seleucid year (513 = A.D. 202; other reckonings of the November date give A.D. 201). Second, the wording: the building is called a temple (Syriac hayklā), and the word church names the community whose temple it is (see Cowper's note). The building is itemized alongside the royal palace among the city's "desirable and beautiful edifices".2

Reception and debate

  • Edessa, capital of the kingdom of Osrhoene, lay beyond the Roman frontier under kings (the Abgarids) who had early embraced Christianity, so the legal and prudential restraints that prevented Christians inside the Empire from holding corporate property or raising conspicuous buildings did not apply, as J. B. Lightfoot argues. He adduced the Edessene church as his earliest datable notice of a Christian building in support of his "necessity, not principle" thesis. Within the Empire, Lightfoot found "not … any explicit notice of a church erected either at Rome or in the provinces before the close of the second century."3
  • The Chronicle is a sixth-century compilation, but its flood entry draws on the Edessene royal archives, which the chronicler cites; the Wikipedia article on the Chronicle describes the notice as reliable ⚠ unverified. The notice concerns a building in early-third-century Edessa, outside the Empire; inside the Empire the surviving material record begins a generation later with the converted house at Dura-Europos.4
