The rescript of Gallienus — restoring the Christians' places of worship

Dates: c. 260 (the rescript followed the capture of Valerian by the Persians in 260; often dated 260–261) · Wikipedia
Summary: When Gallienus became sole emperor he ended his father Valerian's persecution and, by a rescript preserved in Eusebius, addressed the bishops' "places of religious worship" in a clause read two ways: as releasing Christians from their retreats, or (with Closs, McGiffert and Lightfoot) as restoring those places to them. A further decree let the Christians repossess their cemeteries. On the restoring reading, Christian communities already had meeting-places to be given back. J. B. Lightfoot writes that by this rescript "all obstacles to the multiplication of churches were at length removed."

Claims & evidence

Gallienus' rescript restores the Christians' places of worship and cemeteries

Shortly after this Valerian was reduced to slavery by the barbarians, and his son having become sole ruler, conducted the government more prudently. He immediately restrained the persecution against us by public proclamations, and directed the bishops to perform in freedom their customary duties, in a rescript which ran as follows: "The Emperor Caesar Publius Licinius Gallienus, Pius, Felix, Augustus, to Dionysius, Pinnas, Demetrius, and the other bishops. I have ordered the bounty of my gift to be declared through all the world, that they may depart from the places of religious worship. And for this purpose you may use this copy of my rescript, that no one may molest you. And this which you are now enabled lawfully to do, has already for a long time been conceded by me. Therefore Aurelius Cyrenius, who is the chief administrator of affairs, will observe this ordinance which I have given." I have given this in a translation from the Latin, that it may be more readily understood. Another decree of his is extant addressed to other bishops, permitting them to take possession again of the so-called cemeteries.

— Eusebius, Church History 7.13, trans. A. C. McGiffert, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. 1.1 The chapter is headed "The Peace Under Gallienus." The clause "that they may depart from the places of religious worship" is read in two ways: McGiffert reports that it "is commonly taken to mean that the 'Christians may come forth from their religious retreats,'" while he prefers, "with Closs, 'that the heathen may depart from the Christians' places of worship,'" those "which they had taken possession of during the persecution." Lightfoot paraphrases the rescript as "expressly restoring to them their 'places of worship,'" and the parallel decree permits the bishops "to take possession again" of the cemeteries.2

Reception and debate

  • The rescript is addressed to the bishops as a body and orders the restitution of property held by them collectively. McGiffert's note in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers holds that "the right of the Christians as a body (the corpus Christianorum) to hold property is recognized in this rescript."2 J. B. Lightfoot cites the rescript in his "necessity, not principle" thesis, which holds that "as a corporation they were not recognized by the law; it was therefore impossible for them to hold corporate property."3
  • The rescript speaks of "places of worship" and "cemeteries" to be restored: by the mid-third century Christian communities inside the Empire held property used for assembly and burial, subject to confiscation in persecution. The converted house-church at Dura-Europos dates from c. 233. Once Gallienus' peace held, Eusebius reports, the old buildings no longer sufficed and Christians "raised from the foundations in all the cities churches spacious in plan" — a building boom that ran until Diocletian ordered those very churches leveled in 303, and that resumed after the Edict of Milan (313).4
