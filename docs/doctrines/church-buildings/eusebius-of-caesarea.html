Eusebius of Caesarea on the Place of Christian Worship

Dates: c. 316 (the dedication of the church at Tyre; recorded in the Ecclesiastical History, published c. 324) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius closes the arc this page traces. Writing as an eyewitness of the moment the persecutions ended, he describes "temples again rising from their foundations to an immense height," and he preserves — in his own words — the panegyric he delivered at the dedication of the great new church at Tyre, addressing its builder-bishop Paulinus as "a new Beseleel … or Solomon, king of a new and much better Jerusalem." Within a generation of the last martyrs, the church that had met in houses is raising "lofty and spacious" basilicas and celebrating "feasts of dedication in the cities" — the shift from house church to church building complete.

Claims & evidence

"Temples again rising from their foundations to an immense height"

Opening his account of the peace under Constantine and Licinius, Eusebius records the visible reversal: where the persecutors had razed the churches, grander ones now rose in their place:

… a certain inspired joy bloomed for all of us, when we saw every place which shortly before had been desolated by the impieties of the tyrants reviving as if from a long and death-fraught pestilence, and temples again rising from their foundations to an immense height, and receiving a splendor far greater than that of the old ones which had been destroyed.

— Ecclesiastical History 10.2, New Advent (cf. full text).1 The passage presupposes that the Church already owned the buildings the tyrants had razed, and that the moment the disability lifted it rebuilt them larger — the pattern the timeline documents from Gallienus (260) through the Edict of Milan (313).

"Feasts of dedication in the cities" — and the panegyric at Tyre

Eusebius then describes the wave of consecrations across the Empire, and reproduces the oration he himself gave at the dedication of the rebuilt church at Tyre, the most splendid in Phoenicia:

After this was seen the sight which had been desired and prayed for by us all; feasts of dedication in the cities and consecrations of the newly built houses of prayer took place, bishops assembled, foreigners came together from abroad … A Certain one of those of moderate talent, who had composed a discourse, stepped forward … and … addressed himself as follows to one who was in all things a most excellent bishop and beloved of God, through whose zeal the temple in Tyre, which was the most splendid in Phoenicia, had been erected. … one might call thee a new Beseleel, the architect of a divine tabernacle, or Solomon, king of a new and much better Jerusalem.

— Ecclesiastical History 10.3–4 (the "Panegyric on the Building of the Churches," addressed to Paulinus, bishop of Tyre), New Advent.1 Later in the same oration he glories openly in the physical building: "great is his house, lofty and spacious and 'comely in beauty above the sons of men.'"2

Notes

  • Eusebius is the post-Constantinian bookend on the Place of Christian Worship timeline. Where Justin's church met above a bath-house and Dura-Europos remodeled a private house, the church of Eusebius' day builds — and dedicates with festal panegyric — purpose-built basilicas "far greater" than what came before. His delight in the "lofty and spacious" house of God is the mirror image of Minucius Felix and Origen's earlier insistence that God needs no building: the building boom tracks the arrival of legal toleration, and Eusebius records no sense that raising grand churches betrayed a principle.
  • The move is honestly two-edged for the "necessity, not principle" question. That the Church rebuilt lavishly the instant it was free supports Lightfoot's reading that the earlier absence was a disability, not a doctrine. Yet the earlier de-localizing strand (Origen; Minucius Felix) did not thereby vanish — it was redirected, later, into critiques of church wealth and ostentation. Eusebius here shows the practice; the weighing belongs on the argument page.
