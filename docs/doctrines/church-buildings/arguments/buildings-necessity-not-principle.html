Was the early Christians' lack of buildings set apart for worship a matter of principle or of circumstance?

Disputed reading · on the Place of Christian Worship · witness: Justin Martyr

The passage

Rusticus the prefect said, "Where do you assemble?" Justin said, "Where each one chooses and can: for do you fancy that we all meet in the very same place? Not so; because the God of the Christians is not circumscribed by place; but being invisible, fills heaven and earth, and everywhere is worshipped and glorified by the faithful." Rusticus the prefect said, "Tell me where you assemble, or into what place do you collect your followers?" Justin said, "I live above one Martinus, at the Timiotinian Bath; and during the whole time (and I am now living in Rome for the second time) I am unaware of any other meeting than his. And if any one wished to come to me, I communicated to him the doctrines of truth."

— The Martyrdom of the Holy Martyrs (the Acts of Justin), ch. 2, Rome, c. 165, source.1 The New Testament greets "the church that is in their house" (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2), and the earliest building set apart for Christian worship that Lightfoot found noticed in literature is at Edessa (c. 202); the earliest surviving in the ground, on the excavators' account, is at Dura-Europos (c. 233).

The question

The first Christians met in houses and raised no buildings set apart for worship before about the close of the second century. What is disputed is why: whether the absence expressed a conviction (about God, or about the home as the proper place of worship), or followed from the position of an unrecognized and intermittently persecuted body; the second- and third-century apologists' statements concern temples, altars and images, and how far they bear on assembly buildings is part of the question. For the reading that the New Testament records the home as precedent rather than obligation, the holders located are popular evangelical teachers; its academic literature was not located.

The absence of temples expressed a conviction about God

On this reading Christians raised no temples because they held that God is not housed or imaged in a building made with hands.

Held by: Minucius Felix (Octavius, c. 200–250);2 Origen (Against Celsus, c. 248)3

Pagan critics put the Christians' lack of cult-buildings to them as a charge. The pagan Caecilius in the Octavius asks: "Why have they no altars, no temples, no acknowledged images?" The Christian Octavius answers by owning the fact: "What temple shall I build to Him, when this whole world fashioned by His work cannot receive Him? … shall I shut up the might of so great majesty within one little building? Were it not better that He should be dedicated in our mind, consecrated in our inmost heart?"2 Origen, answering Celsus, says that Christians "not only avoid temples, altars, and images, but are ready to suffer death when it is necessary, rather than debase by any such impiety the conception which they have of the Most High God." Christians, he says, have an altar and a temple of their own kind: "the spirit of every good man" is the altar, "our bodies are the temple of God," and what they "refuse" is "to build lifeless temples to the Giver of all life," because "we have learnt from Jesus Christ the true way of serving God."3 On this reading these writers give a theological reason for the absence of Christian temples. The earlier scene at Rome has the same note: Justin's first answer to the prefect is that "the God of the Christians is not circumscribed by place."1 The scriptural ground is the theme that "the Most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands" (Acts 7:48; 17:24) and that true worshippers will worship the Father "neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem" but "in spirit and in truth" (John 4:21–24).4 On this reading the plainness of early Christian worship carried a positive conception of God, and the apologists presented it as a mark of a purer worship.

A necessity of the Christians' legal position

On this reading Christians built no houses of worship because, as an unrecognized body, they could hold no corporate property and prudence forbade display.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican bishop of Durham, Historical Essays, 1895)5

Lightfoot states the thesis: "they were not yet in the habit of erecting buildings devoted solely to divine worship. This, however, was not a principle of their faith, but rather a necessity of their position. As a corporation they were not recognized by the law; it was therefore impossible for them to hold corporate property. Moreover, common prudence would deter them from any display which might arouse the fury of the populace, or invite the repression of the magistrates." He calls the plainness of early worship "not a deliberate choice, but an enforced abstention," and "a necessary discipline, a providential design, in the early education of the Church," so that the later elaboration of architecture and ritual is no "perversion of the simplicity of the Gospel."5 The case rests on where and when buildings appear. Lightfoot's earliest notice of a church building is the "temple of the Church of the Christians" at Edessa, destroyed in a flood of 201/202, beyond the Empire, where under Christian kings "no such restraints would be imposed upon the Christians."6 At Rome c. 165 Justin, pressed for a place, names only his lodging "above one Martinus."1 Inside the Empire a private house at Dura-Europos was remodeled for worship c. 233. When Gallienus ended the persecution (c. 260) his rescript restored the Christians' "places of religious worship" to the bishops; by this rescript, Lightfoot writes, "all obstacles to the multiplication of churches were at length removed."7 Eusebius then reports churches "spacious in plan" raised "in all the cities," leveled under Diocletian in 303; after 313 Licinius "exhorted the Christians to rebuild their religious edifices." L. Michael White maps the architectural sequence from house church to domus ecclesiae, aula ecclesiae and basilica.8 On this reading, buildings appear where and when the legal and prudential restraints were lifted.

The New Testament records the home as precedent, not obligation

On this reading the New Testament's house churches show where the first believers met. They are not a rule confining worship to the home.

Held by: GotQuestions (evangelical Protestant website); John Piper (Reformed Baptist pastor, Desiring God) ⚠ unverified9

"Churches in the New Testament era were indeed small assemblies that met in homes (Acts 2:46; 20:20). … Home churches are 'biblical' in the sense that there is precedent in Scripture, but there is no biblical obligation to attend a home church."

GotQuestions adds that "there is nothing unbiblical about Christians gathering regularly in houses or large buildings or any other venue."9 On this reading the proof-texts are greetings and narratives: Paul greets the church in a house, and Luke narrates the breaking of bread in houses. Acts 2:46 names two venues, the believers "continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house"; John Chrysostom reads the converts as those who "did nothing else great or small, than assiduously attend at the temple," breaking bread in houses besides.10 Paul taught "publickly, and from house to house" (Acts 20:20), and Acts finds the apostles in the temple courts and Solomon's portico, synagogues, "the school of one Tyrannus," and a riverside place of prayer. On this reading the home is one venue among several, used as opportunity allowed.11

The New Testament sets the home as the pattern of Christian worship

On this reading the household assembly is the settled, blessed shape of apostolic worship, a pattern to be restored.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The reading starts from the number and consistency of the texts. Paul four times greets "the church that is in their house": Prisca and Aquila's (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19), Nymphas' (Colossians 4:15), and that in Philemon's house (Philemon 2). Acts reports the first believers "breaking bread from house to house" (Acts 2:46).12 For the first generations the household was the unit of Christian assembly, and the household carried honor. Chrysostom, expounding the greetings, says Paul "was not in the habit of calling any houses Churches, save where there was much piety," that Prisca and Aquila "had been so estimable as even to make their house a Church," that it was "no small excellency, that they had made their very house a Church," and of Nymphas that "he shows the man to be great, seeing his house was a church."12 On this reading the home was the apostolic norm, not a stopgap, and the household assembly carries a positive theological weight of its own. The later move to buildings set apart for worship is then a departure from that norm, and the question "why aren't we breaking bread in homes anymore?" asks for it to be restored.
