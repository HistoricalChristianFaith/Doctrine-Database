Origen on the Place of Christian Worship

Dates: c. 248 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering the pagan philosopher Celsus around 248, Origen states outright why Christians (with the Jews) will not raise "altars, or temples, or images" to God — not from indifference but on principle: God is not to be housed or imaged in "lifeless temples," the true temple is the body and the community of the faithful, and the true altar is "the spirit of every good man" offering the incense of prayer. This is the clearest articulation on this timeline of the theological strand — the de-localizing of worship — that underlay the early church's lack of sacred buildings, held so firmly that Christians "are ready to suffer death" rather than betray it.

Claims & evidence

Christians "not only avoid temples, altars, and images" but will die rather than debase their conception of God

Celsus had catalogued the Christians (with the Jews, the Scythians, the Persians, and others) among those who "cannot tolerate temples, altars, or images." Origen accepts the description but insists the Christian motive is unique — a refusal to degrade the worship of God into the worship of "material things wrought by the hands of men":

… so in the same way with those who cannot allow in the worship of the Divine Being altars, or temples, or images. The Scythians, the Nomadic Libyans, the godless Seres, and the Persians, agree in this with the Christians and Jews, but they are actuated by very different principles. For none of these former abhor altars and images on the ground that they are afraid of degrading the worship of God, and reducing it to the worship of material things wrought by the hands of men. … It is in consideration of these and many other such commands, that they not only avoid temples, altars, and images, but are ready to suffer death when it is necessary, rather than debase by any such impiety the conception which they have of the Most High God.

— Against Celsus (Contra Celsum) 7.64, New Advent (cf. full text).1

The altar is "the spirit of every good man"; the temple is the body and the community

In the following book Origen answers the same charge head-on. Celsus had said the Christians "shrink from raising altars, statues, and temples," treating that abstention as the secret badge of an illicit society. Origen replies that Christians do have altars, statues, and temples — but spiritual ones:

Celsus then proceeds to say that "we shrink from raising altars, statues, and temples; and this," he thinks, "has been agreed upon among us as the badge or distinctive mark of a secret and forbidden society." He does not perceive that we regard the spirit of every good man as an altar from which arises an incense which is truly and spiritually sweet-smelling, namely, the prayers ascending from a pure conscience. … And the statues and gifts which are fit offerings to God are the work of no common mechanics, but are wrought and fashioned in us by the Word of God, to wit, the virtues in which we imitate "the First-born of all creation."

— Against Celsus 8.17, New Advent (cf. full text).2 He then draws the distinction sharply: Christians do not object to temples as such, but "refuse to build lifeless temples to the Giver of all life," because "our bodies are the temple of God" — and the abstention is not a conspirator's badge but obedience to Christ:

And if, further, temples are to be compared with temples … we do refuse to build lifeless temples to the Giver of all life, let any one who chooses learn how we are taught, that our bodies are the temple of God, and that if any one by lust or sin defiles the temple of God, he will himself be destroyed. … It is not therefore true that we object to building altars, statues, and temples, because we have agreed to make this the badge of a secret and forbidden society; but we do so, because we have learnt from Jesus Christ the true way of serving God.

— Against Celsus 8.19–20, New Advent.2

Notes

  • Origen is the most explicit witness on the Place of Christian Worship timeline to a genuine principle behind the absence of church buildings: the de-localizing of worship (God "cannot receive" a house; the temple is the body and the assembly of the faithful). This is exactly the theological strand that the "necessity, not principle" argument concedes did "color" the early church's practice — though Origen's target is the pagan temple-cult and the localizing and imaging of God, not the bare existence of a hall in which Christians might assemble. He nowhere says an assembly building is forbidden; he says a lifeless temple to house or represent God is.
  • Origen wrote the eight books of Against Celsus late in life at Caesarea, c. 248, refuting the True Discourse that the Middle-Platonist Celsus had published against Christianity some seventy years earlier. Because he quotes Celsus at length, the work preserves both the pagan taunt that Christians had no temples or altars and the Christian answer to it.
