John Chrysostom on the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 349–407 (Homilies on Genesis preached at Antioch, c. 386–388) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching through Genesis, Chrysostom reads the episode as a lesson in temperance and filial honour, not race. The vine is not evil, only its abuse; and the puzzle of why Canaan pays for Ham’s sin he answers on two fronts — Ham was spared because already blessed by God, and suffered more through his son than in himself, while Canaan bore the curse for his own sins, each man “liable for the sins he has committed.”

Claims & evidence

The vine is not evil — only its intemperate use

“Perhaps, on the other hand, someone might say, ‘Why was vine dressing, source of such terrible wickedness, introduced into life?’ Do not idly blurt out what comes into your head, O man: vine dressing is not wicked nor is wine evil—rather, it is use of them in excess. You see, dreadful sins arise not from wine as such but from intemperate attitudes of human depravity…”

— Homilies on Genesis 29.10. Chrysostom frames the whole episode moralistically: the sin is intemperance, and Noah himself is excused as the first vintner, ignorant of wine’s strength.

Why Canaan, not Ham? Each is “liable for the sins he has committed”

“To be sure, some will say, this shows that the reason he did not curse Ham was that he had enjoyed blessing from God. Nevertheless, why is it that though Ham was the sinner, Canaan had to pay the penalty? This does not happen idly either. Ham did not endure less punishment than his son. He too felt its effects… Seeing their children bearing punishment proves a more grievous form of chastisement for the fathers than being subject to it themselves… even if in the present instance he bears the curse on account of his father’s sin, nevertheless it was likely that he was atoning for his own failings… After all, for proof that parents are not punished for their children, nor children for their parents, each being liable for the sins he has committed, you can find frequent statements among the inspired authors—as, for instance, when they say, ‘The teeth of the one eating sour grapes shall be set on edge,’ ‘The soul that shall die is the soul that sins,’ and again, ‘Parents shall not die for their children, nor children for their parents.’”

— Homilies on Genesis 29.21. Two answers laid side by side: Ham escapes explicit cursing because God had already blessed him (Genesis 9:1); and Canaan bears the curse partly for his father’s deed but chiefly, Chrysostom insists, for his own future sins — guarding the principle of individual desert (Ezekiel 18; Deuteronomy 24:16) against any notion of an inherited racial doom.

Notes

  • Chrysostom nowhere mentions skin colour, an inherited curse of servitude on a race, or Africa. The curse is servitude, and its resolution is moral and individual — the exact opposite of the later racialized reading weighed on the racial-curse argument page.
  • His “Ham was already blessed, so the curse fell on his son” motif is shared with Ephrem and appears again on the wiki in his reading of Nimrod; it feeds the why-Canaan argument.
