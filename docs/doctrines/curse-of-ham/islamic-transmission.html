The Islamic transmission of the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 7th–14th century (early ḥadīth and qiṣaṣ traditions; al-Ṭabarī d. 923; Ibn Khaldūn d. 1406)
Summary: In the medieval Islamic world the two rabbinic strands — blackness and servitude — are fused for the first time into a single “dual curse” on Ham: he looks at Noah’s nakedness, and Noah prays that his color be changed and his descendants enslaved, making Ham the ancestor of black Africans held in bondage. Yet the same tradition also produced its sharpest early critic: Ibn Khaldūn flatly denied that the Torah says anything about skin color and explained African complexion by climate instead.

Claims & evidence

The “dual curse”: al-Ṭabarī and the storytellers

The early Muslim historians and Tales of the Prophets collections make Noah’s curse fall on Ham himself and join blackness to slavery. In al-Ṭabarī’s History, Noah “prayed that Ham’s color would be changed and that his descendants would be slaves to the children of Shem and Japheth”; in the genealogy of Wahb b. Munabbih, “Ham was the father of the Blacks.”1 An earlier tradition ascribed to Ibn Mas‘ūd runs: “Noah was bathing and saw his son [Ham] looking at him and said to him, ‘Are you watching me bathe? May God change your color!’ And he is the ancestor of the sūdān [the blacks].”2

The historian David Goldenberg’s judgement is that “the new dual-curse interpretation of the Noah story seems to have evolved out of the earlier Muslim dark-skin etiologies… joined to the story of Noah’s curse of slavery” precisely as black African slavery expanded after the conquests — and, tellingly, that “the Muslim writers all put the dual curse on Ham, while the Jewish and Christian authors generally see Canaan as the one who was cursed.”3

Ibn Khaldūn rejects the Ham-curse explanation of blackness

“Some genealogists who had no knowledge of the true nature of things imagined that the Negroes are the children of Ham, the son of Noah, and that they were singled out to be black as the result of Noah’s curse… The curse of Noah upon his son [is found] in the Torah. No reference is made there to blackness. The curse included no more than that Ham’s descendants should be the slaves of his brothers’ descendants.”

— Ibn Khaldūn, Muqaddimah, trans. Franz Rosenthal (Princeton/Bollingen, 1958), vol. 1, “first prefatory discussion.”4 Ibn Khaldūn (d. 1406) reads the Torah correctly — the curse is servitude, and it says nothing of color — and derives dark skin instead from the “composition of the air” in the hot equatorial zones, noting that black populations who settle in temperate zones lighten over generations, and vice versa. It is a striking pre-modern refutation, from within the very tradition that transmitted the dual curse, of the reasoning that would later prop up the racial curse.

Notes

  • The dual-curse fusion is the hinge of the whole racialized reading: only when blackness (the rabbinic ark-etiology) is welded to servitude (Noah’s actual curse) does “the curse of Ham” become a doctrine of racial slavery — and that weld is medieval, not biblical.
  • The al-Ṭabarī wording is Brinner’s SUNY translation verbatim (Prophets and Patriarchs, p. 14 for the color/slavery prayer, p. 11 for “the father of the Blacks” — two distinct passages, the second from the genealogy of Wahb b. Munabbih). The Ibn Mas‘ūd bathing tradition is not in al-Ṭabarī’s History; it is cited via Goldenberg to its own chain (Ibn Ḥakīm), as are the Tales of the Prophets quotations.
