The pro-slavery weaponization of the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 1840–1930 (antebellum American pro-slavery apologetic; the colonial “Hamitic hypothesis”)
Summary: This is the load-bearing modern reception — the point at which the medieval fusion of blackness and servitude was pressed into a system: Genesis 9:25 as the divine charter for the enslavement of black Africans. American pro-slavery writers made Noah’s curse the “first ordination” of slavery and stretched it from Canaan to the whole “Hamite race”; colonial anthropology then inverted the same racial category into the “Hamitic hypothesis,” with lethal downstream effects. That this reading was influential is a fact of history; that it is true is a separate question, answered on the racial-curse argument page — and the answer is no.

Claims & evidence

Thornton Stringfellow — the curse as the “first recorded” ordination of slavery

“The first recorded language which was ever uttered in relation to slavery, is the inspired language of Noah. In God’s stead he says, ‘Cursed be Canaan;’ ‘a servant of servants shall he be to his brethren.’… God decreed slavery—and shows in that decree, tokens of good-will to the master.”

— Thornton Stringfellow, A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery (1841; here the 1856 Scriptural and Statistical Views), Documenting the American South. Stringfellow, a Virginia Baptist minister, made Genesis 9 the opening proof-text of the “Bible argument” and read the curse as a standing divine decree, not a mere prediction.

Josiah Priest — the curse stretched from Canaan to “the whole Hamite race”

“the curse of Noah upon Ham had a general and an interminable application to the whole Hamite race, in placing them under a peculiar liability of being enslaved by the races of the two other brothers.”

— Josiah Priest, Slavery, As It Relates to the Negro, or African Race (Albany, 1843), archive.org (reissued 1851 as Bible Defence of Slavery). Priest performs the two decisive moves the text forbids: he transfers the curse from Canaan to Ham (repeatedly glossing the verse “Canaan (Ham)”), and he extends it from a named people to a race — the exegetical sleight the whole edifice depends on.

John Henry Hopkins and Frederick A. Ross — the reading made respectable

“The first appearance of slavery in the Bible is the wonderful prediction of the patriarch Noah: ‘Cursed be Canaan, a servant of servants shall he be to his brethren…’ (Gen. 9:25.)… The Almighty, foreseeing this total degradation of the race, ordained them to servitude or slavery under the descendants of Shem and Japhet, doubtless because he judged it to be their fittest condition.”

— John Henry Hopkins (Episcopal bishop of Vermont), Bible View of Slavery (1863), archive.org. The Presbyterian Frederick A. Ross, in Slavery Ordained of God (1857), likewise reads Ham “cursed to render service, forever,” and maps the three sons onto three continents (“God sent Ham to Africa, Shem to Asia, Japheth to Europe”) — though Ross leans more on Romans 13 than on Genesis 9.

A caution against over-claiming: not every pro-slavery divine rested on the curse. James Henley Thornwell’s governing text was Colossians 4:1, and Benjamin Palmer argued a “providential trust,” not a curse-of-Ham exegesis; both are witnesses to the milieu more than to this proof-text. And Samuel Cartwright’s pseudo-medical racism is often said to invoke the curse, but the exact primary wording is not yet located here — noted, not quoted.

The “Hamitic hypothesis” — the same racial category, inverted

The traveler John Hanning Speke read the curse straight off the peoples of the Nile: the African is “our poor elder brother Ham… cursed by his father, and condemned to be the slave of both Shem and Japheth… a strikingly existing proof of the Holy Scriptures.”1 But once European scholarship wished to claim Egypt’s high civilization for a “white” origin, the same Hamitic category was flipped from cursed to superior. In C. G. Seligman’s influential Races of Africa (1930), “the civilizations of Africa are the civilizations of the Hamites… The incoming Hamites were pastoral ‘Europeans’—arriving wave after wave—better armed as well as quicker witted than the dark agricultural Negroes.”2 As Edith Sanders showed, “Hamite” was a chameleon term — a badge of the cursed slave before 1800, and after Napoleon’s Egyptian campaign a badge of the light-skinned civilizer.3 The inverted form was not harmless: cast onto East Africa, the myth of the Tutsi as alien “Hamitic” conquerors of the Bantu Hutu helped prime the racial logic of the 1994 Rwandan genocide.4
