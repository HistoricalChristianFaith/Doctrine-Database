Modern scholarship and the repudiation of the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 1969–present (Sanders 1969; Peterson 1978; Haynes 2002; Goldenberg 2003/2017; SBC 1995; LDS 1978/2013)
Summary: The modern study of the passage converges on a single result: the racial “curse of Ham” is a post-biblical construction, not a reading the Genesis text supplies. Historians of interpretation (Peterson, Haynes, Johnson) trace how the myth was built and used; a philologist (Goldenberg) shows the two pillars it rests on — the “Ham = black” etymology and the blackness-as-slavery fusion — to be false and late; and the churches that most invoked it (the Southern Baptists, the Latter-day Saints) have formally repudiated it.

Claims & evidence

Goldenberg: the text is silent on color, and the etymology is false

“The Bible says nothing about skin color in the story of Noah, but this feature was somehow woven into the biblical text… Even putting aside for the moment the fact that according to the Bible it was Canaan, not Ham, who was cursed, why was Ham identified with black Africa?”

— David M. Goldenberg, “Black and Slave: the Origins and History of the Curse of Ham” (2017), summarizing The Curse of Ham: Race and Slavery in Early Judaism, Christianity, and Islam (Princeton, 2003; open access at Project MUSE). Goldenberg’s two decisive philological findings: there is “no etymological relationship” between the name Ham and the Hebrew words for “black,” “dark,” or “hot” — the belief that there was is itself an ancient error — and the black-skin etiology and the slavery etiology were originally separate, fused into a single “dual curse” only in the medieval Near East as black African slavery expanded.

Peterson and Haynes: how the antebellum myth was constructed and used

Thomas Virgil Peterson’s Ham and Japheth (1978) documents that “white southern Christians overwhelmingly thought that Ham was the aboriginal black man,” resting the belief partly on the mistaken “Ham = black/hot” etymology — a world-defining myth that sacralized the plantation order rather than an exegesis of the text.1 Stephen R. Haynes’s Noah’s Curse (2002) shows the reading was organized around Southern codes of honour and order — Ham as the type of dishonour (and so “fit” for slavery) — and that “by the 1830s Noah’s curse had become a stock weapon in the arsenal of slavery’s apologists”; he concludes the text still “requires a cure.”2 Sylvester Johnson’s The Myth of Ham (2004) extends the story to the irony that black Christians too inherited Ham as a racial ancestor and had to wrestle with the stigma.3

The ecclesial repudiations

The churches that leaned hardest on the curse have disowned it. The Southern Baptist Convention, at its 150th anniversary, resolved that “many of our Southern Baptist forbears defended the right to own slaves,” apologized “to all African-Americans for condoning and/or perpetuating individual and systemic racism,” and “genuinely repent[ed] of racism.”4 The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which had barred those of black African descent from its priesthood on a curse-of-Cain/Ham rationale, lifted the ban by revelation in 1978 and in 2013 “disavow[ed] the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse… or that blacks or people of any other race or ethnicity are inferior in any way to anyone else.”5

Notes

  • Modern scholarship and the earliest Fathers land in the same place: the curse is Canaan’s, is servitude, and names a people. The racial reading is the anomaly — a medieval fusion weaponized in the modern West. This is the evidentiary spine of the racial-curse argument.
