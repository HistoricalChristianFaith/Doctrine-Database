Augustine of Hippo on the Curse of Ham

Dates: 354–430 (City of God, c. 413–426; Against Julian, c. 421) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine makes Noah’s curse the locus classicus for the doctrine that slavery is not natural but penal — the first appearance of the word “slave” in Scripture, introduced “by sin and not by nature.” No one is a slave by nature or by race; servitude is a consequence of sin under God’s just order. He treats the passage typologically and, separately, uses it to illustrate that descendants may bear the temporal effects of a forebear’s sin.

Claims & evidence

Slavery is penal, from sin — not from nature

“God… did not intend that His rational creature, who was made in His image, should have dominion over anything but the irrational creation,—not man over man, but man over the beasts… And this is why we do not find the word ‘slave’ in any part of Scripture until righteous Noah branded the sin of his son with this name. It is a name, therefore, introduced by sin and not by nature… The prime cause, then, of slavery is sin, which brings man under the dominion of his fellow… But by nature, as God first created us, no one is the slave either of man or of sin. This servitude is, however, penal.”

— City of God 19.15. This is the single most consequential patristic reading of the passage: Augustine derives from it not a warrant for enslaving anyone but a theology in which slavery is a disorder introduced by sin, contrary to creation. Nothing in it attaches the curse to a race or a colour.

Descendants may bear the temporal effects of a forebear’s sin

“Why did Ham sin and yet vengeance was declared against his son Canaan? Why was the son of Solomon punished by the breaking up of the kingdom?… How do we read in the sacred books, ‘Returning the iniquity of the fathers into the bosom of their children after them’…? Then carnal generation even of the people of God of the Old Testament binds children for the sins of their parents.”

— Against Julian 6.25.82. Augustine offers the transmitted-consequence answer to the why-Canaan puzzle — a principle of corporate solidarity in temporal penalty, held alongside (not against) individual desert for eternal guilt.

Notes

  • Augustine’s “penal, not natural” reading was, ironically, later wrenched into the antebellum defence of chattel slavery — but his own point is the reverse: no man is a slave by nature, and the master’s pride harms him more than the servant’s lowliness harms the servant.
