The rabbinic tradition on the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 3rd–6th century (Amoraic traditions in the Babylonian Talmud and Genesis Rabbah, redacted before the mid-6th c.)
Summary: The classical rabbinic sources carry two traditions not found in the Genesis text: an account of what Ham did to Noah (castration or sexual abuse, not looking alone), and — in a separate tradition in Sanhedrin 108b — the idea that Ham was punished by his skin turning black. They are the earliest attestations on this timeline linking Ham to dark skin; in Sanhedrin 108b the blackening is told as a punishment for the ark-sin, apart from Noah’s curse of slavery.

Claims & evidence

What did Ham do? Castration or sexual abuse

“Rav and Shmuel [disagree]. One says that Ham castrated Noah, and one says that Ham sodomized him… The one who says that Ham castrated Noah [reasons that] since he prevented him from having a fourth son, Noah cursed him by [cursing] his fourth son [Canaan].”

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 70a (William Davidson translation). The sages read “saw the nakedness of his father” (Genesis 9:22) as a euphemism, and the “youngest son” and “fourth son” language of the curse as its clue — the castration reading in particular accounts for why Canaan, Ham’s fourth son, bears it (Ham deprived Noah of a fourth son, so Noah struck Ham’s fourth son).

The color-coding: Ham “smitten in his skin” for violating the ark

“The Sages taught: Three violated that directive and engaged in intercourse while in the ark, and all of them were punished for doing so. They are: The dog, and the raven, and Ham, son of Noah… Ham was afflicted in that his skin turned black.”

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 108b (Davidson; Aramaic Ḥam laqah be-‘oro, “Ham was smitten in his skin”). In this passage the punishment belongs to a separate aggadah about intercourse on the ark, and the thing inherited is dark skin, not servitude. Genesis Rabbah preserves the same tradition with a play on the assumed etymology of Ham’s name:

“You prevented me from performing an act that is done in darkness… therefore, that man [Canaan] will be ugly and darkened… So, Ḥam and the dog engaged in sexual relations in the ark, and that is why Ḥam emerged darkened.”

— Genesis Rabbah 36:7 (Sefaria). Here the darkening is joined to Noah’s words about Canaan by a play on darkness; the philologist David Goldenberg rejects any etymology of the name “Ham” as “dark/hot.”

Reception and debate

  • Two etiologies. The philologist David Goldenberg holds that the skin tradition (Ham blackened for the ark-sin) and the slavery tradition (Canaan cursed with servitude for Ham’s deed to Noah) were originally separate, and that their fusion — blackness as the curse of slavery on Ham’s African line — came later, in the Islamic sources.
  • The rabbinic castration/abuse reading is a different register from the Christian typological reading (Noah’s exposure as a figure of Christ’s passion) held by Bede, Cyprian, and Jerome.
