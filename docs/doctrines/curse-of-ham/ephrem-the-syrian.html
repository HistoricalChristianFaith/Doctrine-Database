Ephrem the Syrian on the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 306–373 (Commentary on Genesis) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading Genesis in Syriac, close to the Hebrew, Ephrem confronts the difficulty head-on — “what sin could Canaan have committed?” — and preserves the tradition that Canaan himself was the first to see and report Noah’s nakedness, so that the curse falls on the actual culprit, not on an innocent grandson. He then reads the servitude as fulfilled historically in Joshua’s conquest of the Canaanites.

Claims & evidence

The difficulty stated, and Canaan named as the culprit

“Noah cursed Canaan, saying, ‘Cursed be Canaan. A slave of slaves shall he be to his brothers.’ But what sin could Canaan have committed even if he had been right behind his father when Ham observed the nakedness of Noah? Some say that because Ham had been blessed along with those who entered the ark and came out of it, Noah did not curse Ham himself… Others, however, say from the fact that Scripture says, ‘Noah knew everything that his youngest son had done to him,’ it is clear that it was not Ham who observed his nakedness, for Ham was the middle son and not the youngest. For this reason they say that Canaan, the youngest, told of the nakedness of the old man. Then Ham went out and jokingly told his brothers. For this reason then, even though it might be thought that Canaan was cursed unjustly… still he was cursed justly for he was not cursed in the place of another.”

— Commentary on Genesis 7.3.1–2. Ephrem records both current answers — Ham spared because blessed, and Canaan the real offender read out of “his youngest son” (Ham being the middle son) — and leans to the second: Canaan is cursed for what Canaan did.

The servitude fulfilled in the conquest under Joshua

“After Ham had been cursed through his one son, Noah blessed Shem and Japheth… And God dwelt in the tent of Abraham, the descendant of Shem, and Canaan became their slave when in the days of Joshua son of Nun, the Israelites destroyed the dwelling places of Canaan and pressed their leaders into bondage.”

— Commentary on Genesis 7.4.1. The curse is discharged not on a race of Africans but on the Canaanite peoples of the land, subdued by Israel — the reading shared by Justin, Bede, and the mainstream of the Fathers.

Notes

  • Ephrem’s “Canaan was the actual offender” solution is one of the two main patristic answers to the why-Canaan puzzle; his statement of the difficulty (“what sin could Canaan have committed?”) is the sharpest in the Fathers.
  • No skin-colour reading appears; the servitude is territorial-political (the conquest), an exegesis incompatible with the later racial curse.
  • Ephrem is elsewhere on the wiki the great voice for the favourable reading of “before the LORD” in the Nimrod tradition — another of Ham’s line (Cush→Nimrod).
