Justin Martyr on the Curse of Ham

Dates: c. 100–165 (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 155–160) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving Christian reading of the passage. Justin makes Noah’s words a prophecy of national history: the curse falls on Canaan (not the God-blessed Ham), and is fulfilled when Shem’s line (Israel) dispossesses the Canaanites and Japheth’s line in turn dispossesses Israel. Servitude is the subjugation of one people by another, and salvation in Christ is offered to “all men everywhere, whether bond or free.”

Claims & evidence

Ham is spared because God blessed him; the curse begins with his son

“For the Spirit of prophecy would not curse the son that had been by God blessed along with [his brothers]. But since the punishment of the sin would cleave to the whole descent of the son that mocked at his father’s nakedness, he made the curse originate with his son.”

— Dialogue with Trypho 139. Justin’s answer to the why-Canaan puzzle: Ham cannot be cursed because he stands under the blessing of Genesis 9:1, so the penalty descends to his line through Canaan.

The curse is fulfilled in the history of nations, not in a race

“For you, who have derived your lineage from Shem, invaded the territory of the sons of Canaan by the will of God; and you possessed it. And it is manifest that the sons of Japheth, having invaded you in turn by the judgment of God, have taken your land from you, and have possessed it… to the two peoples there was the one people of Canaan handed over for servants… And hence all men everywhere, whether bond or free, who believe in Christ… know that they shall be with Him in that land.”

— Dialogue with Trypho 139. The servitude is the historical subjection of the Canaanite people — first to Israel, then Israel’s land to Japheth’s (the Greeks/Romans) — and its horizon is the gospel offered to bond and free alike. Nothing here attaches the curse to skin colour or to Africa.

Notes

  • Justin sets the pattern the Fathers keep: the curse is Canaan’s, and it is political servitude of a named people, read off the conquest — the same reading in Ephrem and Bede. This is the antithesis of the later racial curse.
