Proclus of Constantinople on Theotokos

Dates: 430 (Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos, preached in the Great Church of Constantinople) · archbishop of Constantinople 434–446 · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching a feast of the Virgin in the Great Church of Constantinople — with Nestorius present, according to the title the homily carries — Proclus praised Mary as Theotokos and grounded the title in the paradox of the Incarnation.

Claims & evidence

Homily 1: a festival of the Virgin

The Virgin’s festival, my brethren, summons us today to words of praise, and the present feast has benefits to bestow on those who assemble to keep it. And surely this is right, for its subject is chastity.

— Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos, trans. Nicholas Constas, source.1 The homily takes its title from the disputed word, and its argument joins praise of Mary to Christology: Mary is called “God-bearer” because the one she bore was God the Word made flesh — “the natures came together and the union remained unconfused.” Where Nestorius ranked Theotokos below Christotokos, Proclus preached the title from the capital’s chief pulpit.

Occasion and later career

The homily’s title, in Constas’s translation, reads “Delivered while Nestorius was seated in the Great Church of Constantinople”.1 The introduction on the same page states that the homily was “most likely delivered on the day after Christmas” and that it was “Delivered in 430”;1 Wikipedia, citing Constas, likewise dates it to 430, “on the occasion of a Feast Day of the Theotokos.”2 Andrew Radde-Gallwitz, introducing the homily in the Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings (2022), writes that Proclus “was bishop of Cyzicus in 430 when he proclaimed this homily in the presence of his archbishop, Nestorius”: “The occasion, it appears, was the feast of the Virgin that had recently been instituted in Constantinople for December 26.”3 On that dating it falls shortly after Cyril of Alexandria’s Third Letter to Nestorius with its anathematisms (November 430)4 and before the Council of Ephesus (431). Proclus later held the see of Constantinople (434–446).
