Alexander of Alexandria on Theotokos

Dates: c. 324 (the letter) · bishop of Alexandria c. 313–328 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his letter against Arius he calls Mary “Mother of God,” in a passage on the Son who took a real body from her without “detriment to His divinity.” It is the earliest literary use of the title whose attribution is not disputed in the sources surveyed here.

Claims & evidence

Christ “carried a body, of Mary Mother of God”

After this we know of the resurrection of the dead, the first-fruits of which was our Lord Jesus Christ, who in very deed, and not in appearance merely, carried a body, of Mary Mother of God, who in the end of the world came to the human race to put away sin, was crucified and died, and yet did He not thus perceive any detriment to His divinity, being raised from the dead, taken up into heaven, seated at the right hand of majesty.

— Epistles on the Arian Heresy, source.1 The phrase stands in an argument about Christ: the one born, crucified, and raised did not “perceive any detriment to His divinity.” Whether the title is chiefly a statement about the child or also an honour paid to the mother is a disputed reading.

Notes

  • This is the earliest datable use not disputed in the sources surveyed here of “Mother of God” (Θεοτόκος) in a literary source. Earlier claimed uses — Hippolytus, Origen (via Socrates, HE 7.32), Dionysius of Alexandria, Pierius, Peter of Alexandria — are textually disputed; see Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century?
  • The word’s antiquity in prayer (rather than in doctrinal prose) turns on the fragment Sub Tuum Praesidium / P.Ryl. 470, whose disputed dating is set out on the prayer to the saints timeline; that debate bears on when the title was invoked, this page on when it was argued.
