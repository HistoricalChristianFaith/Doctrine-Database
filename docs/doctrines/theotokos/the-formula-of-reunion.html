The Formula of Reunion on Theotokos

Dates: 433 (the reconciliation of Cyril of Alexandria with John of Antioch and the Eastern bishops, two years after Ephesus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephesus (431) had deposed Nestorius but left Cyril and the Antiochene bishops under John of Antioch in mutual excommunication. The Formula of Reunion — an Antiochene creed that Cyril accepted in his letter Laetentur caeli (“Let the heavens rejoice”) — healed the schism. In it the Antiochene bishops, who had opposed Cyril’s anathemas, confess “Theotokos” (a title Theodoret of Cyrus had already confessed in his reply to those anathemas) in a creed that also speaks of “a union of two natures.”

Claims & evidence

The reunited churches confess Mary “Mother of God”

We confess, therefore, our Lord Jesus Christ, the Only Begotten Son of God, perfect God, and perfect Man of a reasonable soul and flesh consisting; begotten before the ages of the Father according to his Divinity, and in the last days, for us and for our salvation, of Mary the Virgin according to his humanity, of the same substance with his Father according to his Divinity, and of the same substance with us according to his humanity; for there became a union of two natures. Wherefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord.

According to this understanding of this unmixed union, we confess the holy Virgin to be Mother of God; because God the Word was incarnate and became Man, and from this conception he united the temple taken from her with himself.

— the Formula of Reunion, embedded in Cyril’s Letter to John of Antioch, source. The creed the Eastern bishops had drafted, and which Cyril accepted, confesses Mary Mother of God because “God the Word was incarnate and became Man.” It keeps the Antiochene “temple” (naos) image — the humanity as the temple the Word “united… with himself” — alongside the confession of “one Christ, one Son, one Lord.”

The Antiochene bishops and the title

The letter opens on Cyril’s note of jubilation — “Let the heavens rejoice, and let the earth be glad” — because the schism between Alexandria and Antioch was over.1 The Antiochenes — John of Antioch, and with him Theodoret of Cyrus, who had written a point-by-point Reproof of Cyril’s anathemas — had opposed Cyril’s anathemas. In that Reproof (c. 431) Theodoret had already confessed the Virgin Theotokos, “not because she gave birth in natural manner to God, but to man united to the God that had fashioned Him.”2 In the Formula the Antiochenes confess the word within a creed of their own drafting.

The rule for the sayings about Christ

make some things of the Evangelical and Apostolic teaching about the Lord common as pertaining to the one person, and other things they divide as to the two natures, and attribute the worthy ones to God on account of the Divinity of Christ, and the lowly ones on account of his humanity.

— the same letter. The letter sets out a rule for reading Scripture: some sayings are referred to the one person, others divided between the two natures, the exalted ones to the Divinity and the lowly to the humanity. Compare Leo’s Tome (449) and the Definition of Chalcedon (451).

Notes

  • On how Nestorius’s own position relates to the Antiochene creed, see the disputed reading Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?
  • Nestorius himself was not restored by the reunion. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that the creed “was apparently drawn up by Theodoret” and that Theodoret “did not enter into the agreement himself because he was not willing to condemn Nestorius as Cyril demanded”; it dates his reconciliation with John of Antioch to “about 435.”3 Theodoret was restored at Chalcedon (451) after anathematizing Nestorius by name.
