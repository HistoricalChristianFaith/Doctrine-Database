Tertullian on Theotokos

Dates: c. 210 (the North-African advocate flourished c. 197–220; On the Flesh of Christ, the anti-docetic treatise that carries this passage, is dated c. 206–212) · Wikipedia
Summary: Arguing against Marcion and the docetists that Christ took real flesh, Tertullian reads Isaiah’s sign of the virgin conceiving as fulfilled in “Emmanuel, God with us,” and writes of the child born of Mary that “in this man God was born.” He does not use the word Theotokos, whose earliest undisputed literary use is in Alexander of Alexandria (c. 324).

Claims & evidence

On the Flesh of Christ 17: the virgin bore “Emmanuel, God with us”

He who was going to consecrate a new order of birth, must Himself be born after a novel fashion, concerning which Isaiah foretold how that the Lord Himself would give the sign. What, then, is the sign? “Behold a virgin shall conceive and bear a son.” Accordingly, a virgin did conceive and bear “Emmanuel, God with us.” This is the new nativity; a man is born in God. And in this man God was born, taking the flesh of an ancient race, without the help, however, of the ancient seed…

— On the Flesh of Christ 17, source.1 The context is anti-docetic polemic: Tertullian is arguing against Marcion and the Valentinians — who made Christ’s flesh a phantom — that the Son of God was really born of the Virgin. His warrant is the Emmanuel prophecy (Isaiah 7:14), which he takes to be fulfilled at the Nativity: the virgin “did conceive and bear ‘Emmanuel, God with us.’” The sentence that follows, “a man is born in God. And in this man God was born,” speaks of the one born of the Virgin as both man and God.

Notes

  • Tertullian never uses the word Theotokos (“God-bearer”), whose earliest undisputed literary use is in Alexander of Alexandria (c. 324); earlier uses are claimed and disputed. On the word’s prehistory, see the disputed reading Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century?; on what the title asserts, Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?
  • The very same paragraph of On the Flesh of Christ 17 supplies Tertullian’s statement of the Eve–Mary typology (“As Eve had believed the serpent, so Mary believed the angel”), for which he appears on the Mary as the New Eve timeline; here the interest is only in the God-bearing confession that opens the paragraph.
  • Tertullian also appears as a witness on several other timelines (the canon, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and others); here the interest is only in this passage. His later turn to the Montanist “New Prophecy,” and how it was judged, is the subject of the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”.
