Did the title “Theotokos” derive from the cult of Isis?

Disputed reading · on Theotokos (Mary as Mother of God) · witness: Alexander of Alexandria

Related readings: Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century? · Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?

The passage

After this we know of the resurrection of the dead, the first-fruits of which was our Lord Jesus Christ, who in very deed, and not in appearance merely, carried a body, of Mary Mother of God, who in the end of the world came to the human race to put away sin, was crucified and died, and yet did He not thus perceive any detriment to His divinity, being raised from the dead, taken up into heaven, seated at the right hand of majesty.

— Alexander of Alexandria, Epistles on the Arian Heresy, source.1

The question

The earliest undisputed literary uses of Theotokos are Alexandrian. Interpreters differ on whether the Christian title, or the devotion and imagery that grew up around it, came from the cult of the Egyptian goddess Isis, honoured as mother of the divine child Horus and shown nursing him, and on whether title and imagery should be treated together.2

Goddess imagery and language adapted into Marian devotion

In brief: Marian devotion, grounded in Christian revelation, took over images, places and words from the goddess cults around it, including the image of Isis with Horus.

Held by: Jean Daniélou (Catholic, 1949); Elizabeth A. Johnson (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1989); Thomas F. Mathews and Norman Muller (art historians, 2005) ⚠ unverified; Sabrina Higgins (2012) ⚠ unverified.3

Elizabeth Johnson, summarizing Daniélou’s study, writes that Daniélou “begins by stressing the radical distinction between the mystery cults and the Christian veneration of Mary,” and then examines “the ways in which Christianity’s Marian cult adapted elements from the mystery cults.” In her own account, “the iconography of Mary seated on a royal throne presenting her child to the world was patterned on the pose of Isis with Horus,” and “in hymns reminiscent of the aretalogies of Isis, Mary was praised with titles and attributes of female deities.” Shrines of the goddess were “rededicated to Mary the Mother of God,” and she judges it “generally conceded to be no accident that the doctrine of the Theotokos was proclaimed to an enthusiastic population” at Ephesus, the city of Diana. “For all the real differences in structure and content between Christian faith and the mystery cults,” she concludes, “the evidence at hand indicates a strong process of assimilation and adaptation of ideas and verbal and artistic imagery in the case of the emerging cult of Mary.”4 Among art historians, Mathews and Muller are reported to argue that Isis’s pose in late antique panel paintings influenced several types of Marian icons, inside and outside Egypt (⚠ unverified). Higgins, as quoted in a 2025 summary of her study, finds that “Tran Tam Tinh’s observation that iconographic similarities between Isis and Mary are confined to the ‘lactans’ type remains valid,” and that the Egyptian evidence “does not justify the conclusion that a deliberate adoption took place between the cults of Isis and Mary” (⚠ unverified).5 On this reading the influence runs through imagery and the language of praise, within a cult whose object and grounds are Christian.

A title arising within Christian teaching

In brief: The word Theotokos came out of the Church’s own confession about Christ, and the goddess cults supplied at most surface traits.

Held by: Hugo Rahner (Catholic, Greek Myths and Christian Mysteries, 1963); John McGuckin (Orthodox archpriest and church historian, “The Early Cult of Mary and Inter-Religious Contexts in the Fifth-Century Church,” 2008) ⚠ unverified; Stephen J. Shoemaker (historian, Mary in Early Christian Faith and Devotion, 2016).6

Hugo Rahner argued, in Johnson’s summary, “for the essential differences” between the goddess cults and the veneration of Mary, while granting that similarities “in superficially observable matters are simply a matter of historical fact.”7 McGuckin is reported to hold that Christians understood such depictions of Mary, the nursing Madonna among them, within their own cultural system from the start, and that the similarities with Isis are incidental (⚠ unverified).8 Shoemaker, as his reviewer Rachel Fulton Brown reports, sees no need “to go outside the Christian tradition in order to explain Mary’s presence in early Christian devotion”: Marian piety “arose from a logic that was native to the early Christian tradition itself.”9 On this reading Theotokos, a compound of the verb for giving birth, says that the one born of Mary is himself God, and it took its meaning from the Christological controversies in which it was used.

A title with Egyptian and Isiac antecedents

In brief: The Christian title continued, or answered to, the Egyptian language of a divine mother long applied to Isis; Witt and Benko add that Marian devotion drew on her cult.

Held by: G. Giamberardini (Franciscan, Marianum, 1969); R. E. Witt (classicist, Isis in the Graeco-Roman World, 1971) ⚠ unverified; Stephen Benko (historian, The Virgin Goddess, 1993).10

Giamberardini argued that the epithet Theotokos “is derived from Egyptian expressions, preserved in both hieroglyphic and Coptic, for ‘Mother of God’,” and that their associations with Isis obliged Christian writers as early as Origen to explain how Mary could properly bear the name.11 Witt is reported to have seen Isis as the “great forerunner” of Mary, to have compared Mary’s title “Mother of God” to Isis’s epithet “mother of the god,” and to have suggested that converts who had worshipped Isis would have seen Mary in much the same terms as their traditional goddess (⚠ unverified). Benko, drawing on several goddess cults and not Isis alone, proposes “that there is a direct line, unbroken and clearly discernible, from the goddess-cults of the ancients to the reverence paid and eventually the cult accorded to the Virgin Mary.”12 On this reading the Christian title gave a new referent to a long-established Egyptian way of naming a divine mother.
