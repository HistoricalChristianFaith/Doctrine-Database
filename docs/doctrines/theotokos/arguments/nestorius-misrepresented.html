Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?

Disputed reading · on Theotokos (Mary as Mother of God) · witness: Nestorius

Related readings: Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?

The passage

That two natures should be united in one prosôpon makes not two Sons or two Christs, the diversities of each one of the natures being preserved.

— Nestorius, The Bazaar of Heracleides (written in exile, c. 450), Book 2, part 1.1

The question

The Council of Ephesus (431) deposed Nestorius for “impious doctrines,” which Cyril’s party read as dividing the one Christ into two. His own apologia, the Bazaar of Heracleides, survived in a single Syriac manuscript and was published in 1910, and readers since have differed on whether his teaching was the Christology condemned, a different error, or no error at all.2 Sebastian Brock (1996) notes that whether Nestorius taught what was charged “is disputed, and even if he did, then what he really meant by this terminology is far from clear.”3 In 1994 the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, which honours Nestorius, jointly confessed “one Lord Jesus Christ” and stated that past divisions “were due in large part to misunderstandings”; the declaration does not name Nestorius.4 Openly accessible Catholic academic monographs after 1950 on whether Nestorius was rightly condemned were not located; the Catholic reading below is given from the Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) and Benedict XVI (2007). For the third reading, beyond Luther, only a short paper by David R. Boisclair was located; its academic literature was not.

Condemned for a Christology that divided the one Christ

On this reading Nestorius’s union of God and man was a union of two subjects, whatever he intended, and the council condemned what he actually taught.

Held by: Cyril of Alexandria (Third Letter to Nestorius, 430); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Fifth Century, preface 1978); Metropolitan Bishoy (Coptic Orthodox, 2000); Benedict XVI (Catholic, General Audience on Cyril of Alexandria, 2007).5

Cyril’s First Anathematism binds the refusal to confess the Virgin Theotokos, “inasmuch as in the flesh she bore the Word of God made flesh.”6 Nestorius did not withdraw his ranking of the word: as late as his Third Letter to Celestine (430), “the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos.”7 John Chapman grants that Nestorius “fully intended to safeguard the unity of subject in Christ. But he gave wrong explanations as to the unity, and his teaching logically led to two Christs, though he would not have admitted the fact.” The Bazaar, on his reading, “shows no real change of opinion,” and so “he was therefore unavoidably condemned.”8 For Florovsky the recovered book “confirms to a great extent — on the more sophisticated theological level — St. Cyril’s position”: “For Nestorius salvation began and ended with a moral and volitional — but not ontological — unity or union of humanity with God.”9 Philip Schaff, writing as a Protestant historian, holds that “the Antiochian and Nestorian theory amounts therefore, at bottom, to a duality of person in Christ, though without clearly avowing it”: it rightly asserted the two natures but “pressed the distinction of the two natures to double personality.”10 Benedict XVI (2007) gave as “one reason” for Nestorius’s preference for Christotokos “his adherence to the Antiochean type of Christology,” under which “the union between God and man in Christ could no longer be true.”5 For the Coptic Orthodox Church, Metropolitan Bishoy states that Cyril “is and will remain a hero of faith,” and that Nestorius “taught that two persons were united externally according to will and image in Christ and not that the person of the Logos himself became man.”11 On this reading the confession of the Virgin as Theotokos states that the one born of her is the Word himself, and the refusal of it expressed a union of two subjects.

Misunderstood: one Christ in one prosôpon

On this reading Nestorius confessed one Christ, and the “two sons” condemned at Ephesus was a construction his own words reject.

Held by: Nestorius (Bazaar of Heracleides, c. 450); J. F. Bethune-Baker (Anglican, Nestorius and His Teaching, 1908); Friedrich Loofs (Lutheran church historian, Nestorius and His Place in the History of Christian Doctrine, 1914); Milton V. Anastos (historian, “Nestorius Was Orthodox,” 1962).12

In the Bazaar Nestorius makes the confession of one Son a premise — “there are not two Gods the Words or two Sons or two only begottens, but one”13 — and places the union “in the one prosôpon of the Messiah.”14 His stated fear was that “God-bearer” taken alone might “advance to the confusion of natures in the manner of the madness of Apollinaris or Arius”; he did not forbid it, but preferred the word “mentioned by the angels and the gospels.”15 Bethune-Baker, reading the newly recovered text, concluded “that Nestorius was not ‘Nestorian’.” Loofs judged that “Nestorius can therefore be regarded as orthodox according to the Antiochian interpretation of the Chalcedonian definition,” and that “to no other heretic has been done such great injustice”; he was condemned before “the council, whose creed he could have accepted, was held.”16 Anastos wrote: “Nestorius was not only thoroughly and indubitably orthodox, but also in many respects the profoundest and most brilliant theologian of the fifth century.”17 The Assyrian Church of the East has held this view as a living tradition: it commemorates Nestorius with Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia, celebrates an anaphora bearing his name, and states its Christology in Babai the Great’s formula of two qnome in one parsopa.18 Its theologian Mar Bawai Soro has written that the trial at Ephesus “has always been viewed by the Church of the East as unfair and illegal.”19 On this reading the two natures, each preserved, are united in a single prosôpon, and that is the one Christ Nestorius confessed.

One Person confessed, but the communication of properties denied

On this reading Nestorius did hold one Christ in one Person, but erred by refusing to say of God what belongs to Christ’s manhood, and that refusal was rightly condemned.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, On the Councils and the Church, 1539); David R. Boisclair (“Martin Luther on Nestorius and Eutyches in On the Councils and the Church (1539),” 2005, revised 2012).20

Luther read the conciliar histories and rejected the two-persons charge as self-contradictory: “It is sure, however, that Nestorius did not believe that there were two Christs, but only one single Christ… Therefore it must be false and wrong to say that he held Christ to be two Persons.” He located the error elsewhere: “Nestorius’ error, then, is not that he holds Christ to be a mere man or that he makes two Persons of Him; on the contrary he confesses that there are two natures in one Person, but he will not admit a communicatio idiomatum” — the predication of the properties of one nature to the one Person. Luther traced the fault to Nestorius’s want of learning rather than to a hidden doctrine: he was “a proud, unlearned man” who “thinks of Christ in a really serious way, but, in his ignorance, does not know what he is saying.” For Luther, “Mary is the true, natural mother of the child called Jesus Christ, and the true mother and bearer of God,” and by the same rule “we could speak of God dying, God’s suffering, God’s blood, God’s death.” He held that “this council condemned too little of Nestorius’ doctrine. It dealt only with the one idioma, viz., that God was born of Mary.”21 Boisclair, reading Luther against the sources recovered since, judged that his assessment of Nestorius “does not seem to be so wide of the mark” and wrote of “Luther’s correct observation that they [Nestorius and Eutyches] were guilty of the same error run in different ways.”22 On this reading the confession of Mary as Theotokos is one application of the communication of properties, which the council should have extended to the cross and passion of the one Person.
