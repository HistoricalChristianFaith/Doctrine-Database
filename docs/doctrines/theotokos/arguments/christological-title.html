Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?

Disputed reading · on Theotokos (Mary as Mother of God) · witness: John of Damascus

Related readings: Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold? · Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century? · Did the title “Theotokos” derive from the cult of Isis?

The passage

… we call the holy Mary the Mother of God. For this name embraces the whole mystery of the dispensation. For if she who bore Him is the Mother of God, assuredly He Who was born of her is God and likewise also man.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith III.12, source.1

The question

The title names Mary, and the councils that adopted it were defining the person of Christ. Interpreters differ on whether its content is a statement about Christ from which any honour of Mary follows, whether the honour and veneration of Mary belong to the title and helped bring it to the fore, and whether, true as a statement about Christ, it is suitable for use in preaching.

A confession of Christ that is also a title of honour for Mary

On this reading the title both confesses who Christ is and honours his mother, and its use in Marian praise and prayer is part of its meaning.

Held by: Proclus of Constantinople (Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos, 430); the Second Vatican Council (Catholic, Lumen Gentium §66, 1964); Sergei Bulgakov (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, 1935); Stephen J. Shoemaker (historian, lecture, 2017).2

Proclus preached the title at a feast of the Virgin: “The Virgin’s festival, my brethren, summons us today to words of praise” — a homily that joins the confession of the Incarnation to the praise of Mary.3 John of Damascus, refusing “Mother of Christ,” says that name was coined “to bring dishonour on the Mother of God.”4 Sergei Bulgakov, as Kallistos Ware quotes him, writes that love and veneration for the Virgin “is the soul of orthodox piety, its heart, that which warms and animates its entire body. A faith in Christ which does not include the virgin birth and the veneration of his Mother is another faith, another Christianity, from that of the Orthodox Church.”2 The Second Vatican Council states that “from earliest times the Blessed Virgin is honored under the title of Mother of God,” and that “after the Synod of Ephesus the cult of the people of God toward Mary wonderfully increased,” a cult that “differs essentially from the cult of adoration which is offered to the Incarnate Word.”5 John Paul II put the two together: “In proclaiming Mary ‘Mother of God’, the Church in a single phrase professes her belief regarding the Son and the Mother,” the title being “a privileged expression of their faith in the divinity of Christ and their love for the Blessed Virgin.”6 The historian Stephen Shoemaker argues that devotion was itself a cause of the controversy: “there is significant evidence that the controversies of this Third Council were themselves fueled, at least partly, by an already vibrant devotion to the Virgin Mary that established itself in Constantinople,” and that “it was Nestorius’ refusal to call Mary, Theotokos, the God bearer, that seems to have turned the tide of popular opinion against him.”7 Shoemaker also counts among “the earliest witnesses to Christian worship” “a prayer to Mary on a papyrus from Egypt dating to the third or fourth century,” the Sub Tuum Praesidium, which addresses Mary as Theotokos; the papyrus’s date is disputed.8 On this reading the title was prayed as well as argued.

A confession about who Christ is

On this reading the title says that the one born of Mary is God the Word made flesh; it is first a statement about Christ, and any honour it gives Mary follows from that.

Held by: Cyril of Alexandria (Third Letter to Nestorius, 430); Martin Luther (Lutheran, On the Councils and the Church, 1539); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, “The Ever-Virgin Mother of God”); Timothy George (evangelical, Christianity Today, 2003).9

Cyril grounds the title in the Incarnation and excludes a divine origin from Mary: “we also call her Mother of God, not as if the nature of the Word had the beginning of its existence from the flesh.”10 Chalcedon confesses her “the Mother of God according to his manhood.”11 Luther treats the title as a case of the communication of properties between Christ’s natures: “We, too, know very well that Christ did not derive His deity from Mary; but it does not follow that it must, therefore, be false to say, ‘God was born of Mary’”; Mary is “the true mother and bearer of God.”12 The Lutheran Formula of Concord and the Reformed Heinrich Bullinger say the same, on the ground that she bore no mere man.13 For Florovsky, “The name Theotokos is an inevitable sequel to the name Theanthropos, the God-Man. Both stand and fall together”; it “was really a key-word to the whole of Christology.”14 Kallistos Ware writes that it “speaks not so much about the person of Mary as about the person of Christ,” and that the fifth-century conflict “was concerned not so much with the person of Mary as with the doctrine of the Incarnation.”15 Elizabeth’s greeting of Mary as “the mother of my Lord” (Luke 1:43) is read of Christ by Cosmas Indicopleustes and Theophylact, and the Catechism of the Catholic Church runs that verse into the title, because the one she conceived “was none other than the Father’s eternal Son”; the Anglican–Roman Catholic commission writes that “the real issue in the question of Mary’s title was the unity of Christ’s person.”16 Timothy George puts it for evangelicals: “The purpose of the title was not so much to exalt Mary as to assert the unity of divinity and humanity in her son.”17

True as a statement about Christ, unsuitable for the pulpit

On this reading the doctrine the title expresses is true, but the phrase “Mother of God” is better not used in preaching, because hearers take it as an exaltation of Mary.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, letter to the French Church in London, 1552).18 A scholarly or confessional statement of this reading beyond Calvin’s letter was not located among the sources surveyed here.

Calvin rebuked those in the London church who had attacked the phrase out of “ignorance,” but wrote that its regular use in sermons was another matter: “I cannot conceal that that title being commonly attributed to the Virgin in sermons is disapproved, and, for my own part, I cannot think such language either right, or becoming, or suitable.” He compares it to speaking “of the blood, of the head, and of the death of God”: “the Scriptures accustom us to a different style,” and in this case “to call the Virgin Mary the mother of God, can only serve to confirm the ignorant in their superstitions.”19 Timothy George reports that “most evangelicals today would agree with Calvin in finding ‘mother of God’ language strange, if not inappropriate,” while noting that Calvin “embraced the doctrinal truth this title was meant to convey.”20 On this reading the concern is pastoral: the doctrine stands, and the question is what the phrase does to hearers who already honour Mary too highly.
