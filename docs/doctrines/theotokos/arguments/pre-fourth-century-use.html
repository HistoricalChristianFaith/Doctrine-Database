Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century?

Disputed reading · on Theotokos (Mary as Mother of God) · witness: Alexander of Alexandria

Related readings: Did the title “Theotokos” derive from the cult of Isis? · Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?

The passage

Origen also in the first volume of his Commentaries on the apostle’s epistle to the Romans, gives an ample exposition of the sense in which the term Theotocos is used.

— Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History VII.32, source.1

The question

Socrates wrote this in answering Nestorius’s presbyter Anastasius, who had preached, “Let no one call Mary Theotocos.”2 The earliest use of the word whose attribution is not disputed is in Alexander of Alexandria’s letter against Arius (c. 324). Uses have also been claimed for Origen, Hippolytus, Dionysius of Alexandria, Pierius and Peter of Alexandria, and for the prayer Sub Tuum Praesidium in the papyrus P.Ryl. III 470. Whether any of these places the title before the fourth century is disputed.

In use in the third century

On this reading the title was already current in Alexandria in the third century, in theology and in prayer, and the fourth-century writers inherited it.

Held by: Socrates Scholasticus (Ecclesiastical History, c. 439); E. Lobel (papyrologist, 1938); G. Giamberardini (Franciscan, Marianum, 1969); Stephen J. Shoemaker (historian, Mary in Early Christian Faith and Devotion, 2016).3

Socrates names earlier users of the word to show that Nestorius had not read the ancients: Origen’s exposition in his Commentary on Romans, and Eusebius, who in the Life of Constantine speaks of “the God-bearing virgin.”4 A notice credited to the historian Philip of Side records that Pierius of Alexandria wrote a work on the Mother of God (peri tes theotokou), listed among his sermons in the Catholic Encyclopedia.5 A fragment of Peter of Alexandria (d. 311) speaks of Christ “born according to the flesh of our holy and glorious lady, Mother of God, and Ever-Virgin,” and Giamberardini cited it as the first instance of the word.6 Giamberardini argued further that the word answered to Egyptian expressions for “Mother of God,” whose associations with Isis “would have obliged Origen to explain how Mary could properly be considered ‘God-bearer’.”7 The prayer evidence points the same way. The papyrologist E. Lobel, on palaeographic grounds, dated the papyrus of the Sub Tuum Praesidium, a prayer addressed to Mary as Theotokos, to the third century, “most probably… between 250 and 280,” and Shoemaker dates it to the third century.8 By the fourth century, as Theodore de Bruyn observes, writers use the term “incidentally and without comment, suggesting that the term had been accepted into theological usage by then”; on this reading that acceptance points to an earlier period of use, in which, as Socrates reports, Origen expounded the word.9

First securely attested in the fourth century

On this reading each earlier attribution rests on a text that is lost, later, or of disputed authorship, and the earliest secure use is Alexander’s.

Held by: Eduard Schwartz (on ps.-Dionysius, 1927); Hans Förster (papyrologist, 1995 and 2005); Theodore de Bruyn (patristics scholar, 2015); Roger Pearse (2023).10

De Bruyn states that “the earliest undisputed instance is in a letter of Alexander of Alexandria,” where Christ “carried a body, of Mary Mother of God.”11 The Commentary on Romans survives in Rufinus’s Latin, and the editor of its English translation notes that the quotation Socrates gives “is from Book 1 of the Commentary but does not correspond to Rufinus’s translation”; the Greek catena fragments ascribed to Origen were compiled when the word had become a badge of loyalty, and Pearse identifies one as Eusebius’s. Pearse concludes that “there is no reliable evidence that Origen referred to Mary as the ‘Mother of God’.”12 The Hippolytan discourse On the End of the World is printed among the “Dubious and Spurious Pieces.”13 The Letter to Paul of Samosata ascribed to Dionysius refers to the Council of Nicaea; N. Bonwetsch and G. Bardy assigned it to an Apollinarist of the late fourth or early fifth century, and Schwartz, its editor, called its author a “bungler.”14 The Pierius notice comes from a compilation that, in Pearse’s judgement, “cannot date prior to the toxic Nestorian disputes,” and it reports a title: “What it said, of course, is another matter.”15 The Peter fragment calls the Son “of the same substance” with the Father and the Spirit, the Nicene term, and is printed among the Fragments (Pseudo-Peter); de Bruyn lists it as disputed.16 Förster’s palaeographic comparisons place P.Ryl. III 470 “between the 6th and 7th centuries,” and later point to “a date of the 8-9th century as more probable”; de Bruyn holds that “it probably belongs to the sixth or seventh century, or even later.”17 On this reading the title’s documented history begins with Alexander’s letter, and its unremarked use by fourth-century writers is the earliest firm evidence of its currency.
