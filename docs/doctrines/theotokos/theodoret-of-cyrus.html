Theodoret of Cyrus on the title Theotokos

Dates: c. 431 (his Counter-Statements — the point-by-point Reproof of Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas, written at Cyrrhus at the request of John of Antioch as the storm broke over Ephesus; Theodoret c. 393 – c. 458/466, bishop of Cyrrhus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, belonged to the Antiochene party that opposed Cyril’s anathemas. He wrote a line-by-line reply to Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas (c. 431), objecting to Cyril’s language of a “natural union,” and in it he confesses the Virgin Theotokos: not because she bore God “in natural manner,” but on account of “the union.” He is credited with drafting the creed behind the Formula of Reunion (433) between Antioch and Alexandria. At the eighth session of the Council of Chalcedon (451) he pronounced anathema on Nestorius and was declared orthodox and restored to his see.

Claims & evidence

The Reproof of Cyril’s First Anathema

Cyril’s first anathematism had bound the whole church to the word: whoever “refuses to confess that the Emmanuel is in truth God, and therefore that the holy Virgin is Mother of God (Theotokos)… let him be anathema.”1 At John of Antioch’s request Theodoret answered the Twelve one by one. His objection is not to the title but to Cyril’s way of grounding it — the language of a “natural union” that, to Antiochene ears, threatened to make the impassible Word originate from the Virgin and be changed into flesh. Against Anathema I he sets down the whole Antiochene grammar of the Incarnation: the Word “was not made flesh by mutation, but took flesh… He Himself is not naturally conceived of the Virgin, fashioned, formed, and deriving beginning of existence from her… but He fashioned for Himself a temple in the Virgin’s womb.”2

His confession of the Virgin Theotokos

In the same reproof Theodoret affirms the title Cyril demanded, with a qualification of his own:

Wherefore also we style that holy Virgin θεοτόκος, not because she gave birth in natural manner to God, but to man united to the God that had fashioned Him.

— Theodoret, Counter-Statements, against Anathema I.2 He then distinguishes the two titles:

we call the Virgin not mother of man (ανθρωποτόκος) but mother of God (θεοτόκος), applying the former title to the fashioning and conception, but the latter to the union.

— Theodoret, Counter-Statements, against Anathema I.2 So the child “is called Emmanuel on account of God Who assumed, and the Virgin θεοτόκος on account of the union of the form of God with the conceived form of a servant.”2 Unlike Nestorius’s ranking of the word below Christotokos, Theodoret here accepts it, referring it to the union rather than to a birth of the Godhead; he also speaks of a “temple” the Word fashioned in the Virgin’s womb.

Reunion (433) and restoration at Chalcedon (451)

The breach between Antioch and Alexandria that Ephesus opened was healed two years later by the Formula of Reunion (433): “a union of two natures took place. Therefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord,” and “we confess the holy virgin to be the mother of God because God the Word took flesh and became man and from his very conception united to himself the temple he took from her.”3 Cyril accepted it in his letter Laetentur caeli.3 Blomfield Jackson writes that Theodoret “himself drew up a declaration of faith which was presented by Paul of Emesa to Cyril, which Cyril accepted”;4 the Catholic Encyclopedia says the symbol “was apparently drawn up by Theodoret.”5 Jackson adds that Theodoret “refused to accept the deposition of Nestorius and his individual condemnation.”4 The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that Theodoret “did not enter into the agreement himself because he was not willing to condemn Nestorius as Cyril demanded,” and that “It was not until about 435 that Theodoret seems to have become reconciled with John of Antioch, without, however, being obliged to agree to the condemnation of Nestorius.”5

At the Council of Chalcedon (451), over the opposition of the Alexandrine party, he “was admitted as a regular member to the sessions … but refrained from voting,” and was admitted to full membership at the eighth session after agreeing to the anathema against Nestorius.5 At that session (26 October 451) he tried to state his orthodoxy at length — “I was brought up by the orthodox, I was taught by the orthodox, I have preached orthodoxy” — but the assembly shouted him down, demanding a plain curse. He gave it:

Anathema to Nestorius and to whoever denies that the Holy Virgin Mary is the Parent of God, and who divides the only begotten Son into two Sons.

— Theodoret at the eighth session of Chalcedon (451).6 Upon these words he was declared orthodox and restored to his see. “Parent of God” here renders Theotokos.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Theodoret’s sequence of acts — the Reproof (c. 431), the Formula of Reunion (433), and his anathema at Chalcedon (451) — spans the period between Ephesus and Chalcedon from the Antiochene side.
  • The sense of his anathema. The Catholic Encyclopedia holds that “probably he meant this agreement only in the sense: in case Nestorius had really taught the heresy imputed to him.”5
  • Later condemnation of his writings. His anti-Cyrilline writings, though he himself was restored at Chalcedon, were posthumously condemned in the Three-Chapter Controversy at the Second Council of Constantinople (553). How Nestorius’s own teaching should be read is a disputed reading: “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?”
  • Dating. The Counter-Statements belong to c. 430–431, in the immediate aftermath of Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas and around the Council of Ephesus; that is the act by which Theodoret is slotted on this timeline (his wider corpus is anchored later, c. 458). He also appears as a witness on the perpetual-virginity timeline (his Pauline commentary, c. 440).
