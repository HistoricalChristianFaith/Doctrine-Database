The Council of Ephesus on Theotokos

Dates: 431 (the third ecumenical council, convened by Theodosius II at Ephesus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Meeting under Cyril’s presidency, the council deposed Nestorius. At its first session the bishops declared Cyril’s Second Letter to Nestorius in accord with the creed of Nicaea, and Cyril’s letter carrying the Twelve Anathematisms was read into the acts.

Claims & evidence

Nestorius deposed for “impious doctrines”

Compelled thereto by the canons and by the letter of our most holy father and fellow-servant Coelestine, the Roman bishop, we have come, with many tears, to this sorrowful sentence against him, namely, that our Lord Jesus Christ, whom he has blasphemed, decrees by the holy Synod that Nestorius be excluded from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion.

— Decree of the Council Against Nestorius, source.1 The decree states that Nestorius “had held and published impious doctrines” and deposes him from his see. Cyril’s Second Letter to Nestorius was read into the acts, and the bishops declared it in accord with the creed of Nicaea; the letter carrying the Twelve Anathematisms was then read.2

The opening of the council and the rival assembly

The Catholic Encyclopedia reports the proceedings as follows. Sixty-eight bishops, Theodoret among them, petitioned Cyril and Juvenal of Jerusalem that the arrival of John of Antioch be awaited, and the imperial commissioner, Count Candidian, “took the same view.” Cyril and the majority opened the council on 22 June, “sixteen days having passed since John had announced his arrival in five or six,” and the session “began by a justification of the decision to delay no longer.” Nestorius, summoned three times, did not attend: “He would not acknowledge Cyril as a judge, and he looked upon the opening of the council before the arrival of his friends from Antioch as a flagrant injustice.” When John arrived he held his own assembly, at which Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus “were deposed by forty-three bishops present.”3

Notes

  • Twenty years later the Council of Chalcedon (451) reaffirmed “Theotokos” within its two-natures Definition.
  • Whether the “two sons” charged against Nestorius was his own teaching is a disputed reading (the Assyrian Church of the East honours him in its liturgy): see Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?
