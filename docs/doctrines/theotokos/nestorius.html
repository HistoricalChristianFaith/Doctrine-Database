Nestorius on Theotokos

Dates: c. 428–430 (patriarch of Constantinople 428–431; the letter quoted is the Third Letter to Celestine, late 430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, objected to the unqualified use of the title. In his letter to Celestine he did not forbid Theotokos but judged it inferior to Christotokos (“Christ-bearer”), which he called the term “mentioned by the angels and the gospels,” and warned against its use if it should “advance to the confusion of natures.” He was deposed at the Council of Ephesus (431).

Claims & evidence

The ranking of Theotokos below Christotokos

In the case of the term Theotokos, I am not opposed to those who want to say it, unless it should advance to the confusion of natures in the manner of the madness of Apollinaris or Arius. Nonetheless, I have no doubt that the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos, as the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels.

— Third Letter to Celestine (late 430), trans. Mark DelCogliano, source.1 In this letter he does not forbid “Mother of God” but places it below “Mother of Christ,” the term he says Scripture uses.

His stated reason for preferring “Christ-bearer”

the term Christotokos keeps the assertion of both parties to the proper limits, because it both removes the blasphemy of Paul of Samosata, who claimed that Christ the Lord of all was simply a human being, and also flees the wickedness of Arius and Apollinaris.

— Third Letter to Celestine, source. Nestorius casts himself as a mediator between two errors — those who would say only “Mother of God” and those who would say only “Mother of a man” (Anthropotokos) — and offers Christotokos as the term that satisfies both. Cyril and the council that deposed him read his position as dividing the one Christ into two subjects.

Notes

  • Whether Nestorius taught “two sons” or was condemned on a construction of his words he rejected is a disputed reading. He protested that he was misunderstood, and his apologia the Bazaar of Heracleides (written in exile and recovered in the twentieth century) disowns “two Sons” and confesses one prosôpon. See Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?
  • Cyril of Alexandria answered him directly; the Council of Ephesus (431) deposed him.
