Isidore of Seville on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 636 (d. 636; the Etymologies was compiled c. 615–630) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the short Bible-book of his encyclopedic Etymologies (Book VI), Isidore relays the Septuagint legend in the form in which the seventy, sealed in separate chambers, rendered the text "through the Holy Spirit" so identically that nothing differed "even in the order of words." He states no charge against the Jews: he lists the later versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) without comment, attributes the one textual "corruption" he names to the gentiles (corrected by Ezra), and calls Jerome's Hebrew-based Vulgate "deservedly preferred" over the others. His testimony to the legend itself belongs to the companion doctrine, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint.

Claims & evidence

The Seventy translated "through the Holy Spirit," identical even in the order of words

"This Ptolemy, moreover, seeking from the pontifex Eleazar the writings of the Old Testament, was responsible for the translation from Hebrew into Greek, by seventy scholars of those writings that he held in the Alexandrian library. Although they were separated, each apart in his own chamber, through the Holy Spirit they translated everything in such a way that nothing was found in the manuscript of any of them that was different from the rest, even in the order of words."

— Isidore of Seville, Etymologies VI.iv.1–2, trans. Barney, Lewis, Beach & Berghof (Cambridge, 2006), p. 139, source.1 This is the inspiration account in the form that has the translators produce not merely the same sense but the same words in the same order, without consulting one another. Other writers on this timeline appeal to the Greek's authority, so grounded, when they treat the Hebrew's divergences as alteration. Isidore frames it with round numbers: Ptolemy Philadelphus, "very perceptive about all kinds of literature," gathered "not only pagan authors, but even divine literature," so that "in his time seventy thousand books could be found in Alexandria."2

He lists the other Greek versions

"There were also other translators who translated the sacred writings from Hebrew into Greek, such as Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion, and also that common (vulgaris) translation, whose authorship is not evident – for this reason the work is designated the Fifth Edition (Quinta Editio) without the name of the translator."

— Isidore of Seville, Etymologies VI.iv.3, trans. Barney et al., p. 139, source.3 Where Epiphanius had told the tale of Aquila as one of apostasy and deliberate distortion of the christological proof-texts, Isidore enumerates the later versions alongside the Septuagint and Origen's "sixth and seventh edition," as items in a bibliography, without a charge against them.

The one "corruption" he names is by the gentiles; and Jerome's Hebrew Vulgate is "truer"

"After the Law (i.e. Torah) was burned by the Chaldeans, the scribe Ezra, inspired with the divine spirit, restored the library of the Old Testament when the Jews had returned to Jerusalem, and he corrected all the scrolls of the Law and Prophets, which had been corrupted by the gentiles…"

— Isidore of Seville, Etymologies VI.iii.2, trans. Barney et al., p. 138, source.4 The corruption motif appears in Isidore with the gentiles as the corrupters: the text was "corrupted by the gentiles," and Ezra, "inspired with the divine spirit," restored and corrected the scrolls. When Isidore turns to the Latin Bible he prefers the version made from the Hebrew: Jerome "translated the same Scriptures from Hebrew into Latin speech… His translation is deservedly preferred over the others, for it is closer in its wording, and brighter in the clarity of its thought" — one manuscript tradition even adding, "inasmuch as it is by a Christian, the translation is truer."5 He reproduces the inspired-Septuagint account and a preference for Jerome's version from the Hebrew (the Hebraica veritas) in the same book.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Isidore is placed on this timeline as a writer who reproduces the inspired Septuagint, the separate cells, the word-for-word agreement and the named later versions without stating a charge of Jewish alteration.
  • The origin legend. The substance of Isidore's testimony — the Holy-Spirit inspiration, the identity "even in the order of words" — is a witness to the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint, the history of which is traced on that page. The Wassersteins write that Isidore "has a strong, if ultimately barren, interest in numbers": seventy thousand books, seventy translators, seventy little houses — and the inspiration "through the Holy Spirit," identical in content and word order.6 Fifty years later in the same Visigothic Church, Julian of Toledo (c. 686) paired the Septuagint's authority with a chronological charge against the Jews; Isidore's chapter carries no such charge.
  • Source note. Isidore's Etymologies is cited from the standard English translation (Barney, Lewis, Beach & Berghof, Cambridge 2006) as hosted online. The brackets "(i.e. Torah)" and "(vulgaris)" are the translators'; the closing clause "inasmuch as it is by a Christian, the translation is truer" is a manuscript addition the editors bracket.
