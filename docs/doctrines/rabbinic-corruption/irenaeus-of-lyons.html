Irenaeus of Lyons on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. AD 189 (Against Heresies Book 3, composed at Lyons) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant statement of the corruption charge after Justin, and the earliest extant one to name later revisers and a single verse. In Against Heresies III.21.1 Irenaeus attacks Theodotion and Aquila, "both Jewish proselytes," for rendering Isaiah 7:14 "a young woman shall conceive" against the Septuagint's "virgin," and the Ebionites who follow them in denying the virgin birth. He defends the older Greek with a dating argument: because the Seventy translated "much before the period of our Lord's advent," no suspicion can attach to them of having rendered the text to suit the Christians. His charge falls on the later Greek versions, not on a rabbinic alteration of the Hebrew.

Claims & evidence

The later Jewish revisers render Isaiah 7:14 "young woman" for the Septuagint's "virgin"

"God, then, was made man, and the Lord did Himself save us, giving us the token of the Virgin. But not as some allege, among those now presuming to expound the Scripture, [thus:] `Behold, a young woman shall conceive, and bring forth a son,' as Theodotion the Ephesian has interpreted, and Aquila of Pontus, both Jewish proselytes. The Ebionites, following these, assert that He was begotten by Joseph; thus destroying, as far as in them lies, such a marvellous dispensation of God, and setting aside the testimony of the prophets which proceeded from God."

— Against Heresies III.21.1, source (also New Advent), trans. ANF 1. This is the earliest extant Christian naming of the two second-century revisers whose Greek the synagogue preferred to the Septuagint — Aquila above all — and the earliest extant attachment of the corruption charge to Isaiah 7:14. Where Justin had accused anonymous "teachers" of cutting whole logia, Irenaeus narrows the quarrel to a named translator and a single word (Hebrew 'almah, Septuagint parthenos "virgin," Aquila/Theodotion neanis "young woman").

The Septuagint is trustworthy because it predates Christ: the dating argument

"But it was interpreted into Greek by the Jews themselves, much before the period of our Lord's advent, that there might remain no suspicion that perchance the Jews, complying with our humour, did put this interpretation upon these words. They indeed, had they been cognizant of our future existence, and that we should use these proofs from the Scriptures, would themselves never have hesitated to burn their own Scriptures."

— Against Heresies III.21.1, source. Here Irenaeus reasons that a version made before Christ was not rendered with Christians in view, and places the suspicion on the later revisions. Chrysostom (c. 407) and Philoxenus (c. 523) are recorded on this timeline using the same dating argument. Irenaeus locates the divergence in the later Greek versions rather than in the Hebrew.

Reception and debate

  • The charge falls on the revisers. Irenaeus does not accuse the synagogue of altering its Hebrew text; his target is the post-Christian Greek versions of Aquila and Theodotion that displaced the Septuagint in synagogue use. The readings of Aquila's version are set out on the Aquila-revision page, where the Jewish Encyclopedia and Dominique Barthélemy read neanis as the ordinary equivalent of the Hebrew 'almah, which the Septuagint had rendered parthenos.
  • The same chapter, read for the other doctrine. III.21.1 introduces the account of III.21.2–3 (the seventy elders sealed in separate cells, their identical drafts taken as showing the Greek divinely inspired); Irenaeus's role in the history of the Septuagint legend belongs to the companion doctrine, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint. In Irenaeus the inspired and early Greek is the standard against which the later versions are judged.
  • Dating. Book 3 is anchored here at c. 189 rather than the earlier c. 175 sometimes given for Against Heresies, because III.21.1 already knows Theodotion's revision — which the ANF editor dates to 181 — so the book must postdate 181; c. 189 sits within the standard c. 180–189 window for the work's composition at Lyons.
