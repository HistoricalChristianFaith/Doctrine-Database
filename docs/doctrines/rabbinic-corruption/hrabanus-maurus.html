Hrabanus Maurus on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 845 (De rerum naturis, a.k.a. De Universo, composed c. 842–847; d. 856) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book V of his encyclopedia De rerum naturis (De Universo), the abbot of Fulda and archbishop of Mainz copies Isidore of Seville's Bible-book almost word for word, carrying the Septuagint legend — the seventy translators sealed in separate cells, rendering the text "through the Holy Spirit" so identically that nothing differed "even in the order of the words" — from seventh-century Spain into the ninth-century Frankish schools. Like Isidore, he states no charge of Jewish alteration: the rival versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) are listed without comment, the one "corruption" he names is by the gentiles (corrected by Ezra), and Jerome's Hebrew-based Vulgate is "deservedly preferred." Where Isidore states the separate-cells account without qualification, Hrabanus inserts "as some assert" (ut quidam asserunt). His testimony to the inspiration legend itself belongs to the companion doctrine, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint.

Claims & evidence

The inspired-Septuagint account, carried verbatim into the Carolingian world

"He [Ptolemy] too, seeking the writings of the Old Testament from the pontiff Eleazar, saw to it that they were rendered from the Hebrew tongue into the Greek speech by seventy translators — copies which he kept in the Alexandrian library; seeing that, separated each apart in his own cell, as some assert, they all translated everything through the Holy Spirit in such a way that nothing was found in the manuscript of any one of them that differed from the rest, even in the order of the words."

— Hrabanus Maurus, De rerum naturis (De Universo) V.4, "On the library" (De bibliotheca), working translation from the Latin of Patrologia Latina 111, cols. 121–122, source.1 The passage is Isidore's Etymologies VI.iv.1–2 transcribed almost exactly — the inspiration account in the form that has the translators produce not merely the same sense but the same words in the same order, without consulting one another. Other writers on this timeline appeal to the Greek's authority, so grounded, when they treat the Hebrew's divergences as alteration. Hrabanus prefaces it with Isidore's round numbers, again copied: Ptolemy Philadelphus, "most sagacious in every kind of literature," gathered "not only the writings of the nations but the divine letters too," so that "seventy thousand books were found at Alexandria in his day."2

He lists the other versions and prefers Jerome's Hebrew-based Vulgate

"There were also other translators who rendered the sacred utterances out of the Hebrew tongue into Greek, such as Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion; as also that common (vulgaris) translation whose author does not appear, and which on that account, having no translator's name, is called the Fifth Edition… Moreover the priest Jerome, skilled in the three languages, turned these same Scriptures from Hebrew into Latin speech and rendered them eloquently. His translation is deservedly preferred to the rest, for it is both truer to the words and clearer in the transparency of its sense."

— Hrabanus Maurus, De rerum naturis V.4, working translation from PL 111, col. 122, source.3 Where Epiphanius had told the tale of Aquila as deliberate, post-Christian distortion of the messianic proof-texts, Hrabanus — following Isidore — enumerates the later versions alongside the Septuagint and Origen's "sixth and seventh edition," as items in a bibliography, without a charge against them, and the chapter closes by ranking Jerome's Hebrew-based Latin above the others.

The one "corruption" he names is by the gentiles, corrected by Ezra

"The scribe Ezra, after the Law had been burned by the Chaldeans, restored the library of the Old Testament — once the Jews had returned to Jerusalem — breathed upon by the divine Spirit; and he corrected all the volumes of the Law and the Prophets that had been corrupted by the gentiles (a gentibus), and arranged the whole Old Testament into twenty-two books."

— Hrabanus Maurus, De rerum naturis V.4, working translation from PL 111, col. 121, source.4 The corruption motif appears in Hrabanus, as in Isidore, with the gentiles as the corrupters: the text was "corrupted by the gentiles," and the restorer was Ezra, "breathed upon by the divine Spirit." He reproduces the inspired-Septuagint account and a preference for Jerome's version from the Hebrew (the Hebraica veritas) in the same chapter.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Hrabanus is placed on this timeline, with Isidore, whom he copies, as a writer who reproduces the inspired Septuagint, the separate cells, the word-for-word agreement and the named later versions without stating a charge of Jewish alteration. The De rerum naturis was a standard reference work of the ninth-century Frankish schools ⚠ unverified.
  • Near-verbatim dependence on Isidore, with one change. Hrabanus's whole chapter "On the library" is Isidore's Etymologies VI.iii–iv joined into one (Isidore had split the matter across De bibliothecis and De interpretibus). Where Isidore writes "singuli in singulis cellulis separati" ("separated each apart in his own cell"), Hrabanus writes "singuli in singulis cellulis ut quidam asserunt separati," inserting "as some assert" before the separate-cells account. Jerome, and later Hugh of Saint Victor, rejected that account.
  • The origin legend. The substance of Hrabanus's testimony — the Holy-Spirit inspiration, the identity "even in the order of the words" — is a witness to the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint, where the legend's history is traced. Before him on this timeline, Julian of Toledo (c. 686) had paired the Septuagint's authority with a chronological charge against the Jews; Hrabanus's chapter carries no such charge.
  • Source note. Hrabanus's De rerum naturis is cited from the standard edition in Migne's Patrologia Latina 111 (cols. 121–122), as transcribed at Documenta Catholica Omnia; the English in the blockquotes is a working translation, with key Latin given in the notes. The verse-by-verse near-identity with the Barney–Lewis–Beach–Berghof English of Isidore (Cambridge, 2006) can be checked against that page.
