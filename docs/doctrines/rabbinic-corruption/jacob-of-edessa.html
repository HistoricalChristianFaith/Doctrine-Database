Jacob of Edessa on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 692 (life c. 640–708; his Chronicon, a continuation of Eusebius, is dated c. 692, but the chronology charge below belongs to his Old-Testament scholia / Commentary on the Octateuch, the work of his last years) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Edessa, reviser of the Syriac Old Testament, grammarian and chronicler, Jacob of Edessa is the Syriac witness to the chronology strand of the corruption charge, and states a motive for it. Where Eusebius "suspected" the Jews had shortened the antediluvian begetting-ages to encourage early marriages, Jacob names an anti-Christian purpose: the Hebrews, "wanting to pervert the computation of years, in order to show that Christ had not yet arrived," cut a hundred years from the patriarchs before their sons were born. He adds that some Hebrew sources he had seen preserved the longer (Septuagint) figure. The charge reaches us through a Genesis scholion transmitted by later editors, and under a documented attribution history (it was long filed under Ephraem the Syrian before it was reassigned to Jacob by the scholars named below).

Claims & evidence

The Hebrews "pervert the computation of years, in order to show that Christ had not yet arrived"

"The Hebrews, wanting to pervert the computation of years, in order to show that Christ had not yet arrived, subtract 100 years from Adam before he fathered Seth."

— Jacob of Edessa, scholion on Genesis (from his Commentary on the Octateuch), preserved in G. S. Assemani, Bibliotheca Orientalis I (Rome, 1719), p. 66; English in William Adler, Time Immemorial: Archaic History and Its Sources in Christian Chronography from Julius Africanus to George Syncellus (Washington, DC: Dumbarton Oaks, 1989), p. 48; reproduced and discussed in Henry B. Smith Jr., "Setting the Record Straight… Part 2."1 The textual observation is the Eusebian one — a hundred years stand between the Hebrew and the Greek at each antediluvian generation (Adam begets Seth at 130 in the Hebrew, 230 in the Greek) — where Eusebius offered a motive ("to encourage early marriages") as a surmise, Jacob states the motive as so that Christ should seem not yet to have come: on his reading the Hebrew totals make the world too young for the Messiah's appointed age, and that is why they were shortened. Julian of Toledo (c. 686) and Agapius (c. 942) also state an anti-messianic chronology motive. The Latin stands in Assemani's 1719 edition — with the Hebrew-histories notice immediately before it — in the Genesis portion of his dissertation on Ephraem (see the attribution note below).

Some Hebrew sources gave the longer figure: Adam begot Seth at 230

"Jacobus Edessenus, who flourished about A.D. 700, states, that he found in some sufficiently accurate Hebrew histories, that Adam begat Seth when he was 230 years old."

— William Hales, A New Analysis of Chronology, vol. 1 (London, 1830), p. 280 (reporting Jacob), as cited in Smith, "Setting the Record Straight… Part 2".2 Jacob does not rest the charge on the Septuagint alone: he reports that he had found the longer figure (230, the Greek reading) in Hebrew sources he judged reliable. Henry B. Smith Jr., who reproduces the notice, argues from external witnesses that a long primeval chronology once circulated in Hebrew before the proto-Masoretic figures were reduced; the readings of that question are set out on the Genesis 5/11 chronology page.

Reception and debate

  • Jacob's place on the chronology strand. On this timeline the chronology strand opens with Eusebius, who suspected the Jews "made bold to shorten" the begetting-ages and offered the motive "to encourage early marriages" as a surmise. In Jacob's scholion the motive is anti-Christian — the deflation was done "to show that Christ had not yet arrived." Julian of Toledo built a treatise on the messianic chronology in 686, and Agapius (c. 942) told the charge as a post-Resurrection plot. Jacob's scholion is the earliest extant text among the chronographers on this timeline to state the textual claim and the messianic motive in one sentence.
  • The Ephraem–Jacob attribution. The scholion comes down through Assemani's Bibliotheca Orientalis, where it (and the Syriac material around it) was filed under Ephraem the Syrian; following Assemani, William Hales cited it now under Ephraem, now under Jacob. Ben-Zion Wacholder, Bas ter Haar Romeny (on Jacob's Genesis work) and William Adler assign the chronology material to Jacob of Edessa; the assignment does not rest on a manuscript ascription to Jacob.3
  • Not the Chronicon. Jacob's Chronicon — a continuation of Eusebius's canon-tables, c. 692, surviving only in fragments — is not where the charge sits; the accusation belongs to his Genesis scholia / Commentary on the Octateuch, from his late work revising the Syriac Old Testament against the Greek. The two works share a date and an author but not the polemic; the timeline anchor here (c. 692) is conventional for this whole phase of his career.
  • The textual question and the motive. Whether the Genesis 5/11 begetting-ages were shortened in the Hebrew or lengthened in the Greek is set out with its readings on the Genesis 5/11 chronology page. Henry B. Smith Jr. and Jeremy Sexton, who hold the longer figures original, argue the direction of change on text-critical grounds rather than from the anti-Christian purpose Jacob names. He shares the antediluvian genealogy with the Flood doctrine's timeline.
  • Sourcing. The page cites the scholion as preserved in Assemani's Bibliotheca Orientalis (1719) with Adler's English (Time Immemorial, 1989, p. 48) and Hales's notice (New Analysis, 1830, 1:280) — reproduced and discussed in Smith's Associates for Biblical Research article "Setting the Record Straight on the Primeval Chronology of the Septuagint (Part 2)," which is the accessible online locus. Jacob is named in the same chain on p. 122 of Smith's ICC 2018 paper. The primary editions are Assemani's Bibliotheca Orientalis I (1719), p. 66, for the Latin, and Hales's New Analysis I (1830), p. 280, for the English notice, both in digitized first printings. Adler's Time Immemorial supplies the English of the longer quote.
