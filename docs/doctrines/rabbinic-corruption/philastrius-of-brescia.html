Philastrius of Brescia on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 397 (the Diversarum Hereseon Liber, compiled c. 380–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Philastrius, bishop of Brescia, compiled the earliest extant Latin catalogue of heresies, the Diversarum Hereseon Liber (Book of Diverse Heresies). In it he carries the Aquila-versus-Septuagint dispute into the Latin West and lists rejection of the Seventy among the heresies: he brands as heretics those "who like the Jews spit out the translation of the Seventy-Two… and prefer to use the text of a certain Aquila, a man of Pontus." The authority he sets against Aquila is the inspiration account — the seventy-two sealed in separate cells under Ptolemy, their versions found to "agree completely." He names no altered verse and charges no rabbinic excision of the Hebrew. His catalogue is a late-fourth-century Latin witness to the listing of a preference for Aquila over the Septuagint among the heresies; in the Greek East, Epiphanius charged Aquila himself.1

Claims & evidence

Rejecting the Seventy for Aquila, listed among the heresies

"There are heretics who like the Jews spit out the translation of the Seventy-Two saintly and wise men, and prefer to use the text of a certain Aquila, a man of Pontus, who spent many years producing (his version)…"

— Philastrius, Diversarum Hereseon Liber, trans. in Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint. Philastrius's catalogue lists deviations of several kinds — doctrinal, moral, and scriptural — as hereses, and here the deviation is textual: on his account, those who set aside the Septuagint in favour of the literal Greek of Aquila (the second-century proselyte, "a man of Pontus") act "like the Jews." Epiphanius (c. 392) charged Aquila personally with distorting the words of the Seventy out of spite; Philastrius's entry is directed at Aquila's readers, for whom preferring his version is the heresy. (The Latin passage at this point gave examples of the divergences between the two Greek versions, abridged in the surviving citation.)

The Septuagint's authority: the separate-cells account under Ptolemy

"But this, that is to say the translation of the Seventy-Two, was published for all under Ptolemy who ruled the Egyptians after Alexander of Macedon… When the Seventy-Two wise and sage translators came, in accordance with the command of the king they translated the Hebrew language into Greek speech and published it in Greek letters. Now when the king Ptolemy had received these men at the start, wanting to find out whether what the Jews read was of divine authorship, he ordered that each of them be secluded in a cell and see no one else except for a secretary who would take down his translation by dictation. Then taking the versions of all of them every day, he found that the versions of all of them agreed completely, and he ordered that (a copy) be made and placed in the temple."

— Philastrius, Diversarum Hereseon Liber, trans. in Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint. The authority Philastrius gives the Seventy rests on the inspiration account: Ptolemy tests "whether what the Jews read was of divine authorship," shuts the translators in separate cells, and finds their independent versions "agree completely." In the catalogue this account is set against Aquila's version. The account itself, and its development across the centuries, is traced on the companion timeline, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Philastrius names no altered verse and specifies no rabbinic excision of the Hebrew; the fault he lists is the preference for a rival Greek version. He is a datable Latin witness, c. 380–390, to the Aquila-versus-Septuagint dispute that Epiphanius set out in Greek, and to its entry in a catalogue of heresies.
  • Aquila's version. Philastrius's entry treats preferring Aquila as siding "like the Jews." Whether Aquila's divergences from the Septuagint — among them neanis ("young woman") where the Septuagint has parthenos at Isaiah 7:14, for the Hebrew 'almah — are anti-Christian distortion or a literal rendering of the Hebrew is set out on Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?
  • The separate-cells account. Its development across the witnesses is traced on the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint; the related question is set out on The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?
  • Text used. Philastrius's Diversarum Hereseon Liber survives in Latin (CCSL 9, ed. F. Heylen, 1957; Patrologia Latina 12). The passage above is quoted from the English translation in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006); the bracketed abridgement of the inter-version examples is theirs. The catalogue is conventionally dated c. 380–390, within Philastrius's episcopate; he died c. 397.
