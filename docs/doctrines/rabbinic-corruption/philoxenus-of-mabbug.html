Philoxenus of Mabbug on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 523 (the argument is reported by Moses bar Kepha, c. 903) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Syriac witness, known secondhand, to a chronological argument for the charge. Philoxenus (Aksenāyā), the miaphysite bishop of Mabbug (Hierapolis) who commissioned the Philoxenian Syriac version of the Bible, is reported to have ranked the Septuagint first among the Greek versions on two grounds: it is the text "Our Lord and his disciples quote in the New Testament," and it was made "long before the appearance of Our Lord" — so the Jews "had neither reason nor pretext" to corrupt it out of envy, whereas in the later translations "the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews." The argument as reported is dating-based: on it, only a version made after Jesus could have been altered against him, so the pre-Christian LXX is exempt. ⚠ The claim is known only secondhand — through Moses bar Kepha's survey of the Greek versions (his Hexaemeron Commentary, ch. 44; ed. Schlimme 1977, 1:173); no work of Philoxenus's own carrying it has been located, so the report reaches no further back than bar Kepha.

Claims & evidence

The Septuagint ranked first, as quoted in the New Testament and made before Christ

"… the version of the LXX is the best, ‘according to Philoxenus of Mabbug, as is proven by the fact that it is this that Our Lord and his disciples quote in the New Testament… The same Philoxenus also says: the version of the LXX was made long before the appearance of Our Lord thanks to Ptolemy king of Egypt. So the Jews had neither reason nor pretext to be moved by envy (to corrupt the text); but in the other translations the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews.’"

— Moses bar Kepha, reporting Philoxenus of Mabbug, quoted in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), p. 22 and n. 14, source. As reported, the reasoning is chronological. Because Ptolemy's translation was made before Jesus, the Jews "had neither reason nor pretext to be moved by envy" to alter it; the New Testament's quotation of the LXX is the other ground given for its rank. The charge falls instead on "the other translations," where "the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews." The later Greek versions made after Christ (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) include the target of Epiphanius's charge against Aquila a century earlier; the argument reported of Philoxenus adds a reason for exempting the LXX.

Notes

  • ⚠ Transmission through Moses bar Kepha. The statement has not been located in any surviving work of Philoxenus; it is known because Moses bar Kepha (c. 813–903) attributes the judgment to him. Bar Kepha's report is in his Hexaemeron Commentary, ch. 44 (the survey of the Greek and Syriac versions), at Schlimme 1977, 1:173, where Sebastian Brock independently places the Philoxenus citation. No text of Philoxenus's own for it has been located, so this page reports it through bar Kepha; the English quoted follows the Wassersteins' translation. (Bar Kepha is himself a separate Syriac witness on this timeline, c. 903.)
  • The "envy" motive as an argument from date. Justin and Epiphanius charge Jewish malice; the Philoxenus argument as bar Kepha reports it ties the charge to the date of each version: alteration out of envy requires a version made after Christ. Later writers on this timeline also argue from the date of a version, on the chronology (Julian of Toledo, Agapius). Whether the Greek–Hebrew divergences are deliberate alterations, or reflect a different Hebrew Vorlage or a method of translation, is set out on Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? and Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?
  • Philoxenus and the Greek text. Philoxenus commissioned the Philoxenian version (508), a revision of the Syriac New Testament by his chorepiscopus Polycarp, and for the Old Testament leaned on the Septuagint.
