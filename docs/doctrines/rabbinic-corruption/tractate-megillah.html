Tractate Megillah on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 200 (tannaitic tradition; redacted in the Babylonian Talmud c. 500) · Wikipedia
Summary: A rabbinic account of the divergence between the Greek and the Hebrew. Christian writers on this timeline, from Justin onward, charge that the Jewish teachers cut or altered the Scriptures against Christ; the tradition preserved in b. Megillah 9a states that the Greek diverges from the Hebrew and attributes the divergence to the seventy-two translators themselves, who, translating for Ptolemy, were divinely moved to change about fifteen specific passages. It places the changes in the Greek and ascribes them to divine prompting. Its relation to the Letter of Aristeas account is set out on The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?

Claims & evidence

The account of Ptolemy's seventy-two elders in separate chambers

"The sages allowed to write in Greek only the Pentateuch, but not anything else. And this was also allowed only because of what occurred with Ptolemy the king, as follows: It happened to Ptolemy the king that he took seventy-two elders from Jerusalem, and placed them in seventy-two separate chambers, and did not inform them to what purpose he had brought them. And afterward he entered to each of them, and said to them: Translate me the Torah of Moses from memory. And the Holy One, blessed be He, sent into the heart of each of them a counsel, and they all agreed to have one mind, and changed as follows…"

— The Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Megillah, ch. 1 (Megillah 9a), jewishvirtuallibrary.org. The frame is the separate-chambers account that Christian writers such as Philastrius cite for the Seventy's authority — each elder sealed in a separate chamber, all producing one text by divine prompting. In this telling the agreed changes from the Hebrew follow from that prompting: God "sent into the heart of each of them a counsel," and they "changed as follows."

The list of changes the translators made

"Instead of `In the beginning God created the world,' they wrote, `God created the world in the beginning'; instead of Gen. i. 26 they wrote, `I will make a man in an image'; instead of Gen. ii. 2 they wrote, `And God finished on the sixth day, and rested on the seventh day'; instead of Gen. v. 2 they wrote, `created him'; instead of Gen. xi. 7 they wrote, `Let me go down'; [xviii. 12]: `And Sarah laughed among her relatives'; instead of xlix. 6, `In their anger they slew an ox, and their self-will lamed a fattened ox.' And instead of Ex. iv. 20, `Set them on a porter (man-carrier)'; instead of ibid. xii. 40, `Dwelt in Egypt and in other lands'; and ibid. xxiv. as ibid., `Against the respectable men of Israel.' Instead of Num. xvi. 15, `Not one precious thing I took away'; and instead of Deut. iv. 19 they wrote, `assigned to light for all nations'; instead of ibid. xvii. 3, `which I have not commanded to worship'; and instead of Lev. xi. 6, `the hare,' which is expressed in the Bible `Arnebeth,' as Ptolemy's wife was named so they wrote, `and the beast that has small feet.'"

— The Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Megillah, ch. 1 (Megillah 9a), jewishvirtuallibrary.org. Several of the listed renderings touch passages on the Creator and the heavenly bodies: "God created the world in the beginning" (where the Hebrew sets "In the beginning" first); "I will make a man in an image" (singular, where the Hebrew of Gen 1:26 has "let us make man"); "assigned to light for all nations" at Deut 4:19 (where the Hebrew says God apportioned the heavenly bodies to the nations). The last item gives its own reason: the hare (Hebrew arnevet) at Lev 11:6 is rendered "the beast that has small feet" because "Ptolemy's wife was named" Arnebeth.

Notes

  • Relation to the Christian charge. The patristic accusation (Justin onward) is that the Jewish teachers altered the text to remove the messianic readings the older Greek preserved. This rabbinic tradition locates the changes in the Greek and credits them to the original translators under divine guidance. None of the listed passages is among those Justin says were cut out.
  • The Letter of Aristeas. The seventy-two elders and King Ptolemy link this account to the Hellenistic-Jewish Letter of Aristeas account of the LXX's origin. How the two relate, and whether the listed "changes" are pious legend, real translation choices, or later harmonization, is set out on The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?
  • Dating. The passage is a baraita — tannaitic material conventionally placed c. 200 (the Mishnaic horizon) — embedded in the Babylonian Talmud, whose redaction is c. 500. It is slotted on the timeline by the earlier date, as a witness to the tradition rather than to the date of its final redaction.
