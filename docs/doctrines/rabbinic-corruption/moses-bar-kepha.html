Moses bar Kepha on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 903 (d. 903; the survey is a late work) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Syriac transmitter. Moses bar Kepha (Mushe bar Kipho, c. 813–903), bishop of the Syriac Orthodox Church, is the channel through which the Philoxenus argument reaches us. In a survey listing the ancient translations of the Bible into Greek, he reports a version of the Septuagint legend (the seventy-two shut in separate chambers) and then, in a short note of his own, ranks the Septuagint the best of the versions — partly on his own authority (the New Testament quotes it), partly "according to Philoxenus of Mabbug," whose dating argument for the LXX's innocence he quotes. He is thus both a witness in his own right and the sole surviving carrier of the Philoxenus testimony. The survey is chapter 44 of his Hexaemeron Commentary — the chapter that treats the ancient Greek and Syriac versions and asks which is best — edited and translated into German by L. Schlimme (1977), 1:173, where Sebastian Brock independently places the Philoxenus citation. The English quoted below follows the Wassersteins' translation; the Syriac/German page has not been collated in this entry.

Claims & evidence

The Septuagint is the best version — on his own authority and Philoxenus's — and the later versions were corrupted by the Jews

"… the version of the LXX is the best, ‘according to Philoxenus of Mabbug, as is proven by the fact that it is this that Our Lord and his disciples quote in the New Testament… The same Philoxenus also says: the version of the LXX was made long before the appearance of Our Lord thanks to Ptolemy king of Egypt. So the Jews had neither reason nor pretext to be moved by envy (to corrupt the text); but in the other translations the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews.’"

— Moses bar Kepha, in his survey of the ancient Greek translations, quoted in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), p. 22 and n. 14, source.1 Bar Kepha does not merely repeat the chronological argument; he frames it. The note opens with his own ground for ranking the LXX first — the New Testament quotes it — before he attaches the fuller rationale to Philoxenus. As the Wassersteins observe, the report sits inside "a listing of the ancient translations of the Bible into Greek," so the verdict on the LXX is delivered comparatively, as the top of a ranked field that also held Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion — the post-Christian versions on which the charge of corruption falls.

He also reports the Septuagint legend itself — the seventy-two in separate chambers

The Philoxenus note is appended to bar Kepha's own retelling of the Septuagint legend in his Hexaemeron Commentary2 — as the Wassersteins put it, "Moses bar Kepha does not just report this little version of the story" — the account of how Ptolemy shut the seventy-two translators in separate cells and they nonetheless agreed — "His report occurs… in a listing of the ancient translations of the Bible into Greek."1 That retelling — its full text, with the separate cells restored after his fellow Syriac Ishodad had dropped them — is given on his Origin of the Septuagint page; here the same legend does corruption-charge work, the LXX's antiquity supplying the premise that the later versions, not it, were the tampered ones. The legend he carries is the same tradition the Tractate Megillah preserves from the Jewish side and that Jerome dismisses as "lying" — here pressed into service, not as evidence the translators changed the text, but as proof of the LXX's antiquity and divine sanction, hence its freedom from the later tampering.

Notes

  • The carrier of a secondhand witness. One layer of distance still stands between us and the argument: bar Kepha attributes it to Philoxenus (d. 523), nearly four centuries before him, whose own text for it is lost — so on the Philoxenus side the report cannot be pushed past bar Kepha. But bar Kepha's own carriage of it is firmly located: the note sits in chapter 44 of his Hexaemeron Commentary, the chapter surveying the Greek and Syriac versions, at L. Schlimme, Der Hexaemeronkommentar des Moses bar Kepha, Wiesbaden 1977, 1:173, where Sebastian Brock independently confirms the Philoxenus citation and notes bar Kepha here draws on Epiphanius's Treatise on Weights and Measures. Schlimme's work is a German translation with introduction and studies, not a critical edition of the Syriac: the Syriac text of the Hexaemeron has never been fully edited (only parts published), so the version we can read is Schlimme's German (and the Wassersteins' and Brock's English drawn from it). The German page itself has not been collated in this entry, so the exact English wording below follows the Wassersteins' translation. The two pages cover one chain of transmission from opposite ends — Philoxenus as the (reported) author of the argument, bar Kepha as its preserver and amplifier.
  • What bar Kepha adds. It would be easy to treat him as a mere conduit, but the framing is his: he supplies the survey context (a ranked list of versions), volunteers his own first reason for the ranking (the New Testament's use of the LXX), and only then quotes Philoxenus for the dating argument. The corruption charge thus reaches the ninth-century Syriac church already systematized — legend, ranking, and rationale bound together — which is roughly the form in which Latin contemporaries (Agapius, and behind him Julian of Toledo) were also transmitting it.
  • Who he was. Bar Kepha was a monk and then bishop of Beth-Raman, Beth-Kiyonaya, and Mosul (taking the name Severus), and among the most prolific Syriac authors of his century — commentaries on much of the Old and New Testaments, a Hexaemeron, and treatises on Paradise, the soul, and the liturgy. His habit of compiling and reinterpreting earlier Syriac and Greek sources is exactly what makes him valuable here: the Philoxenus testimony survives because that was the kind of thing he gathered.
