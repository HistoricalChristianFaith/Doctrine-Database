Origen of Alexandria on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. AD 240 (Letter to Africanus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Replying to Julius Africanus — who had objected that the Greek story of Susanna could be no true Scripture, standing in no Hebrew Daniel — Origen grants the Hebrew omission but draws the opposite inference: the Jewish leaders "hid from the knowledge of the people" passages that told against their elders, so that, on his account, the Greek tradition preserves what the Hebrew custodians dropped. The same letter describes his Hexaplaric marking of Greek–Hebrew divergences running in both directions, and declines to set aside the copies the Churches use in favour of the synagogue's Hebrew. His charge concerns suppressed passages, ascribed to the elders' shame; it does not concern whole books or the christological proof-texts.

Claims & evidence

The Jewish leaders suppressed passages that shamed their elders

"But probably to this you will say, Why then is the `History' not in their Daniel, if, as you say, their wise men hand down by tradition such stories? The answer is, that they hid from the knowledge of the people as many of the passages which contained any scandal against the elders, rulers, and judges, as they could, some of which have been preserved in uncanonical writings (Apocrypha)… Now we know very well that tradition says that Esaias the prophet was sawn asunder; and this is found in some apocryphal work, which probably the Jews have purposely tampered with, introducing some phrases manifestly incorrect, that discredit might be thrown on the whole."

— A Letter to Africanus §9, source, trans. ANF 4 (also printed as A Letter from Origen to Africanus, Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 4). The motive Origen assigns is the elders' shame (the scandal-narratives about the two elders of Susanna, the sawing of Isaiah), not the suppression of prophecies of Christ; what is hidden are passages and narratives, some surviving in the apocrypha, not whole books of the canon. How his testimony bears on the charge that the Jews removed writings from Scripture is set out on Did the Jews remove writings from Scripture, or not receive them (Wisdom 18:15)?

His marking of the Greek–Hebrew divergences by obelisk and asterisk

"But why should I enumerate all the instances I collected with so much labour, to prove that the difference between our copies and those of the Jews did not escape me?… other instances are to be found in Genesis, which I marked, for the sake of distinction, with the sign the Greeks call an obelisk, as on the other hand I marked with an asterisk those passages in our copies which are not found in the Hebrew."

— A Letter to Africanus §4, source. Origen — whose Hexapla set the Hebrew and the Greek versions in parallel columns — describes the divergences between the Septuagint and the Hebrew as running in both directions: in the same section and the one before it he lists places where "our copies have more than the Hebrew ones" and "many passages in the Hebrew which are wanting in our copies," and he names the obelisk and the asterisk as the signs with which he marked them. He describes them as "the difference between our copies and those of the Jews."

He declines to set aside the copies the Churches use

"And, forsooth, when we notice such things, we are forthwith to reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches, and enjoin the brotherhood to put away the sacred books current among them, and to coax the Jews, and persuade them to give us copies which shall be untampered with, and free from forgery!"

— A Letter to Africanus §5, source. Origen puts the course he declines as a rhetorical question: he will not discard the Greek copies in use in the Churches because they diverge from the synagogue's Hebrew, nor ask the Jews for copies "untampered with, and free from forgery." His stated course is to keep the copies the Churches hold.

Notes

  • The letter as a whole. The Letter to Africanus contains both the §9 charge that the Jewish leaders hid passages, for the elders' shame, and the §§4–5 account of his marking of the divergences and his refusal to replace the Churches' copies with the synagogue's. How these bear on the charge that the Jews excised whole books, or the christological proof-texts, is set out on Did the Jews remove writings from Scripture, or not receive them (Wisdom 18:15)?
  • The correspondent. The books Origen defends here (Susanna, Bel) he defends as the Church's, against a fellow Christian, Julius Africanus, who doubted them; the letter is addressed to that dispute. Later discussions of the Greek–Hebrew divergence on this timeline include Jerome's and Augustine's.
  • Dating. Anchored at c. 240, the usual date of the Letter to Africanus (Origen's Caesarean period), consistent with its citation on Did the Jews remove writings from Scripture, or not receive them (Wisdom 18:15)?; his Contra Celsum (c. 248) is the anchor used where his broader corpus is in view.
