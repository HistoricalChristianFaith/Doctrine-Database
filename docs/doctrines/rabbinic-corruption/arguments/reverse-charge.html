The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Tractate Megillah

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · the Septuagint legend · Aquila's revision · the tikkune soferim · the rabbinic text-apparatus

The passage

"from the ninety-fifth (ninety-sixth) Psalm they have taken away this short saying of the words of David: `From the wood'… they have left, `Tell ye among the nations, the Lord hath reigned'"

"Whether [or not] the rulers of the people have erased any portion of the Scriptures, as you affirm, God knows; but it seems incredible."

— Justin Martyr, and Trypho's reply, Dialogue with Trypho 73 (c. 165).1

The question

Christian writers from Justin on charged Jewish teachers with altering Scripture. Whether Jewish writers answered with the same charge, that Christians had falsified the text, or answered chiefly by disputing how the shared text was read and translated, is disputed; the answer shapes whether the polemic over the diverging texts is described as a two-way exchange of accusations or as one charge met by another of a different kind.

A counter-charge of Christian falsification

On this reading the polemic ran in both directions, Jews answering the charge of corruption with a charge that Christians had added to or altered the text.

Held by: Irven M. Resnick ("The Falsification of Scripture and Medieval Christian and Jewish Polemics," 1996), for the medieval dispute.2

The Jews were not silent under the charge. From the first recorded debate they answered it, and by the high Middle Ages a polemical literature (Sefer Yosef ha-Meqanne; Milḥamot ha-Shem; the Nizzaḥon Vetus) answered the Christian reading of the Hebrew Bible verse by verse. Irven Resnick documents that Jews "turned the charge of falsification against Christians themselves," and finds two further turns in the medieval dispute: the Christian claim of a conspiracy presupposed a uniformity among Jewish copies that the Christians' own variant copies lacked, and the eastward turn of Christian polemic left Christians defending the Hebrew Bible's text against the Muslim charge of taḥrīf.3 The text gives the counter-charge material. The "from the wood" of Psalm 96:10, Justin's chief example, stands in neither the Hebrew nor the main Greek tradition but in a branch of Christian copies, and Rahlfs brackets it as a Christian addition; the descent-oracle Justin says the Jews cut out survives in no manuscript of any prophet. Jerome, in the Church, ranked the synagogue's Hebrew above the Christians' Greek and corrected the Greek by it.4,5 This reading also counts the synagogue's liturgy among the answers. b. Berakhot 28b records the institution of the Birkat ha-Minim at Yavneh: "Rabban Gamliel said to the Sages: Is there anyone who knows to institute the blessing of the heretics? Shmuel ha-Katan stood and instituted it." Cairo Geniza copies name the Christians: "may the notzrim (Nazarenes) and the minim perish in a moment," and Uri Ehrlich and Ruth Langer argue from the manuscripts that notzrim was not a secondary insertion. Justin tells Trypho of Jews "cursing in your synagogues those that believe on Christ" and of those who "have anathematized and do anathematize this very Christ in the synagogues." On this reading the dispute over the texts was answered in kind, in deed and in word, with the Christian additions to the text among its grounds.6

A charge of misreading, not of tampering

On this reading the charge of tampering ran chiefly from Christians to Jews, and the Jewish answer was that Christians misread and mistranslated a text both held.

Held by: Abraham & David J. Wasserstein (The Legend of the Septuagint, 2006); David Berger (The Jewish-Christian Debate in the High Middle Ages, 1979).7

The Wassersteins describe the tampering charge as "a frequent accusation brought by early Christian writers against their former co-religionists," with its own verbs of excision (anairein, periairein, paragraphein, ekkoptein). The Hebrew text was in Jewish hands, so a mirror charge would have had to be that Christians corrupted their own Greek.8 The Jewish tradition explained the divergences in its own way: in b. Megillah 9a the seventy-two translators, divinely prompted, changed a set list of passages for Ptolemy. Where the rabbis branded Christian writings as falsehood, the target was the new books: the Tosefta lets the gilyonim and "the books of the minim" burn on the Sabbath with the divine Names in them, and a rabbinic pun turns euangelion into 'awon gillayon, "sin of the margin."9 Justin reports the Jewish objection to Isaiah 7:14 as one of rendering and reference: "you and your teachers venture to affirm that in the prophecy of Isaiah it is not said, `Behold, the virgin shall conceive,' but, `Behold, the young woman shall conceive, and bear a son;'" and "you explain the prophecy as if [it referred] to Hezekiah." Trypho meets the charge of erasure with "it seems incredible." The Nizzaḥon Vetus, in Berger's edition, rebuts christological exegesis verse by verse and attacks the Christians' Latin and Greek as misrenderings of the Hebrew.10 Reuven Kimelman reads the Birkat ha-Minim as aimed at minim, a broad category, with notzrim perhaps a narrower Jewish-Christian group, and notes that Justin describes scoffing "after prayers" rather than a curse naming Christ; Ruth Langer leaves its original target open. On this reading the Jewish answer to the corruption charge was a defence of the Hebrew text and a charge that Christians read and rendered it wrongly.11
