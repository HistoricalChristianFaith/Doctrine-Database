The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Agapius of Hierapolis, Tractate Soferim

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · the Septuagint legend · Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture? · the tikkune soferim

The passage

"[Annas and Caiaphas] changed and mutilated in the Books of the Prophets all that they could, of what referred to the prophecies on the Messiah… [and,] having the libraries in their possession, they cut off from the history of the world, from Adam to the time of Abraham, 1389 years."

— Agapius of Hierapolis, Universal History (c. 942).1 In its earliest extant form the charge is Justin's, c. 165: "they have altogether taken away many Scriptures… it is only a short time since they were cut out."

The question

The charge of Jewish alteration of Scripture describes a deed done to the text, and so assumes a transmission in which the custodians of the Hebrew could remove readings. After AD 70 a single proto-Masoretic text-type became the Hebrew text of Judaism; whether that consolidation, and the rabbinic handling of variant scrolls it records, removed the readings in dispute or selected among texts while preserving their variants is disputed.

The consolidation removed the readings the Church relied on

On this reading the custodians of the Hebrew, holding the books, cut from them the passages that bore witness to Christ.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 71–73, c. 165); Agapius of Hierapolis (Universal History, c. 942).2

Justin speaks of a removal that is recent and still visible: the passages have been "cut out" of the synagogue's copies, but only "a short time since," so that some copies still carry them. Agapius, writing in the tenth century, names the agents as the high priests and their successors, men "having the libraries in their possession," able to change "all that they could" of the prophecies of the Messiah and to cut 1,389 years from the reckoning between Adam and Abraham. After AD 70 the proto-Masoretic text-type became the single authoritative Hebrew text of Judaism, and the rival Hebrew forms, among them the kind that underlies the Septuagint and the Samaritan type, dropped out of Jewish use. On this reading, as Justin and Agapius tell it, those who held the books removed from the synagogue's copies the passages the Church read as witness to Christ.3

A conservative selection that recorded its variants

On this reading rabbinic transmission corrected copies toward a received text, archived the variants it did not adopt, and marked its own interventions.

Held by: Shemaryahu Talmon (Textus, 1962); Emanuel Tov (2012; 2014; 2018); Nathan Jastram (2014).4

The tradition of the "three scrolls found in the Temple court" (Sifre Deuteronomy 356 and parallels) records three disagreements: a spelling, ma'on/me'ona, at Deuteronomy 33:27; a count of the grammatical hi for hu; and za'atutei for na'arei, "young men," at Exodus 24:5, 11. In each the sages "upheld the reading of the two and set aside the one," correcting a deviant copy toward the received consensus by majority witness; none of the three is a messianic proof-text or a chronology figure. When the rabbis met a divergent text, they wrote its readings down: the Severus Scroll, said to have been carried to Rome after AD 70, and the Torah of R. Meir have some thirty-odd departures from the standard text preserved in Genesis Rabbah and Bereshit Rabbati, among them "and behold, death was good" (tov mavet) at Genesis 1:31 and "garments of light" at Genesis 3:21. Tov calls the older picture of a council at Yavneh fixing the text "the myth of the stabilization": the proto-Masoretic text prevailed because the other types left Jewish hands, the Qumran texts hidden in caves and the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch "cherished by religious groups that were no longer considered Jewish." The apparatus also marks its own seams: the tikkune soferim are numbered in the Masorah, the dotted words are pointed, and the kethiv/qere sets the written and the read form side by side. The readings behind the Septuagint survive outside Jewish transmission, at Qumran in manuscripts sealed before Christ (4QSamuela; the short Hebrew Jeremiah), in the Church's Greek, and for the Torah in the Samaritan. On this reading the rabbinic scribes treated the inherited text as a deposit to be guarded, and the narrowing after AD 70 was a change in which text-type the synagogue copied.5,6,7,8,9
