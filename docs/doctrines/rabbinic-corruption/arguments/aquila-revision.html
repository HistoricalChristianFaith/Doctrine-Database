Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Epiphanius of Salamis, Irenaeus of Lyons

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · the Septuagint legend · The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture? · the tikkune soferim · The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them? · the Simeon-the-Just legend

The passage

"He was moved not by the right motive, but (by the desire) so to distort certain of the words occurring in the translation of the seventy-two that he might proclaim the things testified to about Christ in the divine Scriptures to be fulfilled in some other way, on account of a certain shame that he felt (to proffer) a senseless excuse for himself."

— Epiphanius of Salamis, Treatise on Weights and Measures §15 (c. 392), historicalchristian.faith.1 Epiphanius calls the whole version "incorrect and perversely translated," and tells Aquila's life as that of a baptized Christian expelled from the church for clinging to astrology, who turned Jew "embittered in mind… puffed up with vain jealousy" and learned Hebrew to make his version.

The question

Aquila of Pontus (c. 130) made a Greek version of the Hebrew Scriptures that the synagogue came to prefer to the Septuagint, and that differs from it at verses the Church read of Christ, among them Isaiah 7:14. Whether those differences were made to turn the Greek away from the Christian proof-texts, or follow from a word-for-word method of rendering the Hebrew applied to the whole text, is disputed; the answer shapes how the patristic charge of Jewish alteration of Scripture is read in its best-documented, single-translator form.

A version made to turn the proof-texts from Christ

On this reading Aquila's departures from the Septuagint were made to deprive the Church of its prophecies of Christ.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies III.21.1, c. 189); Epiphanius (Weights and Measures, c. 392); Philastrius of Brescia (Book of Diverse Heresies, c. 397); John Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew 5).2

The Septuagint was translated by Jews before Christ came, and so, Irenaeus argues, carries no suspicion that its renderings were made to suit the Christians; a version made by Jews after Christ, at the verses the Church cites, carries the opposite suspicion. Irenaeus names "Theodotion the Ephesian… and Aquila of Pontus, both Jewish proselytes" as the source of the "young woman shall conceive" that the Ebionites followed in denying the virgin birth, and adds that the Jews, had they foreseen the Christian use of their Scriptures, "would themselves never have hesitated to burn" them. Chrysostom makes the same point of the later translators: they "made their translation after Christ's coming, continuing to be Jews," and "may justly be suspected as having spoken rather in enmity, and as darkening the prophecies on purpose." The setting supports the suspicion. Aquila worked under Hadrian, c. 128–130, and is remembered by Jerome and the Talmud as a disciple of Rabbi Akiba, in the generation of the Bar Kokhba revolt, when the synagogue was settling on a single Hebrew text and disputing the proof-texts with the Church. His version did replace Septuagint readings the Christians relied on with readings closer to the rabbinic Hebrew, and at Isaiah 7:14 it put neanis, "young woman," where the Church read parthenos, "virgin." Epiphanius gives the motive in biographical form: a convert turned against the Church, who learned Hebrew in order "so to distort certain of the words" of the Seventy that the testimonies to Christ might seem "fulfilled in some other way." Philastrius carries the reading into the Latin West, counting among the heretics those who "like the Jews spit out the translation of the Seventy-Two" and "prefer to use the text of a certain Aquila." On this reading a new Greek version, made by a convert in Akiba's circle, that moved the Greek away from the christological readings and won the synagogue's preference, was made for that use.3

A word-for-word rendering of the Hebrew, applied to the whole text

On this reading Aquila's departures from the Septuagint follow from a literal method of translation that he applied to every book alike.

Held by: the Jewish Encyclopedia article "Aquila"; Dominique Barthélemy (Les Devanciers d'Aquila, 1963).4

Aquila renders the Hebrew word for word and particle for particle, even where Greek cannot bear it. Jerome, who ridicules the result, gives the examples: the untranslatable accusative marker 'et rendered by syn, "with," so that Genesis 1:1 reads "syn ton ouranon kai syn tēn gēn," "a thing which neither Greek nor Latin admits of," and grain, wine and oil construed by their etymologies as "pouring," "fruit-gathering" and "shining." That syn is the mark of a school. In b. Pesachim 22b, Shimon (Neḥemya) ha-Amsoni expounds every 'et in the Torah, and Akiba revives the method, ruling that the 'et of "you shall fear the LORD your God" "comes to include the disciples of the wise"; the Jewish Encyclopedia explains Aquila's "scrupulous exactness… in translating the particles" by "his having been a disciple of Akiba, whose method… was to lay great stress upon the meanings hidden in the lesser parts of speech." The method is also older than Aquila. Barthélemy, publishing the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever (8ḤevXIIgr), found a revision of the Old Greek toward the proto-Masoretic Hebrew already under way in the first century AD, the kaige recension of his "forerunners of Aquila," which reworked books such as Habakkuk and Jonah as thoroughly as Isaiah. At Isaiah 7:14 the Hebrew word, 'almah, stands unchanged; the Septuagint rendered it parthenos, and Aquila, with Theodotion and Symmachus, by its ordinary equivalent, neanis, a young woman of marriageable age. The Church's own scholars read Aquila for this closeness to the Hebrew: Origen gave him a column of the Hexapla, Jerome cites him throughout his work on the Hebrew, and Epiphanius, in the treatise quoted above, appeals to him with Symmachus and Theodotion to recover Hebrew words the Seventy had left out. On this reading the version is the work of a translator carrying his school's doctrine of the letter into Greek, on Genesis 1:1 as on any prophecy.5,6,7,8
