The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Tractate Megillah, the Letter of Aristeas

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture? · The tikkune soferim: did the rabbis admit they altered the wording of Scripture? · The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them? · on the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint, the Simeon-the-Just legend

The passage

"It happened to Ptolemy the king that he took seventy-two elders from Jerusalem, and placed them in seventy-two separate chambers, and did not inform them to what purpose he had brought them. And afterward he entered to each of them, and said to them: Translate me the Torah of Moses from memory. And the Holy One, blessed be He, sent into the heart of each of them a counsel, and they all agreed to have one mind, and changed as follows: Instead of `In the beginning God created the world,' they wrote, `God created the world in the beginning'… and instead of Lev. xi. 6, `the hare,' which is expressed in the Bible `Arnebeth,' as Ptolemy's wife was named so they wrote, `and the beast that has small feet.'"

— The Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Megillah, ch. 1 (Megillah 9a; the tradition tannaitic, c. 200, redacted c. 500), jewishvirtuallibrary.org.1 The same story, without the list and the miracle, is told in the Letter of Aristeas (c. 130 BC): Ptolemy II sends to Jerusalem for the Law and for translators, seventy-two elders come and render it, and the version is acclaimed and a curse pronounced on anyone who would alter it.2

The question

Jewish and Christian sources tell that the Greek translation of the Torah was made under divine guidance, and the rabbinic form of the story adds a list of passages the translators "changed." Whether the story records a divinely guided translation whose departures from the Hebrew carry God's sanction, or a narrative that grew over centuries around a royal commission and around real translation choices, is disputed; the answer bears on the corruption charge, because it places the differences between the Greek and the Hebrew in the translators' own work.

A divinely guided translation, its departures from the Hebrew sanctioned

On this reading God moved the translators, and the places where their Greek differs from the Hebrew are His work through them.

Held by: Philo of Alexandria (Life of Moses II.37–40, c. AD 30); Irenaeus (Against Heresies III.21.2, c. 189); the baraita of b. Megillah 9a (tannaitic, c. 200); Augustine (City of God XVIII.42–43, c. 425).3

Philo writes that the translators, "like men inspired, prophesied, not one saying one thing and another another, but every one of them employed the self-same nouns and verbs, as if some unseen prompter had suggested all their language to them," and calls them "not mere interpreters but hierophants and prophets," so that Hebrew and Greek are to be revered "as sisters." Irenaeus has Ptolemy separate the elders to test them, and all of them read out "the common translation … in the very same words and the very same names, from beginning to end," so that "the Scriptures had been interpreted by the inspiration of God." The baraita joins the miracle to the changes themselves: "the Holy One, blessed be He, sent into the heart of each of them a counsel, and they all agreed to have one mind, and changed as follows." Several of the changes it names stand in the Greek. At Genesis 2:2 the Septuagint has God finish His works "on the sixth day," with the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Peshitta, where the Masoretic text reads "seventh"; at Leviticus 11:6 it avoids the ordinary word for the hare, lagōs, and writes dasypous, "rough-foot." Augustine draws the conclusion for the whole version: "the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them," so that "whatever is in the Septuagint and not in the Hebrew copies, the same Spirit chose rather to say through the latter, thus showing that both were prophets." On this reading the Greek's departures from the Hebrew are deliberate and inspired, and both texts stand as the word of the one Spirit.4

A legend grown around a royal commission and real translation choices

On this reading the story of inspired agreement developed over centuries, and the list of "changes" mixes genuine Septuagint renderings with later rabbinic exegesis.

Held by: Jerome (Prologue to the Pentateuch, c. 400); Emanuel Tov (Journal for the Study of Judaism, 1984); Giuseppe Veltri (Eine Tora für den König Talmai, 1994); Abraham & David J. Wasserstein (The Legend of the Septuagint, 2006).5

The earliest account, the Letter of Aristeas, has no separate cells and no miracle: its translators sit together, "compare" their work, and reach an agreed text "by collation." Philo adds the motif of inspired, identical wording; the separate chambers appear in the later rabbinic and Christian retellings. Jerome wrote: "I don't know who was the first author to construct with his lying the seventy cells in Alexandria… For it is one thing to be a seer, another to be an interpreter."6 The list of changes varies from source to source: b. Megillah, the Mekhilta, y. Megillah, Soferim and the Tanhuma differ in which changes they give and how many, which Tov and Veltri read as the signature of a literary tradition elaborated over time.7 Tov sorts the listed alterations into those that correspond to readings in the extant Septuagint, among them Genesis 2:2 and Leviticus 11:6, and those that do not. At Genesis 1:1 the Greek keeps the Hebrew word order, "In the beginning God made"; at Genesis 1:26 it keeps the plural "let us make"; at Genesis 11:7 it again reads a plural. The reasons the rabbis give for these items, lest Bereshit be taken as the name of a creator or the plural verbs as plural gods, are on this reading anti-heretical exegesis attached to the translators.8 The items the tradition records are anti-anthropomorphic, anti-polytheistic and courtly, the concerns of Jewish translators setting their Law before a Greek king. On this reading the tradition preserves a memory that the Greek's differences from the Hebrew are the translators' own renderings, told within a narrative that grew from a royal commission into a miracle.9
