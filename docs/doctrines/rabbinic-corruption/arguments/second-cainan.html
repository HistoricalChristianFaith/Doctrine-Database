The "second Cainan" (Genesis 11 LXX / Luke 3:36) — original reading the Jews deleted, or a secondary plus?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witness: Agapius of Hierapolis

Related readings: Were the Genesis 5/11 begetting-ages shortened in the Hebrew or lengthened in the Greek? · Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text?

The passage

"They cut out his name from the Torah; and they cut out his years… to make their followers believe that they were in the middle of the duration of the world… You have cut out his name. But his years, where did you put them? — and they remained stuck and without arguments."

— Agapius of Hierapolis, Universal History (Kitāb al-ʿUnwān), c. 942, tertullian.org (trans. from A. Vasiliev's Patrologia Orientalis edition).1 The "second Cainan" is a Kainan placed between Arpachshad and Shelah in the genealogy of Genesis 11, so that the line runs Arpachshad → Kainan → Shelah. He stands in the Septuagint of Genesis 11:13–14 (with a begetting-age of 130 and 330 years after), in the Septuagint of Genesis 10:24, and at Luke 3:36 in most manuscripts, and is absent from the Masoretic Hebrew.

The question

Whether the second Cainan belonged to the original text of Genesis 11 or entered the Greek tradition later, and, if he was original, whether his absence from the Hebrew comes from deliberate removal or from accidental loss in copying, is disputed. The answer bears on the most concrete example offered for the charge that the Hebrew chronology was altered, and on a textual question in Luke's genealogy, where this Kainan is the one generation Luke 3:36 has beyond the Hebrew list (see the two genealogies of Jesus).

A name removed from the Hebrew on purpose

On this reading Kainan stood in the Hebrew Genesis and was struck out of it deliberately.

Held by: Agapius of Hierapolis (Universal History, c. 942); Helen R. Jacobus ("The Curse of Cainan," Journal for the Study of the Pseudepigrapha, 2009) ⚠ unverified.2

Agapius sets the removal in the days after the Resurrection. Annas and Caiaphas, holding the libraries, found the name of the second Kainan among the descendants of Noah, cut it from the Torah, and cut his years with it, "to make their followers believe that they were in the middle of the duration of the world." The disciples, he writes, forced them to acknowledge the excision and pressed them on the missing years: "But his years, where did you put them?" Agapius points to the name's survival outside the synagogue's copies, "in the Torah which is in the hands of the Samaritans, and also in the Gospel of Luke." The Greek witnesses that carry the name are many and early: the Septuagint of Genesis 11:13–14 and 10:24, with a begetting-age for Kainan, and Luke 3:36, whose genealogy of Jesus runs through him. On this reading the shorter Hebrew line is the altered text, and the Greek and Lucan line preserves the original. Jacobus, as reported in the reference literature, also argues that the omission of Kainan from the Masoretic text is deliberate.3

A secondary addition on the Greek side

On this reading Kainan was never in Genesis 11 and was added to the Greek tradition, from which he reached Luke.

Held by: Marshall D. Johnson (The Purpose of the Biblical Genealogies, 2nd ed. 1988); Jonathan Sarfati (Journal of Creation, 2004); Andrew E. Steinmann ("Challenging the Authenticity of Cainan, son of Arpachshad," JETS, 2017); Umberto Cassuto (A Commentary on the Book of Genesis, vol. 2).4

The name is absent from the Masoretic Text, from the Samaritan Pentateuch, from 1 Chronicles 1:18 and 1:24 (which carry the post-Flood line twice), from Josephus, from Philo and from the Vulgate; in Luke it is absent from 𝔓75 and Codex Bezae. Kainan's figures in the Septuagint of Genesis 11 (a begetting-age of 130, then 330 years) are the same as Shelah's, which these scholars read as the mark of a mechanical insertion that copied the next entry rather than an independent notice of an ancestor. Sarfati, as Smith reports him, traces the name to a scribal error in a single manuscript of Luke 3:36. Steinmann holds that the name is not original to Genesis 11, to the Septuagint, to Jubilees or to Luke, and that Christian scribes inserted it into those traditions, including the Syriac and Ethiopic manuscripts of Jubilees after the fourth century.5 The chronographers who built the long Septuagint reckoning worked without him: Smith reports that Josephus, Julius Africanus, Theophilus of Antioch and Eusebius "accepted the longer chronology, but did not include Kainan," and Bar Hebraeus noted that "EUSEBIUS doth not reckon this KAINAN and his years." On this reading the shorter line of the Hebrew, the Samaritan text and Chronicles is the original, and the Greek-side Kainan is a later addition that spread into the Septuagint manuscripts and into Luke.6

An original name lost from the Hebrew by accident

On this reading Kainan is original to Genesis 11 and dropped out of one stream of the Hebrew tradition through a copying slip.

Held by: Henry B. Smith Jr. (Associates for Biblical Research; "The Case for the Septuagint's Chronology in Genesis 5 and 11," 2018).7

Smith calls Kainan's originality in the Septuagint of Genesis 11 "virtually indisputable." He stands in nearly all its manuscripts, among them the early papyrus 911, Codex Alexandrinus, Cottonianus and Coislinianus. His begetting-age of 130 is needed for the post-Flood chronology of Demetrius the Chronographer (c. 220 BC), which places him in Demetrius's copy of Genesis within a lifetime of the translation. He appears between Arpachshad and Shelah in Jubilees (8:1–6, c. 160 BC), where his 57-year begetting-age is integral to that book's Adam-to-Conquest total of 2,450 years. He stands at Luke 3:36 in more than forty manuscripts. Smith reads these pre-Christian witnesses as showing a Kainan circulating in Jewish chronography long before any dispute between Church and synagogue.8 The loss, on his account, was a scribe's slip "likely during the Babylonian Exile": the vocabulary and numbers of Genesis 11 are highly repetitive, and one slip of the eye could drop a whole entry, after which Hebrew scribes removed Kainan from Genesis 10:24 and 1 Chronicles to harmonize them with Genesis 11. The tangled witness of those passages is what such a history would leave. Smith adds that "the originality of the longer chronology is not dependent on Kainan's inclusion," since Josephus and several Fathers held the longer chronology without him.9
