The tikkune soferim: did the rabbis admit they altered the wording of Scripture?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witness: the Masoretic and midrashic lists of the "emendations of the scribes"

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text? · Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture? · The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them?

The passage

"And Abraham stood yet before the LORD" (Genesis 18:22) — "This is one of the emendations of the Scribes… the text should have read, and the LORD stood yet before Abraham, but the Scribes altered it," lest it seem that the Deity stood in attendance on the patriarch.

— the first item of the standard list of the eighteen tikkune soferim, as transmitted in the Masoretic notes and the midrashic lists (Mekhilta, Sifre, Tanḥuma) and collected by C. D. Ginsburg.1 The tikkune soferim are a traditional roster of places where the received Hebrew is said to depart from an earlier wording — "eighteen words emended by the scribes in Scripture" — each concerning an expression that could be felt as irreverent toward God (or, in a few cases, toward His representatives): Genesis 18:22, Numbers 11:15 ("that I may not see my wretchedness" for "thy wretchedness"), Numbers 12:12, 1 Samuel 3:13, Habakkuk 1:12, Zechariah 2:12 ("the apple of His eye" for "my eye"), and the rest.

The question

Whether the tradition records changes that scribes actually made to an earlier wording of the Hebrew, or observes that Scripture itself chose a reverent expression, is disputed. Because the tradition is transmitted by the rabbis about their own predecessors, it is also cited in the debate over whether Jewish scribes altered the text of Scripture.

Scripture's own reverent wording, noted as euphemism

On this reading the received text was always the wording, and the tradition notes that the verse speaks euphemistically.

Held by: the tannaitic midrash (Sifre Numbers 84; the Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael); Solomon ibn Adret (the Rashba); Carmel McCarthy (The Tiqqune Sopherim, 1981; for the bulk of the eighteen, while classing some items as scribal emendation).2

The oldest sources for these verses, the Mekhilta and Sifre Numbers 84, use the formula kinnah ha-katuv, "the text spoke euphemistically." The formula describes the wording as it stands: the verse itself chose a reverent turn of phrase where a blunter one was possible. The term tiqqun soferim, "emendation of the scribes," is later, with the amora R. Shimon ben Pazzi the earliest named user of it, and the fixed, numbered lists belong to the medieval Masorah. On this reading the "emendation" language is a later way of naming what the tannaim had described as Scripture's euphemism. The classical commentators Elijah Mizrachi, the Rashba and Joseph Albo took the readings "not as actual changes in the text, but rather as meaning that the original author acted like one who corrects."3 McCarthy's monograph distinguishes "original euphemism," "secondary euphemism" and "scribal emendation," and judges the bulk of the eighteen to be reverent expressions of the original text. At Genesis 18:22, the head of the list, the received "and Abraham stood yet before the LORD" is the reading the Masoretic Text, the Samaritan Pentateuch, the Qumran scrolls, the Septuagint and the Peshitta attest, and no witness preserves the reconstructed "and the LORD stood yet before Abraham." Saul Lieberman set the phenomenon in the scribal culture of the age, which took care for the honour of the Divine Name. On this reading the tradition is a record of Scripture's reverence toward God, preserved and openly numbered by the scribes who transmitted it.4

Real emendations of an earlier wording by the early scribes

On this reading the scribes did change the wording of these verses, and the Masorah preserves the memory of it.

Held by: Abraham Geiger (Urschrift und Übersetzungen der Bibel, 1857); C. D. Ginsburg (Introduction to the Massoretico-Critical Edition of the Hebrew Bible, 1897); the Jewish Encyclopedia, "Scribes."5

The tradition speaks the language of alteration. The marginal Masoretic note labels a reading "one of the eighteen emendations of the scribes," tiqqun means "correction" or "emendation," and several sources say outright that "the scribes altered it" or "reversed the phrase." The Jewish Encyclopedia explains Genesis 18:22 accordingly: the received "and Abraham stood yet before the LORD" stands for an underlying "and the LORD stood yet before Abraham," altered because "it appeared offensive to say that the Deity stood before Abraham." The changes are credited to the soferim, the scribes from Ezra and the Men of the Great Assembly onward, the class after whom the phenomenon is named. The lists overlap without fully agreeing, about twenty-five distinct verses appearing across them, with eighteen the traditional Masoretic count. Geiger took the eighteen as a small visible sample of a much larger body of theologically motivated textual change. On this reading the tradition is the scribes' own memory that an older wording was revised for the honour of God, kept in the apparatus that guarded the letter of the text.6

An admission that opens the proof-texts to the same suspicion

On this reading the tradition is a Jewish witness that the guardians of the text were willing to change it, which bears on the charge that they changed the messianic passages.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

This reading builds on the second. The tikkune soferim are not a Christian accusation from outside but a tradition the rabbis transmit about their own predecessors, fixed at eighteen in the Masorah, the very apparatus that guarded the letter of the Hebrew. Taken at face value, the guild charged with preserving Scripture records that it changed Scripture's wording, and did so for theological reasons. On this reading a willingness to alter the consonantal text, once granted for eighteen reverential cases, establishes the principle that the master corruption charge alleges for the christological ones, and it comes from the tradition of those accused.7
