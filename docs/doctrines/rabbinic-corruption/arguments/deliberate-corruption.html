Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Eusebius, Epiphanius, Philoxenus, Julian of Toledo, Agapius

Related readings: the Septuagint legend · Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture? · the tikkune soferim · The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them? · the Genesis 5/11 chronology

The passage

"[The Jewish teachers] have altogether taken away many Scriptures from the translations effected by those seventy elders who were with Ptolemy, and by which this very man who was crucified is proved to have been set forth expressly as God, and man, and as being crucified, and as dying… (for it is only a short time since they were cut out)."

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 71–72 (c. 165), historicalchristian.faith.1 Its fullest narrative form comes in Agapius of Hierapolis (c. 942): Annas and Caiaphas, after the Resurrection, "changed and mutilated in the Books of the Prophets all that they could, of what referred to the prophecies on the Messiah… The acts of the Messiah Our Lord which are in the books of the Seventy are, on the contrary, faithful and clear."2

The question

The Septuagint and the rabbinic Hebrew diverge, among other places at messianic proof-texts and in the Genesis 5–11 chronology. Whether the divergence records a deliberate alteration of the Hebrew by Jewish teachers after Christ, or two forms of the Hebrew text already in circulation before the dispute between Church and synagogue, is the question at the centre of this timeline. Within the patristic record Jerome made the Hebrew the court of appeal, and Isidore of Seville and Hrabanus Maurus held the Septuagint inspired without charging alteration.3

A deliberate alteration of the Hebrew after Christ

On this reading Jewish teachers after Christ removed or altered the passages and numbers that bore witness to him.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 165); Eusebius of Caesarea (Chronicon, c. 325); Julian of Toledo (De comprobatione aetatis sextae, c. 686); Agapius of Hierapolis (Universal History, c. 942).4

The Septuagint was made by Jews before Christ, and so carries readings no one could have shaped to suit the Church; where the later Hebrew differs from it at the very texts the Church cites, the difference points to a hand at work after Christ. The divergences cluster where the reading would predict. Isaiah 7:14 reads parthenos, "virgin," in the Greek against the synagogue's 'almah; Justin finds "from the wood" missing from Psalm 96:10 and "take away the righteous" turned into "bind the righteous"; and the Genesis begetting-ages differ by a patterned hundred years apiece, yielding the 586-year gap from Adam to the flood that Eusebius tabulated, while the totals agree, which tells him the Hebrew figures were shortened. Justin calls the cutting recent, with some synagogue copies still carrying the passages. The setting he describes fits the period. After AD 70 the rabbinic movement promoted the proto-Masoretic text-type as the one authoritative Hebrew, and the rival forms, among them the kind underlying the Septuagint, dropped out of Jewish use; the Greek revision the synagogue came to prefer, Aquila's (c. 130), moved the Greek away from the Church's readings and toward that Hebrew. Julian of Toledo and Agapius carry the chronology strand forward: the shorter Hebrew totals made the world too young for the Messiah's appointed age. On this reading the Greek of the Seventy preserves the older text, and the Hebrew the synagogue handed on after Christ is the text that was changed.5,6,7

Divergent Hebrew text traditions older than the dispute

On this reading the Septuagint renders a Hebrew text that differed from the proto-Masoretic one before Christ, and the rabbinic movement chose among forms already in circulation.

Held by: Frank Moore Cross and Eugene Ulrich (editors of the Qumran biblical scrolls); Emanuel Tov (Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible, 3rd ed., 2012); Ronald S. Hendel (The Text of Genesis 1–11, 1998).8

Hebrew manuscripts copied before Christ and sealed at Qumran by AD 68 already show the variety: some agree with the proto-Masoretic text, 4QSamuela agrees with the Septuagint against the Masoretic again and again, and Jeremiah survives in a short Hebrew edition (4QJerb) matching the Septuagint's shorter book and a long one matching the Masoretic. The Septuagint translated a different Hebrew Vorlage. Many such divergences carry no christological weight: at 1 Samuel 17:4, 4QSamuela, the Septuagint and Josephus make Goliath "four cubits and a span" where the Masoretic has "six," a difference J. Daniel Hays puts down to an accidental eye-skip. At Psalm 96:10, "from the wood" stands in neither the Hebrew nor the main Septuagint tradition, only in a branch of Christian-transmitted copies and in Christian writers, and Rahlfs brackets it as a Christian addition. At Isaiah 7:14 the Hebrew 'almah is unchanged; the Septuagint and Aquila chose different Greek words for it. The descent-oracle Justin says was cut from Jeremiah survives in no manuscript of any prophet, and Irenaeus assigns it to different books. The proto-Masoretic type is itself attested at Qumran, so the rabbis selected an existing text rather than composing one. When Akiba read Daniel 7:9's plural "thrones" messianically ("one for Him and one for David"), Yose the Galilean rebuked the interpretation, and the plural stands in the text. In Genesis 5 and 11 Hendel judges the longer Greek figures secondarily inflated. On this reading the Septuagint and the rabbinic Hebrew are two witnesses to a Hebrew Scripture that was already plural before the Church and the synagogue parted.9,10,11,12,13,14,15
