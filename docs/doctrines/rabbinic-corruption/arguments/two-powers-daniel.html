Did rabbinic Judaism suppress a divine-Messiah ("two powers in heaven") reading of Daniel 7?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: b. Ḥagigah 14a and b. Sanhedrin 38b

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · on the Enoch timeline, Did the patriarch Enoch become the angel Metatron?

The passage

"One throne for Him and one for David; this is the statement of Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Yosei HaGelili said to him: Akiva, how long shall you make the Divine Presence profane, by presenting it as though one could sit next to Him?"

— b. Ḥagigah 14a, on the plural "thrones" of Daniel 7:9, Sefaria.1

The question

The rabbis named belief in "two powers in heaven" a heresy, and Rabbi Akiba's reading of Daniel 7:9's plural thrones was rebuked and re-glossed. Whether those rebukes suppressed an older Jewish reading of Daniel 7 in which a second divine figure, the "one like a son of man," is enthroned beside the Ancient of Days, or answered a more diffuse challenge, is disputed among historians of rabbinic Judaism. The dispute concerns interpretation: the plural karsavan, "thrones," stands in the Masoretic Text of Daniel 7:9.2

The suppression of an ancient reading with a second divine figure

On this reading Daniel 7 preserved a Jewish belief in a second, enthroned divine figure, which the rabbis later policed as the "two powers" heresy.

Held by: Alan Segal (Two Powers in Heaven, 1977); Daniel Boyarin (The Jewish Gospels, 2012).3

Segal identifies "two powers in heaven" (shtei rashuyot ba-shamayim) as an early rabbinic heresy-category, attested in the Tannaitic period: the rabbis' name for the belief that a second divine power sat enthroned beside God, aimed at minim who read plural or doubled theophanies as two deities. Boyarin reads Daniel 7's "one like a son of man" as an enthroned second divine figure, and holds that "the theology of the Gospels, far from being a radical innovation within Israelite religious tradition, is a highly conservative return to the very most ancient moments within that tradition."4 The rabbinic sources show the policing. The midrash and both Talmuds repeatedly stage a min pressing a plural theophany, "let us make man" (Genesis 1:26), the two divine names, the plural "thrones" of Daniel, with the standing rule that "any place in the Bible from where the heretics attempt to prove their heresy… the response to their claim is alongside them."5 Daniel 7:9 is one of those flashpoints. In b. Ḥagigah 14a the plural, "till thrones were placed," draws Akiba's gloss, "One throne for Him and one for David," and Rabbi Yosei the Galilean's reproof, which reads the two thrones as "One for judgment and one for righteousness." A second baraita then gives that judgment-and-righteousness reading as Akiba's own, and Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya rebukes it in turn and reads the pair as a seat and a footstool. The parallel at b. Sanhedrin 38b sets the same two-step exchange in the two-powers frame: Elazar ben Azarya answers Akiba's "one throne is for judgment and one throne is for righteousness" with "Akiva! What are you doing near, i.e., discussing, matters of aggada?", and reads the thrones as "one is for a throne and one is for a stool."6 Boyarin makes Akiba's baraita the rabbinic survival of an apocalyptic reading in which the Son of Man is a younger divine figure enthroned beside the Ancient of Days, and traces a continuous line, from Second-Temple apocalyptic (Daniel, 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra) through the Palestinian Mekhilta to the Babylonian Talmud, of a binitarian idea the rabbis progressively rejected. On this reading Akiva voiced the two-thrones reading on the verse Christian writers read of Christ, and the tradition re-glossed it to remove the second seat.

A response to a diffuse second-god idea, not a suppressed exegesis of Daniel

On this reading the rabbis reacted to a broad and partly later idea of a second divine figure, and Daniel 7 itself does not teach two divine thrones.

Held by: Peter Schäfer ("The Jew Who Would Be God," 2012; Two Gods in Heaven, 2020); Menahem Kister, as Schäfer reports his position (⚠ unverified).7

Schäfer accepts that a "second god" idea is ancient and Jewish, and reads the evidence for its rabbinic reception differently. What the baraita records is a throne "for David," the Davidic Messiah, a human king enthroned beside God, and the rebuke treats even that as compromising God's uniqueness, "as though one could sit next to Him." A second divine figure is, on this reading, drawn from Daniel 7:13, 1 Enoch's enthroned Chosen One and later Christology rather than from the baraita.8 Daniel 7:9 names an unspecified plurality of thrones, "thrones were placed," not two, and the vision itself supplies their occupants: the heavenly court that "sat" in judgment beside the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:10). Reading the plural as exactly two, one for an older and one for a younger God, is on Schäfer's account a rabbinic-era gloss carried back into the vision.9 Schäfer examines the reception of Daniel 7 in both Palestinian and Babylonian rabbinic sources, reads the Palestinian Mekhilta through the later Babylonian Talmud rather than as a preserved early link (⚠ unverified), and locates the sharp rejection of a second power, and a partial revival of the idea, chiefly in Babylonian Judaism rather than in a single early Palestinian purge. Schäfer places Kister further along this line, describing Kister's picture of "a clearly defined rabbinic Judaism (which resisted binitarian temptations of any kind)." On this reading the rabbinic rebukes are piecemeal responses to a diffuse inheritance, worked out over centuries and in more than one centre.10
