Did the Jews remove writings from Scripture, or not receive them (Wisdom 18:15)?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Origen of Alexandria, Isidore of Seville

Related readings: Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · on the Old Testament canon timeline, Did the Jews reject the deuterocanon?

The passage

"For while all things were in quiet silence, and the night was in the midst of her swift course, thine Almighty word leaped down from heaven out of thy royal throne, as a fierce man of war into the midst of a land of destruction."

— Wisdom of Solomon 18:14–15 (KJV).1

The question

The Greek Old Testament the Church received contains writings and passages the later rabbinic Hebrew Bible does not. Whether Jewish teachers removed them from Scripture, or the synagogue never received them into a canon that was still open, is disputed, and the answer bears on how the difference between the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testament and the shorter Jewish and Protestant one is described. Justin and Origen charge the removal of passages; the extension of the charge to whole books, with Wisdom 18:15 as its example, is reported here only from popular apologetics, and no academic statement of that form was located. Isidore of Seville (Etymologies VI, compiled c. 615–630), the third witness listed above, relays the inspiration account of the Seventy, ascribes the one textual corruption he mentions to the gentiles, and ranks Jerome's Hebrew-based version "truer."2

Removed from Scripture

On this reading Jewish teachers cut from Scripture what told against them, and the Church's Greek Old Testament keeps what the synagogue's lost.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 165, of passages); Origen (Letter to Africanus, c. 240, of passages and narratives).3

Justin's charge is one of excision. The teachers "have altogether taken away many Scriptures" from the Greek of the Seventy, "it is only a short time since they were cut out," and he names what is missing: a Jeremiah logion ("Come, let us lay on wood on His bread"), a descent oracle, the passover saying of Esdras, and "from the wood" in Psalm 96:10.

Origen, pressed by Julius Africanus that the Greek story of Susanna stands in no Hebrew Daniel, answers that the Greek tradition preserves what the Hebrew custodians dropped: "they hid from the knowledge of the people as many of the passages which contained any scandal against the elders, rulers, and judges, as they could, some of which have been preserved in uncanonical writings (Apocrypha)." The sawing of Isaiah survives in a work "which probably the Jews have purposely tampered with." On that ground he will not "reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches" and seek from the synagogue copies "untampered with."4

A rabbinic act of exclusion stands in the record: the Tosefta rules that "the books of Ben Sira and all books written from then on do not defile the hands," a ruling against a book some Jews treated as Scripture, which the Talmud goes on quoting with "as it is written." The writings the Church kept were read of Christ. Bonaventure takes Wisdom 18:15 of "the coming of Christ in the flesh," the "almighty word of God" come "from the royal thrones"; Rabanus Maurus reads the word as "the Son of God"; and the Roman Introit Dum medium silentium for the Sunday within the Octave of Christmas is these verses nearly verbatim, "an early Christmas text" in Allen Cabaniss's title. On this reading the writings and passages the Church reads of Christ are those the synagogue had cause to remove, and the Church's longer Old Testament keeps them.5,6,7

Not received into a canon still open

On this reading no Jewish canon was yet closed, and the shorter Hebrew Bible is a later settlement that left these writings out rather than cutting them.

Held by: Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (The Old Testament of the Early Church, 1964); Lee Martin McDonald (1996); Timothy H. Lim (The Formation of the Jewish Canon, 2013).8

"Throughout the post-exilic period up to around 100 CE," Lim writes, "there was not one official ‘canon’ accepted by all Jews." No list, Palestinian or Alexandrian, was closed in the first century, and the Hebrew-only table is a rabbinic settlement after AD 70 that drew the line narrower. The ruling on Ben Sira is a stream of Judaism declining to count a book some Jews used, while the canon was still being settled. The patristic charge concerns passages and narratives: Justin's list is of verses, and Origen's instances, Susanna and the sawing of Isaiah, are stories he says were hidden for the elders' shame.9 The Wisdom of Solomon was composed in Greek, in Alexandria, in the late second or first century BC, and has no Hebrew original; it never stood in the rabbinic Hebrew canon. Its eighteenth chapter retells the Passover night, with the "almighty word" that "filled all things with death" as the agent of the tenth plague. On this reading the Church and the synagogue each received a Scripture formed while the Jewish canon was open, and the difference between them is one of reception.10,2
