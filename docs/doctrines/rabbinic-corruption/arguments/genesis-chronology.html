Were the Genesis 5/11 begetting-ages shortened in the Hebrew or lengthened in the Greek?

Disputed reading · on The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture · witnesses: Eusebius, Julian of Toledo, Agapius, Augustine · the antediluvian genealogy also appears on the Flood timeline

Related readings: the "second Cainan" (Genesis 11 LXX / Luke 3:36) · Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text?

The passage

"It is only the number of years before their sons were born which is shorter in the Jewish copies. Therefore we suspect that this was something which the Jews did. They made bold to shorten the time before the fathering of children to encourage early marriages."

— Eusebius of Caesarea, Chronicon Book 1 (c. 325), historicalchristian.faith (trans. from the Armenian).1 Eusebius set the Septuagint, Hebrew, and Samaritan reckonings side by side and found the Greek and Hebrew agreeing in each patriarch's total lifespan but differing by a hundred years apiece in the begetting-ages before the flood: Adam to the flood comes to 2,242 years in the Greek against 1,656 in the Hebrew. Julian of Toledo built an anti-Jewish treatise on the same gap, and Agapius (c. 942) says Annas and Caiaphas "cut off from the history from the world, from Adam to the time of Abraham, 1389 years."2

The question

From Adam to Abraham the Masoretic Text yields about 2,008 years, the Samaritan Pentateuch 2,249, and the primary Septuagint manuscripts about 3,394, the begetting-ages differing by a recurring hundred years.3 Which tradition preserves the earlier figures, and whether the change was made to bear on the Christian reckoning of the Messiah's time, is disputed; the Qumran scrolls do not preserve these figures in a form that decides between the traditions.4

The Hebrew figures were shortened

On this reading the Septuagint keeps the earlier begetting-ages, and the proto-Masoretic Hebrew lowered them.

Held by: Eusebius (Chronicon, c. 325); Jacob of Edessa (Genesis scholion, c. 692); Henry B. Smith Jr. (2018); Jeremy Sexton.5

Eusebius reasons from the totals: since the begetting-age and the years lived after it sum to the same lifespan in both versions, and the Hebrew matches the Greek at Jared, Methuselah and Lamech, the Hebrew shows a hand that shortened the first figure. Smith sharpens the point. The begetting-ages left unreduced are those whose reduction would have produced a visible contradiction with other figures (Methuselah, Lamech, Noah, Shem, Terah), as an editor deflating the chronology would leave them; whereas "if the goal truly were inflation, Jared, Methuselah and Lamech's begetting ages could (and should) have been increased by one hundred years each." A long reckoning is on record before any Christian–Jewish dispute: Demetrius the Chronographer (c. 220 BC) and Eupolemus (c. 160 BC) compute from long figures; the first-century Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum, a Hebrew-composed retelling, carries the longer Genesis 5 ages, which Smith calls "a devastating witness against the MT's shorter chronology"; and Thackeray and Nodet read Josephus's ages as following a long Hebrew Vorlage. Theophilus of Antioch and Julius Africanus inherited the same long reckoning. Jacob of Edessa names a motive, that the Hebrews wished "to show that Christ had not yet arrived," and Smith grounds it in rabbinic messianic chronology. The Seder Olam Rabbah (c. 140–160) fixes Creation at 3761 BC; on the anno mundi 4000 scheme of b. Sanhedrin 97a that set the Messiah near AD 240, and after Bar Kokhba (c. 135) the rabbis had cause to defer the date and read Daniel 9:24–27 toward the Temple's fall. The same work compresses the Persian period to about fifty-two years so that the span between the two destructions equals Daniel's 490. Eusebius's preface to his Canons, in Jerome's Latin, says of the year-counts: "Nor has it escaped me that differing numbers of years are to be found in the Hebrew books… and I would rather follow that which a great number of copies have guaranteed." On this reading the Greek figures are the older text and the Hebrew reckoning was lowered to move the Messiah's appointed time.6,7,8,9

The Greek figures were lengthened

On this reading the shorter begetting-ages are the earlier ones, and the hundred years were added on the Greek side.

Held by: Augustine (City of God XV.13, c. 420); Ronald S. Hendel (The Text of Genesis 1–11, 1998); Emanuel Tov (2012); Andrew E. Steinmann (JETS, 2021).10

Augustine finds in the uniform hundred-year differences "a methodical resemblance in the falsification" that "savors not of accident, but of design," and ascribes it "neither to the malice of the Jews nor to men so diligent and prudent as the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist" of the first copy from Ptolemy's library, who added a hundred years wherever he found a begetting-age too young. Hendel, Tov and John Wevers trace the hundred-year differences to the Septuagint's Hebrew Vorlage rather than to the translators, and judge the longer figures the secondary, schematic ones: a hundred years added to carry the begetting-ages past the round century. Steinmann's catalogue of all 860 variants in Genesis finds that "MT is the most conservative, having only a few intentional changes introduced by scribes," and the Septuagint marked by "a pervasive tendency toward harmonization"; in Genesis 11 the added century eases the difficulty of too many ancestors still alive in Abraham's day. He reads the unchanged ages of Jared, Methuselah and Lamech as the sign of the Masoretic figures' priority: "LXX makes X fairly uniform by adding 100 years for seven men in the genealogy, whereas SP achieves uniformity by reducing X for Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech… It is the irregularity of the ages in MT that recommends it as original." The reading does not make every Masoretic figure original: Steinmann takes the Septuagint's 167 for Methuselah's begetting-age and its 370 for Eber's remaining years as the earlier readings, and judges the Masoretic Lamech figures (a lifespan of 777) ideologically shaped. A short chronology also stands in the Samaritan Pentateuch's Genesis 5, paralleling the Book of Jubilees (c. 160–150 BC), a pre-Christian line outside the rabbinic one. The "second Cainan" of Genesis 11, which Agapius says the Jews removed, stands in the Septuagint, Jubilees, Demetrius and Luke 3:36 but not in the Masoretic, Samaritan, Josephus or Philo, and M. D. Johnson and U. Cassuto judge it a harmonizing insertion. On this reading the Septuagint's longer reckoning is the product of a harmonizing tradition, and the Hebrew numbers preserve the earlier text.11,12,13
