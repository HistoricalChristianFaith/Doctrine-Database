Tractate Soferim on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. 750 (minor post-Talmudic tractate, redacted about the middle of the eighth century in the Land of Israel) · Wikipedia
Summary: A rabbinic account of the Greek translation of the Torah in a different key from Tractate Megillah's. Where b. Megillah 9a presents the Greek as a divinely guided rendering, this minor tractate (c. 750) records that the day the Torah was put into Greek for Ptolemy "was as ominous for Israel as the day on which the golden calf was made." It charges no alteration of the Hebrew and does not address the Christian corruption charge; its lament concerns the rendering of the Torah into Greek. A medieval fast of the eighth of Tevet later commemorated the day.

Claims & evidence

The day the Torah was written in Greek "was as ominous for Israel as the day… the golden calf was made"

"It once happened that five elders wrote the Torah for King Ptolemy in Greek, and that day was as ominous for Israel as the day on which the golden calf was made, since the Torah could not be accurately translated."

— Tractate Soferim 1:7, Sefaria. The tractate does not say that the Jews altered the Hebrew, nor that the Greek was falsified against Christ. The reason it gives is that "the Torah could not be accurately translated": the loss it mourns is the rendering of the Hebrew into Greek. The same passage goes on to give the seventy-two-elders account as a second, adjacent account (Ptolemy sealing elders apart, who agree on the same changes), so that the two accounts stand side by side.

The eighth of Tevet in the later fast-day list: "darkness… in the world for three days"

"On the eighth of Tevet the Torah was written in Greek during the days of King Ptolemy and darkness was in the world for three days."

— Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 580:2, Sefaria, codifying the fast-day list of the medieval Megillat Ta'anit Batra scholion. The "three days of darkness" clause is not in Soferim; it belongs to the fast-day list (8 Tevet) of the scholion, codified in the Shulchan Arukh. The lament thus appears in a tractate, a scholion, and a code's fast-day list; none of these texts accuses the rabbis of altering the Hebrew.

Notes

  • Date and scope. Soferim is a minor tractate redacted about the middle of the eighth century — later than most of the Christian witnesses to the corruption charge on this timeline, and centuries after the tannaitic tradition in Megillah. Its complaint concerns the putting of the Torah into Greek; it does not claim that either side altered the text, and it does not reply to the Christian accusation of Hebrew tampering.
  • Favourable accounts of the Greek in the older Talmudic material. The Babylonian tradition presents the Greek as divinely superintended (b. Megillah 9a: "the Holy One… sent into the heart of each of them a counsel"), and the Palestinian tradition holds the Torah "may be correctly translated only into Greek," reading Bar Qappara's Genesis 9:27 — "that one should speak Japhet's language in Shem's tents" — as praise of Greek in the synagogue.1 Rabbinic texts thus carry both favourable and unfavourable accounts of the Greek translation. The phrase kol tzorkah ("in its entirety / fittingly") is used negatively in Soferim's setting and positively in the Palestinian Talmud's — the same idiom bent to opposite moods.
  • Five elders and seventy-two. Soferim's opening account names five elders who wrote the Torah for Ptolemy, where Megillah (and the Letter of Aristeas tradition) has seventy-two. The count, like the list of "changes," differs between the rabbinic retellings.
  • Dating. Masekhet Soferim is one of the "minor tractates" appended to the Talmud, generally thought to have originated in the eighth-century Land of Israel and finally redacted about the middle of that century — hence its place on the timeline at c. 750.2
