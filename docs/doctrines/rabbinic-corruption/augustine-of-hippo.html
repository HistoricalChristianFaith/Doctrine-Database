Augustine of Hippo on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture

Dates: c. AD 420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, addresses the divergence between the Septuagint's longer antediluvian begetting-ages of Genesis 5 and the shorter Hebrew figures. He reports the tampering-motive ("the Jews, envying us… have made alterations in their texts so as to undermine the authority of ours") as the opinion of others and leaves it to the reader's judgment; in his own voice he attributes the discrepancy to a single Greek-side copyist, says it is "not… a falsification," and holds that where the versions conflict on a matter of fact the truth is to be sought in the text "out of which our own version has been translated" — the Hebrew. In his Expositions on the Psalms he calls the Jews the keepers of the text — "our librarians," carrying the books whose prophecies he produces to show pagans that Christians forged nothing. In the same work, The City of God, he holds that the Seventy translated by "the liberty of prophesying" (traced on the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint page).

Claims & evidence

The tampering-motive he reports and leaves to "each man's judgment"

Augustine raises the divergence through the puzzle of Methuselah, whose years in the Septuagint reckoning run fourteen past the flood he did not survive. He sets out the position of those who resolve it by faulting the Hebrew — and it is here, attributed to them, that the tampering-motive appears:

"They say that it is not credible that the seventy translators, who simultaneously and unanimously produced one rendering, could have erred, or, in a case in which no interest of theirs was involved, could have falsified their translation; but that the Jews, envying us our translation of their Law and Prophets, have made alterations in their texts so as to undermine the authority of ours. This opinion or suspicion let each man adopt according to his own judgment."

— The City of God XV.11, source (trans. Marcus Dods, NPNF). In Augustine the line is the reported opinion of unnamed others ("they say"), set down with the comment "let each man adopt [it] according to his own judgment."1 Julian of Toledo, quoting this same sentence in 686, introduces it as something Augustine "interposes by way of a reportable opinion" (relatione opinabili).

His own account: a single Greek-side copyist

Turning to whether to follow the Hebrew or the Septuagint, Augustine rejects the malice explanation on the ground that the manuscripts are too many and too scattered to have been altered in concert:

"But far be it from any prudent man to believe either that the Jews, however malicious and wrong-headed, could have tampered with so many and so widely-dispersed manuscripts; or that those renowned seventy individuals had any common purpose to grudge the truth to the nations."

— The City of God XV.13, source. He locates the hundred-year pattern instead on the Greek side, in the deliberate scheme of one early copyist who (Augustine reconstructs) padded each begetting-age by 100 years to make the antediluvians' enormous lifespans seem credible as short "years," then deducted the same 100 after the son's birth so the totals would still agree:

"that diversity of numbers which distinguishes the Hebrew from the Greek and Latin copies of Scripture, and which consists of a uniform addition and deduction of 100 years in each lifetime for several consecutive generations, is to be attributed neither to the malice of the Jews nor to men so diligent and prudent as the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist who was first allowed to transcribe the manuscript from the library of the above-mentioned king."

— The City of God XV.13, source. On Augustine's account the extra years are an addition on the Greek side, not an excision from the Hebrew; Eusebius had written that "the Jews… made bold to shorten the time."2 And where the two cannot both be right, he prefers the source: "I would have no manner of doubt that when any diversity is found in the books, since both cannot be true to fact, we do well to believe in preference that language out of which the translation was made into another by translators."

"Not… a falsification," and the facts taken from the Hebrew

In the chapter on the length of the ancient year Augustine holds that the chronological discrepancy does not touch the patriarchs' longevity, and that where it forces a choice the real facts are to be taken from the Hebrew original:

"But that discrepancy of numbers which is found to exist between our own and the Hebrew text does not touch the longevity of the ancients; and if there is any diversity so great that both versions cannot be true, we must take our ideas of the real facts from that text out of which our own version has been translated. However, though any one who pleases has it in his power to correct this version, yet it is not unimportant to observe that no one has presumed to emend the Septuagint from the Hebrew text in the many places where they seem to disagree. For this difference has not been reckoned a falsification; and for my own part I am persuaded it ought not to be reckoned so. But where the difference is not a mere copyist's error, and where the sense is agreeable to truth and illustrative of truth, we must believe that the divine Spirit prompted them to give a varying version, not in their function of translators, but in the liberty of prophesying."

— The City of God XV.14, source (this sentence stands at the close of XV.13 in the Latin: "Non enim est illa diuersitas putata mendositas; nec ego ullo modo putandam existimo… non interpretantium munere, sed prophetantium libertate").3 In this passage Augustine treats both versions as authoritative: the Hebrew as the original from which the facts are taken, and the Greek's divergences, where they are not a copyist's error, as given by the divine Spirit "in the liberty of prophesying."

The Jews as the church's "librarians," keepers of the text

In the Expositions on the Psalms Augustine appeals to the Jewish-kept text as a witness, held by opponents, that the prophecies of Christ are not Christian forgeries. Expounding Psalm 41, he reads the prophecy "the elder shall serve the younger" as fulfilled in this custodial service — the Jews preserve and carry the books a Christian then reads against them:

"now, brethren, the Jews serve us, they are as our satchellers, we studying, they carry our books… When we have to do with Pagans, and show this coming to pass in the Church of Christ, which before was predicted… lest they think that we have forged these predictions… we bring forth the books of the Jews. The Jews forsooth are our enemies, from an enemy's books convince we the adversary… If any enemy clamour and say, 'Ye for yourselves have forged prophecies;' be the books of the Jews brought forth."

— Expositions on the Psalms 41.13, source (trans. NPNF 1.8). The Latin capsarii — the slaves who carried a schoolboy's book-satchel — is the figure Augustine presses: the synagogue is the church's book-bearer.4 On Psalm 57 he names the Jews "our librarians" and rests the apologetic appeal on the text they keep:

"For whenever we wish to show Christ prophesied of, we produce to the heathen these writings. And lest perchance men hard of belief should say that we Christians have composed these books… by this we convince them; namely, that all the very writings wherein Christ hath been prophesied are with the Jews, all these very writings the Jews have. We produce documents from enemies, to confound other enemies… Our librarians they have become, just as slaves are wont behind their masters to carry documents."

— Expositions on the Psalms 57.7, source (trans. NPNF 1.8).5 The appeal answers a forged-prophecy charge by pointing to "the very writings wherein Christ hath been prophesied" in the hands of the Jews. In the chronology chapters above Augustine declines to call the Hebrew's shorter figures a Jewish "falsification"; here he rests his case against the pagans on the Jewish-kept text.

Reception and debate

  • The same passages in the canon dispute. The "librarians" passages are also cited on the Old Testament canon. James White holds that Augustine's longer canon reflects his unfamiliarity with the Hebrew/Jewish Bible — that "if he had known the Hebrew canon" he would have sided with Jerome; the readings of that question are set out on the ot-canon "thought the deuterocanon was in the Hebrew canon" page.
  • How Julian of Toledo used him. Julian of Toledo, building his De comprobatione aetatis sextae on the chronology, quotes these chapters, introducing the tampering-line as Augustine's "reportable opinion" (relatione opinabili) and the copyist-reconstruction as argued "not by affirming, but by surmising" (non… affirmando, sed existimando). This page gives the Augustine text that Julian's page cites.
  • Augustine across two doctrines. The same Augustine who here attributes the chronology divergence to a Greek copyist and prefers the Hebrew on the facts retells, on the origin of the Septuagint, the separate-cells account and holds the Seventy's divergences inspired "because in very deed the one Spirit had been in them all" (City of God XVIII.42–43; XV.23); that is traced on his Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint page. In XV.13–14 he places the two positions side by side: the Greek's divergences that are not a copyist's error he ascribes to "the liberty of prophesying," and he declines to call the difference a "falsification."
  • Jerome. His contemporary Jerome, then translating the Old Testament afresh from the Hebrew, rejected the cells story; the two corresponded over the authority of the Hebraica veritas and the Septuagint. On the chronology both men take the facts from the Hebrew where the numbers diverge.
  • Date and text. The City of God was written across 413–426; Book XV belongs to the earlier-middle stretch, conventionally c. 419–420, and Augustine is placed on this timeline at c. 420, between John Chrysostom (c. 407) and Philoxenus of Mabbug (c. 523). The English is the standard Marcus Dods translation (New Advent / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers); the New Advent chapter division places the "liberty of prophesying" sentence in XV.14, the Latin at the end of XV.13.
