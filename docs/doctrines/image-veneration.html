The Veneration of Images and Icons

May a Christian make images of Christ and the saints, and bow before them, kiss them, burn lights and incense to them? The churches answer differently. The Orthodox and Catholic churches distinguish the veneration (Greek proskynēsis, “honourable reverence”) offered to a holy image — which passes, they hold, to the person it depicts — from the worship (latreia) due to God alone; the honour given the image, in Basil's formula, which John of Damascus took up, “passes over to the prototype.” The Reformed churches, reading the Decalogue's ban on graven images as binding, have rejected the distinction and called the practice idolatry, removing images from their churches; Luther held images to be things indifferent, and Lutheran churches kept theirs. In the Byzantine East two iconoclast periods (726–787, 815–843) saw imperial condemnations of images, each followed by their restoration. This timeline records how Christian images were used, defended, opposed and defined, earliest extant witness first.

Related doctrines: Sacred images and the veneration of the holy — see also the veneration of relics · the veneration of the Cross

Who holds what. Figural Christian art survives from the third century (the Dura-Europos house-church, the catacomb frescoes), and several early writers speak against the making of idols. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) defined the honour paid to images as part of the traditions handed down “whether in writing or verbally,” and the Orthodox and Catholic churches hold it so (so Leonid Ouspensky, Orthodox, Theology of the Icon, 1978, and the Catechism of the Catholic Church §1160, 1993); Calvin held that for five hundred years the churches were free of images and that their admission came later; Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, 1910) holds that the fathers' polemic was aimed at pagan idols and that the veneration of images developed gradually; Paul Corby Finney (1994) holds that belief in God's invisibility, rather than opposition to images as such, inhibited early Christian images, and Robin Jensen (2017) that early Christian art served didactic rather than devotional purposes. These readings are set out under Disputed readings.1

Timeline

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius both records Christian images and, in a letter attributed to him, declines to supply one. In his Church History he reports a bronze statue at Caesarea Philippi (Paneas) of the woman with the issue of blood kneeling before Christ, and adds that “likenesses of his apostles Paul and Peter, and of Christ himself, are preserved in paintings,” the ancients being accustomed, “according to a habit of the Gentiles, to pay this kind of honor indiscriminately to those regarded by them as deliverers.” Yet in a letter to the empress Constantia, who had asked him for an image of Christ, he objects that no one could portray in “dead colours” the radiance of Christ's glorified nature. (The letter survives in fragments of the iconoclast Horos of 754 quoted in the acts of 787, with further fragments in the patriarch Nikephoros; David Gwynn (2007) reports that scholars remain divided on its authenticity and on its authorship by Eusebius.)2

Gregory the Great and the “books of the illiterate” (c. 600)

A Western statement comes from Gregory the Great, in two letters (599 and 600) to Serenus of Marseilles, who had smashed the images in his church because people were adoring them. Gregory blames Serenus for the destruction but praises his zeal against adoration, and rules that the images be kept — because “what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold… a picture is instead of reading.” He also directs that the people must be “prohibited… from adoration of them”: “to adore a picture is one thing, but to learn through the story of a picture what is to be adored is another.” The Frankish Libri Carolini (c. 790–793) cited the letter to Serenus against the Second Council of Nicaea, and Pope Hadrian I, defending that council, claimed Gregory's teaching as his own; whether the letters support the veneration of images is disputed (see Disputed readings).3

Leo III opens the first iconoclasm (c. 726)

The Byzantine emperor Leo III the Isaurian began imperial measures against images. By tradition he read the 726 eruption at Thera as a divine judgement on image-worship, ordered the icon of Christ over the palace's Chalkē Gate taken down and replaced with a cross, and c. 730 issued an edict against images that forced out the iconophile patriarch Germanus I. He left no treatise; his iconoclasm survives through its acts and the hostile record of the icon party. Marie-France Auzépy (1990) has questioned whether the Chalkē-Gate removal took place as the iconophile chroniclers tell it.4

John of Damascus defends the holy images (c. 730)

Against the first Byzantine iconoclasm, John of Damascus wrote in defence of the images. He grants that God, being invisible, cannot be depicted — but since God became incarnate and visible in Christ, the image is now warranted: “I do not worship matter, I worship the God of matter.” And the veneration is not idolatry, for “the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype” — the image is venerated relatively, God alone absolutely.5

The iconoclast Council of Hieria under Constantine V (754)

Leo's son Constantine V gave iconoclasm its first conciliar definition. His Council of Hieria (754), of some 338 bishops, issued a horos cursing “every likeness which is made out of any material” and declaring that the only admissible image of Christ's humanity is “bread and wine in the holy Supper.” The Second Council of Nicaea (787) rejected its definition.6

The Second Council of Nicaea (787)

The Second Council of Nicaea (787), reckoned by the Orthodox and Catholic churches the seventh ecumenical council, ended the first iconoclasm and defined the veneration of images: icons of Christ, the Virgin, the angels, and the saints are to be given “honourable reverence (proskynēsis), not indeed that true worship (latreia) which pertains alone to the divine nature” — and “he who venerates the image venerates… the person represented in it.”7

The Frankish rejection: the Libri Carolini and the Synod of Frankfurt (794)

When a Latin translation of the council's acts reached Charlemagne's court — a version whose translator, Schaff reports, the papal librarian Anastasius later charged with ignorance of both Greek and Latin — his theologians answered with the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum — the Libri Carolini (c. 790–793, drafted largely by Theodulf of Orléans) — which rejected both the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) and Nicaea II, holding images to be for memory and adornment, neither to be destroyed nor adored. Schaff notes that the Latin version rendered proskynēsis uniformly by adoratio; Baronius, Bellarmine and Hefele, as he reports, charged the Frankish synod with misrepresenting Nicaea. Thomas Shahan (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) writes that the Libri Carolini “had its origin in a very faulty… Latin version of the Greek acts,” in which “the council was made to assert that the images were to be adored as the Trinity itself, whereas the genuine Greek text is quite orthodox.” The Synod of Frankfurt (794), under Charlemagne, then formally condemned the “Greek synod”: its second canon rejected a decree, as it understood Nicaea, that images be given “service and adoration as to the Divine Trinity.” Francesco Stella (2022) reads the Libri Carolini as developing “the mediating and realistic solutions of Gregory the Great”; Pope Hadrian I defended Nicaea II against the Frankish objections.8

Theodore the Studite defends images in the second iconoclasm (c. 815)

When Emperor Leo V the Armenian renewed iconoclasm in 815, a leading opponent was Theodore the Studite, abbot of the Stoudios monastery, who resisted from inside the empire and suffered exile and scourging for it. He set out a Christological argument: because Christ truly became man he became circumscribable, and whatever is circumscribed can be depicted — so to deny the icon of Christ is to deny his Incarnation. He also restated Basil's rule that “the honor paid to the image ascends to the archetype.”9

The Triumph of Orthodoxy (843)

The second iconoclasm ended when the Empress Theodora, ruling for her young son Michael III, convened a synod at Constantinople that deposed the iconoclast patriarch and re-affirmed the Second Council of Nicaea. The Triumph of Orthodoxy was celebrated with a procession of icons on the first Sunday of Lent, 11 March 843 — still kept in the East as the “Sunday of Orthodoxy,” whose Synodikon proclaims the honour of the icons. The synod ended the second iconoclasm.10

Andreas Karlstadt and the Wittenberg iconoclasm (1522)

Andreas Karlstadt, Luther's Wittenberg colleague, argued for removing images from the churches in his tract On the Abolition of Images (1522), and his preaching helped touch off the Wittenberg image-smashing while Luther was in hiding at the Wartburg. His thesis: “that we have images in churches and houses of God is wrong and contrary to the first commandment,” for God “considers them an abomination,” so “it is good, necessary, praiseworthy, and pious that we remove them.”11

Martin Luther: images as a thing indifferent (1522)

Returning from the Wartburg in March 1522, after the Wittenberg image-removals, Martin Luther opposed the forcible removal of images. In the Invocavit sermons (1522) and Against the Heavenly Prophets (1525) he held images to be adiaphora, things neither commanded nor forbidden: “according to the Law of Moses no other images are forbidden than an image of God which one worships. A crucifix… is not forbidden.” The idol is to be torn “out of the heart through God's Word,” not off the walls by force, and “images for memorial and witness… are to be tolerated.” Lutheran churches retained their pictures; Luther wrote that he said this “not because I would defend images.”12

Zwingli strips the churches of Zurich (1524)

In Zwingli's Zurich the removal of images became civic policy. In June 1524 a deputation of church and civic authorities, with masons and carpenters, emptied the city's churches of images, relics, crucifixes, and altars, effaced the frescoes, and whitewashed the walls — the orderly, magistrate-led stripping Zwingli preferred to mob iconoclasm. His fullest treatment, An Answer to Valentin Compar (1525), condemns images “misused for worship” as idols “diametrically opposed to the Word of God,” while allowing that images not used in worship are lawful, even “God's gifts.”13

John Calvin (c. 1559)

Calvin sets out the Reformed rejection in the Institutes (Book 1, ch. 11). He holds that “the majesty of God is defiled by an absurd and indecorous fiction” whenever he is given visible form, that Scripture's ban on images is no ceremonial relic but a standing law, and that the scholastic distinction between veneration (dulia) and worship (latria) is a verbal evasion that cannot save the practice from idolatry; and that “for five hundred years, during which religion was in a more prosperous condition, and a purer doctrine flourished, Christian churches were completely free from visible representations.”14

The Council of Trent's reply (1563)

Against the Reformers, the Council of Trent, in its last session (XXV, December 1563), reaffirmed the veneration of images for the Latin West. Its decree orders that images “be had and retained particularly in temples,” that “due honour and veneration… be given them,” and, restating Nicaea II’s prototype formula, that “the honour which is shown them is referred to the prototypes,” so that “by the images which we kiss, and before which we… prostrate ourselves, we adore Christ,” expressly “as… the second Synod of Nicaea, has been defined.” At the same time it ordered that “all superstition shall be removed.”15
