The Veneration of Images and Icons

May a Christian make images of Christ and the saints, and bow before them, kiss them, burn lights and incense to them? The question divides the churches to this day. The Orthodox and Catholic traditions distinguish sharply between the veneration (Greek proskynēsis, Latin dulia) offered to a holy image — which passes, they hold, to the person it depicts — and the worship (latreia) due to God alone; the honour given the image, in the classic formula, “passes over to the prototype.” Much of Protestantism, reading the Second Commandment's ban on graven images as binding, has denied the distinction and called the practice idolatry — the Reformed stripping and whitewashing their churches. Between these stand the two great iconoclast crises of the Byzantine East (726–787, 815–843), in which the empire itself twice condemned and then restored the images. This timeline traces how the use of Christian images arose, how it was defended and attacked, and how it was dogmatized, earliest first.

A note on the shape of this debate. The evidence is genuinely two-sided in the early centuries. Figural Christian art is attested from the third century (the Dura-Europos house-church, the catacomb frescoes), yet several early writers are wary or hostile to images of the divine, and the practice of venerating them — as against merely having them — is harder to date early than the images themselves. The theological distinctions that make veneration defensible (dulia vs. latria; honour passing to the prototype) were forged in the heat of the eighth- and ninth-century controversy, above all by John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea. Whether that dogmatic settlement is a faithful development of apostolic practice or a later accommodation is the central dispute, weighed adversarially in the Arguments section below as the dedicated pages are added.

Timeline

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius is an early double witness — he records the images and doubts them. In his Church History he reports a bronze statue at Caesarea Philippi (Paneas) of the woman with the issue of blood kneeling before Christ, and adds that “likenesses of his apostles Paul and Peter, and of Christ himself, are preserved in paintings,” the ancients honouring them “without reserve.” Yet in a letter to the empress Constantia, who had asked him for an image of Christ, he objects that no one could portray in “dead colours” the radiance of Christ's glorified nature — an early caution against images of the divine. (The letter survives only as quoted by the later iconoclasts, and its authenticity is disputed.)1

Leo III opens the first iconoclasm (c. 726)

The Byzantine emperor Leo III the Isaurian began the assault on images. By tradition he read the 726 eruption at Thera as a divine judgement on image-worship, ordered the icon of Christ over the palace's Chalkē Gate taken down and replaced with a cross, and c. 730 issued an edict against images that forced out the iconophile patriarch Germanus I. He left no treatise; his iconoclasm survives through its acts and the hostile record of the icon party — and some modern scholars doubt the Chalkē-Gate story is more than a founding legend.2

John of Damascus defends the holy images (c. 730)

Against the first Byzantine iconoclasm, John of Damascus wrote the classic defence. He grants that God, being invisible, cannot be depicted — but since God became incarnate and visible in Christ, the image is now warranted: “I do not worship matter, I worship the God of matter.” And the veneration is not idolatry, for “the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype” — the image is venerated relatively, God alone absolutely. He fixes the distinction the later doctrine would canonize.3

The iconoclast Council of Hieria under Constantine V (754)

Leo's son Constantine V gave iconoclasm its first conciliar definition. His Council of Hieria (754) — some 338 bishops, but no patriarch of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, or Jerusalem — issued a horos cursing “every likeness which is made out of any material” and declaring that the only admissible image of Christ's humanity is “bread and wine in the holy Supper.” This is the high-water mark of iconoclast doctrine, and the “false council” the Second Council of Nicaea was convened to overturn.4

The Second Council of Nicaea (787)

The seventh ecumenical council, Nicaea II, ended the first iconoclasm and dogmatized the veneration of images: icons of Christ, the Virgin, the angels, and the saints are to be given “honourable reverence (proskynēsis), not indeed that true worship (latreia) which pertains alone to the divine nature” — and “he who venerates the image venerates… the person represented in it.” This is the definitive Catholic–Orthodox charter for image-veneration and the latria/dulia line.5

Theodore the Studite defends images in the second iconoclasm (c. 815)

When Emperor Leo V the Armenian renewed iconoclasm in 815, its leading opponent was Theodore the Studite, abbot of the Stoudios monastery, who resisted from inside the empire and suffered exile and scourging for it. He sharpened the defence into a Christological argument: because Christ truly became man he became circumscribable, and whatever is circumscribed can be depicted — so to deny the icon of Christ is to deny his Incarnation. He also restated the Damascene rule that “the honor paid to the image ascends to the archetype.”6

The Triumph of Orthodoxy (843)

The second iconoclasm ended when the Empress Theodora, ruling for her young son Michael III, convened a synod at Constantinople that deposed the iconoclast patriarch and re-affirmed the Second Council of Nicaea. The Triumph of Orthodoxy was celebrated with a procession of icons on the first Sunday of Lent, 11 March 843 — still kept in the East as the “Sunday of Orthodoxy,” whose Synodikon proclaims the settled doctrine. This closes the iconoclast era for good in the Byzantine world.7

John Calvin (c. 1559)

The Reformed rejection finds its fullest voice in Calvin. In the Institutes (Book 1, ch. 11) he holds that “God's majesty is defiled by an unseemly and absurd fiction” whenever he is given visible form, that Scripture's ban on images is no ceremonial relic but a standing law, and that the scholastic distinction between veneration (dulia) and worship (latria) is a verbal evasion that cannot save the practice from idolatry. The whitewashed Reformed church is the practical result.8

Arguments

Sub-claims marshaled for or against the practice, each weighed adversarially on its own page — does the Second Commandment forbid all religious images, or only idols of false gods? does the Incarnation warrant picturing Christ? is “honour passing to the prototype” a real distinction or a cover for idolatry? More of these dedicated pages are still being built out.

Does the Second Commandment forbid all religious images? (assessment: contested)

The Decalogue objection — the anchoring Protestant case against the practice. The commandment bans making a graven image and “bowing down” to it (Ex 20:4–5); Deuteronomy grounds the ban on the fact that at Horeb Israel “saw no manner of similitude” (Deut 4:15–16); and Scripture's own precedent is Nehushtan — the bronze serpent God commanded Moses to make, which Hezekiah destroyed once Israel burned incense to it (Num 21:8–9; 2 Kgs 18:4). Read so, and pressed by Calvin, the practice of venerating icons falls under the prohibition whatever it is called. The objection holds where it targets images of God and images given the worship due God — but overreaches as a blanket ban, since the same law commands the ark's cherubim and the temple carvings (Ex 25:18–20; 1 Kgs 6:23–29), so image-making as such is not forbidden. What remains genuinely undecided is whether the honour paid an icon is the forbidden bowing-and-serving or a lesser relative honour — the question adjudicated separately under the veneration-vs-worship distinction.9
