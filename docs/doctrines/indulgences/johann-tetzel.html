Johann Tetzel and the St. Peter's indulgence

Dates: c. 1465–1519 (the St. Peter's indulgence campaign, 1516–1517) · Wikipedia
Summary: Johann Tetzel (c. 1465–1519), a Saxon Dominican and papal inquisitor, preached the St. Peter's indulgence in the territories of Albrecht of Brandenburg, and his preaching was the occasion of Luther's Ninety-Five Theses. He did not devise the campaign: Pope Leo X had proclaimed a plenary indulgence to fund the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica, and Albrecht of Brandenburg — already Archbishop of Magdeburg, now Archbishop of Mainz as well — was entrusted with its publication in his German territories, reportedly on an arrangement that half the proceeds would repay the Fugger bankers of Augsburg for a sum Albrecht had borrowed for the see ⚠ unverified. Tetzel was Albrecht's sub-commissioner and preacher (from 1516), working from an official manual, the Instructio Summaria, which set out four “graces,” with payments graded by rank, including one for the souls in purgatory. A couplet attached to his name, that the soul flies from purgatory the instant the coin rings in the chest, became the best-known summary of the campaign; whether it is his in substance is disputed (see below).

Claims & evidence

The financing of the campaign

Albrecht of Brandenburg, already holding Magdeburg and Halberstadt, wanted the archbishopric of Mainz too — a pluralism requiring a papal dispensation he could not afford. He borrowed the fee (some 24,000 ducats) from the Fuggers, and Leo X, needing funds for St. Peter's, struck a bargain: Albrecht might preach the St. Peter's indulgence across his lands for eight years, with the takings split — half to Rome for the basilica, half to Albrecht to clear the Fugger loan ⚠ unverified.1 So closely was the money watched that the collection-chest carried two or three locks, one key held by a Fugger agent who travelled with the preachers.2 The commission barred preaching in electoral Saxony — the land of Albrecht's rival Frederick the Wise, Luther's prince — so Tetzel worked its borders (Jüterbog, Zerbst), close enough to draw Wittenbergers across.3

The Instructio Summaria: graded payments, and the fourth grace for the dead

The fourth distinctive grace is the plenary remission of all sins for the souls that exist in purgatory, which the Pope grants and concedes by means of intercession, so that a contribution placed by the living in the repository on their behalf counts as one which a man might make… nor is it needful that the persons who contribute on behalf of the souls in purgatory should be contrite in heart and confess with the mouth.

— Instructio Summaria ad Subcommissarios, issued by Albrecht (1516), in B. J. Kidd, Documents Illustrative of the Continental Reformation (Oxford, 1911), no. 8, archive.org. The manual set out “four principal graces” for preachers to commend — complete remission of sins, a confessional letter, participation in the Church's goods, and this fourth for the dead — each with its own tariff of payment scaled to the buyer's rank. In the fourth grace the indulgence for the souls in purgatory is said to work by means of intercession, and the living contributor need not be contrite in heart or confess.4

The jingle: “as soon as the coin in the coffer rings…”

As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, / the soul from purgatory springs.

— the couplet (German Sobald der Gulden im Becken klingt, im huy die Seel im Himmel springt) attached to Tetzel's name. Henry Ganss (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that the couplet, “though verbally spurious,” can “in substance unfailingly” be traced to Tetzel's writings: “By Tetzel they are substantially acknowledged in his Frankfort theses,” where he accepted “the mere school opinion of a few obscure writers, which overstepped the contents of papal indulgence Bulls”; and: “By the teaching he laid himself open to just censure and reproach.” Wikipedia's account calls the couplet “almost certainly not a literal quotation” of his sermons.5

Tetzel's reply to Luther and his last years

Tetzel did not stay silent. With the Frankfurt theologian Konrad Wimpina he drew up counter-theses (1518) defending papal power over purgatory, and he took a doctorate to argue them; his Vorlegung (Rebuttal) answered the Ninety-Five Theses point by point. When the papal legate Karl von Miltitz investigated the affair in 1519 he laid much of the blame on Tetzel. Ganss (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912), quoting the Protestant Oscar Michael (1901), holds that Miltitz “has been so discredited that he no longer carries historical weight.” Tetzel withdrew to the Dominican house at Leipzig, where he died in August 1519 — reportedly consoled by a letter from Luther.6

Placement on the timeline

  • On the indulgences timeline Tetzel follows the public penance of the early Church, the confessors' letters, the bishop's relaxation at Nicaea, the penitentials, the crusade grant, and Clement VI's treasury, and precedes Luther's theses and the Council of Trent.
  • The Instructio's provision for the dead and its no-contrition wording are printed in Kidd's documents; the “coin…soul springs” couplet is attached to Tetzel's name, verbally spurious but substantially his according to Ganss (1912). Luther's theses 27–28 address the couplet's claim.
  • Tetzel's counter-theses with Wimpina defended the pope's power over the treasury and over the souls in purgatory. The Council of Trent's decree of 1563 affirmed the Church's power to grant indulgences and ordered “all evil gains” abolished (see the timeline).
