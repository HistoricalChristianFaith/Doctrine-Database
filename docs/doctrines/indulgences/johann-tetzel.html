Johann Tetzel and the St. Peter's indulgence

Dates: c. 1465–1519 (the St. Peter's indulgence campaign, 1516–1517) · Wikipedia
Summary: Johann Tetzel (c. 1465–1519), a Saxon Dominican and papal inquisitor, is the human face of the indulgence-sale that provoked the Reformation. He did not devise the campaign: Pope Leo X had proclaimed a plenary indulgence to fund the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica (1514), and Albrecht of Brandenburg — already Archbishop of Magdeburg, now buying the see of Mainz as well — was licensed to preach it in his German territories, on a secret arrangement that half the proceeds would repay the Fugger bankers of Augsburg the vast sum Albrecht had borrowed to purchase the pallium. Tetzel was Albrecht's sub-commissioner and star preacher (from 1516), armed with an official sales-manual, the Instructio Summaria, that reduced the theology of the treasury and purgatory to a graded price-list. His preaching near the Saxon border in 1517 — and the jingle attached to his name, that the soul flies from purgatory the instant the coin rings in the chest — is what put the pen in Luther's hand. Tetzel is thus the point where the long medieval development of the indulgence meets its Reformation reckoning.

Claims & evidence

The money behind the campaign: a bishopric bought, a loan to repay

The St. Peter's indulgence in Germany was, at bottom, a debt-collection scheme. Albrecht of Brandenburg, already holding Magdeburg and Halberstadt, wanted the archbishopric of Mainz too — a pluralism requiring a papal dispensation he could not afford. He borrowed the fee (some 24,000 ducats) from the Fuggers, and Leo X, needing funds for St. Peter's, struck a bargain: Albrecht might preach the St. Peter's indulgence across his lands for eight years, with the takings split — half to Rome for the basilica, half to Albrecht to clear the Fugger loan.1 So closely was the money watched that the collection-chest carried two or three locks, one key held by a Fugger agent who travelled with the preachers.2 The commission pointedly barred preaching in electoral Saxony — the land of Albrecht's rival Frederick the Wise, Luther's prince — so Tetzel worked its borders (Jüterbog, Zerbst), close enough to draw Wittenbergers across.3

The Instructio Summaria: a graded price-list, and the fourth grace for the dead

The fourth distinctive grace is the plenary remission of all sins for the souls that exist in purgatory, which the Pope grants and concedes by means of intercession, so that a contribution placed by the living in the repository on their behalf counts as one which a man might make… nor is it needful that the persons who contribute on behalf of the souls in purgatory should be contrite in heart and confess with the mouth.

— Instructio Summaria ad Subcommissarios, issued by Albrecht (1516), in B. J. Kidd, Documents Illustrative of the Continental Reformation (Oxford, 1911), no. 8, archive.org. The manual set out “four principal graces” for preachers to commend — complete remission of sins, a confessional letter, participation in the Church's goods, and this fourth for the dead — each with its own tariff of payment scaled to the buyer's rank. The decisive point Luther seized on is in the fourth grace: for the souls in purgatory the indulgence was said to work per modum suffragii, by intercession, and — on this manual's reading — the living contributor need not even be contrite or confessed. The remission of the dead had been made a straightforward purchase.4

The jingle: “as soon as the coin in the coffer rings…”

As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, / the soul from purgatory springs.

— the couplet (German Sobald der Gulden im Becken klingt, im huy die Seel im Himmel springt) attached to Tetzel by his opponents and reported by later Lutheran tradition. It is almost certainly not a verbatim quotation of his sermons, and it overstates the official teaching (which required intercession, not a mechanical release); but it is a fair caricature of the Instructio's fourth grace, and it is the form in which the scandal was remembered.5 The wiki flags the caution the sources do: the rhyme is a hostile summary, not a transcript — but the manual it summarizes is authentic, and it is the manual, not the rhyme, that Luther answered.

Tetzel answers Luther — and is disowned

Tetzel did not stay silent. With the Frankfurt theologian Konrad Wimpina he drew up counter-theses (1518) defending papal power over purgatory, and he took a doctorate to argue them; his Vorlegung (Rebuttal) answered the Ninety-Five Theses point by point. But the tide turned against him fast. When the papal legate Karl von Miltitz investigated the affair in 1519 he laid much of the blame on Tetzel's excesses, and the broken preacher withdrew to the Dominican house at Leipzig, where he died in August 1519 — reportedly consoled by a generous letter from the very Luther he had opposed.6

Notes

  • Tetzel is the pivot of the indulgences timeline: everything before him (public penance, the confessors' letters, the bishop's relaxation at Nicaea, the redeemable penitentials, the crusade grant, and Clement VI's treasury) is the slow build-up; everything after (Luther's protest, then Trent's reform) is the reckoning. The abuse he embodied was real, but it was the end of a long development, not its beginning.
  • Be precise about what was and was not official. The sale of the grace for the dead, and the no-contrition-required wording of the Instructio, are documented; the mechanical “coin…soul springs” jingle is a hostile paraphrase, not Tetzel's ipsissima verba. Both matter: Luther's theses 27–28 attack the jingle's claim, but they land because the manual behind it really did price the release of souls.
  • Tetzel's own defence — that the pope's power over the treasury and over purgatory per modum suffragii is genuine — is not nothing; the Church would reaffirm that power at Trent even as it abolished the traffic he ran. The quarrel of 1517 was less over whether the Church may grant indulgences than over the sale, the claim over the dead, and the theology of the treasury — the very points the power-of-the-keys argument turns on.
