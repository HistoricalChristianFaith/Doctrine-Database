Did the Catholic reforms of indulgences (Trent, 1562–1563; Paul VI, 1967) concede the Reformers' critique?

Disputed reading · on Indulgences · witness: Martin Luther (the critique of 1517)

Related readings: Did the crusade indulgence give rise to the indulgence practice Luther protested in 1517?

The passage

Whereas the power of conferring Indulgences was granted by Christ to the Church; and she has, even in the most ancient times, used the said power, delivered unto her of God; the sacred holy Synod teaches, and enjoins, that the use of Indulgences, for the Christian people most salutary, and approved of by the authority of sacred Councils, is to be retained in the Church; and It condemns with anathema those who either assert, that they are useless; or who deny that there is in the Church the power of granting them. In granting them, however, It desires that, in accordance with the ancient and approved custom in the Church, moderation be observed; lest, by excessive facility, ecclesastical discipline be enervated. And being desirous that the abuses which have crept therein, and by occasion of which this honourable name of Indulgences is blasphemed by heretics, be amended and corrected, It ordains generally by this decree, that all evil gains for the obtaining thereof,–whence a most prolific cause of abuses amongst the Christian people has been derived,–be wholly abolished.

— Council of Trent, Session XXV (4 December 1563), Decree concerning Indulgences, J. Waterworth trans.1

n. 4—A partial indulgence will henceforth be designated only with the words "partial indulgence" without any determination of days or years.

— Paul VI, apostolic constitution Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1 January 1967), Norms on Indulgences.2

The question

Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (1517) attacked the preachers who promised release from “every penalty” and the claim that a soul flies from purgatory when the coin is paid, and named the Church's true treasure as “the Most Holy Gospel”; the Council of Trent abolished the quaestores and “all evil gains” while retaining indulgences under anathema, and Paul VI in 1967 dropped the days and years of partial indulgences.3 The Lutheran–Roman Catholic Commission on Unity records jointly that the Council “eliminated some abuses in matters of jurisdiction, ordination, patronage, benefices, and indulgences”;4 what is disputed is how those reforms stand to the Reformers' critique: as the Catholic Church's own correction of abuses, as an admission that leaves the disputed doctrine in place, or as a partial concession or convergence.

The Church corrected abuses in a practice she retains

On this reading the reforms were the Church's own discipline, purging misuse of a power given by Christ, and the doctrine itself was never in question.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session XXV, 1563); William Kent (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Indulgences,” 1910); Paul VI (Catholic, Indulgentiarum Doctrina, 1967); Peter J. Beer (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1978).5

Trent placed its reform inside its teaching. The same decree that declares the power “granted by Christ to the Church” asks for moderation “in accordance with the ancient and approved custom” and orders the abuses “amended and corrected”; its twenty-first session had already abolished the “name and use” of the quaestores.6 Kent sets this in a longer line of the Church's own corrections, from Cyprian's rebuke of blank libelli to Clovesho (747) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): the Church, “while holding fast to the principle and intrinsic value of indulgences, has repeatedly condemned their misuse.” Since the grants were also “being properly used by sincere Christians,” the Church, “instead of abolishing the practice of indulgences, aimed rather at strengthening it by eliminating the evil elements.” The days and years, on his account, measured equivalence to “the ancient canonical penance”; “the reckoning makes no claim to absolute exactness; it has only a relative value.”7

Paul VI writes that the practice “has at times been improperly used,” through “untimely and superfluous indulgences” and “illicit profits,” and that the Church, “in deploring and correcting these improper uses,” teaches that their use must be preserved. His changes are “innovations into its discipline of indulgences,” made “in order to give greater dignity and esteem to the use of indulgences”; the day-and-year measure gives way to one that “takes into consideration the action itself of the faithful Christian,” in proportion to the charity with which the work is done. Peter J. Beer, a Jesuit of the Union Theological Institute, Hunters Hill, writes of Trent's decree that the bishops “put forward these two main points of doctrine” and that “the decree, however, went on to condemn the existing abuses”; he finds in the Tridentine debate “no evidence for episcopal jurisdiction to achieve the fruit of an indulgence independently of the recipient's disposition”; in Paul VI's teaching, he writes, the Church “authoritatively dispenses of the treasury of satisfaction of Christ and his saints, but the role of the recipient's love and sorrow and desire of union with Christ's expiation is decisive.”8 John Paul II calls indulgences “a sensitive subject, which has suffered historical misunderstandings,” and presents them, “far from being a sort of ‘discount’ on the duty of conversion,” as “an aid to its prompt, generous and radical fulfilment.” On this reading the reforms restored a practice to its purpose: the Church's intercession for penitents in the communion of saints.9

An admission of abuses that leaves the disputed doctrine in place

On this reading Trent owned the abuses but retained indulgences and the claims behind them, and those claims were what the Reformers disputed.

Held by: Martin Chemnitz (Lutheran, Examen Concilii Tridentini, part IV, 1573); the systematic theology departments of the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod's two seminaries (Lutheran, evaluation of the Joint Declaration, 1999); Salvatore Ricciardi (Reformed, c. 2000).10

Chemnitz opens his examination of the decree with the “impudentem et impiam indulgentiarum nundinationem,” the shameless and impious traffic in indulgences, which many learned and pious men had deplored for years without effect, until God made it the occasion for purging the Church's doctrine. Many expected, he writes, that Trent would bury indulgences or suspend its approval of them; instead its last session framed a decree for retaining them. There the fathers “dissimulare sane non audent abusus, pravos quaestus, corruptelas, superstitiones”: they do not dare to hide the abuses, the corrupt gains, the corruptions and superstitions. But they leave the correction of these to the will of the Roman pontiff, and they command that indulgences be retained (“Ipsas vero indulgentias in Ecclesia retinendas praecipiunt”), teaching that their use is most salutary and that the power to grant them came from Christ and was used in the most ancient times. Chemnitz sets out to test those claims against Scripture and the early Church, since on his account they were the matter in dispute.11

The Missouri Synod faculties make the same distinction four centuries later. The Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999), they write, “does not address itself directly to disputed beliefs and practices such as the ‘meritorious’ value of good works, purgatory, indulgences,” and “Lutherans cannot speak of consensus on justification as long as these related issues remain unsettled”; “Practice mattered as applied doctrine.” Their study guide describes the Jubilee indulgence proclaimed for 2000 as “very similar to indulgences at Luther's time.”12 Ricciardi, withdrawing from the Jubilee's ecumenical commission, placed the indulgence in “Christ crucified and risen” and held that “the Church cannot be an administrator to its conditions, but only a pure and simple witness.” On this reading the reformed practice still rests on the Church's power to dispense a treasury of satisfactions, and that power is the point on which the Reformers differed.13

The reforms gave up the money tie and recast the practice; a deeper difference remains

On this reading the Catholic reforms met part of the 1517 critique, the link between indulgences and money and much of the pastoral concern, while the underlying theology and the Church's mediating role stay in dispute.

Held by: Michael Root (Lutheran, “The Indulgence Controversy, Again,” First Things, 2001); Lawrence G. Duggan (historian, Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Indulgence”).14

Duggan writes that the Roman Catholic Church “conceded very few points to Luther or the other reformers,” and that one of them was “the fateful connection between money and indulgences”: Trent condemned “all base gain for securing indulgences” in 1563 and Pius V abolished their sale in 1567, while “the system and its underlying theology otherwise remained intact.” Paul VI then modified it in 1967 “by shifting the stress away from the satisfaction of punishment to the inducement of good works,” reducing the plenary indulgences and “eliminating the numerical system.”15

Root, writing as a Lutheran after the 2001 Rome consultation on indulgences, notes that many of the early-Reformation abuses “were banned already by the Council of Trent,” that the historical work of Nikolaus Paulus and Bernhard Poschmann lay behind statements that “have recast the theological understanding of indulgences,” with Karl Rahner's essays of the 1950s and 1960s as a leading influence, and that indulgences “are not quite what they used to be.” Recent Catholic teaching, he writes, situates them “within a genuine pursuit of repentance in daily life” and is “far closer to the first of Luther's Ninety-five Theses” than most recent Protestant statements on the Christian life. Two questions remain for him. The 1967 measure for partial indulgences “may do away with the crudest quantification, but it still seems closer to a commercial transaction than the dynamics of conversion”; and indulgences “still point to what may be the nub of Catholic-Protestant differences, the question of the role of the Church in the mediation of Christ's grace.” On this reading the reforms answered much of what Luther protested in 1517 and left the ecclesiological question for ecumenical dialogue.16
