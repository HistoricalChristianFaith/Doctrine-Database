Cyprian of Carthage: the libelli pacis — an embryonic commutation of penance

Dates: c. 250–251 (the Decian persecution and its aftermath) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the persecution under Decius (250–251) huge numbers of Christians lapsed — sacrificed to the gods or bought certificates saying they had. Church law assigned them a long public penance. But the imprisoned confessors (those awaiting martyrdom) began issuing letters of peace (libelli pacis) asking that named lapsed be restored to communion ahead of schedule, on the credit of the confessors' own suffering. Here, in the mid-third century, is the earliest recognizable ancestor of the indulgence: the penance owed by one person shortened on the strength of another's merit. Cyprian neither invents nor abolishes the practice; he tries to regulate it — and in doing so hands the later doctrine both its most suggestive precedent and its earliest warning about abuse.

Claims & evidence

The confessors' letters ask peace for the lapsed before their penance is done

you sent letters to me in which you ask that your wishes should be examined, and that peace should be granted to certain of the lapsed … those presbyters, contrary to the Gospel law, contrary also to your respectful petition, before penitence was fulfilled, before confession even of the gravest and most heinous sin was made, before hands were placed upon the repentant by the bishops and clergy, dare to offer on their behalf, and to give them the eucharist.

— Epistle X (Oxford ed. Ep. 15), A.D. 250, historicalchristian.faith. The mechanism is explicit: the confessors petition, and some presbyters then reconcile the lapsed “before penitence was fulfilled.” The confessor's standing before God — earned by enduring for the faith — is spent on someone else's behalf to abbreviate a satisfaction that would otherwise run for years. Strip away the anachronistic vocabulary and the shape is that of a later indulgence: a remission of assigned penance, granted through the church, drawing on a store of merit that is not the penitent's own.

Cyprian regulates the practice — named persons only, and the bishop must ratify

I beg you that you will designate by name in the certificate those whom you yourselves see, whom you have known, whose penitence you see to be very near to full satisfaction … For I hear that certificates are so given to some as that it is said, “Let such a one be received to communion along with his friends,” which was never in any case done by the martyrs.

— Epistle X (Ep. 15), historicalchristian.faith. Cyprian does not forbid the libelli; he polices them. The certificate must name individuals whose “penitence is very near to full satisfaction,” not vaguely cover “such a one and his friends” — a blanket formula that, he warns, could sweep in “twenty or thirty or more.” And peace granted on a confessor's letter still awaits the bishop's ratification once persecution ends. This is the earliest attempt to draw the line the medieval church would spend centuries redrawing: between a disciplined remission of penance and an indiscriminate traffic in it.

Hasty peace, before the debt is paid, is no peace — the church's power is not unlimited

before their sin is expiated, before confession has been made of their crime, before their conscience has been purged by sacrifice and by the hand of the priest, before the offence of an angry and threatening Lord has been appeased … They think that that is peace which some with deceiving words are blazoning forth: that is not peace, but war.

— On the Lapsed (De lapsis) 16, historicalchristian.faith. Cyprian's caution is doctrinally weighty. A remission granted before the satisfaction is made is not mercy but harm: the debt to an offended God is real and must be “appeased.” And he sets a firm limit on how far the confessors' merit can reach: “we believe, indeed, that the merits of martyrs and the works of the righteous are of great avail with the Judge; but that will be when the day of judgment shall come” (De lapsis 17). The living church may relax its own discipline; it does not thereby settle accounts before the divine tribunal. That distinction — between remitting ecclesial penance and remitting God's punishment — is precisely the hinge on which the developed indulgence doctrine, and the Reformation quarrel over it, would eventually turn.

Notes

  • The libelli pacis are the strongest ancient precedent for the core idea of an indulgence: one person's penance shortened on the credit of another's merit, administered through the church. But the differences are decisive and must not be blurred: the confessors' merit is applied to the living, not the dead; it remits assigned ecclesiastical penance, not a “temporal punishment” owed to God in an afterlife; there is no treasury of pooled, transferable merit dispensed by the pope; and no money changes hands. Cyprian is the ancestor, not the doctrine.
  • Cyprian sounds, in the third century, both notes the later debate will amplify: the practice's usefulness (mercy to the penitent, honour to the confessors) and its danger (indiscriminate, unratified, cheapened remission). His remedy — name the persons, verify the penitence, reserve the bishop's judgment — is the first disciplinary rule-book for what would become the indulgence.
  • Cyprian also insists the confessors' credit does not bind the bishops’ hands — martyrs “can do nothing against the Gospel” (De lapsis 20) — a check on the very mechanism the later treasury-of-merit doctrine would systematize. On his sense that martyr-merit avails only at the judgment, compare his witness on the purgatory timeline.
