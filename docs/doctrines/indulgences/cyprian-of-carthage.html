Cyprian of Carthage: the libelli pacis and the penance of the lapsed

Dates: c. 250–251 (the Decian persecution and its aftermath) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the persecution under Decius (250–251) huge numbers of Christians lapsed — sacrificed to the gods or bought certificates saying they had. Church law assigned them a long public penance. But the imprisoned confessors (those awaiting martyrdom) began issuing letters of peace (libelli pacis) asking that named lapsed be restored to communion ahead of schedule, on the credit of the confessors' own suffering. Cyprian neither forbids the letters nor leaves them unchecked; he tries to regulate them. William Kent's article “Indulgences” in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910) includes the libelli in its history of indulgences.

Claims & evidence

The confessors' letters ask peace for the lapsed before their penance is done

you sent letters to me in which you ask that your wishes should be examined, and that peace should be granted to certain of the lapsed … those presbyters, contrary to the Gospel law, contrary also to your respectful petition, before penitence was fulfilled, before confession even of the gravest and most heinous sin was made, before hands were placed upon the repentant by the bishops and clergy, dare to offer on their behalf, and to give them the eucharist.

— Epistle X (Oxford ed. Ep. 15), A.D. 250, historicalchristian.faith. The confessors petition, and some presbyters then reconcile the lapsed “before penitence was fulfilled.” The confessors' petition on behalf of others shortens a penance that would otherwise run for years.

Cyprian regulates the practice — named persons only, and the bishop must ratify

I beg you that you will designate by name in the certificate those whom you yourselves see, whom you have known, whose penitence you see to be very near to full satisfaction … For I hear that certificates are so given to some as that it is said, “Let such a one be received to communion along with his friends,” which was never in any case done by the martyrs.

— Epistle X (Ep. 15), historicalchristian.faith. Cyprian does not forbid the libelli; he polices them. The certificate must name individuals whose “penitence is very near to full satisfaction,” not vaguely cover “such a one and his friends” — a blanket formula that, he warns, could sweep in “twenty or thirty or more.” And peace granted on a confessor's letter still awaits the bishop's ratification once persecution ends.

Peace granted before the sin is expiated, and the martyrs' merits at the judgment

before their sin is expiated, before confession has been made of their crime, before their conscience has been purged by sacrifice and by the hand of the priest, before the offence of an angry and threatening Lord has been appeased … They think that that is peace which some with deceiving words are blazoning forth: that is not peace, but war.

— On the Lapsed (De lapsis) 16, historicalchristian.faith. Cyprian calls a peace granted before the satisfaction is made “not peace, but war,” and speaks of the offence of the Lord that must be “appeased.” In De lapsis 17 he writes: “We believe, indeed, that the merits of martyrs and the works of the righteous are of great avail with the Judge; but that will be when the day of judgment shall come.” In De lapsis 20 he writes that the martyrs “can do nothing against the Gospel, since they become martyrs on account of the Gospel.”

Peace on the martyrs' certificates for the dying

they who have received certificates from the martyrs, and may be assisted by their privilege with God, if they should be seized with any misfortune and peril of sickness, should, without waiting for my presence, before any presbyter who might be present, or if a presbyter should not be found and death begins to be imminent, before even a deacon, be able to make confession of their sin, that, with the imposition of hands upon them for repentance, they should come to the Lord with the peace which the martyrs have desired, by their letters to us, to be granted to them.

— Epistle XII (Oxford ed. Ep. 18), A.D. 250, §1, historicalchristian.faith. Writing to his clergy as the summer sicknesses began, Cyprian directs that lapsed holders of a martyr's certificate who fall dangerously ill may confess before a presbyter, or a deacon if no presbyter is found, and receive the peace the martyrs asked for, without waiting for his return.

Reception and debate

  • William Kent's article “Indulgences” (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) reads Cyprian as holding “the middle course” between Novatian and Felicissimus: “he condemns the abuses connected with the libellus, in particular the custom of having it made out in blank by the martyrs and filled in by any one who needed it,” and “he recognizes the value of these memorials” (newadvent.org).
