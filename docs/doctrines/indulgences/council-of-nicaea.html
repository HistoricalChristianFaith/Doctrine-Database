The Council of Nicaea (325): fixed penance — and the bishop's discretion to shorten it

Dates: 325 (the First Ecumenical Council) · Wikipedia
Summary: Nicaea's disciplinary canons on the lapsed (canons 11–13) do two things that matter for the later indulgence. First, they fix canonical penance as a public, graded, and strictly measured satisfaction — so many years as a “hearer,” so many as a “prostrator,” before restoration. Second, and decisively, canon 12 gives the bishop discretion to shorten that fixed term for those who show genuine zeal. That discretionary power to relax an assigned penance is, in the judgment of the standard editors of the canons, “the basis of ‘indulgences’ in their original form.”

Claims & evidence

Canon 11 — penance is fixed, graded, and counted in years

As many as were communicants, if they heartily repent, shall pass three years among the hearers; for seven years they shall be prostrators; and for two years they shall communicate with the people in prayers, but without oblation.

— Council of Nicaea (325), canon 11 (Percival, NPNF2 vol. 14), newadvent.org. The lapsed under Licinius are assigned a twelve-year graduated satisfaction, moving through the recognized grades of penitents — hearers, prostrators, and finally those admitted to the prayers but not yet the oblation. This is penance as a quantified debt: a set term that must be served before the church restores communion. Everything the later indulgence would remit — a measured penalty owed for forgiven sin — is here in its ancient, public, this-worldly form.

Canon 12 — the bishop may “determine yet more favourably”

For as many as give evidence of their conversions by deeds, and not pretence, with fear, and tears, and perseverance, and good works, when they have fulfilled their appointed time as hearers, may properly communicate in prayers; and after that the bishop may determine yet more favourably concerning them.

— canon 12, newadvent.org. This is the load-bearing clause. The fixed term is not absolute: where the penitent's conversion is proved “by deeds, and not pretence,” the bishop may cut it short — a relaxation of assigned penance granted by church authority on the strength of manifest fervour. The same discretionary power appears in canon 5 of Ancyra and canon 16 of Chalcedon. Percival's editors render the one Greek phrase variously — the canon reads “yet more favourably,” the Ancient Epitome “more leniently,” W. Bright “a yet more indulgent resolution” — and it is this episcopal discretion, not any doctrine of a treasury, that is the germ of the indulgence.

Canon 13 — the dying may not be denied the Viaticum

Concerning the departing, the ancient canonical law is still to be maintained, to wit, that, if any man be at the point of death, he must not be deprived of the last and most indispensable Viaticum.

— canon 13, newadvent.org. Even under the strict regime of counted years, the council makes a mercy-exception: a penitent at the point of death receives communion regardless of how much of his term remains. The rule is disciplinary, not doctrinal — but it shows the same instinct that drives the whole development: that the church holds, and may exercise, a power to remit the remainder of an assigned satisfaction.

Notes

  • The editors of the standard English edition of the canons draw the line to indulgences explicitly. On canon 12, W. Bright notes that the bishop's discretionary power to dispense with part of a penance-time “was the basis of ‘indulgences’ in their original form”; a second editorial note (Lambert) adds that the abuse of exactly this power — relaxing canonical penance — “led, in later times, to the practice of commuting such exercises for money payments.” (Philip Schaff & Henry R. Percival, eds., The Seven Ecumenical Councils, NPNF2 vol. 14, notes to canon 12, CCEL.)
  • What Nicaea supplies is the institutional pivot between Cyprian's ad-hoc third-century letters of peace and the medieval system: penance is now conciliar law — fixed, graded, church-administered — and the power to relax it is vested in the bishop as a matter of canon. The relaxation is still of the penitent's own ecclesiastical penance; there is no treasury of merit, no application to the dead, and no money. But the authority that later popes would exercise in granting indulgences is, in embryo, the bishop's canon-12 discretion.
  • The next step is the early-medieval penitentials, which turn this discretionary relaxation into a routine, tariffed commutation of penance — the mechanism the Catholic Encyclopedia and the editors above both name as the immediate ancestor of the indulgence proper.
