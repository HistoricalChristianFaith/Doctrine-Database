The Council of Nicaea (325): fixed penance — and the bishop's discretion to shorten it

Dates: 325 (the First Ecumenical Council) · Wikipedia
Summary: Nicaea's disciplinary canons on the lapsed (canons 11–13) fix canonical penance as a public, graded, and measured satisfaction — so many years as a “hearer,” so many as a “prostrator,” before restoration — and in canon 12 give the bishop discretion to shorten that term for those who show genuine zeal. The editors of the canons in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers call that discretionary power to relax an assigned penance “the basis of ‘indulgences’ in their original form.”

Claims & evidence

Canon 11 — penance is fixed, graded, and counted in years

As many as were communicants, if they heartily repent, shall pass three years among the hearers; for seven years they shall be prostrators; and for two years they shall communicate with the people in prayers, but without oblation.

— Council of Nicaea (325), canon 11 (Percival, NPNF2 vol. 14), newadvent.org. The lapsed under Licinius are assigned a twelve-year graduated satisfaction, moving through the recognized grades of penitents — hearers, prostrators, and finally those admitted to the prayers but not yet the oblation: a set term served before the church restores communion.

Canon 12 — the bishop may “determine yet more favourably”

For as many as give evidence of their conversions by deeds, and not pretence, with fear, and tears, and perseverance, and good works, when they have fulfilled their appointed time as hearers, may properly communicate in prayers; and after that the bishop may determine yet more favourably concerning them.

— canon 12, newadvent.org. The fixed term is not absolute: where the penitent's conversion is proved “by deeds, and not pretence,” the bishop may cut it short — a relaxation of assigned penance granted by church authority on the strength of manifest fervour. The same discretionary power appears in canon 5 of Ancyra and canon 16 of Chalcedon. Percival's editors render the one Greek phrase variously — the canon reads “yet more favourably,” the Ancient Epitome “more leniently,” W. Bright “a yet more indulgent resolution.” Bright's note identifies this episcopal discretion as the basis of indulgences in their earliest form (see below).

Canon 13 — the dying may not be denied the Viaticum

Concerning the departing, the ancient canonical law is still to be maintained, to wit, that, if any man be at the point of death, he must not be deprived of the last and most indispensable Viaticum. But, if any one should be restored to health again who has received the communion when his life was despaired of, let him remain among those who communicate in prayers only. But in general, and in the case of any dying person whatsoever asking to receive the Eucharist, let the Bishop, after examination made, give it him.

— canon 13, newadvent.org. Within the regime of counted years, the canon provides for the dying: a penitent at the point of death receives communion, whatever remains of his term; one who recovers after receiving it returns to the grade of those who “communicate in prayers only.”

Reception and debate

  • The editors of the standard English edition of the canons connect canon 12 with indulgences. W. Bright notes that the bishop's discretionary power to dispense with part of a penance-time “was the basis of ‘indulgences’ in their original form”; a second editorial note (Lambert) adds that the abuse of this power — relaxing canonical penance — “led, in later times, to the practice of commuting such exercises for money payments.” (Philip Schaff & Henry R. Percival, eds., The Seven Ecumenical Councils, NPNF2 vol. 14, notes to canon 12, CCEL.)
  • William Kent's article “Indulgences” (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) lists Nicaea with Ancyra (314), Laodicea and Arles among councils that show “similar leniency” in relaxing penance (newadvent.org).
  • Constantin Rus (Orthodox, Faculty of Orthodox Theology, Arad; Teologia, 2023) reads canon 12 within the Orthodox canonical discipline of penance, as the council “applying the principle of economy”: canon 12, he writes, “strengthens” canon 5 of Ancyra, “giving it a universal character,” and recognized the bishops “the right to erase or reduce the punishment for crimes” and to receive those who “give evidence of their conversions by deeds” (pp. 30–31). He writes that the Orthodox canons “speak of epitimia as medicines or remedies, and not as punishments” (p. 43). (Constantin Rus, “The Holy Mystery of Confession – Some Canonical Remarks,” Teologia 94/1 (2023), pp. 24–56, revistateologia.ro.)
  • The timeline continues with the early-medieval penitentials, which set out tariffs of penance and their commutation, and which the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Penitential Redemptions” and the editors of the penitentials also place in the history of indulgences.
