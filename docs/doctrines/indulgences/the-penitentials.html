The penitentials: when penance became redeemable

Dates: c. 550–800 (the Irish and insular tariff-books and their Continental diffusion) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Irish and insular penitentials — handbooks of Finnian, Columbanus, Cummean, Theodore, and their successors — took the ancient counted penance and turned it into a detailed tariff: a fixed satisfaction for every sin. They also let that satisfaction be commuted (Irish arreum, “redeemed”): a long fast could be discharged by psalm-singing, genuflexions, almsgiving — or by a money payment. Where Nicaea left the shortening of penance to the bishop's discretion, the penitentials set out standing tables of substitutions. Their modern editors, quoting H. Leclercq, and the Catholic Encyclopedia place these commutations in the history of indulgences.

Claims & evidence

A day of penance is redeemable — by psalms, genuflexions, alms, or a coin

… for one day on bread and water he shall sing fifty psalms kneeling, or without kneeling, seventy … and two hundred genuflexions are the equivalent of a day, or one denarius is the equivalent of one day, and three gifts of alms to three poor men are the equivalent of a day.

— Penitential ascribed to Bede, §11, in John T. McNeill & Helena M. Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance (Columbia University Press, 1938), p. 231, archive.org. Here the substitution is spelled out as an exchange-rate: a day of the assigned fast is worth so many psalms, so many genuflexions, three alms — or one denarius.

The Irish arreum: years of hard penance redeemed by prayer

An arreum of pure prayers for seven years of hard penance to save a soul from the tortures of hell, viz. one hundred offerings, hundred and fifty psalms, one hundred Beati, one hundred genuflexions at every beatitude, one hundred paternosters, one hundred soul-hymns.

— Old Irish Table of Commutations, §14, in McNeill & Gamer, p. 142, archive.org. The editors gloss the key term: “arreum” renders Old Irish arra, “equivalent, substitute, commutation.” Seven years of hard penance are discharged by a fixed quota of prayers, and the table states the purpose: “to save a soul from the tortures of hell.”

Leclercq and the Catholic Encyclopedia on commutations and indulgences

Commutations and redemptions played a considerable rôle in the Middle Ages, as the most effective causes of the debasement of tariff-penance; at the same time these practices greatly favored the introduction of indulgences properly so-called.

— H. Leclercq, quoted in McNeill & Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance, editors' introduction, archive.org. McNeill and Gamer quote Leclercq for the view that commutations and redemptions both debased tariff-penance and favoured the introduction of indulgences. The Catholic Encyclopedia's article on penitential redemptions writes that these redemptions “allow an alleviation, or a shortening of the time of penance; they thus resemble an indulgence, and have a place in the history of indulgences” — and continues: “Eventually these redemptions were offered indiscriminately to all, especially at the Council of Clermont of 1095 … when the crusade was suggested as a ransom from all penance. This was the modern indulgence” (newadvent.org).

Reception and debate

  • At Nicaea the relaxation of penance was an episcopal act for the manifestly fervent; the penitentials set out a standing tariff of substitutions, including a cash equivalent. The Catholic Encyclopedia's “Penitential Redemptions” connects these redemptions with Urban II's offer of the crusade at Clermont (1095) as “a ransom from all penance.”
  • The Old Irish table states its purpose as saving “a soul from the tortures of hell.”
  • The insular tariff-books passed onto the Continent in the eighth and ninth centuries. McNeill and Gamer report that the synod of Chalon-sur-Saône in 813 denounced the “libelli called penitentials, of which the errors are certain, the authors uncertain,” and that the council of Paris in 829 ordered them burned; they write that the “(ineffective) condemnations of the offending booklets by the councils of 813 made no distinction between the more rigorous and the laxer documents,” and that some of the laxer books “were among the most widely circulated of the entire series” (Medieval Handbooks of Penance, introduction, archive.org).
