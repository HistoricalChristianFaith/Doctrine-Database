The penitentials: when penance became redeemable

Dates: c. 550–800 (the Irish and insular tariff-books and their Continental diffusion) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Irish and insular penitentials — handbooks of Finnian, Columbanus, Cummean, Theodore, and their successors — took the ancient counted penance and turned it into a detailed tariff: a fixed satisfaction for every sin. Crucially, they also let that satisfaction be commuted (Irish arreum, “redeemed”): a long fast could be discharged by psalm-singing, genuflexions, almsgiving — or by a money payment. This is the missing link. The bishop's occasional discretion at Nicaea becomes, in the penitentials, a routine, priced substitution — and it is this practice that historians of the doctrine name as the immediate ancestor of the indulgence proper.

Claims & evidence

A day of penance is redeemable — by psalms, genuflexions, alms, or a coin

… for one day on bread and water he shall sing fifty psalms kneeling, or without kneeling, seventy … and two hundred genuflexions are the equivalent of a day, or one denarius is the equivalent of one day, and three gifts of alms to three poor men are the equivalent of a day.

— Penitential ascribed to Bede, §11, in John T. McNeill & Helena M. Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance (Columbia University Press, 1938), p. 231, archive.org. Here the substitution is spelled out as an explicit exchange-rate: a day of the assigned fast is worth so many psalms, so many genuflexions, three alms — or one denarius. The moment a coin can stand in for a day of penance, the satisfaction has become a purchasable commodity, and the road to the sold indulgence lies open.

The Irish arreum: years of hard penance redeemed by prayer

An arreum of pure prayers for seven years of hard penance to save a soul from the tortures of hell, viz. one hundred offerings, hundred and fifty psalms, one hundred Beati, one hundred genuflexions at every beatitude, one hundred paternosters, one hundred soul-hymns.

— Old Irish Table of Commutations, §14, in McNeill & Gamer, p. 142, archive.org. The editors gloss the key term: “arreum” renders Old Irish arra, “equivalent, substitute, commutation.” This is the technical machinery of redeemed penance — seven years of severe satisfaction discharged by a fixed quota of prayers — and note the stated purpose: “to save a soul from the tortures of hell.” The commutation is already being aimed past this-worldly discipline toward the sinner's fate in the world to come, the very direction the developed indulgence would take.

The scholars' verdict: commutations “favored the introduction of indulgences properly so-called”

Commutations and redemptions played a considerable rôle in the Middle Ages, as the most effective causes of the debasement of tariff-penance; at the same time these practices greatly favored the introduction of indulgences properly so-called.

— H. Leclercq, quoted in McNeill & Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance, editors' introduction, archive.org. The standard modern edition of the penitentials states the causal claim plainly: the practice of buying-out penance both debased the old tariff discipline and prepared the ground for the indulgence. The Catholic Encyclopedia's article on penitential redemptions concurs — these redemptions “allow an alleviation, or a shortening of the time of penance; they thus resemble an indulgence, and have a place in the history of indulgences” — and traces the line forward: “Eventually these redemptions were offered indiscriminately to all, especially at the Council of Clermont of 1095 … when the crusade was suggested as a ransom from all penance. This was the modern indulgence” (newadvent.org).

Notes

  • The penitentials are the hinge in the whole development. At Nicaea the relaxation of penance was an occasional episcopal act for the manifestly fervent; in the penitentials it is a standing tariff of substitutions, applied routinely and — fatally — admitting a cash equivalent. When Urban II at Clermont (1095) offered the crusade as a “ransom from all penance,” he was generalizing a redemption-mechanism the penitentials had already made ordinary.
  • The commutations still remit assigned penance, not (yet) a defined “temporal punishment” in purgatory, and they rest on no worked-out treasury of merit — that theology comes only in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. But two features already point beyond the discipline of the living: the appearance of the money payment, and the stated aim of saving “a soul from the tortures of hell.”
  • The insular tariff-books migrated onto the Continent in the eighth and ninth centuries — the Frankish church's councils (813) condemned the laxer booklets even as they circulated widely — carrying tariffed penance and its commutations into the Carolingian West, the seedbed of the medieval indulgence.
