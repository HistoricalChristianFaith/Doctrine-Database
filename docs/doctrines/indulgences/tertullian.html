Tertullian: public penance and the “satisfaction” that expunges punishment

Dates: c. 203 (On Repentance, pre-Montanist) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian's On Repentance is the earliest extant Latin treatise on the public penance of the church. It describes a single second repentance after baptism, carried out in a public act he calls by its Greek name exomologesis; it calls that act a satisfaction for post-baptismal sin; and it says that its “temporal mortification” can expunge eternal punishments. William Kent's article “Indulgences” in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910) cites other works of Tertullian in its history of indulgences.

Claims & evidence

One second repentance after baptism — “the plank after shipwreck”

That repentance, O sinner, like myself … do you so hasten to, so embrace, as a shipwrecked man the protection of some plank.

— On Repentance (De paenitentia) 4, historicalchristian.faith. Tertullian holds that after the “first” repentance sealed in baptism, the Christian who sins gravely is granted one further remedy — a “second, and only remaining” repentance, the “plank after shipwreck.”

Exomologesis: the outward, public satisfaction for sin

This act, which is more usually expressed and commonly spoken of under a Greek name, is exomologesis, whereby we confess our sins to the Lord, not indeed as if He were ignorant of them, but inasmuch as by confession satisfaction is settled … And thus exomologesis is a discipline for man's prostration and humiliation … it commands (the penitent) to lie in sackcloth and ashes, to cover his body in mourning … to bow before the feet of the presbyters, and kneel to God's dear ones; to enjoin on all the brethren to be ambassadors to bear his deprecatory supplication (before God).

— On Repentance 9, historicalchristian.faith. The passage describes the public penance as a bodily discipline of fasting, sackcloth, and prostration, performed before the presbyters and the assembled brethren, whose intercession the penitent enlists. Tertullian calls it a satisfaction, and it takes place before the church's presbyters and people.

Temporal mortification “expunges eternal punishments”

All this exomologesis (does), that it may enhance repentance … and by temporal mortification (I will not say frustrate, but) expunge eternal punishments. Therefore, while it abases the man, it raises him.

— On Repentance 9, historicalchristian.faith. Tertullian sets a temporal suffering — the penitent's self-mortification here and now — against eternal punishments, and says the former can expunge the latter.

Reception and debate

  • William Kent's article “Indulgences” in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910) cites Tertullian in its history of indulgences: To the Martyrs 1, on the peace that some “are accustomed to beg from the martyrs in prison,” and the Montanist De pudicitia, in which Tertullian “attacks the pope for his alleged laxity in admitting adulterers to penance and pardon” (newadvent.org). In On Modesty 21 Tertullian cites the Paraclete speaking through the new prophets, “The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it, lest they commit others withal,” and writes that “the Church,” it is true, “will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops” (historicalchristian.faith).
  • Kent's article also treats the relaxation of penance by the bishop at Nicaea and other councils, and the commutations of the penitentials, as part of the same history.
