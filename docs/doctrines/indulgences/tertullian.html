Tertullian: public penance and the “satisfaction” that expunges punishment

Dates: c. 203 (On Repentance, pre-Montanist) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the earliest Latin witness to the disciplined system of public penance out of which, a millennium later, the indulgence would grow. He does not teach indulgences — there is no notion here of a treasury of merit or of remitting punishment in an afterlife. What he supplies is the root vocabulary and machinery: a single second repentance after baptism, carried out in a public act he calls by its Greek name exomologesis; the idea that this act is a satisfaction owed for post-baptismal sin; and the striking claim that its “temporal mortification” can expunge punishment. Change what the satisfaction is measured against — from Tertullian's discipline of the living penitent to the developed doctrine's “temporal punishment due to sin” — and you have the conceptual skeleton the later system inherited.

Claims & evidence

One second repentance after baptism — “the plank after shipwreck”

That repentance, O sinner, like myself … do you so hasten to, so embrace, as a shipwrecked man the protection of some plank.

— On Repentance (De paenitentia) 4, historicalchristian.faith. Tertullian holds that after the “first” repentance sealed in baptism, the Christian who sins gravely is granted one further remedy — a “second, and only remaining” repentance, the famous “plank after shipwreck.” This one-shot post-baptismal penance is the disciplinary frame within which every later development — canonical satisfactions, their relaxation, their commutation, and finally the indulgence — unfolds.

Exomologesis: the outward, public satisfaction for sin

This act, which is more usually expressed and commonly spoken of under a Greek name, is exomologesis, whereby we confess our sins to the Lord, not indeed as if He were ignorant of them, but inasmuch as by confession satisfaction is settled … And thus exomologesis is a discipline for man's prostration and humiliation … it commands (the penitent) to lie in sackcloth and ashes, to cover his body in mourning … to bow before the feet of the presbyters, and kneel to God's dear ones; to enjoin on all the brethren to be ambassadors to bear his deprecatory supplication (before God).

— On Repentance 9, historicalchristian.faith. Here is the whole machinery of ancient public penance in one paragraph: a bodily discipline of fasting, sackcloth, and prostration, performed before the presbyters and the assembled brethren, whose intercession the penitent enlists. Two words matter for the later story. The penance is a satisfaction (satisfactio) — the very term the developed doctrine will use for what an indulgence remits — and it is a public, church-supervised act, so the church already has a recognized role in measuring and administering it.

Temporal mortification “expunges eternal punishments”

All this exomologesis (does), that it may enhance repentance … and by temporal mortification (I will not say frustrate, but) expunge eternal punishments. Therefore, while it abases the man, it raises him.

— On Repentance 9, historicalchristian.faith. This is the single most consequential sentence for the developmental line. Tertullian sets a temporal suffering — the penitent's self-mortification here and now — against punishment, and says the former can cancel the latter. He is speaking of the eternal penalty of unrepented sin, not the later “temporal punishment” in purgatory; his framework has no purgatory and no transferable merit. But the grammar of the developed doctrine — a punishment owed, and a satisfaction that discharges it — is already fully present. The Latin West would keep the structure and, over centuries, relocate the debt from the penitent's public discipline to the “temporal punishment due to sin” that an indulgence remits.

Notes

  • Tertullian is a witness to the penitential system, not to indulgences. The distance is enormous: no treasury of merit, no application to the dead, no remission granted by pontifical authority in exchange for a work or a payment. Reading him as an early proponent of indulgences would be an anachronism — he is the soil, not the plant.
  • His load-bearing contribution is threefold: (1) the one-penance discipline that frames the whole later development; (2) the language of satisfaction for sin; and (3) the notion that a temporal act can discharge a debt of punishment. The developed doctrine changes what the satisfaction pays off and who may remit or commute it; it did not have to invent the idea that punishment can be satisfied.
  • The same treatise's picture of penance as a church-supervised, public act — the penitent at the presbyters' feet, the brethren interceding — is what gives the church a standing role over the penance, the role it will later exercise in relaxing, commuting, and finally indulging it. Tertullian himself, in his rigorist Montanist years, would come to deny that the church could forgive the gravest sins at all (On Modesty) — a reminder that the scope of the church's power over penance was contested from the start.
