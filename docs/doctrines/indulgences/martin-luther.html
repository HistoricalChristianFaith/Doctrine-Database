Martin Luther: the Ninety-Five Theses on indulgences (1517)

Dates: c. 1517 (the Ninety-Five Theses, 31 October 1517) · Wikipedia
Summary: On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther (1483–1546), a friar and Wittenberg professor, sent to Archbishop Albrecht — and, in the traditional account, posted — ninety-five theses Disputatio pro declaratione virtutis indulgentiarum, provoked by Johann Tetzel's preaching of the St. Peter's indulgence near the Saxon border. It is essential to read the 1517 Luther as of 1517: this is an academic disputation, in Latin, by a man still inside the Roman obedience, who in the same document affirms true papal indulgences (thesis 71) and the pope's power to remit the penalties he himself imposes (theses 5–6). His fire in 1517 is trained on two things: the sale and its attendant false security, and the specific claim that money buys a soul out of purgatory. The deeper attack — on the treasury of merit itself — is present in germ (thesis 62) but not yet the wholesale rejection of the Roman church he would reach in the years after. The theses were never meant to found a movement; they founded one anyway.

Claims & evidence

Against the preachers' overreach and the false security of a pardon-letter

21. Therefore those preachers of indulgences are in error, who say that by the pope's indulgences a man is freed from every penalty, and saved;

32. They will be condemned eternally, together with their teachers, who believe themselves sure of their salvation because they have letters of pardon.

— The Ninety-Five Theses (1517), theses 21 and 32, Project Wittenberg. Luther's first target is not the institution of the indulgence but the preachers (“those preachers … are in error”) — Tetzel and his kind — who oversell it. The abuse he names is a pastoral one: the letter of pardon breeds a false assurance of salvation, which he calls damning. This is the wedge; the theology comes behind it.

The jingle answered: no divine authority for a soul that springs when the coin rings

27. They preach man who say that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out [of purgatory].

28. It is certain that when the penny jingles into the money-box, gain and avarice can be increased, but the result of the intercession of the Church is in the power of God alone.

— theses 27–28, Project Wittenberg. These are the theses aimed straight at the couplet fastened to Tetzel and at the fourth grace of the Instructio Summaria. Luther does not deny that the Church may intercede for the dead; he denies that the transaction is mechanical — the coin's ring guarantees nothing, for “the result … is in the power of God alone.” What the money reliably produces is not a released soul but “gain and avarice.”

The laity's shrewd question: why not empty purgatory for free?

82. Why does not the pope empty purgatory, for the sake of holy love and of the dire need of the souls that are there, if he redeems an infinite number of souls for the sake of miserable money with which to build a Church? The former reasons would be most just; the latter is most trivial.

— thesis 82, Project Wittenberg. Luther puts the sharpest objection in the mouth of the laity (theses 82–90, “the shrewd questionings”): if papal power can spring souls from purgatory, charity — not cash — should move the pope to empty it outright. The wiki weighs this dilemma on its own page and finds it weak as stated (the Church claims no jurisdiction over the dead, only intercession per modum suffragii) — but as a rhetorical exposure of the sale, it was devastating.1

The theological root: the Church's true treasure is the gospel, not a fund of merits

62. The true treasure of the Church is the Most Holy Gospel of the glory and the grace of God.

— thesis 62, Project Wittenberg. Here alone the 1517 protest reaches past the abuse to the doctrine that underwrites it: against Clement VI's treasury of Christ's and the saints' superabundant merits (defined in Unigenitus, 1343), Luther redefines the Church's “treasure” as the gospel itself. This is the seed of the mature Protestant rejection — but in 1517 it is one thesis among ninety-five, not yet a programme, and Luther still frames the whole as a disputation inviting reply.

What Luther still affirmed in 1517

71. Let him be anathema and accursed who denies the apostolic character of the indulgences.

— thesis 71, Project Wittenberg. This is the thesis most often forgotten. In 1517 Luther still grants that indulgences rightly understood have “apostolic” standing, and he affirms the pope's real power to remit the penalties the pope himself or the canons impose (theses 5–6) — while denying any papal power over the guilt of sin or over the punishments God reserves. The 1517 Luther is a reformer of an abuse, not yet the breaker of the whole edifice; that reading develops only through the Leipzig disputation (1519), the bull Exsurge Domine (1520), and his excommunication (1521).2

Notes

  • Represent 1517 accurately, not anachronistically. The Ninety-Five Theses attack the sale, the false security it bred, and the claim over the dead — and, in thesis 62, the treasury-theology behind it — but they do not yet reject papal indulgences as such (thesis 71) or the Roman church. Reading the mature Luther back into 1517 flatters the document and misses its real force: it was radical precisely as a Catholic disputation.
  • Luther's later, settled teaching on the neighbouring question — whether a Christian may still pray for the dead once purgatory is denied — is set out on his purgatory detail page (he permits a restrained, faith-resting prayer; he tears out the endowed Masses and the obligation). This page treats only the 1517 protest against indulgences.
  • The scriptural warrant the indulgence-defenders appealed to — the power of the keys (Matt 16:19 // 18:18) — and its scope are weighed on its own argument page (assessment contested); the historical origin thesis on the crusade-indulgence page (sound).
