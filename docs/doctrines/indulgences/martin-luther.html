Martin Luther: the Ninety-Five Theses on indulgences (1517)

Dates: c. 1517 (the Ninety-Five Theses, 31 October 1517) · Wikipedia
Summary: On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther (1483–1546), a friar and Wittenberg professor, sent to Archbishop Albrecht — and, in the traditional account, posted — ninety-five theses Disputatio pro declaratione virtutis indulgentiarum, provoked by Johann Tetzel's preaching of the St. Peter's indulgence near the Saxon border. The theses are an academic disputation, in Latin, by a friar still inside the Roman obedience; in the same document Luther anathematizes whoever “speaks against the truth of apostolic pardons” (thesis 71) and affirms the pope's power to remit the penalties he himself imposes (thesis 5). They attack the sale and the security he held it bred, and the claim that money buys a soul out of purgatory; thesis 58 denies that the treasures from which the pope grants indulgences are “the merits of Christ and the Saints” (the treasury of merit), and thesis 62 names the Church's true treasure as the gospel. The Leipzig disputation (1519), the bull Exsurge Domine (1520) and Luther's excommunication (1521) followed.

Claims & evidence

Against the preachers and the trust placed in pardon-letters

21. Therefore those preachers of indulgences are in error, who say that by the pope's indulgences a man is freed from every penalty, and saved;

32. They will be condemned eternally, together with their teachers, who believe themselves sure of their salvation because they have letters of pardon.

— The Ninety-Five Theses (1517), theses 21 and 32, Project Wittenberg. Thesis 21 names the preachers (“those preachers … are in error”); thesis 32 says that trust in a letter of pardon as assurance of salvation leads to condemnation.

Theses 27–28: the coin and the soul

27. They preach man who say that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out [of purgatory].

28. It is certain that when the penny jingles into the money-box, gain and avarice can be increased, but the result of the intercession of the Church is in the power of God alone.

— theses 27–28, Project Wittenberg. These theses answer the claim summed up in the couplet fastened to Tetzel, and the fourth grace of the Instructio Summaria. Thesis 28 speaks of “the intercession of the Church” for the dead and places its result “in the power of God alone”; what the coin's ring increases, it says, is “gain and avarice.”

The laity's questions: why not empty purgatory for love?

82. Why does not the pope empty purgatory, for the sake of holy love and of the dire need of the souls that are there, if he redeems an infinite number of souls for the sake of miserable money with which to build a Church? The former reasons would be most just; the latter is most trivial.

— thesis 82, Project Wittenberg. Luther gives the objection to the laity (theses 81–90, “the shrewd questionings”): if papal power can release souls from purgatory, charity rather than money should move the pope to empty it. The historian Lawrence Duggan writes that in thesis 82 Luther “vitiated papal control of the Treasury of Merit” (“Indulgence,” Encyclopaedia Britannica).

Theses 58 and 62: the treasures of the Church

58. Nor are they the merits of Christ and the Saints, for even without the pope, these always work grace for the inner man, and the cross, death, and hell for the outward man.

62. The true treasure of the Church is the Most Holy Gospel of the glory and the grace of God.

— theses 58 and 62, Project Wittenberg. Where Clement VI's Unigenitus (1343) had spoken of a treasury of the merits of Christ and the saints, thesis 58 says that the “treasures of the Church” out of which the pope grants indulgences (thesis 56) are not those merits, and thesis 62 names the gospel as the Church's “true treasure.” The Lutheran–Roman Catholic Commission on Unity (2013) records that Luther “also wondered about the nature of the treasury of the church out of which the pope offered indulgences” (From Conflict to Communion, §42).

What the theses affirm

71. He who speaks against the truth of apostolic pardons, let him be anathema and accursed!

— thesis 71, Project Wittenberg. In thesis 71 Luther anathematizes whoever “speaks against the truth of apostolic pardons,” and he affirms the pope's real power to remit the penalties “which he has imposed either by his own authority or by that of the Canons” (thesis 5); guilt the pope remits only “by declaring that it has been remitted by God,” though “he may grant remission in cases reserved to his judgment” (thesis 6). The Leipzig disputation (1519), the bull Exsurge Domine (1520), and his excommunication (1521) followed.1

Reception and debate

  • The Lutheran–Roman Catholic Commission on Unity (2013) describes the theses as meant “to inaugurate an academic disputation on open and unresolved questions regarding the theory and practice of indulgences,” and records that “Luther repeatedly insisted that, apart from the first four propositions, the theses were not his own definitive assertions but rather propositions written for disputation” (From Conflict to Communion, §§40, 44).
  • Lawrence Duggan writes that Luther “was not (as is widely thought) moved originally to a critique of the system by these abuses but rather by his own terrible spiritual suffering” (“Indulgence,” Encyclopaedia Britannica).
  • Luther's later teaching on the neighbouring question — whether a Christian may still pray for the dead once purgatory is denied — is set out on his purgatory detail page. This page treats only the 1517 protest against indulgences.
