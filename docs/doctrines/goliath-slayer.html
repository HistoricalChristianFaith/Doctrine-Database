Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19)

The Hebrew Bible reports the death of Goliath twice, and the second time it names a different man. In 1 Samuel 17 the shepherd David fells the champion of Gath; yet 2 Samuel 21:19, listing David's later Philistine wars, reads — in the Masoretic Hebrew — "Elhanan the son of Jaare-oregim, the Bethlehemite, struck down Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam." The parallel in 1 Chronicles 20:5 has a third form: "Elhanan the son of Jair struck down Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite."

Modern translations handle the verse differently. The King James and NIV bring Chronicles' "the brother of" into 2 Samuel, the NIV noting that "the Hebrew does not have the brother of," while the ESV keeps "struck down Goliath" and notes, "Contrast 1 Chronicles 20:5, which may preserve the original reading."1 Among the witnesses gathered here, the ancient and medieval ones keep a single Goliath slain by David, by omission or by identifying Elhanan with David; from Kennicott onward the commentators below hold that the Masoretic text of 2 Samuel 21:19 has suffered in copying, and differ on the direction of the change. Kennicott and Keil & Delitzsch take 1 Chronicles 20:5 ("Elhanan slew Lahmi the brother of Goliath") to preserve the original; Wellhausen, Driver, H. P. Smith and McCarter take Samuel's reading as the older and Chronicles as a harmonizing correction, a view a 2015 commentary is quoted as attributing to "most scholars"; Ozoliņš takes both verses to descend from a shared original in which Elhanan killed Goliath's unnamed brother.2

The dispute turns on a few small Hebrew differences: the first ʾōregîm ("weavers"), which the commentators from Kennicott onward take as duplicated from the "weaver's beam" at the verse's end; the object-marker ʾet against ʾaḥî ("brother of"), whose letters resemble it; "Lahmi" (laḥmî) against "the Bethlehemite" (bêt hallaḥmî). Kennicott, Keil & Delitzsch, Heiser and Ozoliņš read these differences as scribal slips; Driver and Smith read the Chronicles side as a deliberate harmonization. Which way the change ran is the crux. The verse also figures in the questions about the Hebrew and Greek textual traditions discussed on the Septuagint-vs.-Masoretic, Septuagint-origin, and Old Testament canon pages: Driver dates the intruded "oregim" before the Septuagint, and Ozoliņš reports that the Dead Sea Samuel material has "a gap at exactly this verse."3 This page gathers the witnesses to the crux, earliest extant attestation first; the readings of the verse are set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

Josephus (c. 93)

Josephus is the earliest extant writer to retell both passages. Retelling 1 Samuel 17 he keeps David the slayer of the named Goliath, "cut off his head with his own sword… but David carried the head of Goliath into his own tent."4 Yet when he reaches the giant-wars of 2 Samuel 21, he renders the Elhanan of verse 19 as "Nephan his kinsman," who "fought in a single combat with the stoutest of all the Philistines, and slew him" — and the name Goliath never recurs in Book 7.5 With the name kept for David's victim and Nephan's giant left anonymous, his narrative contains a single Goliath, killed by David; he does not say whether he did this deliberately or followed a text in which the second giant was unnamed.

Targum Jonathan (c. 400)

The Aramaic Targum Jonathan identifies Elhanan with David. Reading the strange patronymic as a bundle of titles, it renders the verse "And David the son of Jesse, weaver of the curtains of the sanctuary, killed Goliath the Gittite, the wood of whose spear was like a weaver's heddle rod."6 The consonants of the Hebrew stand, but "Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite" becomes a description of David, and the verse restates 1 Samuel 17. It is the earliest extant attestation of the identification.

Pseudo-Jerome, Hebrew Questions (traditionally c. 400)

The Christian-Latin form of the same identification. The Quaestiones Hebraicae in libros Regum et Paralipomenon, read for centuries as Jerome's and now listed among works of uncertain authorship, translates "Elhanan" as Adeodatus ("God-given") and argues from the etymologies — and from the chapter's closing note that the giants "fell by the hand of David" — that "Adeodatus is David himself… called Adeodatus because he was chosen by God for the kingdom."7 Its parallel note on 1 Chronicles 20:5 gives a different account: David killed Goliath, and Elhanan killed Goliath's brother. (The authorship and date are uncertain; the c. 400 is the traditional ascription, not a secure one.)

Rashi (c. 1090)

Rashi, the medieval Jewish commentator, repeats the Targum's identification: "Elhanan — David. We know that it was David who killed Goliath in Shmuel I, 17:51, consequently this Elhanan must be David."8 He glosses the patronymic through the same "weaver of the sanctuary curtains" image — a single Goliath, and 2 Samuel 21:19 a poetic byname for David.

David Kimhi — Radak (c. 1200)

Radak reports both answers — the Chronicles-harmonization (the man slain was Goliath's kinsman) and the Targum's identification of Elhanan with David — and then says the identification puzzles him. Having quoted the Targum's "David… killed Goliath," he adds, "but as to why [Scripture] called David 'Elhanan,' I do not know the reason for it."9

Benjamin Kennicott (1753)

With Benjamin Kennicott, the Oxford Hebraist and collator of Hebrew manuscripts, the verse becomes an argument that the printed Hebrew text contains transcribers' errors. "Goliath the Gittite was slain by David," he reasons, "and therefore there must have been a Mistake of some Transcriber here"; the reading of 1 Chronicles 20:5 — "Elhanan… slew Lahmi the Brother of Goliath" — is "plain and consistent," and "these words have evidently been corrupted into the words now found in Samuel."10 He reconstructs the corruption letter by letter and holds that Chronicles preserves the original.

Julius Wellhausen (1871)

Julius Wellhausen, in his monograph on the text of Samuel, isolates the mechanical fault — "the spelling Jaare instead of Jair… was probably occasioned by the following oregim ('weavers'), whose accidental intrusion from the line below is easy to recognize" — but warns against importing the Chronicler's text, "given his tendency to avoid archaisms."11 He treats the Chronicler as a reviser.

Keil & Delitzsch (c. 1875)

The Lutheran commentary of Keil & Delitzsch likewise holds Chronicles original. The two verses "presuppose the same original text, so that the difference can only have arisen from an error in copying"; therefore "we must regard the reading in the Chronicles as the correct one, and alter our text accordingly; since the assumption that there were two Goliaths is a very improbable one."12 On their reading Samuel is emended to match Chronicles, and David is the only slayer of Goliath.

S. R. Driver (1890)

S. R. Driver's Notes argues the reverse direction. Comparing the two readings, he finds it "scarcely credible that a scribe having before him a text identical with that of [Chronicles]… could, with the knowledge of I 17 that he must have possessed, have so altered… it as to make it state that 'Elhanan… slew Goliath of Gath!'" — whereas "a motive for the correction of the text of Samuel by the Chronicler… is obvious."13 On his reading Samuel is prior and Chronicles the harmonization; he lists Ewald, Thenius, Bertheau, Wellhausen and Kuenen on the same side.

Henry Preserved Smith (1899)

The International Critical Commentary of Henry Preserved Smith states it in a sentence: "Elhanan ben Jair the Bethlehemite slew Goliath the Gittite; the harmonistic purpose of the Chronicler in making the victim the brother of Goliath is evident."14 He also applies the difficulty to 1 Samuel 17, noting that Elhanan's deed "would weigh somewhat against the present form of that narrative."

P. Kyle McCarter (1984)

The Anchor Bible commentary of P. Kyle McCarter extends the Samuel-prior reading from the text to the tradition. The Chronicler's "'Laḥmi, the brother of Goliath'" is "a scribal error with the effect of harmonizing this notice with I Samuel 17"; and beyond the text, "there is no doubt that the tradition attributing the slaying of Goliath to Elhanan is older than that which credits the deed to David," the contradiction "eliminated in the targumic and midrashic literature by identifying David with Elhanan."15 On this reading the champion of 1 Samuel 17 was originally anonymous, and "Goliath" was drawn into that story from Elhanan's older notice.

Michael S. Heiser (2014)

Michael S. Heiser presents the crux to a popular audience as ordinary scribal transmission, not contradiction: "somewhere along the chain of copying… one scribe made a mistake in 2 Sam 21:19," so that the verse is "a defective reading… the result of a scribe's sincere effort to cope with a problematic manuscript," resolved by 1 Chronicles 20:5.16 He takes Chronicles as the reading by which the Samuel verse should be corrected, and describes the Masoretic verse as a copying error.

Kaspars Ozoliņš (2022)

Kaspars Ozoliņš of Tyndale House lays out three possibilities and favours the reconstruction that "Elhanan the Bethlehemite actually killed the brother of Goliath, who himself was left unnamed" — both texts descending from a shared original that each branch garbled, "Lahmi" being not a real name but a scribal slip for "the Bethlehemite" — while stressing that "we are still left with uncertainty" and that "scribes copying the Hebrew Bible rarely made deliberate changes to their text."17
