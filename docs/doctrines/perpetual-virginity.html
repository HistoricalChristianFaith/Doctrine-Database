The Perpetual Virginity of Mary

Did Mary remain a virgin her whole life — ever-virgin, the mother of Jesus and of no one else — or did she and Joseph have other children after his birth? Much of the written debate turns on the “brethren of the Lord” whom the Gospels and Epistles name (Matthew 13:55; Mark 6:3; John 7:5; Acts 1:14; 1 Corinthians 9:5; Galatians 1:19), four of them by name — James, Joseph (Joses), Simon, and Jude — alongside unnamed sisters. Writers below identify them as Mary’s later-born children, as Joseph’s children by an earlier marriage, or as cousins; the first identification excludes the perpetual virginity, and the other two are compatible with it. Others below speak of the virginity itself without taking up the brethren at all.

Three historic answers recur, and this page uses the names J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican) gave them in his 1865 study of the question, after a fourth-century advocate of each:1

  • The Helvidian view — the brethren are sons of Joseph and Mary, born after Jesus; on this view Mary was not ever-virgin. Named for Helvidius, who argued it at Rome (c. 383); Lightfoot counted Tertullian its earliest patristic representative. Richard Bauckham (Anglican) describes it as the view “which probably most modern exegetes, even including some Roman Catholic scholars, hold.”
  • The Epiphanian view — the brethren are sons of Joseph by a former wife, older step-brothers of Jesus and no blood-kin of Mary. Named for Epiphanius of Salamis; its witnesses include the second-century Protevangelium of James, Clement, Origen, Eusebius, and Gregory of Nyssa. Bauckham describes it as “the traditional view in the Eastern Orthodox churches”; the Orthodox theologian Thomas Hopko writes that the brethren “according to Orthodox doctrine were not children born of Mary who is confessed as ‘ever-virgin,’ but were either cousins or children of Joseph.” Lightfoot held this view.
  • The Hieronymian view — the brethren are maternal cousins, sons of “Mary of Clopas,” taken as the Virgin’s sister, so that “brother” means kinsman; on this view both Mary and Joseph remained virgins. Named for Jerome, in whose reply to Helvidius (c. 383) it is earliest extant. Bauckham describes it as the view “which through Jerome’s influence became the traditional western Catholic view”; Florentine Bechtel, in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), holds that “James, Joseph, and Jude are undoubtedly His cousins.” Whether it had witnesses before Jerome is disputed (see the reading on its antiquity).

The witnesses below are set out earliest first, each with his own words where they survive. Lightfoot’s three names are used throughout as labels for the readings.

Timeline

The Gospel of Peter — c. 150

The Gospel of Peter, an apocryphal gospel whose own words on the brethren do not survive, is named by Origen beside the Protevangelium as a source for one identification of the brethren: he reports that “some… basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter… or the Book of James, say that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary,” those who say so wishing to “preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end.”2 J. B. Lightfoot noted that the text was esteemed by the second-century Docetae and already in circulation when Serapion of Antioch found it at Rhossus, and dated it “about the middle of the second century at the latest, and probably somewhat earlier”; on that dating it carries the Epiphanian identification as early as the Protevangelium. The attestation is second-hand, so the Gospel of Peter’s own testimony remains ⚠ unverified.3

The Protevangelium of James — c. 150

The earliest extant text bearing on the question that survives entire is an anonymous infancy narrative, the Protevangelium of James, presented as the work of James the Lord’s brother and dated by J. B. Lightfoot “probably not later than the middle of the second century.” It is the earliest extant source of the Epiphanian reading: it makes Joseph an aged widower who already “had children” before he was betrothed to Mary, so that the “brethren of the Lord” are his sons by a former marriage and no blood-kin of Mary.4 The same detail recurs through the narrative — Joseph enrols “my sons” at Bethlehem and leaves “his two sons” with Mary at the cave — while no daughter is named,5 and the closing colophon presents “James” the supposed author as already a grown man at Herod’s death.6 Lightfoot judged the book “a purely fictitious… narrative” and treated it as evidence that belief in Mary’s perpetual virginity, and the explanation of the brethren as Joseph’s earlier children, were in circulation by about 150; a family of later infancy apocrypha follow it on this point.7

Hegesippus — c. 175

Hegesippus, a second-century Palestinian Hebrew Christian who gathered the traditions of the Lord’s kindred, wrote five books of Memoirs that survive only where Eusebius quotes them. He uses two different terms for the Lord’s relatives: James and Jude are styled the Lord’s brother, while Symeon, who succeeded James as bishop of Jerusalem, is the Lord’s cousin — “All proposed him as second bishop because he was a cousin of the Lord,” the son of Joseph’s brother Clopas.8 Eusebius twice repeats the point from him — Symeon “was a cousin, as they say, of the Saviour, for Hegesippus records that Clopas was a brother of Joseph.”9 Where Hegesippus names James he calls him “the brother of the Lord,” and Jude he calls one “who is said to have been the Lord’s brother according to the flesh.”10 J. B. Lightfoot read the distinction between the brethren (James, Jude) and the cousin (Symeon son of Clopas) as opposed to the Hieronymian reading, which makes the brethren themselves cousins, and took the qualifier “said to be” as leaning Epiphanian rather than Helvidian: “the testimony of Hegesippus seems distinctly opposed to the Hieronymian view, while of the other two it favours the Epiphanian rather than the Helvidian.”11 How Hegesippus’s description of Symeon should be construed is disputed; see the reading of Church History iv.22.4.

Clement of Alexandria — c. 200

Clement of Alexandria is the earliest extant father to state the Epiphanian reading in his own voice. Commenting on the Epistle of Jude — in notes from his lost Hypotyposeis that survive in a sixth-century Latin abridgement by Cassiodorus — he explains why Jude, though kin to the Lord, styles himself only “brother of James”: because he was one of Joseph’s sons and reverence forbade the nearer claim. “Jude, who wrote the Catholic Epistle, the brother of the sons of Joseph… did not say that he himself was His brother… he was His brother, (the son) of Joseph.”12 J. B. Lightfoot argued that the statement must be Clement’s own and not the translator’s, since Cassiodorus, a Latin churchman, would have held the Hieronymian view. Of a separate Greek extract in Eusebius, where Clement notes that “there were two Jameses,” Lightfoot wrote that it “neither states nor implies that there were two only” and does not contradict the note on Jude; he judged Clement’s Epiphanian statement “explicit” and set him second, after the Gospel of Peter and Protevangelium, among the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” witnesses.13 How far the Latin transmission bears on Clement’s witness is disputed; see the reading of Clement’s surviving texts.

Tertullian — c. 210

Tertullian is the earliest father whom J. B. Lightfoot counted a Helvidian — reading the “brethren” as Mary’s own later children — though Tertullian states it nowhere as a thesis; it is drawn from his anti-Marcionite polemic. Insisting against Marcion and Apelles that Christ was truly born, he argues that the mother and brethren who “stood without” must have really been His — “if… He made them ‘His mother and His brethren’ who were not so, how could He deny them these relationships who really had them?”14 His argument, as Lightfoot put it, “seems to imply that the Lord’s brethren were His brothers in the same sense in which Mary was His mother.” Where he speaks of Mary’s virginity directly, he confines it to the conception and denies that it continued through the birth: “although she was a virgin when she conceived, she was a wife when she brought forth her son… under the very law of ‘opening the womb,’” a virgin only “about to marry once for all after her delivery.”15 Helvidius later claimed his authority, and Jerome replied that Tertullian was “not a man of the Church”; Lightfoot judged it “highly probable” (though, he cautioned, not absolutely certain) that this “strenuous… advocate of asceticism” held the Helvidian view.16 Whether Tertullian taught the Helvidian reading of the brethren, or spoke only of the manner of the birth, is disputed; see “Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus?”

The Clementine Literature — c. 220

Between Tertullian and Origen stands a witness of a different kind: the Pseudo-Clementine literature, a romance from Jewish-Christian (Ebionite) circles, whose extant Greek Homilies and Latin Recognitions descend from a third-century source drawing on second-century Ebionite writings. These circles denied the virgin birth, but the work bears on the brethren question at one point: it exalts James the Lord’s brother and keeps him apart from the Twelve. The prefixed Epistle of Clement hails James as “the bishop of bishops, who rules Jerusalem,” and the Homilies make him, “called the brother of my Lord,” the standard by which every “apostle or teacher or prophet” must be tested.17 The Recognitions — in a Temple-disputation scene founded on the early Ebionite Ascents of James — has the twelve apostles, “James the son of Alphaeus” among them, dispute in turn and report to “James, the chief of the bishops,” the Lord’s brother who presides over them.18 J. B. Lightfoot set the work in his catena for this feature: James is “distinguished from and in some respects exalted above the Twelve,” while “James the son of Alphaeus is distinctly mentioned among the Twelve as one of the disputants.” Like Hegesippus, he set it against the Hieronymian identification of the Lord’s brother with James son of Alphaeus; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the two distinct.19

Origen — c. 248

Origen, Clement’s successor at Alexandria, is the earliest extant writer to give the Epiphanian reading a documentary pedigree. Commenting on Matthew 13:55 he reports and endorses the view that “the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary,” a tradition he traces to two named second-century texts, the Gospel according to Peter and the Book of James (the Protevangelium); those who so read it “wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end,” and Origen adds in his own voice that he thinks it “in harmony with reason that Jesus was the first-fruit among men of the purity which consists in chastity, and Mary among women.”20 Elsewhere he glosses “the Lord’s brother” as owing something to James’s character and not only to kinship, a gloss Lightfoot judged “not inconsistent with the explicit statement.” J. B. Lightfoot judged that Origen “declares himself very distinctly in favour of the Epiphanian view.”21

Victorinus of Pettau — c. 300

Victorinus of Pettau (Poetovio, martyred c. 304), the earliest Latin commentator on Scripture whose work survives, was claimed by both sides of the later Roman controversy. Helvidius quoted him for the reading that the brethren were Mary’s own sons; Jerome denied that sense and claimed him in turn — “as regards Victorinus, I assert… that he spoke of the brethren of the Lord not as being sons of Mary, but brethren in… kinship, not by nature.”22 His own comment does not survive, and his two extant works — the Commentary on the Apocalypse and On the Creation of the World — speak of Mary’s virginity in the conception (the Son “has at this time taken a beginning from the Virgin”) but say nothing of who the brethren were. J. B. Lightfoot inferred that Victorinus “did little more than repeat the statements of the evangelists respecting the Lord’s brethren,” naming them without deciding whose sons they were, and in his conspectus files Victorinus of Pettau under the lone heading Uncertain; his own reading of the point remains ⚠ unverified. (He is not the later Marius Victorinus the Philosopher, c. 360, a separate writer who read Galatians 1:19 as denying that James was an apostle.)23

Eusebius of Caesarea — c. 324

Eusebius of Caesarea, through whose quotations Hegesippus’s Palestinian traditions survive, explains the title in the Epiphanian way. James “was called the brother of the Lord because he was known as a son of Joseph, and Joseph was supposed to be the father of Christ” — a reputed brotherhood built on Joseph’s reputed fatherhood, so that James is Joseph’s son and no blood-kin of Mary.24 He also reckons James the Lord’s brother a supernumerary apostle like Paul — “one of the so-called brethren of the Saviour,” numbered among “many others who were called apostles, in imitation of the Twelve, as was Paul himself” (i.12), and holder of an episcopal chair received “from the Saviour himself and the apostles” (vii.19) — a reckoning that, with Hegesippus’s, J. B. Lightfoot set against the Hieronymian identification of the Lord’s brother with one of the Twelve. Lightfoot set Eusebius in his conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, next after Origen, and judged that his and Epiphanius’ dependence on Hegesippus “materially strengthens” the case that the earliest Palestinian evidence leans Epiphanian.25

Cyril of Jerusalem — c. 350

Cyril of Jerusalem, whose Catechetical Lectures (c. 350) do not pose the brethren question, twice treats James the Lord’s brother as a figure apart from the Twelve. Rehearsing the resurrection appearances of 1 Corinthians 15 as a series of witnesses — Cephas, then the Twelve, then the five hundred, then James, then Paul — he names “James, His own brother, and first Bishop of this diocese” as a step beyond the Twelve, and elsewhere couples “the Apostles” and “James the bishop of this Church” as distinct parties.26 J. B. Lightfoot listed him, with a query — “Cyril of Jerusalem (?)” — beside Hegesippus, the Clementine writings, and Marius Victorinus among the witnesses for “James the Just not one of the Twelve,” a heading he set against the Hieronymian identification; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the Lord’s brother distinct from the Twelve. Lightfoot cautioned that “as this… is only an inference from his language, and not a direct statement of his own, too much stress must not be laid on it,” and the classification rests on that inference.27

Hilary of Poitiers — c. 353

The earliest extant Latin statement of the Epiphanian reading is in Hilary of Poitiers, whose Commentary on Matthew — written about 353, and the earliest Latin exposition of a Gospel to survive almost intact — answers the brethren question in its author’s own voice. He makes them Joseph’s sons by an earlier marriage and argues it from the cross: “if there had been sons of Mary who were not rather produced from a previous marriage of Joseph’s, Mary never would have been transferred to the apostle John as his mother at the time of the Passion” — the care of a son being needless had Mary’s own sons survived to give it.28 Hilary names two options, the Epiphanian solution and the Helvidian denial, and adopts the former. J. B. Lightfoot set him in his conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, next after Eusebius.29

Marius Victorinus the Philosopher — c. 360

Marius Victorinus, the aged rhetorician who converted late in life and the earliest extant Latin commentator on St Paul, reads the “James not one of the Twelve” question from the grammar of Galatians 1:19. Paul writes that at Jerusalem he saw “other of the apostles… none, save James the Lord’s brother,” and Victorinus takes the “save” (Greek ei mē) as opposition, not exception: it places James outside the apostolic number. He writes: “Therefore Paul here denied that James was an apostle… when he called him brother, he denied him to be an apostle” — and sets the reading against the Symmachiani, the Jewish-Christian sect that “make James an apostle, as it were a twelfth.”30 James is the Lord’s brother “according to the flesh” and no apostle, visited by Paul only “out of honour.” J. B. Lightfoot set him in the catena for this — he “takes ei mē… as expressing not exception but opposition, and distinctly states that James was not an Apostle” — classing him with Hegesippus, the Clementine writings, and the Apostolic Constitutions under “James the Just not one of the Twelve,” a heading he set against the Hieronymian identification; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the Lord’s brother distinct from the Twelve.31

Athanasius of Alexandria — c. 360

Athanasius of Alexandria, defending the reality of Christ’s flesh against the Arians in his Four Discourses Against the Arians (c. 356–360), calls the Lord’s mother “Mary Ever-Virgin” (Greek aēiparthenos) in the course of the argument: “let those who deny that the Son is from the Father by nature… deny also that He took true human flesh of Mary Ever-Virgin.”32 The point he is arguing is the reality of the flesh — against the Valentinian docetists on one side (“though Valentinus rave”) and the Arians on the other — and the title appears as part of Mary’s name, not as a thesis argued for. The passage dates from mid-fourth-century Alexandria, a generation before Helvidius raised the question at Rome (c. 383). Athanasius does not take up the identity of the brethren, and is not filed under the Epiphanian or Hieronymian account.33

Eunomius of Cyzicus — c. 360

Eunomius of Cyzicus, leader of the Anomoeans — the “Neo-Arian” party that held the Son unlike (anomoios) the Father in essence — is named as a holder of the Helvidian reading in a later report. A generation after his brief episcopate, the anonymous author of the Incomplete Commentary on Matthew (Opus imperfectum, c. 400), expounding “he knew her not until she had given birth to a son,” reports that “some people, following Eunomius, think that until Mary gave birth Joseph did not have carnal relations with her, but afterwards he knew her and she gave birth to children.”34 The reading reported is the Helvidian one; on this report Eunomius stands beside the Roman Helvidius and the Arabian Antidicomarianites as a fourth-century holder of it. The attribution comes only from this later notice, whose author rejects the reading, and nothing on Mary survives from Eunomius’s own hand, so his position remains ⚠ unverified. The reading reported turns on the “until” of Matthew 1:25.35

Ephraem the Syrian — c. 363

Ephraem the Syrian (Ephrem), the deacon-poet of Nisibis and Edessa, is the Syriac witness to the Epiphanian reading. In his Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles — the Syriac lost, surviving in an Armenian version — he treats the brethren as sons of Joseph and no children of Mary: expounding the apostle-roll of Acts 1:13 he calls Jude “son of Joseph, who also was brother of the Lord,” and Simon likewise “son of Joseph father of the Lord, and brother of the Lord.”36 He differs from the other Epiphanian witnesses on a second point: where Hegesippus, the Clementines, Marius Victorinus, Cyril of Jerusalem, and the Apostolic Constitutions keep the Lord’s brethren apart from the Twelve, Ephraem places two of them within it. He reads the apostle “Judas of James” (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13) as Jude the brother of James — the Lord’s brother and author of the Epistle of Jude, and the same man Matthew and Mark call Lebbaeus and Thaddaeus — and the apostle “Simon the Zealot” (the Cananaean) as Simon the Lord’s brother, while keeping both distinct from the apostle Judas Thomas. He does so on the Epiphanian basis (they are Joseph’s sons, not cousins), and he does not place James among the Twelve.36

The Antidicomarianites — c. 370

The Antidicomarianites, a group in the Arabian churches of the later fourth century, are the one named party on this timeline reported to have “maintained that the Lord’s mother bore children to her husband Joseph.”37 Nothing they wrote survives: the group is known only from Epiphanius of Salamis, who wrote against them — “not very long before Jerome wrote… against the Helvidians at Rome” — in a pastoral letter he later folded into his catalogue of heresies as Panarion 78. There he reports the claim he means to refute, that “certain persons are venturing to say that she had marital relations after the Savior’s birth,” and answers it with the Epiphanian account: the Virgin was “entrusted” to Joseph, an aged widower with children, “not… for marriage… He was called her husband because of the Law.”38 Their teaching survives only in Epiphanius’s summary, so their own voice remains ⚠ unverified; J. B. Lightfoot files them in his conspectus under “sons of Joseph and Mary,” beside Tertullian and Helvidius, while noting that, though Augustine equated the group with Helvidius’ Roman party, “considerations of time and place alike resist this identification.” Lightfoot also notes, from Epiphanius, another Arabian group of the same years, the Collyridians, who “paid divine honours to the Virgin.”39

Ambrosiaster — c. 375

Ambrosiaster — the modern name for the anonymous Roman commentator on Paul whose work long passed under Ambrose’s name, and whom J. B. Lightfoot called “the Ambrosian Hilary” — is a second Latin witness to the Epiphanian reading before Jerome. Expounding “James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), he makes James a son of Joseph and reads the title as he reads “son of Joseph” applied to Jesus — a reputed relationship, not a blood-tie to Mary: “This James was the son of Joseph, who was therefore called the brother of the Lord, because Joseph was also called the father of Him, the Lord.”40 He adds an argument against those who deny the virginity: the two titles stand or fall together, so “if these were His true brothers, then Joseph will be His true father.” He names two options, the Epiphanian solution and the Helvidian denial. J. B. Lightfoot judged that “his testimony entirely coincides with that of his greater namesake” Hilary of Poitiers, and set him in his conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, next after Hilary and before Gregory of Nyssa.41

Basil of Caesarea (Basil the Great) — c. 377

Basil the Great, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, holds Mary’s perpetual virginity while stating that a contrary conclusion would not touch the teaching of the faith. Preaching on the Nativity, he takes up the wording of Matthew 1:25 (Joseph “knew her not till she had brought forth”), grants that it might be read as the Helvidian reads it, and says that such a conclusion “would not have affected the teaching of our religion at all,” since Mary’s virginity was necessary up to the service of the Incarnation. He affirms the ever-virginity, grounding it in the testimony of the faithful: “since the lovers of Christ do not allow themselves to hear that the Mother of God (Theotokos) ceased at a given moment to be a virgin, we consider their testimony to be sufficient.”42 Basil does not say who the brethren were, so J. B. Lightfoot files him in his conspectus among the witnesses for the “perpetual virginity of Mary” who leave the brethren question open; from Basil’s appeal to an apocryphal story — that Zacharias was slain for testifying to Mary’s virginity, a tale close to the Protevangelium (§§ 23–24) — Lightfoot infers that he took the Epiphanian account found in those infancy gospels. That step is Lightfoot’s inference from a detail he reports, not a statement quoted here from Basil, so Basil’s view of the brethren remains ⚠ unverified.43

Epiphanius of Salamis — c. 377

Epiphanius of Salamis, bishop in Cyprus, after whom J. B. Lightfoot named the Epiphanian view, gives its fullest fourth-century statement in his reply to the Antidicomarianites in Panarion 78 (c. 377). The reading is attested before him in the infancy gospels, Clement, Origen, and Eusebius; he sets it out in the greatest detail: Joseph was a widower of “over eighty” when the Virgin was entrusted to him, already father of six children by a former wife — “Joseph begot James when he was somewhere around forty years old. After him he had a son named Joses—then Simeon after him, then Judah, and two daughters, one named Mary and one, Salome; and his wife died.”44 The brethren are so called, he argues, because they were the elder sons of that household and Joseph its reputed father, not because Mary bore them; and the Virgin remained ever-virgin — a point he supports with the argument that the mother of the Lion of Judah, like a lioness, could bear but a single cub. J. B. Lightfoot set him in the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” column of his conspectus, and judged that the shared dependence of Eusebius and Epiphanius on Hegesippus “materially strengthens” the Epiphanian case.45

The Apostolic Constitutions — c. 380

The Apostolic Constitutions, a Syrian church order issued in the apostles’ own name and compiled around 380 out of older, partly third-century material, count James the Lord’s brother outside the Twelve. Its closing apostolic roster runs through the Twelve, “James the son of Alphaeus” among them, and then adds two more names past Matthias: “James the brother of the Lord and bishop of Jerusalem, and Paul the teacher of the Gentiles, the chosen vessel,” so that the Lord’s brother stands as a fourteenth apostle, a supernumerary like Paul.46 The same threefold reckoning — the Twelve, then Paul, then James — runs through the body of the work (ii.55, vii.46, viii.4), and its Ante-Nicene Fathers editor noted that “the clear distinction it makes between James the son of Alphaeus and James the brother of our Lord” is “an incidental proof of the early origin of this compilation,” since “the theory of Jerome, which identifies them, was later.”46 J. B. Lightfoot set the work in his catena for this feature — it distinguishes the two Jameses, “making him, like St. Paul, a supernumerary apostle, and thus counting fourteen in all” — and set it against the Hieronymian identification; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the two Jameses distinct.47

Gregory of Nyssa — c. 382

Gregory of Nyssa, Basil’s younger brother, in a festal homily On the Resurrection of Christ, “certainly adopted the Epiphanian account,” in J. B. Lightfoot’s words — the brethren are Joseph’s sons, not Mary’s — and keeps the Lord’s brother apart from the Twelve, reading Mark’s “James the little” (Mark 15:40) as distinct from “James the son of Alphaeus” the “great,” who was an apostle, “which the Lord’s brother was not.”48 He adds an identification of his own: the “Mary the mother of James and Joses” of the Passion narratives (Matthew 27:56; Mark 15:40) is, he says, “the mother of God and none else” — the Virgin styled so not because she bore them but because she “undertook the education of these her stepsons,” Joseph’s sons by his former wife. Lightfoot, who filed Gregory in his catena of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities next after Ambrosiaster, judged that Marian identification “very untenable,” noting that it is found “similarly” in Chrysostom and in the infancy apocryphon History of Joseph the Carpenter. The resurrection homily survives in Migne’s Patrologia Graeca (In Christi resurrectionem oratio ii, PG 46, col. 647–648), whose Greek matches Lightfoot’s report; the oration is of disputed authenticity, excluded from the modern critical edition as a dubium, so the question is whether the sermon is Gregory’s, not what it says.49 A second strand of his Marian teaching survives in his Homily on the Nativity of Christ, where he reads Mary’s “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34) as evidence that she had already consecrated her virginity to God, and ties the verse to the infancy tradition of the Protevangelium.50

Helvidius — c. 383

Helvidius, a layman of Rome after whom J. B. Lightfoot named the Helvidian reading, published about 383 a tract maintaining that the “brethren of the Lord” were the sons of Joseph and Mary, born after Jesus, so that Mary was not ever-virgin. On Jerome’s account his aim was to set the example of the Lord’s mother, who (he held) had gone on to bear children to her husband, against the preference for virginity over married life. His treatise is lost, surviving only where Jerome quotes it to answer it in The Perpetual Virginity of Blessed Mary, Against Helvidius — so his own words come to us at second hand and remain ⚠ unverified.51 Jerome preserves his arguments: from Matthew’s “knew her not till she had brought forth” and her “first-born son” Helvidius argued that both phrases imply later children, and he declared that “the same persons are there brethren of the Lord and sons of Mary”; for older warrant he appealed to Tertullian and Victorinus of Pettau. Lightfoot notes that “contemporary writers speak of the Helvidians as a party,” and that like views were advocated by Bonosus of Sardica and, apparently, the monk Jovinian; he files Helvidius in his conspectus under “sons of Joseph and Mary,” at the head of the Helvidian column beside Tertullian and the Antidicomarianites. On Lightfoot’s reckoning, Jerome’s answer to Helvidius is where the Hieronymian cousin reading first appears.52

Jerome — c. 383

Jerome’s The Perpetual Virginity of Blessed Mary, Against Helvidius (c. 383), written in reply to Helvidius, is the earliest extant statement of the Hieronymian reading: the brethren are the Lord’s maternal cousins, sons of “Mary of Clopas” the Virgin’s sister, so that “brother” means kinsman and the virginity of Mary and Joseph alike is preserved. He grounded it on the range of the word in Scripture — “In Holy Scripture there are four kinds of brethren—by nature, race, kindred, love” (of which the third, kinship, fits the brethren, as when Abraham calls his nephew Lot “brother”) — and on identifying “the Mary who is described as the mother of James the less” with “the wife of Alphaeus and sister of Mary the Lord’s mother… called by John the Evangelist ‘Mary of Clopas,’” making the brethren her sons and the Lord’s cousins.53 He extended the virginity to Joseph — “I claim still more, that Joseph himself on account of Mary was a virgin, so that from a virgin wedlock a virgin son was born” — and restated the reading a decade later in Lives of Illustrious Men (392): James is “the son of Joseph by another wife, as some think, but, as appears to me, the son of Mary sister of the mother of our Lord.” For the virginity he appealed to “the whole series of ancient writers—Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and many other apostolic and eloquent men,” and he answered Helvidius’ appeal to Tertullian by saying that Tertullian “did not belong to the Church.” J. B. Lightfoot, who gave the reading Jerome’s name, set him in the column of his conspectus headed “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister.” In the Letter to Hedibia (c. 406) Jerome lists Mary of Clopas and the mother of James and Joses as distinct women.54 Whether the cousin reading had witnesses before Jerome, and whether he later let it go, is disputed; see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome?”

Didymus the Blind — c. 386

Didymus the Blind, the blind exegete who headed the Alexandrian catechetical school for half a century, takes up Matthew 1:25 in the treatise On the Trinity (c. 386) traditionally ascribed to him, to illustrate the terms “first-born” and “only-begotten.” He reads “until she brought forth her first-born son” as compatible with a virginity after the birth: Mary “neither… marry anyone else, nor did she ever become the Mother of anyone else, but even after childbirth she remained always and forever an immaculate virgin.”55 He reads the Matthean “till” and “firstborn” as Chrysostom and Chromatius also do, against the Helvidian denial. Like Athanasius before him he speaks of the virginity itself, not of the identity of the brethren, and is not filed under any of the three readings. (The attribution of On the Trinity to Didymus is disputed on stylistic grounds; the passage is a fourth-century Greek reading of Matthew 1:25 on any of the proposed attributions.)56

Bonosus of Sardica and Jovinian — c. 390

Two further Western voices rose against the perpetual virginity in the same decade as Helvidius, and J. B. Lightfoot files both in the Helvidian column of his conspectus — marking the second with a query, for they did not hold one position. Bonosus, bishop of Sardica in Illyria, is reported to have held the Helvidian reading, that Mary bore children to Joseph after Jesus; his case came before the synod of Capua (391 or 392), which referred it to the bishops of Macedonia; Lightfoot describes him as condemned by that synod. He left no text, so his teaching survives only in Lightfoot’s notice and the conciliar record and remains ⚠ unverified.57 Jovinian — a monk who taught at Rome, condemned by synods at Rome and Milan about 390 — is the better attested: his campaign was against ranking virginity above marriage (“a virgin is no better as such than a wife”), and the position on Mary that the Council of Milan reported was not the Helvidian claim of later children but a denial of her virginity in the birth itself (in partu): “She was a virgin when she conceived, but not a virgin when she brought forth.” That formula, which the Council of Milan noted “differs from that of Helvidius,” is preserved firsthand in the synod’s reply to Pope Siricius drawn up by Ambrose, who answered it with the shut “gate” of Ezekiel 44; Jerome answered Jovinian’s wider case at length in the two books Against Jovinianus.58

Ambrose of Milan — c. 392

Ambrose of Milan devoted a treatise — On the Instruction of the Virgin and the Perpetual Virginity of Mary (De Institutione Virginis, c. 392) — to answering those who denied the perpetual virginity, the party that had gathered around Bonosus of Sardica. Reaching the “brethren of the Lord,” Ambrose takes the Epiphanian line — “brother” is a wide term (Psalm 22:22; Romans 9:3), so “the brothers could be from Joseph, not from Mary” — and adds that one who looks into it “will find” this to be so; but he declines to press the point: “We did not consider it necessary to pursue this, since it is clear that the name of brotherhood is common to many.”59 He asserts the virginity without reserve — Mary is the “closed gate” of Ezekiel 44 that “shall not be opened by Joseph” — while leaving the exact standing of the brethren open. J. B. Lightfoot read that reticence as deliberate: Ambrose “had heard of, though possibly not read, Jerome’s tract,” and, prizing “coöperation against a common enemy,” declined to divide the defenders of the virginity “by the discussion of details”; Lightfoot set him last in the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” column of his conspectus, after Epiphanius.60

Augustine of Hippo — c. 394 → later

Augustine first left the question open and later adopted Jerome’s kinsman reading. In his early Commentary on Galatians (on Galatians 1:19, c. 394, while still a presbyter) he wrote that James “is to be understood either as [one] of the sons of Joseph by another wife” (the Epiphanian account) “or as of the kindred of Mary his mother” (the kinsman reading Jerome had argued), committing to neither; in later works he holds Jerome’s reading.61 Expounding John 2:12 he lays it down as a rule of scriptural usage: “The kinsmen of Mary, of whatever degree, are the brethren of the Lord… when thou hast known this rule, thou wilt find that all the blood relations of Mary are the brethren of Christ” — affirming the virginity in the same passage (“surely Mary did not give birth a second time? Far from it! With her begins the dignity of virgins”).62 Against the Manichaean Faustus he writes that those “called in the Gospel the Lord’s brothers are certainly not children of the Virgin Mary, but all the blood relations of the Lord.”63 He does not add the identification of Clopas with Alphaeus — that step, Lightfoot notes, “does not anywhere appear” in Augustine. Lightfoot judged that, “thus supported, it won its way to general acceptance in the Latin Church,” and files him in his conspectus under the Hieronymian column, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister,” between Jerome and Theodoret.61

John Chrysostom — c. 398

John Chrysostom, as J. B. Lightfoot observed, “wrote both before and after Jerome’s treatise had become generally known, and his expositions of the New Testament mark a period of transition.” In the Antiochene homilies he takes the Epiphanian reading: expounding Matthew 1:25 he defends the perpetual virginity (Joseph “knew her not till…” fixes only what was true before the birth, and “not even after this… could that righteous man ever have endured to know her”) and, asked in what sense the “brethren” are brethren, answers that they are so called as Joseph was reputed husband — “How then… are James and the others called His brethren? In the same kind of way as Joseph himself was supposed to be husband of Mary” — no blood-kin of Mary; the same reading recurs in his homilies on John 7:5 and 1 Corinthians 9:5, which keep the Lord’s brother distinct from the Twelve and do not mention the Hieronymian account.64 In the Commentary on Galatians (on 1:19) he writes that Paul might have named James “the son of Cleophas, as the Evangelist does” — which Lightfoot read as grafting Jerome’s cousin identification onto the older scheme and, by taking Clopas for the apostolic Alphaeus, as appearing to complete it. Lightfoot accordingly enters “[Chrysostom]” in both the Epiphanian and the Hieronymian columns of his conspectus, though he judged the Galatians passage “somewhat confused” and perhaps interpolated. In both groups of texts Mary bore no child but Jesus.65

Pelagius — c. 406

Pelagius, expounding Galatians 1:19 at Rome a few years after Jerome, gives a kinship reading of the brethren — though which text of Pelagius is meant is a textual question. His note, as edited by Alexander Souter apart from the later additions, is brief and aimed at those who denied the virginity: “Against those who say that blessed Mary had other sons — for we read that there were two apostles named James, one the son of Alphaeus and the other of Zebedee, neither a son of Mary or of Joseph; rather, they are called the Lord’s brothers from kinship (de propinquitate).”66 This gives the Hieronymian substance — Mary bears no later children, the brethren are kinsmen — without Jerome’s supporting apparatus: Pelagius neither names “Mary of Clopas” nor rehearses the fourfold sense of “brother.” The fuller gloss that repeats Jerome’s “four kinds of brethren” and identifies James as a son of “Mary of Clopas, the sister of the Lord’s mother” survives only in the interpolated Pseudo-Jerome recension and the St Gall manuscript — a possibility J. B. Lightfoot raised when he wrote that Pelagius “adopts his theory and even his language… unless his text has been tampered with here.” Souter’s edition separates Pelagius’s text from the Pseudo-Jerome additions; and Lightfoot files Pelagius in his conspectus under column C, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister,” the name next after Jerome’s in it.67

Chromatius of Aquileia — c. 407

Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia and a friend of Jerome, Rufinus, Ambrose, and Chrysostom, takes up the perpetual-virginity question at the “until” of Matthew 1:25 rather than at the brethren. Preaching on the verse in his Tractates on Matthew, he answers the inference that “he knew her not till she had borne her firstborn son” implies Joseph knew her afterward: “not a few careless people insist on asking whether after the Lord’s birth the holy mother Mary had relations with Joseph. But this is not admissible on the grounds of either faith or truth… Far be it indeed that after the sacrament of so great a mystery and after the birth of the sublime Lord, one should believe that the Virgin Mary was intimate with a man.” He supports it with Old-Testament types — Miriam who stayed a virgin unsullied after beholding the heavenly signs, Noah and Moses who abstained after they were made worthy to speak with God, so much the more the Virgin who was “worthy of carrying him in her virginal womb.”68 Like his Eastern contemporary Chrysostom, he reads the Matthean “till” as compatible with a virginity after the birth, against the Helvidian denial. His surviving tractates do not take up who the “brethren” were, so, unlike Pelagius, he is not filed Epiphanian or Hieronymian.69

Leporius — c. 426

Leporius, a Gallic monk and priest who had taught that Christ endured His human life and Passion by the power of His manhood alone, was expelled from Gaul and, in Africa, under Aurelius of Carthage and Augustine, drew up a Document of Amendment (Libellus emendationis, c. 426). Rehearsing the two births of the one Son, his confession names the Lord’s mother with the perpetual-virginity title as a clause of the creed: Christ is “born of the Father before the ages, and in times most recent, made man of the Holy Spirit and the ever-virgin Mary” (semper virgo).70 The phrase appears in a formal confession submitted as evidence of his amended faith. Like Athanasius, Didymus, and Chromatius, Leporius speaks of the virginity itself and does not take up the brethren question.

Cyril of Alexandria — c. 428

Cyril of Alexandria, patriarch of Alexandria and roughly the contemporary of Theodoret, reads the “brethren of the Lord” in the Epiphanian way. Expounding John 7:3–5, he explains the brethren’s unbelief by the low estimate they still had of Jesus, whom they reckoned a kinsman “in truth begotten of their father Joseph”; and pressing Jeremiah’s type “the house of thy father,” he fixes whose sons they were — “Very watchfully did the Prophet, having named His brethren, profitably add, The house of Thy father, lest they too should be supposed to have been of the blessed Virgin, rather than of His father Joseph alone.”71 The brethren belong to “His father Joseph alone” and are no children of the Virgin — a brotherhood reputed, resting on Joseph’s reputed fatherhood, as Eusebius and Epiphanius had framed it. J. B. Lightfoot cites Cyril (from the seventh book of the Glaphyra on Genesis, a passage reported here from Lightfoot and so ⚠ unverified; the Commentary on John passage above is firsthand) as evidence that, the late Chrysostom and Theodoret aside, “the Epiphanian view maintained its ground in the East… and seems to have been held by later Greek writers almost, if not quite, universally,” and files him in the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” column of his conspectus.72

Theodoret of Cyrus — c. 440

Theodoret of Cyrus, expounding Galatians 1:19 in his commentary on the fourteen Pauline epistles (c. 440), states the Hieronymian reading and names the rival Epiphanian solution in order to reject it. James “the brother of the Lord” was not so by nature, and was “not, as some suppose, the son of Joseph by a previous marriage but the son of Clopas and cousin of the Lord. For his mother was the sister of the Lord’s mother” — the apparent brotherhood arising from the two Marys’ shared name and the families’ shared house.73 He then uses the argument from the cross for the Hieronymian reading: the Gospels show “the Blessed Virgin had no other son,” for the dying Lord “gave her to the most divine John, but he would not have committed her to another if the blessed James… had been her son” — the argument Hilary had used for Joseph’s first family, here placing the brethren in the family of Mary’s sister. A generation after Chrysostom’s Galatians commentary, Theodoret holds the Hieronymian view outright; J. B. Lightfoot files him last of all in his conspectus of column C, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister.”74

Peter Chrysologus — c. 440

Peter Chrysologus (“golden-worded”), bishop of Ravenna, confesses Mary’s virginity before, in and after the birth. In a sermon on the two Adams (Sermon 117, “The First Adam, and the Last Adam, Born of a Virgin”), setting the Virgin’s childbearing apart from that of ordinary women as a sign of God’s power rather than a work of nature, he states Mary’s virginity through all three moments — conception, birth, and after: “Where are they who think that the Virgin’s conception and giving birth to her child are to be likened to those of other women?… The Virgin conceives, the Virgin brings forth her child, and she remains a virgin.”75 The threefold formula — virgin in conceiving, in bearing, and remaining so (virginitas ante partum, in partu, post partum) — appears also in Augustine (virgo concepit, virgo peperit, virgo permansit, Sermon 186) and in his contemporary Leo the Great. Like Leporius, Athanasius, and Chromatius, Chrysologus speaks of the perpetual virginity itself — here with weight on the in partu and post partum virginity — and does not take up the identity of the brethren.

Leo the Great — c. 450

Leo the Great, bishop of Rome (440–461), whose dogmatic Tome was acclaimed at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, states the same confession. Preaching on the Feast of the Nativity, he grounds Mary’s virginity in the “new character” of Christ’s birth — “not by intercourse with man but by the power of God was it brought about” — and states it through all three moments: “a Virgin conceived, a Virgin bare, and a Virgin she remained,” the reader to consider “not the condition of her that bare but the will of Him that was born.”76 On Leo’s account the virginity belongs to the pattern of the Incarnation — “the uncorrupt nature of Him that was born had to guard the primal virginity of the Mother” — the same reading his Tome gives when it makes the Virgin Mother “bring Him forth without the loss of her virginity, even as she conceived Him without its loss.” The formula is the threefold one (virginitas ante partum, in partu, post partum) found in Augustine and in Peter Chrysologus, Leo’s contemporary at Ravenna. Like Leporius, Athanasius, and Chromatius, Leo speaks of the perpetual virginity itself — with weight on the in partu and post partum virginity — and does not take up the identity of the brethren.

Second Council of Constantinople — 553

The Second Council of Constantinople (553), counted as the fifth ecumenical council and convened by Justinian on the Christological questions that followed Chalcedon, uses the title in its anathemas. Anathematizing those who deny the Word’s two nativities, its second capitulum names the mother of the second nativity as “the holy and glorious Mary, Mother of God and always a virgin” (Greek aeiparthenos): “coming down from heaven and being made flesh of the holy and glorious Mary, Mother of God and always a virgin, and born of her: let him be anathema.”77 The title is not the council’s subject — its business is the two natures and the “Three Chapters” — and appears in passing within the anathema. The council does not take up the identity of the brethren. Its use of aeiparthenos figures in the dispute over when the doctrine was first given conciliar form; see “When was the perpetual virginity first defined?”

The Lateran Council of 649 — 649

The Lateran Council of 649, a Roman synod of 105 bishops convened under Pope Martin I to condemn Monothelitism, devotes its third canon to Mary and confesses her ever-virgin through all three moments — before, in, and after the birth. The “holy Mother of God and ever Virgin and immaculate Mary… conceived of the Holy Spirit without seed… and… incorruptibly bore [Him], her virginity remaining indestructible even after His birth” — and denial is put under anathema: “let him be condemned.”78 Where Constantinople II had named Mary aeiparthenos in a single clause, the Lateran canon spells out the threefold virginity (virginitas ante partum, in partu, post partum) found in Leo the Great and Augustine. It was a local synod rather than an ecumenical council, presided over and sanctioned by the pope; its faith was afterward received in the East at the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681) ⚠ unverified. It does not take up the identity of the brethren. It too figures in the dispute over when the doctrine was first defined; see “When was the perpetual virginity first defined?”

The Venerable Bede — c. 735

Bede, the Northumbrian monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, reads Mary’s reply to Gabriel in his Commentary on Luke — “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34) — as her disclosure of a settled purpose of virginity. She “reverently expressed the purpose of her mind, that is, that she had decided to lead a virginal life” (Latin: propositum suae mentis reverenter exposuit, vitam videlicet virginalem se ducere decrevisse) — “the first among women to devote herself to such great virtue.” Bede sets her question apart from Zachariah’s: she did not ask “How will I know this?” — a demand for a sign — but “How will this be?”, inquiring after “the order of obedience to which she should submit, not asking for a sign to believe”; having read Isaiah’s “a virgin shall conceive” but not how, she asks the angel “what she did not find in the prophet.”79 Bede’s gloss is a firsthand Latin statement of the reading of Luke 1:34 as a resolved purpose (propositum) of virginity.

John of Damascus — c. 749

John of Damascus, whose Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (c. 743) is a systematic summary of Greek patristic teaching, devotes a chapter of it to the “ever-virgin” (aeiparthenos) Mother of God. He states that she “thus remains even after the birth still virgin, having never at any time up till death consorted with a man,” and answers the two objections from Matthew 1:25 as earlier writers had. To “her first-born son” he replies that “he who is first-begotten is first-born even if he is only-begotten… the word ‘first-born’ means that he was born first but does not at all suggest the birth of others”; and to “knew her not till she had brought forth,” that “the word ‘till’ signifies the limit of the appointed time but does not exclude the time thereafter” — illustrating it, as Jerome had, with the risen Christ’s “lo, I am with you always, even unto the end of the world” (Matthew 28:20), which does not mean he departs after it.80 The chapter does not take up the identity of the brethren, and he is not filed under any of the three readings.

Theophylact of Ohrid — c. 1075

Theophylact of Ohrid, a Byzantine archbishop and exegete, combines the two virginity-preserving readings. Commenting on both Matthew 13:55 and Galatians 1:19 he harmonizes the Epiphanian and Hieronymian accounts by the Mosaic levirate: “Cleopas and Joseph were brothers; when Cleopas died childless, Joseph raised up seed for him and begot him and his other brothers” — so James is the Lord’s reputed brother as Joseph’s natural son and his cousin as Clopas’s legal son, Joseph the natural and Clopas the legal father.81 The Matthew comment spells the mechanism out: Clopas “died childless,” Joseph “took his wife in accordance with the law and had six children by her,” the daughter Mary “called the daughter of Cleopas, in accordance with the law.”82 On this account the brethren are not Mary’s children on either line. J. B. Lightfoot notes that this “novel suggestion… found but little favour, and the Eastern Churches continued to distinguish between James the Lord’s brother and James the son of Alphaeus,” the Greek, Syrian, and Coptic calendars keeping a separate day for each.83

Bernard of Clairvaux — c. 1153

Bernard of Clairvaux, the Cistercian abbot called the “mellifluous doctor,” reads Mary’s “How shall this be done, because I know not man?” (Luke 1:34) as presupposing a vow in the fourth of his early homilies In Praise of the Virgin Mother (the “Missus est” sermons). He raises the objection the vow invites — how could a woman betrothed to Joseph have vowed never to know a man? — and answers it from inside Mary’s imagined reasoning. She “doubts not the fact, but only inquires about the manner”; then, imagined as one already under vow, she reckons with the cost: “Since my Lord knows… that His handmaid has made a vow to know no man, by what law shall it please Him to work this wonder? If I must break my vow that I may bring forth such a Son, I rejoice on account of the Son, but I grieve because of my vow. Nevertheless, His will be done.”84 The tension is resolved by the virginal conception itself — she bears the Son “as a Virgin” and her resolve is kept. Bernard speaks of a vow already made, where Bede speaks of a resolved purpose (propositum).

Thomas Aquinas — c. 1270

Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican theologian of the Latin schools, gives Mary’s virginity a whole question of his Summa Theologiae (III, q. 28), and in its third article rejects the Helvidian denial — “without any hesitation we must abhor the error of Helvidius, who dared to assert that Christ’s Mother, after His Birth, was carnally known by Joseph, and bore other children.” He gives four reasons: the denial derogates from Christ’s perfection as the Only-begotten; it insults the Holy Ghost, “whose ‘shrine’ was the virginal womb”; it dishonours the dignity of God’s Mother; and it imputes “extreme presumption” to Joseph — concluding that Mary, “as she was a virgin in conceiving Him and a virgin in giving Him birth, so she remained a virgin ever afterwards.”85 On the identity of the brethren he follows Jerome and the Hieronymian reading: he makes them “not sons of Joseph, but cousins of the Saviour,” holds that Joseph too “is rather to be believed to have remained a virgin,” and in his Commentary on Matthew rejects the alternative — “Helvidius understood these to be sons of Mary. But this is false; rather they were his cousins” — holding the virginity of Mary and of Joseph alike.86

Huldrych Zwingli — c. 1522

Huldrych (Ulrich) Zwingli, the reformer of Zürich, professed the perpetual virginity in a sermon on the Virgin Mary preached in Zürich on 17 September 1522 (Eine Predigt von der ewig reinen Magd Maria, printed 1524). He disclaims ever having “thought, still less taught, or declared publicly, anything concerning the subject of the ever Virgin Mary, Mother of our salvation, which could be considered dishonorable, impious, unworthy or evil,” and confesses “with all my heart according to the word of holy gospel that this pure virgin bore for us the Son of God and that she remained, in the birth and after it, a pure and unsullied virgin, for eternity.”87 The sermon speaks of the virginity itself and does not take up the identity of the “brethren” (as Luther and Calvin elsewhere do), so Zwingli is not filed under any of the three readings.

Martin Luther — c. 1523

Martin Luther defended Mary’s perpetual virginity in That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523). He meets the Helvidian denial at the Matthean texts and rejects it — the reading that “before they came together” and “knew her not till she had brought forth her first-born son” (Matthew 1:18, 25) imply Joseph knew Mary afterward is, he says, a “carnal” reading, so that “one cannot from these words conclude that Mary, after the birth of Christ, became a wife in the usual sense; it is therefore neither to be asserted nor believed.” He argues from idiom: the Scriptural “until” is “the common idiom” that fixes what held before a terminus without implying any change after — “Pharaoh believed not Moses, until he was drowned in the Red Sea” (yet never believed at all), and likewise “Sit at my right hand, till I make your enemies your footstool” (Psalm 110:1), Genesis 28:15, and Isaiah 42:4 — “so that this babble of Helvidius is without justification.”88 He reads the Matthean “till” as Chrysostom, Chromatius, John of Damascus, and Aquinas had. The 1523 tract does not take up the identity of the brethren; his later Sermons on the Gospel of St. John (1537–39) do, taking them as cousins — “‘brothers’ really mean ‘cousins’ here, for Holy Writ and the Jews always call cousins brothers” — the Hieronymian reading.88

John Calvin — c. 1562

John Calvin expounds Matthew 1:25 both in his Harmony of the Evangelists and in a Geneva sermon on Matthew 1:22–25 (pub. 1562). In the commentary he holds that the text does not decide what followed the birth — “no just and well-grounded inference can be drawn from these words of the Evangelist, as to what took place after the birth of Christ,” for “first-born” is used “for the sole purpose of informing us that he was born of a virgin,” and Joseph’s knowing her not “till” the birth “is limited to that very time… What took place afterwards, the historian does not inform us.” He calls pressing the question from the verse “curiosity” and “an extreme fondness for disputation”; from the pulpit, in an English rendering whose translator is not named (⚠ unverified), he dismisses the suggestion “that the Virgin Mary had other children… but what folly this is!”89 On Matthew 13:55 he takes “brothers” “to denote any relatives whatever” and writes that “Helvidius displayed excessive ignorance in concluding that Mary must have had many sons, because Christ’s brothers are sometimes mentioned,” without saying whose sons the brethren were; his commentary on John 7:3 glosses the Lord’s “brethren” as reaching to “all cousins and other relations,” a gloss that includes cousins, as the Hieronymian reading does.89 In the sermon he rejects the suggestion that Mary had other children; in the commentary he declines to rest the question on the verse.90

John Wesley — c. 1749

John Wesley, the Anglican priest and founder of Methodism, professed the perpetual virginity in A Letter to a Roman Catholic (Dublin, 18 July 1749). The letter is an appeal for peace between Protestants and Catholics in Ireland that sets out the creed of “a true Protestant” to show how much the two hold in common; within its confession of the Incarnation Wesley affirms that Christ was “born of the blessed Virgin Mary, who, as well after as before she brought Him forth, continued a pure and unspotted virgin.”91 Like Zwingli, he speaks of the virginity itself and does not take up the identity of the “brethren” (as Luther and Calvin elsewhere do), so he is not filed under any of the three readings.

J. B. Lightfoot — c. 1865

J. B. Lightfoot, whose 1865 dissertation “The Brethren of the Lord” gave the three views their names and assembled the patristic catena cited throughout this page, also gave his own conclusion. Weighing the solutions on the scriptural notices, he set aside the Hieronymian reading — “there is not the slightest indication that the Hieronymian solution ever occurred to any individual or sect or church, until it was put forward by Jerome himself” (whether the reading had earlier witnesses is disputed; see the reading on its antiquity) — and favoured the Epiphanian: “taking the scriptural notices alone, the Hieronymian account must be abandoned; while of the remaining two the balance of the argument is against the Helvidian and in favour of the Epiphanian,” a reading that “has the highest claims to the sanction of tradition… seems especially to represent the Palestinian view.” On his conclusion the brethren are step-brothers, “sons of Joseph by a former wife, before he espoused the Virgin.” On the two Helvidian proof-texts he writes that Matthew’s “till she brought forth” “neither asserts nor implies any subsequent intercourse,” and that Luke’s “firstborn” marks “not the birth of other children, but the special consecration of this one.”92

Disputed readings

How particular witnesses and texts bearing on the brethren, and on the origin of the belief, have been read.

The cousin theory

Three readings bear on the Hieronymian identification of the brethren as the Lord’s cousins.

  • Does the report that James the Lord’s brother and the apostle James son of Alphaeus were both stoned at Jerusalem bear on whether they were one man? — on a notice in the Pseudo-Hippolytan On the Twelve Apostles and the martyrdom of James the Just.93
  • Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go? — on the pre-Jerome writers, the “Papias” fragment, and Jerome’s Letter to Hedibia.94
  • Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]” (Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4)? — on the Greek deuteron and Hegesippus’s terms for the Lord’s kin.95

The Lord’s family in early tradition

Four readings concern what the early traditions about Jesus’ relatives preserve.

  • What does Hegesippus’s report of Jude’s grandsons show about the relationship called “brother of the Lord”? — on the family of Jude “the Lord’s brother according to the flesh.”96
  • Do the names Mary and Salome given to Jesus’ sisters preserve early family tradition? — on Epiphanius, the Gospel of Philip, and the Protevangelium.97
  • Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family? — on the Protevangelium, the Gospel of Peter, and the traditions that travel with them.98
  • Was the apostle Judas Thomas, called Jesus’ twin in Syriac tradition, the Lord’s brother Jude? — on the Acts of Thomas and the Book of Thomas.99

The Epistle of Jude

Four readings concern whether features of the Epistle of Jude, whose author calls himself “brother of James,” bear on its authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother. The same Enoch passages in Jude (Jude 6; 14–15) are discussed on the Nephilim timeline, on whether the early Church received the book of Enoch as Scripture, and on the Old Testament canon, on whether Jude’s quotation shows the canon still open.

  • Does the Epistle of Jude’s pesher-style exegesis bear on its authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother? — on the structure and techniques of Jude 4–19.100
  • Does Jude 9’s allusion to the Testament of Moses bear on the letter’s authorship? — on the dispute of Michael and the devil over Moses’ body.101
  • Does the letter’s title “Master” (despotēs) for Christ, beside the relatives’ name desposynoi, bear on its authorship? — on Jude 4 and Julius Africanus.102
  • Does the Enochic structure of the Lukan genealogy link it to Jude’s circle, and does that bear on the letter’s authorship? — on Luke 3:23–38 and Jude 6 and 14.103

How two early witnesses are read

Two readings concern what particular early witnesses attest.

  • Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus? — on Against Marcion iv.19 and On the Flesh of Christ 7 and 23.104
  • Does the Latin transmission of Clement of Alexandria’s notes affect the weight of his witness? — on Cassiodorus’s abridgement and the Greek Stromateis VII.16.105

The origin of the belief

Three readings concern where the belief came from and when it was defined.

  • Did the belief rest on a typology of the consecrated womb, the new tomb, and the shut gate of Ezekiel 44:2, as well as on esteem for virginity? — on Lightfoot’s account and Origen, Against Celsus ii.69.106
  • Is the perpetual virginity a late development — when was it first held and first defined? — on the second-century witnesses, Constantinople II (553), and the Lateran Council (649).107
  • Did belief in the perpetual virginity arise in Gnostic circles or elsewhere in early Christianity? — on the second-century milieu of the Protevangelium.108
