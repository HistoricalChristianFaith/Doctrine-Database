Who Was Cain?

In Genesis, Cain is the firstborn of the human race and its first murderer — the son of Adam and Eve who, when his offering was not accepted and his brother Abel’s was, rose up in the field and killed him (Genesis 4:8). For that blood the ground is cursed under him and he is sentenced to be “a fugitive and a vagabond… in the earth” (Genesis 4:12); yet when he protests that “every one that findeth me shall slay me,” the LORD sets a mark upon him, “lest any finding him should kill him” (Genesis 4:15). From him Genesis then traces a whole line: he builds the first city and names it for his son Enoch, and out of his descendants come the tent-dwelling herdsmen, the first musicians, and Tubal-cain the first metalworker (Genesis 4:17–22), a line closed by Lamech’s boast of a sevenfold revenge (Genesis 4:23–24). The New Testament names Cain three times: he was “of that wicked one” (1 John 3:12), false teachers “have gone in the way of Cain” (Jude 11), and Abel offered “a more excellent sacrifice than Cain” (Hebrews 11:4).

Later readers built a large reception on that narrative. The mark was read by the rabbis as a sign whose form they debated, by Ambrose as a slave’s brand of the conscience, by Bede as a figure of the Jewish people, and in modern popular reception as a racial brand (⚠ unverified); the line of Cain became the medieval “sons of Cain,” joined in Beowulf to the giants of Genesis 6; and a strand of rabbinic aggadah, and in the twentieth century William Branham and Christian Identity writers, made Cain the offspring not of Adam but of Sammael or “the Angel” (in the aggadah) or of Satan through the serpent (in the modern teachings) — the “serpent seed.” Cain’s name is also tied to the Kenites (Hebrew qeni, sharing the consonants of qayin, “Cain”; read by A. H. Sayce as “smith”), the desert clan of Moses’ father-in-law Jethro, the clan at the centre of the modern Kenite hypothesis, and the people Balaam singles out in his oracle on “the Kenite” (Numbers 24:21–22).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Cain but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Genesis portrait and its New Testament echoes, Josephus’s grasping city-builder, the rabbis who debated the mark and told of Cain’s paternity by Sammael, and the Fathers (Ephrem, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Bede), among whom Augustine made Cain the founder of the earthly city and Bede read the mark as a figure of the wandering, preserved Jew. Three questions recur through that reception — what the mark was and what it signified, whether the Kenites descend from Cain, and who fathered Cain — and each source’s answer is reported in its own entry.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 4; the New Testament echoes) — the fratricide, the mark, and the line of Cain

The tradition about Cain draws on Genesis 4. He is Eve’s firstborn, greeted at birth with “I have gotten a man from the LORD” (Genesis 4:1), a tiller of the ground whose offering is not respected while shepherd Abel’s is; God warns him that “sin lieth at the door” (Genesis 4:7), but Cain says to his brother, “Let us go out to the field,” and there kills him — then answers God’s question, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis 4:9). The blood cries from the ground; the ground is cursed to him; he is driven out “a fugitive and a vagabond” (Genesis 4:12). Cain fears for his life, and the LORD answers — “the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him” (Genesis 4:15), promising sevenfold vengeance on any who would.1

Genesis then traces to Cain and his line the first city and the first crafts. He “knew his wife… and he builded a city, and called the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch” (Genesis 4:17) — the first city in Scripture. His line runs to Lamech, who takes two wives, and to Lamech’s children: Jabal “the father of such as dwell in tents,” Jubal “the father of all such as handle the harp and organ,” and Tubal-cain, “an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron” (Genesis 4:20–22) — and the line closes with Lamech’s song of vengeance, “If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold” (Genesis 4:24). Genesis then names Seth, the “other seed” appointed in Abel’s place, in whose days “began men to call upon the name of the LORD” (Genesis 4:26). In the New Testament Cain was “of that wicked one, and slew his brother… because his own works were evil, and his brother’s righteous” (1 John 3:12); false teachers “have gone in the way of Cain” (Jude 11); and Abel, by contrast, “offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain” (Hebrews 11:4).2

One branch of Cain’s memory runs sideways out of the primeval history and into Israel’s later story. A clan called the Kenites — in Hebrew qeni, sharing the consonants of qayin, “Cain” — are the desert people of Moses’ Midianite father-in-law: they “went up… and dwelt among the people” of Judah (Judges 1:16); Heber the Kenite (Judges 4:11) has a wife, Jael, who is “blessed above women” (Judges 5:24); Saul spares them “for ye shewed kindness to all the children of Israel” (1 Samuel 15:6); and Balaam, looking on “the Kenites,” sets the name beside qen, “nest” — “thou puttest thy nest [qen] in a rock… the Kenite shall be wasted” (Numbers 24:21–22). How the clan’s name relates to Cain’s is taken up by the modern scholars in the last entry below.3

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 1.2.1–2, c. AD 93) — Cain the grasping inventor of the fenced city

Josephus retells Cain as an acquisitive tyrant. He writes that “Cain was not only very wicked in other respects, but was wholly intent upon getting,” the man who ended the age of simplicity: “he was the author of measures and weights… he changed the world into cunning craftiness. He first of all set boundaries about lands: he built a city, and fortified it with walls: and he compelled his family to come together to it: and called that city Enoch.” Cain’s posterity only worsens — “the posterity of Cain became exceeding wicked, every one successively dying one after another more wicked than the former… intolerable in war, and vehement in robberies” — and it climaxes in Tubal, who “exceeded all men in strength, and was very expert and famous in martial performances… and first of all invented the art of making brass.” In this retelling Cain and his line are the inventors of measures, boundaries, the walled city, war, and the working of brass.4

The rabbinic tradition (Targum, Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer, Genesis Rabbah; traditions c. AD 200–800) — what was the mark, and whose son was Cain?

Rabbinic tradition takes up two questions in the chapter. On the mark, the rabbis of Genesis Rabbah could not agree what the “sign” (ʾot) was: R. Judah said “He caused the orb of the sun to shine for him,” R. Nehemiah objected that God would not so honour the wicked and made it “leprosy” that glowed on him; Rav “said: He gave him a dog,” while Abba Yose “said: He caused a horn to sprout forth for him” — and the sign was read both as a warning “for murderers” and as an emblem “for penitents.” Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer gives a further version: “He took one letter from the twenty-two letters, and put it upon Cain’s arm that he should not be killed,” and adds the dog “which was guarding Abel’s flock.”5

On Cain’s paternity the same body of tradition preserves a further strand. The Targum of Pseudo-Jonathan says that Eve “had desired the Angel” and bore Cain saying “I have acquired a man, the Angel of the LORD,” and Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer states: “(Sammael) riding on the serpent came to her, and she conceived” Cain, and only “afterwards Adam came to her, and she conceived Abel.” In this aggadah Sammael, not Adam, is Cain’s father, and Adam is Abel’s.6

Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Genesis; Hymns on Paradise, c. AD 363–370) — the Cainites below, the Sethites above

Ephrem reads the chapter as the first parting of two peoples. After the sentence and the sign, “Cain went away from the presence of the Lord and dwelt in the land of Nod, east of Eden… Cain, therefore, separated himself from his parents and his kin because he saw that they would not intermarry with him.” In the Hymns on Paradise Ephrem joins that geography to the Genesis-6 story: Cain “lived in the land of Nod, a place lower still than that of Seth and Enosh,” while those “who lived on higher ground, who were called ‘the children of God,’ left their own region and came down to take wives from the daughters of Cain down below.” Here the Cainites are the “daughters of men” and the Sethites the “sons of God” — the reading of the Nephilim passage known as the Sethite reading. Whether it originated as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading is taken up on the Nephilim timeline (Did the Sethite reading originate as a Rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading?).7

Ambrose of Milan (Flight from the World; Letter 37, c. AD 390) — the mark that shows evil is not removed

Ambrose reads the mark morally, as the visible token of a persisting evil. “It was not without reason that the mark was set upon Cain, that no one might kill him. Thus it was indicated that evil is not destroyed or removed from the earth… evil is augmented and amassed by the practice of evil.” And he reads the sign as a slave-brand of the conscience: “as a slave he received a sign, but even thus he could not escape death… the sinner is a slave to fear, to cupidity, to avarice, to lust, to malice, to anger.” On Ambrose’s reading the sign keeps Cain alive while he remains a slave to his sins.8

Jerome (Hebrew Questions on Genesis, c. AD 393) — the philologist on “sevenfold” and the first idols

Jerome goes to the Greek versions. On “whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold” he notes the Greek translators’ renderings — “Aquila interpreted it sevenfold. Symmachus, the seventh. Theodotion, by the week” — and points to his own letter to Damasus on the puzzle. On the counter-line to Cain, Enosh, he reports a Jewish interpretation: though the text says men then “began… to call upon the name of the LORD,” “many Hebrews interpret it differently, that at that time idols were first made in the name of the Lord and in his likeness” — the beginning either of true worship or of idolatry.9

Augustine (City of God 15, c. AD 417–420) — Cain, founder of the earthly city

In Book 15 of the City of God Augustine makes Cain and Abel the first citizens of the two cities: “it is recorded of Cain that he built a city, while Abel, as though he were merely a pilgrim on earth, built none. For the true city of the saints is in heaven.” For Augustine the city Cain founds is the earthly city, propagated by generation and rooted in this age; he reads the named wives of Lamech — a detail “unheard of before the flood, except in the case of Eve” — as the sign “that to the very end of its existence the earthly city will be propagated by physical births.” In his reading Cain is the first builder of the order of men who live for this world, and Abel and Seth, who build no city, are the pilgrims of the city of God.10

The Venerable Bede (Commentary on Genesis, c. AD 720) — the mark as the preserved, wandering Jew

Bede’s Commentary on Genesis reads the sign that keeps Cain alive of the Jewish people, preserved and marked among the nations: “the Jewish people, whether under pagan or Christian rulers, have the mark of their law, by which they are distinguished from other nations… every emperor or king who finds them in his kingdom finds them with that mark, nor does he kill them” unless they “turn to Christ.” Cain wandering in “the land of Naid” — which Bede, following the Hebrew, glosses as “fugitive… unstable motion and fluctuation” — images the Jews scattered but preserved, “always unstable and fluctuating and of uncertain abodes.” And Cain’s line, ending in Lamech’s daughter Noema (“Pleasure”), is, for Bede, the progeny that “not long after… was finished by the flood.”11

The medieval “sons of Cain” — the line of Cain fused with the giants

In the Middle Ages Cain’s line was drawn together with the monstrous. The Old English Beowulf descends Grendel and the gigantas “in Caines cynne” — “in Cain’s kin” — tracing ogres, elves, and giants to Cain and setting the Flood as God’s answer to that brood. How Beowulf’s “sons of Cain” genealogy of the giants bears on the reading of Genesis 6 is the subject of a disputed reading on the Nephilim timeline, which also discusses Cain’s own line of Jabal, Jubal and Tubal-cain.12

Modern scholarship — the Kenite smiths and the serpent-seed revival

Two modern lines of reading close the timeline. The first is philological: A. H. Sayce (1899) took the Kenites for a guild of smiths, reading the tribal name qeni and the personal name qayin as a Semitic word for “metalworker” (cf. the biblical Tubal-cain, “instructer… in brass and iron”), and Wilhelm Gesenius derived “Kenite” from “Cain.” The same clan stands at the centre of the modern Kenite hypothesis, that Israel received Yahweh-worship from Jethro’s desert people. Ronald Hendel reads the biblical linkage of the Kenites to Cain as a way to give a neighbouring clan a “shameful, violent ancestral origin.” The second is a twentieth-century teaching on Cain’s paternity: William Branham’s “Serpent’s Seed” teaching and the “two-seedline” doctrine of Christian Identity, which made Cain the literal offspring of Satan and the ancestor of whole races; a comparable claim of non-Adamic paternity appears in the Targum and Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer (above).13
