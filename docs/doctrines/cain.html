Who Was Cain?

Cain is the firstborn of the human race and its first murderer — the son of Adam and Eve who, when his offering was not accepted and his brother Abel’s was, rose up in the field and killed him (Genesis 4:8). For that blood the ground is cursed under him and he is sentenced to be “a fugitive and a vagabond… in the earth” (Genesis 4:12); yet when he protests that “every one that findeth me shall slay me,” the LORD does the unexpected thing and protects him — setting a mark upon him so that no one who found him would kill him (Genesis 4:15). From him Genesis then traces a whole line: he builds the first city and names it for his son Enoch, and out of his descendants come the tent-dwelling herdsmen, the first musicians, and Tubal-cain the first metalworker (Genesis 4:17–22) — the earthly city’s first culture-heroes, closed by Lamech’s boast of a sevenfold revenge (Genesis 4:23–24). The rest of Scripture makes Cain a byword: he is “of that wicked one” (1 John 3:12), his “way” a road of apostasy (Jude 11), the foil to faithful Abel (Hebrews 11:4).

Around that spare narrative grew an outsized reception. The mark — a sign of God’s mercy in the text — became in later hands a badge of the reprobate and, catastrophically, a supposed racial brand; the line of Cain became the medieval “sons of Cain,” sometimes fused with the giants of Genesis 6; and a strand of rabbinic aggadah, revived by modern sectarians, made Cain the offspring not of Adam but of the serpent — the “serpent seed.” Cain also anchors a genealogical claim that reaches across this wiki: his supposed descendants the Kenites (Hebrew qeni, from qayin, “Cain”/“smith”) are the desert clan of Moses’ father-in-law Jethro, the very tribe on which the modern Kenite hypothesis is built, and the people Balaam singles out in his oracle on “the Kenite” (Numbers 24:21–22).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Cain but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Genesis portrait and its New Testament echoes, Josephus’s grasping city-builder, the rabbis who debated the mark and told the serpent-seed, and the Fathers (Ephrem, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Bede) who made Cain the founder of the earthly city and the mark a figure of the wandering, preserved Jew. The genuinely contested questions — was the mark a protective sign or a curse (and never a race-mark)? are the Kenites really Cain’s descendants? was Cain fathered by the serpent? — are weighed on their own argument pages (see the Arguments section) rather than settled here.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 4; the New Testament echoes) — the first murderer, the mark of mercy, and the earthly line

Everything the tradition made of Cain begins in Genesis 4. He is Eve’s firstborn, greeted at birth with “I have gotten a man from the LORD” (Genesis 4:1), a tiller of the ground whose offering is not respected while shepherd Abel’s is; God warns him that “sin lieth at the door” (Genesis 4:7), but Cain says to his brother, “Let us go out to the field,” and there kills him — then answers God’s question with the world’s first evasion, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis 4:9). The blood cries from the ground; the ground is cursed to him; he is driven out “a fugitive and a vagabond” (Genesis 4:12). And then the hinge on which the whole later reception turns: Cain fears for his life, and the LORD, far from adding execution to exile, guards him — “the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him” (Genesis 4:15), promising sevenfold vengeance on any who would.1

From there Genesis makes Cain the founder of civilization’s darker half. He “knew his wife… and he builded a city, and called the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch” (Genesis 4:17) — the first city in Scripture. His line runs to Lamech, the first bigamist, and to Lamech’s children, the archetypal culture-heroes: Jabal “the father of such as dwell in tents,” Jubal “the father of all such as handle the harp and organ,” and Tubal-cain, “an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron” (Genesis 4:20–22) — the line sealed by Lamech’s song of vengeance, “If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold” (Genesis 4:24). Against this stands Seth, the “other seed” appointed in Abel’s place, in whose days “began men to call upon the name of the LORD” (Genesis 4:26). The New Testament fixes the verdict: Cain was “of that wicked one, and slew his brother… because his own works were evil, and his brother’s righteous” (1 John 3:12); false teachers “have gone in the way of Cain” (Jude 11); and Abel, by contrast, “offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain” (Hebrews 11:4).2

One branch of Cain’s memory runs sideways out of the primeval history and into Israel’s later story. A clan called the Kenites — in Hebrew qeni, from the same root as qayin, “Cain” — are the desert people of Moses’ Midianite father-in-law: they “went up… and dwelt among the people” of Judah (Judges 1:16); Heber the Kenite’s wife Jael is “blessed above women” (Judges 4:11); Saul spares them “for ye shewed kindness to all the children of Israel” (1 Samuel 15:6); and Balaam, looking on “the Kenites,” puns on the name — “thou puttest thy nest [qen] in a rock… the Kenite shall be wasted” (Numbers 24:21–22). Whether this smith-clan is genuinely descended from Cain, or only bears a punningly similar name, is weighed in the Arguments below.3

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 1.2.1–2, c. AD 93) — Cain the grasping inventor of the fenced city

The first sustained retelling turns Cain into the type of the acquisitive tyrant. Josephus writes that “Cain was not only very wicked in other respects, but was wholly intent upon getting,” the man who ended the age of simplicity: “he was the author of measures and weights… he changed the world into cunning craftiness. He first of all set boundaries about lands: he built a city, and fortified it with walls: and he compelled his family to come together to it: and called that city Enoch.” Cain’s posterity only worsens — “the posterity of Cain became exceeding wicked, every one successively dying one after another more wicked than the former… intolerable in war, and vehement in robberies” — and it climaxes in Tubal, who “exceeded all men in strength, and was very expert and famous in martial performances… and first of all invented the art of making brass.” The Genesis culture-heroes have become the inventors of property, war, and metal — the technology of the earthly city.4

The rabbinic tradition (Targum, Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer, Genesis Rabbah; traditions c. AD 200–800) — what was the mark, and whose son was Cain?

Jewish tradition worried at the two hardest cruxes of the chapter. On the mark, the rabbis of Genesis Rabbah could not agree what the “sign” (ʾot) was: R. Judah said “He caused the orb of the sun to shine for him,” R. Nehemiah objected that God would not so honour the wicked and made it “leprosy” that glowed on him; Rav “said: He gave him a dog,” while Abba Yose “said: He caused a horn to sprout forth for him” — and, decisively for the meaning, the sign was read both as a warning “for murderers” and as an emblem “for penitents.” What no rabbi took it to be was a curse or a racial brand; it was a protective sign whose form was debated. Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer gives the version that entered art and legend: “He took one letter from the twenty-two letters, and put it upon Cain’s arm that he should not be killed,” and adds the dog “which was guarding Abel’s flock.”5

On Cain’s paternity the same corpus preserves a darker strand. Where the Targum of Pseudo-Jonathan already hints that Eve “had desired the Angel” and bore Cain saying “I have acquired a man, the Angel of the LORD,” Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer makes it explicit: “(Sammael) riding on the serpent came to her, and she conceived” Cain, and only “afterwards Adam came to her, and she conceived Abel.” This is the ancient germ of the “serpent seed” — a minority aggadah severing Cain from Adam’s line and fathering him on the fallen angel — and it is weighed, and rated, in the Arguments below.6

Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Genesis; Hymns on Paradise, c. AD 363–370) — the Cainites below, the Sethites above

Ephrem reads the chapter as the first parting of two peoples. After the sentence and the sign, “Cain went away from the presence of the Lord and dwelt in the land of Nod, east of Eden… Cain, therefore, separated himself from his parents and his kin because he saw that they would not intermarry with him.” In the Hymns on Paradise that geography becomes a moral topography that feeds straight into the Genesis-6 story: Cain “lived in the land of Nod, a place lower still than that of Seth and Enosh,” while those “who lived on higher ground, who were called ‘the children of God,’ left their own region and came down to take wives from the daughters of Cain down below.” Here the Cainites are the “daughters of men” and the Sethites the “sons of God” — the Sethite reading of the Nephilim, of which Cain’s line is the lower half, and whose origin as a rabbinic reaction against the angelic reading is weighed on the Nephilim timeline.7

Ambrose of Milan (Flight from the World; Letter 37, c. AD 390) — the mark that shows evil is not removed

Ambrose reads the mark morally, as the visible token of a persisting evil. “It was not without reason that the mark was set upon Cain, that no one might kill him. Thus it was indicated that evil is not destroyed or removed from the earth… evil is augmented and amassed by the practice of evil.” And he reads the sign as a slave-brand of the conscience: “as a slave he received a sign, but even thus he could not escape death… the sinner is a slave to fear, to cupidity, to avarice, to lust, to malice, to anger.” The mark protects Cain’s body only to prolong the punishment of his soul — a preserving sign, not an executing one, but no honour.8

Jerome (Hebrew Questions on Genesis, c. AD 393) — the philologist on “sevenfold” and the first idols

Jerome, characteristically, goes to the versions. On “whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold” he notes the Greek translators’ renderings — “Aquila interpreted it sevenfold. Symmachus, the seventh. Theodotion, by the week” — and points to his own letter to Damasus on the puzzle. On the counter-line to Cain, Enosh, he preserves a striking Jewish gloss: though the text says men then “began… to call upon the name of the LORD,” “many Hebrews interpret it differently, that at that time idols were first made in the name of the Lord and in his likeness” — the beginning either of true worship or of idolatry.9

Augustine (City of God 15, c. AD 417–420) — Cain, founder of the earthly city

Augustine gives Cain the role that governs the whole second half of the City of God. Cain and Abel are the first citizens of the two cities: “it is recorded of Cain that he built a city, while Abel, as though he were merely a pilgrim on earth, built none. For the true city of the saints is in heaven.” The city Cain founds is the earthly city, propagated by generation and rooted in this age; Augustine even reads the named wives of Lamech — a detail “unheard of before the flood, except in the case of Eve” — as the sign “that to the very end of its existence the earthly city will be propagated by physical births.” Cain is not merely a murderer but the archetype and first builder of the whole order of men who live for this world; Abel and Seth, who build no city, are the pilgrims of the city of God.10

The Venerable Bede (Commentary on Genesis, c. AD 720) — the mark as the preserved, wandering Jew

Bede gathers the Latin reading into the commentary that fed the medieval West, and it is his page that carries the great — and fateful — Augustinian typology of the mark. The sign that keeps Cain alive is read of the Jewish people, preserved and marked among the nations: “the Jewish people, whether under pagan or Christian rulers, have the mark of their law, by which they are distinguished from other nations… every emperor or king who finds them in his kingdom finds them with that mark, nor does he kill them” unless they “turn to Christ.” Cain wandering in “the land of Naid” — which Bede, following the Hebrew, glosses as “fugitive… unstable motion and fluctuation” — images the Jews scattered but preserved, “always unstable and fluctuating and of uncertain abodes.” And Cain’s line, ending in Lamech’s daughter Noema (“Pleasure”), is the reprobate progeny that “not long after… was finished by the flood.” This preserving-sign reading is the patristic mainstream — the mark as mercy and a mark of distinction — but its fusion of Cain with the Jews would later be turned to cruelty the Fathers did not intend.11

The medieval “sons of Cain” — the line of Cain fused with the giants

In the Middle Ages Cain’s cursed line was drawn together with the monstrous. The Old English Beowulf descends Grendel and the gigantas “in Caines cynne” — “in Cain’s kin” — tracing ogres, elves, and giants to the first murderer and setting the Flood as God’s answer to that brood. The move routes the giants of Genesis 6 through Cain’s human line rather than through fallen angels — a variant of the Sethite reading — and it made “the seed of Cain” a byword for a race of monsters. This wiki weighs Beowulf’s witness, and the “sons of Cain” genealogy of the giants, in a dedicated argument on the Nephilim timeline; Cain’s own culture-hero line (Jabal, Jubal, Tubal-cain) is the human, four-chapters-earlier rival to the angelic-arts reading weighed there.12

Modern scholarship — the Kenite smiths and the serpent-seed revival

Two modern afterlives close the story. The first is scholarly: since A. H. Sayce (1899) many have taken the Kenites for a guild of smiths, reading the tribal name qeni and the personal name qayin as a Semitic word for “metalworker” (cf. the biblical Tubal-cain, “instructer… in brass and iron”), and Gesenius long since derived “Kenite” straight from “Cain.” On this reading Israel’s tie to the Kenites — and behind it the modern Kenite hypothesis that Israel received Yahweh-worship from Jethro’s desert people — grows out of the very genealogy Genesis 4 sketches; some, like Ronald Hendel, read the biblical linkage of the Kenites to Cain as a way to give a neighbouring clan a “shameful, violent ancestral origin.” The second afterlife is sectarian and dark: the ancient serpent-seed aggadah was revived in the twentieth century by William Branham’s “Serpent’s Seed” teaching and by the “two-seedline” doctrine of Christian Identity, which made Cain the literal offspring of Satan and the ancestor of whole races — a reading with no basis in the Genesis text and weighed, and rejected, in the Arguments.13

Arguments

The genuinely contested questions about Cain are weighed on their own argument pages, each rated on its own evidence.

Was the mark of Cain a protective sign, or a curse (and was it ever a racial mark)? — sound (the sign is protective) / unsupported (a race-mark)

The Genesis text is explicit that the mark is God’s answer to Cain’s fear of being killed — “lest any finding him should kill him” (Genesis 4:15) — a sign of mercy and preservation, not of condemnation, and the Fathers and rabbis read it so (Ambrose, Bede, Genesis Rabbah). Rated sound in that sense. What is unsupported is the modern folk-reading that the mark was a darkening of Cain’s skin marking a black race — the text says nothing of skin or race, the “mark” is a protective token not a hereditary trait, and Cain’s entire line is drowned in the Flood, leaving him no descendants at all. The race-mark reading is a late American graft, kin to the racial “curse of Ham”, and rests on nothing in Genesis 4.

Are the Kenites the descendants of Cain? — contested, leaning weak

The Kenites (qeni) share a name with Cain (qayin), the Genesis line runs through the metalworker Tubal-cain, and Balaam puns Kenite against Cain in Numbers 24:21 — a real onomastic and thematic tie. But a literal genealogical descent runs hard against the text’s own claim that Cain’s whole line perished in the Flood, and the philology points at least as naturally to qayin = “smith” (a guild name for a coppersmith clan) as to descent from the man Cain. The name-tie is best read as etymological and traditional, not a preserved bloodline; the point bears on Jethro and the Kenite hypothesis, which this page does not re-litigate.

Was Cain fathered by the serpent? The “serpent seed” — unsupported

The reading that Cain was begotten not by Adam but by the serpent (or the fallen angel Sammael) — ancient in a strand of aggadah (Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 21), revived by William Branham and Christian Identity as a doctrine of two literal seedlines — contradicts Genesis 4:1, which names Adam as Cain’s father in the same breath as his conception, and is condemned by 1 John 3:12, which makes Cain “of the wicked one” morally, not biologically. Rated unsupported — a reading with no textual basis whose modern form is the seedbed of explicit racism.
