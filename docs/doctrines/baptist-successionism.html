Baptist Successionism

Baptist successionism (also “Baptist perpetuity,” and in the form given it by J. M. Carroll the “Trail of Blood”) is the claim that Baptists, or the believers'-baptism, free-church Christians they identify with, form an unbroken succession of true churches from Christ and the apostles, independent of Rome, and so did not originate at the Reformation. Its holders trace the line through a series of dissenting movements (Montanists, Novatianists, Donatists, Paulicians, Bogomils, Albigenses, Waldenses, Anabaptists). The view is tied to the nineteenth-century Landmark movement (J. R. Graves) and was spread by Carroll's 1931 pamphlet The Trail of Blood. Other Baptist historians date the first Baptist churches to English Separatists of the early seventeenth century (the General Baptists of John Smyth and Thomas Helwys, c. 1609; the Particular Baptists, c. 1638), and a third group traces a “spiritual kinship” between the Baptists and earlier dissenters and Anabaptists without an organic chain of churches. Writers differ both on whether the claim concerns a genealogy of congregations or a perpetuity of Baptist principles, and on whether the groups named in the line held Baptist convictions.

Timeline

Witnesses are listed earliest first. The disputed question of Baptist origins is set out under Disputed readings below.

J. R. Graves (c. 1855)

James Robinson Graves, the Southern Baptist editor of the Tennessee Baptist and a leader of the Landmark movement, set the thesis out as a doctrinal necessity: Christ's kingdom, “the kingdom which He set up 'in the days of John the Baptist,' has had an unbroken continuity until now,” carried by churches that “alone hold, and have alone ever held, and preserved the doctrine of the gospel in all ages since the ascension of Christ,” so that the Catholic and Protestant bodies, “originated and set up by men many ages after the ascension,” are “not divine — but human institutions.” He sponsored and prefaced the American edition of Orchard's Baptist history (1855), endorsing its claim of “a succession of Baptist churches… from the days of Paul until now.”1 For Graves the succession follows from Christ's promise, so that to deny it “is to impeach His veracity.” James Edward McGoldrick (1994) names Graves and J. M. Pendleton as Landmarkism's “two most influential early leaders” and dates its first formal statement to 1851. Whether the historical record bears out the succession is disputed; see the interpretation page.2

Charles Spurgeon (c. 1861)

Charles Spurgeon, welcoming the Baptist churches of London to the newly opened Metropolitan Tabernacle in 1861, said: “We believe that the Baptists are the original Christians,” who “did not commence our existence at the Reformation” and “never came from the Church of Rome, for we were never in it. We have an unbroken line up to the Apostles themselves!” Their principles, “sometimes veiled and forgotten like a river which may travel underground for a little season,” have “always had honest and holy adherents.”3 The address is often quoted in the debate: the Wikipedia article on the Baptists lists Spurgeon among holders of the successionist view, and the successionist writer R. L. Vaughn cites him as a holder who was not a Landmarker.4

W. H. Whitsitt (c. 1896)

William Heth Whitsitt, professor of church history and third president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, argued from the documents that believers' baptism by immersion was not practised in England before 1641 — that the earlier Anabaptists, like their Dutch Mennonite kin, baptized by “sprinkling or pouring,” and that “none of the Anabaptists of England in the sixteenth and first half of the seventeenth centuries were immersionists.”5 On his account immersion was introduced in the mid-seventeenth century, and the Baptists grew out of English Separatism. He first published the thesis anonymously in 1880 and set it out again in an encyclopedia article and in his 1896 book. John T. Christian, then a Louisville Baptist pastor, replied the same year in Did They Dip?, arguing from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century sources that the English Anabaptists and Baptists practised dipping before 1641. For the Landmark Baptists, who held that an unbroken succession of immersions was necessary to the existence of true churches, the thesis struck at that succession; after threats of schism and of withheld gifts, Whitsitt tendered his resignation on 13 July 1898, effective in 1899.6

James Milton Carroll (1931)

James Milton Carroll, a Texas Southern Baptist minister, gave the thesis its charted form in The Trail of Blood — five lectures with a fold-out chart, completed before his death in January 1931 and published posthumously that year. Distinguishing his claim from apostolic succession — “Baptists do not believe in Apostolic Succession… It is to His churches that He promised a continual existence… until He comes again” (Matt. 16:18) — he charted an unbroken line of “true” churches from Christ through persecuted dissenting groups (Montanists, Novationists, Donatists, Paulicians, Albigenses, Waldenses, and others) to the modern Baptists, who on this reading are neither Catholic nor Protestant, never having come from the Church of Rome.7 James Edward McGoldrick (1994) characterizes Montanism as “much more akin to Pentecostalism than to any historic expression of the Baptist faith” and, as Douglas Douma summarizes him, the Paulicians, Bogomils and Cathars as adoptionist, dualist and neo-Manichaean. Rodney A. Harrison (2007), who studied Carroll's manuscripts and correspondence in the archives of the A. Webb Roberts Library, Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, reports that Carroll's references “were often misleading.” The successionist writer R. L. Vaughn calls the booklet “a book for the Baptist people” and holds that “even if Carroll may have misidentified some, he got others right.”8 On the Montanists, the group Carroll places first, the counsel of Tertullian, the movement's best-known adherent, to delay the baptism of little children (On Baptism 18) and the standing of the New Prophecy toward the catholic church are set out on the Tertullian timeline.9

John MacArthur (c. 2018)

John MacArthur, the American Reformed-Baptist pastor of Grace Community Church, spoke in a filmed interview of a perpetual remnant outside Rome: “throughout the years of the Roman Catholic monolith in the Western world there were always dissenters… whether you were talking about Waldensians or Anabaptists or Huguenots or whatever,” who “really were the remnant of those who held to the true New Testament gospel.”10 He speaks of a “remnant”; he does not name a succession of Baptist churches. The Catholic apostolate Catholic Truth answers that the groups named held contrary doctrines — the Anabaptists rejecting infant baptism, the Waldensians accepting it — and that the Waldensians of Peter Waldo were Catholics who sought reform of the church.11
