Cyril of Alexandria and Nestorius: the road to Ephesus

This timeline follows how a dispute over one word — whether Mary may be called Theotokos, “God-bearer” — became a conflict between the bishops of Constantinople and Alexandria, their sees, and their theological schools. Nestorius (c. 386–c. 451), an Antiochene monk and preacher, was consecrated bishop of Constantinople in 428 and deposed three years later. Cyril of Alexandria (patriarch 412–444), theologian of the hypostatic union, wrote against him, appealed to Rome and to the imperial court, and presided over the council that deposed him at Ephesus in June 431. A rival council of the Syrian bishops deposed Cyril in turn; Cyril returned to Alexandria, and Nestorius to his monastery near Antioch and then to exile in Egypt.

Unlike the belief-timelines elsewhere on this site, this page is a relationship told through its dated moments: the sermons, letters, anathemas, synods, and imperial edicts of the dispute, set down earliest first, each footnoted to its source. It runs from Nestorius's consecration in 428 and the sermons against Theotokos, through the exchange of letters and the appeals to Rome, Cyril's Twelve Anathemas and the rival councils at Ephesus that deposed each leader in turn, the imperial commissioner Candidian, the bribery charges and the gifts Cyril's side sent to the court, to the Formula of Reunion between Cyril and John of Antioch (433), Nestorius's exile to the Egyptian desert, and the modern debate — renewed when Nestorius's own defence became known in the West around 1900 — over whether the council condemned him for a Christology he held.

The doctrine at issue — the person of Christ, the hypostatic union, the title Theotokos — is treated on the christology timeline and the Theotokos timeline, where both men have detail pages, and the disputed reading “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?” has its own page. This page records the dispute between the two men itself.

Timeline

Nestorius is consecrated at Constantinople and addresses the emperor (10 April 428)

Nestorius, a presbyter of Antioch known as a preacher, was brought to the capital and consecrated bishop of Constantinople on 10 April 428. Socrates records the words of his first sermon, addressed to the emperor:

Give me, my prince, the earth purged of heretics, and I will give you heaven as a recompense. Assist me in destroying heretics, and I will assist you in vanquishing the Persians.

— Socrates, Church History VII.29. Socrates adds that “although these utterances were extremely gratifying to some of the multitude, who cherished a senseless antipathy to the very name of heretic,” those “skillful in predicating a man's character from his expressions” detected “his levity of mind, and violent and vainglorious temperament.”1

The sermons against “Theotokos” (428)

The dispute began over a single title. Nestorius's chaplain, a presbyter named Anastasius whom he had brought from Antioch, preached against calling the Virgin Theotokos, and Nestorius defended him from the pulpit:

Let no one call Mary Theotocos: for Mary was but a woman; and it is impossible that God should be born of a woman.

— Socrates, Church History VII.32, reporting Anastasius. Evagrius records Nestorius's own words on the point: “I could never be induced to call that God which admitted of being two months old or three months old.” The two historians assess him differently. Socrates, who says he had read Nestorius's writings, “found him an unlearned man” yet wrote: “I cannot then concede that he was either a follower of Paul of Samosata or of Photinus, or that he denied the Divinity of Christ: but he seemed scared at the term Theotocos, as though it were some terrible phantom”; he reports that Nestorius “thus acquired the reputation among the masses of asserting the blasphemous dogma that the Lord is a mere man.” Evagrius calls Nestorius “the real teacher of the blasphemy.”2

Cyril intervenes: the Second Letter to Nestorius (early 430)

Word reached Alexandria, and Cyril took up the case first in his paschal letter and a warning of his own, then in the Second Letter to Nestorius. Against what he read as a division of Christ in Nestorius's preaching, Cyril set out a union of the Word with the flesh “hypostatically”:

Rather do we claim that the Word in an unspeakable, inconceivable manner united to himself hypostatically flesh enlivened by a rational soul, and so became man and was called son of man, not by God's will alone or good pleasure, nor by the assumption of a person alone.

— Cyril, Second Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 4). Because the union is at the level of the person, he argued, to deny Mary the title “Mother of God” is to deny that her child is God — “not as though the nature of the Word… received the origin of their being from the holy virgin, but because there was born from her his holy body rationally ensouled, with which the Word was hypostatically united.”3

Nestorius answers: “mother of Christ” (430)

Nestorius replied. He attributed the birth and the suffering to the humanity, not the Godhead, and so preferred a title that, in his view, named the united Christ rather than one nature:

Holy scripture, wherever it recalls the Lord's economy, speaks of the birth and suffering not of the godhead but of the humanity of Christ, so that the holy virgin is more accurately termed mother of Christ than mother of God.

— Nestorius, Second Letter to Cyril. In his letters he presented his objection as a caution rather than a rejection of the title: “God-bearer,” he wrote, risked either making the eternal Godhead take its origin from a creature or blending the natures as Apollinaris had, while “Christ-bearer” kept each to its proper limits. Whether the council read his Christology as he meant it is disputed; see “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?”4

Both sides appeal to Rome (430)

Each man wrote to Pope Celestine. Nestorius's letters raised the case of Pelagian refugees he was sheltering, ranked Christotokos above Theotokos (“the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels”), and charged that Cyril was raising the dispute to escape charges pending against himself:

I have learned that Cyril… has become worried about reports against him that we received, and is now hunting for subterfuges to avoid a holy synod taking place due to these reports. In the meantime he is devising some other disturbances over terms…

— Nestorius, third letter to Pope Celestine. Cyril had sent Rome a dossier of Nestorius's sermons in Latin translation. Against the motive charge, the Catholic historian of the councils Karl Joseph von Hefele objected, as Friedrich Loofs (1914) reports him, that Cyril's first letter to Nestorius already carried the doctrinal charges, before Cyril spoke of any accusations against himself; Loofs, who reports the objection, disputes it.5

Rome condemns Nestorius and deputes Cyril to execute the sentence (August 430)

A Roman synod under Celestine condemned Nestorius's teaching, gave him ten days from notice to recant, and committed the carrying-out of the sentence to Cyril of Alexandria.6 The commission is read differently. Nestorius, writing in exile, treated it as making his accuser his judge: “Who was judge? Cyril. And who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril.” John Chapman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) reads it as Celestine's own judgment, delegated: the pope charged Cyril “to assume his authority and give notice in his name to Nestorius,” and at Ephesus the council, as Firmus of Caesarea told the Roman legates, “had executed upon him the papal sentence,” which Chapman calls “an accurate account of the work of the first session and of the sentence.” Friedrich Loofs (1914) held that “it must be charged to Cyril that Celestine of Rome came to the firm conviction that Nestorius was a heretic.” In the account of Fr. Moses Samaan of the Coptic Orthodox Diocese of Los Angeles (2009), Celestine had the dossier examined before the synod met, and Cyril at Ephesus “considered himself as representing Alexandria and Rome.”7

The Twelve Anathemas: Cyril's Third Letter (30 November 430)

With Rome's mandate, an Alexandrian synod sent Nestorius Cyril's Third Letter, closing with twelve anathemas. The first concerned the title itself:

If any one confess not that Emmanuel is in truth God and that the holy Virgin is therefore Mother of God, for she bare after the flesh the Word of God made Flesh, be he anathema.

The third anathematized anyone who would “sever the Persons of the One Christ after the Union,” and the twelfth confessed that “the Word of God suffered in the Flesh and hath been crucified in the Flesh and tasted death in the Flesh.” — Cyril, Third Letter to Nestorius, the Twelve Anathemas. The Antiochene bishops objected to the anathemas: the sentence of deposition they later passed on Cyril at Ephesus, as the council majority reported it, called his party “Apollinarians, and Arians, and Eunomians,” and Loofs (1914) writes that after Ephesus “the Antiochians continued to hold Nestorius in esteem and to treat as heretical the anathematisms of Cyril.”8

The Council of Ephesus opens without the Syrians — Cyril presiding (22 June 431)

Theodosius II summoned a general council to Ephesus for Pentecost 431. Nestorius came early; so did Cyril, with a large Egyptian contingent; but John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops were still on the road. Cyril opened the council on 22 June, over the protest of the imperial commissioner Candidian and sixty-eight bishops. Cyril's words at the opening are preserved in the acts that Nestorius later quoted, together with those of Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, that “the period which was fixed in the letters of the pious and most Christian Emperor” had passed — “sixteen days have passed”:

This great and holy Council has been patient enough, waiting for the coming of the godly bishops, who are expected to come.

— Cyril at the opening, as quoted by Nestorius.9 The two sides gave different accounts of the decision to open. The council majority, writing to Celestine, said it had waited “sixteen whole days” while “many of the bishops and clerics were overtaken with illness, and much burdened by the expense, and some even died,” and that two of John's metropolitans, arriving ahead of him, had told them: “He gave us command to announce to your reverence, that if anything should happen to delay him, not to put off the Synod, but to do what was right.” Evagrius writes that the bishops proceeded “considering that the Orientals would not join them at all, or, at least, after a considerable delay”; John Chapman's article on the council in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1909) and Fr. Moses Samaan's Coptic Orthodox account (2009) report John's message in the same sense. Nestorius, by contrast, wrote that the absent bishops “had been delayed in coming by an important reason and besought you to wait,” and John of Antioch's report to the emperor states that Candidian had “testified unto them in writing and without writing that they should await the coming of all the holy bishops.”10

Nestorius refused to attend. The council's side read the soldiers at his lodging as a refusal — Juvenal of Jerusalem said that “with no good conscience did he decline to come unto the holy Council” — while Nestorius wrote that the guard was for his own safety: “Thou accusest me of posting soldiers around my house: [it was] not that they might do any wrong unto you but that they might hinder you from doing wrong unto me.”11

Nestorius is deposed (22 June 431)

Summoned three times and three times refusing, Nestorius was condemned in a single day's session by the council majority, which described itself to Celestine as “in all over two hundred bishops”:

Compelled thereto by the canons and by the letter… of our most holy father and fellow-servant Cœlestine, the Roman bishop, we have come, with many tears, to this sorrowful sentence against him, namely, that our Lord Jesus Christ, whom he has blasphemed, decrees by the holy Synod that Nestorius be excluded from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion.

— Decree of the Council against Nestorius. Socrates dates these proceedings to 28 June. When the sentence was published, according to John Chapman's Catholic Encyclopedia article, “the people of Ephesus, full of rejoicing, escorted the fathers to their houses with torches and incense”; Philip Schaff notes that Cyril himself relates this in a letter to his friends in Egypt. Nestorius, for his part, had told the synod's messengers, in Socrates's report, “I cannot term him God who was two and three months old… I am therefore clear of your blood, and shall in future come no more among you.”12

John of Antioch arrives and deposes Cyril and Memnon (26–27 June 431)

A few days after the deposition the Syrians reached Ephesus, learned what had been done in their absence, and, under the protection of the imperial count Candidian, held a council of their own that deposed Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus in turn. Now each side had condemned the other:

the most illustrious count Candidianus, who was sent by your most Christian authority, testified unto them in writing and without writing that they should await the coming of all the holy bishops… For this reason we have caused the deposition of those two who have been mentioned above, of Cyril and of Memnon, and interdicted them from every spiritual ministration.

— John of Antioch's report to the emperor, preserved in Nestorius's Bazaar of Heracleides. Evagrius sets the sequence down: “John, the bishop of Antioch, arrives with his associate priests, five days after the act of deposition; and having convened all his company, he deposes Cyril and Memnon.” He adds that John, on “not appearing after a thrice repeated summons” from the other council, was himself cut off from communion, and “Cyril and Memnon are released from their sentence.” Friedrich Loofs (1914) writes that the Antiochenes “opened with Nestorius and others the council or rather their party-council,” so that “there was, therefore, then, one party-council standing in opposition to the other.” Theodosius, receiving both sets of acts, at first confirmed both depositions.13

The Antiochene charge of bribery (431)

The Antiochene side accused Cyril of having turned the court by bribery. Acacius of Beroea, in a letter written in 431 to Alexander of Hierapolis, reported what he had heard from John of Antioch, Theodoret and others: that Cyril had bribed the influential eunuch Scholasticus and many others. Nestorius, in exile, wrote that Cyril gave or promised much money to Count John, the imperial treasurer sent to Ephesus, and through him to the emperor; Evagrius writes instead that Theodosius changed course “on being fully informed of his blasphemy,” that is, Nestorius's. Nestorius wrote of the count and of Cyril:

both he who sold the truth, that is John, and he who [did] more than he, that is Cyril, when the gold of iniquity was exacted…

— Nestorius, Bazaar of Heracleides. Karl Joseph von Hefele (Catholic historian of the councils, 1883) doubted Acacius's report, “which Acacius, as he declares himself, had only from hearsay,” though he would “not directly deny that Cyril may at that time have offered gifts to Scholasticus and others.” Loofs (1914) wrote that Nestorius here “is repeating party-gossip,” adding: “Nevertheless there may be a foundation of truth in this gossip.”14

Cyril returns to Alexandria; Nestorius retires to his monastery (autumn 431)

Cyril and Memnon were held under guard for a time; Cyril left Ephesus and was back in Alexandria before the council was dissolved. Nestorius asked to be allowed to return to his old monastery near Antioch, and was. Nestorius described his rival's departure:

Cyril on the other hand was kept under guard for a while; but he who was being guarded with all caution hid himself from them that were guarding him in Ephesus… and set out from Ephesus and gained his [own] city without being hindered by any one.

— Nestorius, Bazaar of Heracleides. Socrates reports that at Ephesus, as the dispute was “tending to the destruction of communion,” Nestorius “called Mary Theotocos, and cried out: ‘Let Mary be called Theotocos, if you will, and let all disputing cease’,” but that “no notice was taken of it.” The council was dissolved; the two men did not meet again.15

Cyril's presents to the court during the reunion negotiations (432–433)

A surviving catalogue sent from Cyril's side itemizes gifts and gold for the officials and eunuchs around the emperor — carpets, ivory chairs and stools, ostriches, and, to one official, “fifty pounds of gold… in order that he would help us in the cause.” Loofs (1914) dates the list to the same time as a letter of Cyril's archdeacon Epiphanius to Maximian of Constantinople, whose aim, he writes, was “that John of Antioch and his friends should be made willing to accept the judgment of his synod against Nestorius”; Hefele (1883) narrates the same letter and presents within the negotiations of 432–433 that led to the union. Totals computed from the list run from more than a thousand pounds of gold to some 2,500 Roman pounds counting the promised instalments ⚠ unverified.16

How to read the gifts is disputed. The catalogue itself calls some of them eulogiae consuetudinariae supplices, “customary suppliant gifts.” Hefele, who would defend the presents “as little as did Tillemont,” held that they “must be judged of not by our customs and circumstances, but by those of the East,” where “these presents are not all bribes; very many are simply customary recommendations of a cause which, in itself, is thoroughly just.” Philip Schaff wrote that Cyril's presents, “according to the Oriental custom of testifying submission to princes by presents, were not necessarily regarded as bribes,” and the annotator of the English translation notes that “if this was customary, the action of Cyril was not so unusual.” Loofs (1914) held that the documents “show clearly that he did not even hold himself back from bribery,” and Volker Menze (2023) writes that Cyril “paid the largest bribe known from antiquity.” Maurits Sterk de Leeuw (2019) studies Cyril's own language of “blessings,” in a world where “gifts and fees were becoming increasingly important as (institutionalized) instruments to gain access to power,” while his adversaries “aimed to reveal that these blessings were nothing less than ordinary bribes.”17

The Formula of Reunion between Cyril and John of Antioch (433)

The break between Alexandria and Antioch that followed the council was closed two years later. Through the imperial envoy Aristolaus, Cyril and John of Antioch agreed a joint confession — the Formula of Reunion — that affirmed Theotokos and spoke of “a union of two natures,” and accepted Nestorius's deposition. The settlement is read in two ways. Hefele (1883) reads it as John's acceptance of the council's sentence on a creed Cyril could own: the Antiochene formula “was quite orthodox in meaning, and therefore Cyril consented without difficulty,” while John wrote, in Hefele's translation, that the Antiochenes had “determined to agree to the judgment pronounced against Nestorius, to recognize him as deposed, and to anathematize his infamous doctrines.” Hefele records that Cyril defended the union in letters to Valerian of Iconium and Acacius of Melitene ⚠ unverified, against some of his own adherents who “thought that he had yielded more than was right, had sacrificed his original doctrine,” showing, in Hefele's summary, “that there was a great difference between their faith and that of Nestorius.” Loofs (1914) reads the settlement as Cyril's acceptance of the Antiochene confession: “He accepted the Antiochian confession of faith and was contented with the fact that his anathematisms were not condemned.” Cyril announced the peace in a letter whose opening is quoted by Evagrius:

Let the heavens rejoice and the earth be glad, for the middle wall of partition is broken down, exasperation is stilled, and all occasion for dissension utterly removed…

— Cyril, Letter 39 (to John of Antioch), quoted by Evagrius. Its body confessed “a union of two natures… one Christ, one Son, one Lord,” and the holy Virgin “Mother of God.” Evagrius writes that Cyril and John “are reconciled to each other, and ratify the act of deposition.” Georges Florovsky (Eastern Orthodox; lectures given in Paris and first published in Russian in 1931–1933) traces the schism that followed Ephesus to “a theological misunderstanding between the ‘Egyptians’ and the ‘Easterners’”; he writes that the Easterners' condition, that Cyril renounce what he had written against Nestorius and especially his “Chapters,” “would mean a renunciation of the Ecumenical Council of Ephesus,” and he reads the letter to John as revealing “St. Cyril’s serious concern over a vital issue of faith,” not an interest only in “the rivalry with the ecclesiastical power obtaining in Constantinople.”18

Nestorius is exiled to the Egyptian desert (435/436)

A first edict confined Nestorius to his monastery at Antioch; a second, in 435, sent him far into Upper Egypt, to the Great Oasis of the western desert:

by a second edict of Theodosius, he is banished to the place called Oasis.

— Evagrius, Church History I.7, summarizing Nestorius's own account. Evagrius gives the reason as Nestorius's continued teaching in retirement, which John of Antioch reported, so that “Nestorius was, in consequence, condemned to perpetual banishment.” Socrates, writing while Nestorius still lived, ends the story “he was banished to the Oasis, where he still remains.” He was carried off in a desert raid and released, moved from place to place under guard, and died in the Thebaid about 451, having written in exile his defence, the Bazaar of Heracleides.19

Cyril's death (444), and later readings of the dispute

Cyril held Alexandria until his death on 27 June 444 ⚠ unverified. He is venerated as a saint in the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and in Anglican and Lutheran calendars (feast 27 June in the Coptic, Catholic and Lutheran calendars; 9 June and 18 January in the Eastern Orthodox), under titles that include “Pillar of Faith” and “Seal of the Fathers,” and Pope Leo XIII named him a Doctor of the Church. The council of 431 is received as ecumenical by the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches and by Lutheran, Anglican and Reformed churches; John Chapman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) calls it “the third ecumenical council,” and Georges Florovsky (Eastern Orthodox) “the Ecumenical Council of Ephesus.” The Church of the East never accepted Nestorius's condemnation; it honours him with Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia among the three “Greek Teachers,” and the Assyrian Church of the East reveres him as a saint.20

In the West the question of Nestorius's teaching was taken up again after a Syriac manuscript of the Bazaar of Heracleides, held in the library of the patriarch of the Church of the East at Kotchanes, was copied from 1889. Readings since then fall along several lines. J. F. Bethune-Baker (Cambridge, writing as a Church of England scholar, 1908), who used the then-unpublished text, framed the question as “Did Nestorius mean what the Council thought he meant?” and concluded that “though Nestorius as an individual was condemned, all that he contended for was amply conceded in Leo's letter to Flavian and at the Council of Chalcedon.” Martin Jugie (Augustinian of the Assumption, Catholic, Paris, 1912), entering the debate that Bethune-Baker's book had reopened, held that in reading Nestorius's sermons Cyril “aperçut promptement le vice radical de la théorie antiochienne” (“promptly perceived the radical flaw of the Antiochene theory”), and concluded that “le Nestorius que nous avons trouvé est celui des anciens, celui de Célestin et de Cyrille, celui d'Ephèse, de Chalcédoine et du cinquième concile” (“the Nestorius we have found is that of the ancients, of Celestine and of Cyril, of Ephesus, of Chalcedon and of the fifth council”). Georges Florovsky (Eastern Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Fifth Century, Russian 1931–1933) held that Nestorius “deviated from tradition and the rules of faith not when he spoke of ‘two natures’ but when he separated two subjects and distinguished two ontological centres of reference or subjects in Christ.” Friedrich Loofs (Halle, 1914) held that, measured by Chalcedon alone, “the statement of Professor Bethune-Baker, that Nestorius was orthodox, is not to be held a false one,” but that “measured by the standard of church-orthodoxy, Nestorius… must be regarded as a heretic,” adding that “his doctrine has more historical right than the Cyrillian orthodoxy.” John A. McGuckin (an Eastern Orthodox priest and historian, 1994) holds, as Wikipedia reports him, that several mid-twentieth-century accounts “romanticise” Nestorius, and that Nestorius was no less dogmatic and uncompromising than Cyril ⚠ unverified. On 11 November 1994 Pope John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, signed a Common Christological Declaration which states that “the controversies of the past led to anathemas, bearing on persons and on formulas,” and in which both churches “recognize the legitimacy and rightness” of “the Mother of God” and “the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour” as “expressions of the same faith.” The doctrinal question is set out, reading by reading, at “Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?”21
