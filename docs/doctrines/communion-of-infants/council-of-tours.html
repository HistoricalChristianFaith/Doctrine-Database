The Council of Tours (813) on giving communion to children

Dates: 813 · Wikipedia
Summary: One of the Carolingian reform councils convened under Charlemagne in 813, the Council of Tours is regularly cited — following the older reference literature — as an early Western restriction of children's communion. Its actual canon (XIX) is narrower than that reputation: it tells priests not to distribute the Eucharist indiscriminately to boys or bystanders who may be in grave sin, on the worthiness ground of 1 Corinthians 11 — not a categorical bar on infant communion, and with no "danger of death" exception.

Claims & evidence

Canon XIX — priests are not to give the Body "indiscriminately" to boys or bystanders

Presbyteri omnino admonendi sunt, ut, cum sacra missarum sollemnia peregerint atque communicaverint, pueris aut aliis quibuslibet personis adstantibus corpus Domini indiscrete non tribuant. Qui si forte maioribus peccatis fuerint irretiti, magis sibi damnationem quam remedium sumere noscuntur iuxta sententiam apostoli dicentis: Quicumque manducaverit panem vel biberit calicem Domini indigne, reus erit corporis et sanguinis Domini. Probet autem se ipsum homo et sic de pane illo edat et de calice bibat.

— Council of Tours (813), canon 19, source (Latin text after A. Werminghoff, ed., Concilia aevi Karolini I, MGH Concilia 2/1, no. 38). In English: "Priests are earnestly to be admonished that, when they have completed the sacred solemnities of the Mass and have communicated, they should not give the Body of the Lord indiscriminately to boys or to any other persons standing by. For if such should chance to be entangled in graver sins, they are known to take damnation upon themselves rather than a remedy, according to that saying of the Apostle: 'Whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord unworthily shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread and drink of that cup'" (cf. 1 Corinthians 11:27–28).

What the canon actually restricts — and what it does not

The canon is a rule about the manner of distribution grounded in worthiness, not an age-bar. Read on its own terms it says three things: (a) its target is indiscriminate distribution (indiscrete) — not the reception of any class of communicant as such; (b) its rationale is the Pauline requirement of self-examination before communion, so that those in grave sin do not "eat and drink judgment" to themselves — a moral-capacity concern about unworthy reception; and (c) it names "boys or any other persons standing by" (pueris aut aliis quibuslibet personis adstantibus) — i.e. bystanders generally, with boys as one instance, not infants singled out. The very ground it gives (liability to grave sin, the duty to examine oneself) presupposes communicants old enough to sin and to examine themselves — which fits older children and adult bystanders, not the nursing infant at the font.

What the canon does not contain is just as important. There is no categorical prohibition of pre-rational children's communion, and there is no "danger of death" (in periculo mortis) exception anywhere in it or in the council's other canons. Those two elements — an absolute age-bar softened only by a death exception — belong to the later Western discipline (and to modern canon law); they are read back into Tours 813 by the summary in the older reference literature.

Notes

  • The secondary claim and its overstatement. The witness enters most modern discussion through the Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion of Children," which states: "In the reign of Charlemagne an edict was published by a Council of Tours (813) prohibiting the reception by young children of Communion unless they were in danger of death, and Odo, Bishop of Paris, renewed this prohibition in 1175." Set beside canon 19 this is a loose paraphrase: it hardens a discretion-in-distribution rule into a categorical age-bar and imports a danger-of-death clause the canon does not have. The honest statement is that Tours 813 told priests to be discriminating about giving the sacrament to "boys or any bystanders" on worthiness grounds — not that it abolished or formally restricted infant communion.
  • Why it still belongs on the timeline. Even read down to its real scope, the canon is a genuine, datable Western waypoint in the long gap between Augustine (c. 412) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215). Its worthiness logic — the requirement of self-examination as a filter on who receives — is the same rationale that would later underwrite the "age of discretion": once fitness to discern the sacrament becomes the test, the pre-rational child drifts outside the ordinary discipline of the table. Tours 813 does not document a Western abolition of infant communion; it documents the Carolingian-era tightening of eucharistic discipline within which that lapse would later occur.
  • A separate frequency canon. The same council's canon 50 sets a minimum communion frequency for all laity — "that, if not more often, lay people should communicate at least three times a year, unless perhaps someone is impeded by graver crimes" — a rule about the whole laity, unrelated to the children question, and it too rests on the worthiness proviso (nisi … maioribus … criminibus impediatur).
