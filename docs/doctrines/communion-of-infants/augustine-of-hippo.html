Augustine of Hippo on the communion of infants

Dates: c. 412 (On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Pelagian controversy Augustine makes infant communion a matter of necessity, applying "except ye eat … and drink … ye have no life in you" (John 6:53) to infants as much as to adults — the high-water mark of the practice's theology, and the reading Trent would later decline.

Claims & evidence

Infants too must feed on Christ, or they have no life

Will, however, any man be so bold as to say that this statement has no relation to infants, and that they can have life in them without partaking of His body and blood — on the ground that He does not say, Except one eat, but "Except ye eat;" as if He were addressing those who were able to hear and to understand, which of course infants cannot do? But he who says this is inattentive; because, unless all are embraced in the statement, that without the body and the blood of the Son of man men cannot have life, it is to no purpose that even the elder age is solicitous of it.

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and the Baptism of Infants I.27 ("Infants Must Feed on Christ"), source. Augustine has just quoted the Lord — "Except ye eat my flesh and drink my blood, ye shall have no life in you" (I.20) — and here turns it against any who would exempt infants. If the words bind adults, they bind infants; the church's answer, he holds, is to communicate them.

At Carthage baptism is called "salvation" and the Eucharist "life"

The Christians of Carthage have an excellent name for the sacraments, when they say that baptism is nothing else than "salvation," and the sacrament of the body of Christ nothing else than "life." Whence, however, was this derived, but from that primitive, as I suppose, and apostolic tradition, by which the Churches of Christ maintain it to be an inherent principle, that without baptism and partaking of the supper of the Lord it is impossible for any man to attain either to the kingdom of God or to salvation and everlasting life?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins I.34 ("Baptism is Called Salvation, and the Eucharist, Life, by the Christians of Carthage"), source. Augustine roots the necessity not in his own inference but in the very speech and settled conviction of the African church — for whom the Eucharist simply is "life," owed to the baptized infant no less than to the adult.

Notes

  • This is the strongest patristic case for infant communion as necessary. Its scriptural prop is the same John 6:53 the Utraquists would later press for the lay cup — and the wiki rates the strong necessity form of that reading weak, since the same discourse promises life to eating the bread alone (vv. 51, 57–58).
  • Augustine's necessity reading is exactly what Trent (Session 21, ch. IV; canon 4) answered, reading the very verse and defining infants under no necessity of communion — the church settling against the African-Augustinian practice on this point.
  • Whether this necessity reading holds, or only a milder "fitting and ancient" claim, is weighed on its own page: Does John 6:53 make communion necessary for infants? — rated weak (the practice is genuinely ancient and apostolic, but the strict necessity turns on reading John 6 as legislating the rite, which Augustine's own faith-gloss and the church's doctrine that baptism already gives the infant life both undercut).
