Pope Pius X (Quam singulari) on the age of first communion

Dates: 8 August 1910 (decree Quam singulari, under Pius X) · Wikipedia
Summary: Quam singulari fixed first communion at the "age of discretion," about the seventh year, against a drift that had delayed it to twelve or fourteen — presented as a restoration of the church's ancient practice of early access, not an innovation.

Claims & evidence

The ancient church communicated even nursing infants

The Catholic Church, bearing this in mind, took care even from the beginning to bring the little ones to Christ through Eucharistic Communion, which was administered even to nursing infants. This, as was prescribed in almost all ancient Ritual books, was done at Baptism until the thirteenth century, and this custom prevailed in some places even later. It is still found in the Greek and Oriental Churches. But to remove the danger that infants might eject the Consecrated Host, the custom obtained from the beginning of administering the Eucharist to them under the species of wine only. … This practice later died out in the Latin Church, and children were not permitted to approach the Holy Table until they had come to the use of reason and had some knowledge of this august Sacrament.

— Quam singulari (1910), source. The decree opens with the memory of infant communion and its Latin disappearance — locating the change in the thirteenth century and tying it, in effect, to the loss of the lay cup — precisely the arc this timeline traces.

First communion belongs at the age of reason, about the seventh year

The age of discretion, both for Confession and for Holy Communion, is the time when a child begins to reason, that is about the seventh year, more or less. From that time on begins the obligation of fulfilling the precept of both Confession and Communion.

— Quam singulari (1910), decree 1, source. A second decree adds that "a full and perfect knowledge of Christian doctrine is not necessary" for first communion — only that the child "distinguish the Bread of the Eucharist from ordinary, material bread" and approach with such devotion as his age allows. The decree was aimed at a Jansenist-tinged rigorism that had delayed children's access, and Pius X ordered it read from the pulpit yearly.

Notes

  • The decree presents itself as a restoration, not a new discipline — "restoring the ancient and universal law of the Church" of early access. Within the Latin frame of the age of reason it moves back toward the childhood communion the West once shared with the East, though it does not restore communion to infants proper.
  • It leaves Trent's ruling untouched: infants under the age of reason remain under no necessity of communion. Quam singulari settles the age of first communion, not the question of infant communion as such.
  • Whether the Latin abandonment of infant communion that this decree partly reverses was a legitimate development of discipline or a rupture with the ancient and Eastern practice is weighed at Was the Western lapse of infant communion a legitimate development of discipline, or a rupture with apostolic tradition? The narrower question of which frame — the modern Latin age of reason, the medieval "years of discretion," or the ancient/Eastern communion of infants — best answers when a Christian should first receive remains distinct from that of the lapse's legitimacy.
