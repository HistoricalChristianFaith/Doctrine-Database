The Eastern retention of infant communion (the infant and the spoon)

Dates: 692 onward (the communion-spoon standard in Constantinople by the 11th c., retained to the present) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Eastern churches never abandoned the communion of infants. They keep the single initiation whole — baptism, chrismation, and Eucharist given together — and communicate baptized infants to this day, under the consecrated wine, by the communion-spoon.

Claims & evidence

Infant communion was universal in the East and endures there

It is now well established that in the early days of Christianity it was not uncommon for infants to receive Communion immediately after they were baptized. … In the East the custom was pretty universal, and even to this day exists in some places, but in the West infant Communion was not so general.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion of Children," source. What the article records as surviving "in some places" is, in Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic practice, the universal rule: every baptized and chrismated member, infants included, is communicated at the Divine Liturgy.

The mechanism: the consecrated wine, by the spoon

The Byzantine rite met the practical difficulty of communicating the very young — a baby cannot swallow the bread — by the same instrument it adopted for all the laity. The consecrated Bread is placed into the chalice and administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), so that an infant receives a drop of the consecrated wine in which the Body is commingled. Robert Taft dates the spoon's emergence to the late seventh century and its establishment at Constantinople to the mid-eleventh, the practice standard there and to this day.

— on the mechanism and its dating, Robert F. Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons: A Review of the Evidence," Dumbarton Oaks Papers 50 (1996): 209–238 (also JSTOR).

Notes

  • The Eastern retention is the standing counter-case to the Western delay of first communion: the practice the Latin West let lapse over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the East simply kept, so the delay is a Latin development, not the church's universal mind — the same counter-case the East supplies against the Western withdrawal of the chalice, of which infant communion's disappearance is a direct consequence.
