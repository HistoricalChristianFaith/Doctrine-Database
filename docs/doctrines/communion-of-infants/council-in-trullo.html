The Eastern retention of infant communion (the infant and the spoon)

Dates: 692 onward (the communion-spoon standard in Constantinople by the 11th c., retained to the present) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Eastern churches never abandoned the communion of infants. They keep the single initiation whole — baptism, chrismation, and Eucharist given together — and communicate baptized infants to this day, under the consecrated wine, by the communion-spoon.

Claims & evidence

Infant communion was universal in the East and endures there

It is now well established that in the early days of Christianity it was not uncommon for infants to receive Communion immediately after they were baptized. … In the East the custom was pretty universal, and even to this day exists in some places, but in the West infant Communion was not so general.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion of Children," source. What that Western reference records at a distance — the practice surviving "in some places" — is stated from the inside by the Byzantine witnesses below: in Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic practice it is the universal rule that every baptized and chrismated member, infants included, is communicated at the Divine Liturgy.

The single initiation kept whole, ending at the chalice (the Byzantine witnesses)

Behind the practice stands a settled Byzantine theology: the three mysteries of Christian initiation — baptism, chrismation, and the Eucharist — are one act, and Holy Communion is its completion, given to everyone the church baptizes, the infant no less than the adult. The classic statement is Nicholas Cabasilas (c. 1350), whose The Life in Christ is built on this sequence:

Baptism reconciles man to God, the chrism makes him worthy of the gifts from on high; the power of the table communicates the Flesh and Blood of Christ to him who is initiated. … Accordingly, we are first washed, then anointed, and thus the banquet receives us in a state of purity and with a sweet odour.

— Nicholas Cabasilas, The Life in Christ (trans. C. J. deCatanzaro; Greek in PG 150), in his account of why the mysteries follow in the order baptism–chrism–Eucharist. For Cabasilas the newly-illumined — who in the Byzantine world is normally the baptized infant, one who by baptism has gained, in his words, "our very being and nature, having previously been nothing" — is carried straight from the font to the table, the Eucharist crowning the birth given in baptism.

A generation later Symeon, archbishop of Thessalonica (d. 1429), the great late-Byzantine commentator on the rites, says the same of the initiation as he administered it: "the reception of the awesome Communion by the one baptized is the completion of this and of every mystery."

— Symeon of Thessalonica, On the Sacraments (De sacramentis) 67–68, PG 155:85B, as translated in E. Hatzidakis, The Heavenly Banquet: Understanding the Divine Liturgy (Orthodox Witness, 2013), 306.

The rubric has never lapsed: the standing Byzantine rite of baptism directs that the newly baptized and chrismated infant be communicated at once — "Holy Communion is given to the infant immediately following baptism to provide nourishment for the spiritual life the neophyte has just received," the newly-illumined, with the sponsors, being "the first to receive after the Priest and Deacon."

— The Order of Holy Baptism (Byzantine rite), rubric and note on the communion of the newly-illumined.

The mechanism: the consecrated wine, by the spoon

The Byzantine rite met the practical difficulty of communicating the very young — a baby cannot swallow the bread — by the same instrument it adopted for all the laity. The consecrated Bread is placed into the chalice and administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), so that an infant receives a drop of the consecrated wine in which the Body is commingled. Robert Taft dates the spoon's emergence to the late seventh century and its establishment at Constantinople to the mid-eleventh, the practice standard there and to this day.

— on the mechanism and its dating, Robert F. Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons: A Review of the Evidence," Dumbarton Oaks Papers 50 (1996): 209–238 (also JSTOR).
