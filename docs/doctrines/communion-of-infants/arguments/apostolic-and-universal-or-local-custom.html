Was the communion of infants a practice received from the apostles, or a custom that arose after them?

Disputed reading · on The communion of infants · witnesses: Cyprian of Carthage, Augustine of Hippo, the Council of Trent, Pius X · see also the infant baptism timeline.

The passage

When, however, the solemnities were finished, and the deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child, by the instinct of the divine majesty, turned away its face, compressed its mouth with resisting lips, and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup. Then there followed a sobbing and vomiting.

— Cyprian of Carthage, On the Lapsed 25 (c. 251).1

The Christians of Carthage have an excellent name for the sacraments, when they say that baptism is nothing else than "salvation," and the sacrament of the body of Christ nothing else than "life." Whence, however, was this derived, but from that primitive, as I suppose, and apostolic tradition, by which the Churches of Christ maintain it to be an inherent principle, that without baptism and partaking of the supper of the Lord it is impossible for any man to attain either to the kingdom of God or to salvation and everlasting life?

— Augustine of Hippo, On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins I.34 (c. 412).2

The Catholic Church, bearing this in mind, took care even from the beginning to bring the little ones to Christ through Eucharistic Communion, which was administered even to nursing infants. This, as was prescribed in almost all ancient Ritual books, was done at Baptism until the thirteenth century, and this custom prevailed in some places even later. It is still found in the Greek and Oriental Churches.

— Sacred Congregation for the Discipline of the Sacraments, under Pius X, Quam singulari (1910).3

The question

The earliest extant attestation of an infant receiving the Eucharist is Cyprian's account in On the Lapsed (c. 251); Augustine (c. 412) calls the conviction behind the practice "apostolic tradition," and Quam singulari (1910) says the Church communicated nursing infants "even from the beginning," while the Council of Trent (1562) speaks of antiquity having observed the custom "in some places, at one time." The dispute is whether the communion of baptized infants goes back to the apostles and was the practice of the churches generally, or is a custom that arose in the post-apostolic church, is first attested in the third century, and spread unevenly. The answer bears on how the Eastern churches' retention of the practice and its lapse in the Latin West are described. Some academic literature for the first reading, among it Keidel's 1975 article, was not openly accessible and is cited here at second hand.

A practice received from the apostles together with baptism

On this reading the baptized infant was brought to the Eucharist from the apostolic age onward, and the third-century sources are the first surviving mentions of an inherited practice.

Held by: Augustine of Hippo (On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, c. 412); the decree Quam singulari (Catholic, 1910); Christian L. Keidel (Reformed, "Is the Lord's Supper for Children?", 1975) ⚠ unverified; Cyrille Argenti (Greek Orthodox, "Children and the Eucharist", 1982).4

Augustine traces the African church's naming of baptism as "salvation" and the Eucharist as "life" to "that primitive, as I suppose, and apostolic tradition," and Hogg notes that in the Pelagian controversy Augustine and Leo the Great "do not argue for the practice; they argue from the practice."5 Tommy Lee (Reformed) reads such early notices as Cyprian's, whose infant takes her "turn" at the cup "as the rest received it," as "accidental remarks about a practice as common and ordinary as going to sleep at night," and holds that "the most logical explanation of the church's third century paedocommunion practice is that it was the same as the church's first and second century paedocommunion practice."6 Hogg asks that "lack of positive evidence" be distinguished from "positive evidence of a lack." Keidel, as Lee quotes him, asks whether the John 6:53 rationale first introduced the practice "or was it a distorted view later imposed upon what was an original apostolic practice," the same question that "may be asked of infant baptism."7 Orthodox writers describe the same order as the Eastern churches' continuing practice. Argenti reads the baptismal hymn of the great feasts as a "confirmation of the ancient practice of the Church" that the newly baptized entered the eucharistic assembly "on the very day and at the very hour they were baptized," and adds: "That is still our custom today." Alciviadis Calivas (Greek Orthodox), describing Orthodox sacramental practice, writes that every baptized "adult, child and infant" is expected to receive; Hogg holds that the practice of the Orthodox Church "as far back as we can tell" was the communion of the baptized.8 On this reading baptism, chrismation and the Eucharist formed one initiation from the apostolic churches onward, and the Byzantine rite keeps that order today.

An ancient custom of some churches, not to be condemned but never necessary

On this reading the communion of infants was a custom that antiquity kept in some places and at some times, for good reason, without its being necessary for salvation; it is neither condemned nor required.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 21, 1562); Mark M. Morozowich (Catholic, 2008).9

Trent teaches that little children "who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion of the Eucharist," since, "having been regenerated by the laver of baptism, and being incorporated with Christ, they cannot, at that age, lose the grace which they have already acquired." It continues: "Not therefore, however, is antiquity to be condemned, if, in some places, it, at one time, observed that custom; for as those most holy Fathers had a probable cause for what they did in respect of their times, so, assuredly, is it to be believed without controversy, that they did this without any necessity thereof unto salvation."10 Morozowich, reviewing the Latin conciliar and canonical texts from Lateran IV through Trent to Quam singulari, finds "nothing in the canonical or conciliar tradition of the Catholic Church forbidding infant communion," and writes that "Eastern and Western Christians shared this tradition for well over a millennium."11 On this reading the communion of infants and first communion at the age of discretion are both legitimate disciplines of particular churches, grounded in the Fathers' "probable cause" for their times; and on Morozowich's account recent Catholic teaching encourages the Eastern Catholic Churches to recover the former.

A post-apostolic custom, first attested in the mid-third century

On this reading the record does not carry infant communion back to the apostles: it first appears in third-century Carthage, became general in the East and less so in the West, and arose, on several of its holders' accounts, from reading John 6:53 as making communion necessary for salvation.

Held by: Patrick Morrisroe (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion of Children," 1908); Roger T. Beckwith (Anglican, "The Age of Admission to Communion," 1971); Cornelis Venema (Reformed, "Paedocommunion in Church History," 2005); the Commission on Theology and Church Relations of the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod (Lutheran, 2014).12

Morrisroe writes that "in the East the custom was pretty universal," but that "in the West infant Communion was not so general," and that "probably it originated in a mistaken notion of the absolute necessity of the Blessed Eucharist for salvation, founded on the words of St. John (vi, 54)."13 For Beckwith, infant baptism has "unmistakable references" in Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus and Origen and implicit ones reaching to the late first century; for infant communion the Hippolytan reference is "quite uncertain," and the first clear references are Cyprian's, "at a time when Origen is denying that infants receive communion." Because Cyprian elsewhere reads John 6:53 as making communion necessary to salvation, Beckwith concludes that infant communion "arose out of a special interpretation of Jn. 6: 53," which "fully explains the absence of earlier patristic evidence for the practice."14 Venema adds the second- and third-century descriptions of who received: Justin's Eucharist is for "the man who believes that the things which we teach are true," Clement of Alexandria's communicants receive "by faith," and the Syrian Didascalia, which Venema reads as admitting believers to the Table "only after a period of instruction in the faith." He places "contrary evidence in sectors of the church, including Palestine, Syria, and Egypt, which are the likeliest to have preserved the earliest practice of the church."15 The Missouri Synod commission finds "no evidence for a widespread practice of paedocommunion in the earliest centuries of the church's history following the time of the Apostles."16 On this reading infant communion, attested later and more unevenly than adult communion and infant baptism, is a custom that grew up in the third-century church.
