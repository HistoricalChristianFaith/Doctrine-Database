The modern codes: Latin and Eastern canon law on infant communion

Dates: 1983 (Latin Code of Canon Law, c. 913) · 1990 (Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches, cc. 697, 710) · CIC / CCEO
Summary: the two current codes of the Catholic communion legislate opposite answers on infant communion — the Latin Church fixing first communion at the age of reason, the Eastern Catholic Churches keeping the ancient order in which the baptized infant is communicated at once. Together they are the clean legislative bookend of the whole East–West arc this timeline traces.

Claims & evidence

The Latin Church: first communion waits on the age of reason (CIC c. 913)

§1. The administration of the Most Holy Eucharist to children requires that they have sufficient knowledge and careful preparation so that they understand the mystery of Christ according to their capacity and are able to receive the body of Christ with faith and devotion.

§2. The Most Holy Eucharist, however, can be administered to children in danger of death if they can distinguish the body of Christ from ordinary food and receive communion reverently.

— 1983 Code of Canon Law, canon 913, source. The Latin code carries the settlement of Lateran IV, Trent, and Pius X into current law: ordinary first communion presupposes "sufficient knowledge" — the age of reason, about the seventh year — and the only exception is the danger-of-death case of §2, itself limited to a child able to "distinguish the body of Christ from ordinary food." A pre-rational infant is, by the ordinary rule, not admitted.

The Eastern Catholic Churches: the infant is communicated at once (CCEO cc. 697, 710)

[c. 697] Sacramental initiation in the mystery of salvation is perfected in the reception of the Divine Eucharist, and thus the Divine Eucharist is administered after baptism and chrismation with holy myron as soon as possible according to the norms of the particular law of the each Church sui iuris.

[c. 710] With respect to the participation of infants in the Divine Eucharist after baptism and chrismation with holy myron, the prescriptions of the liturgical books of each Church sui iuris are to be observed with the suitable due precautions.

— 1990 Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches, canons 697 and 710, 697 / 710. The Eastern code keeps the single initiation whole: canon 697 makes the Eucharist the completion of baptism and chrismation, administered "as soon as possible," and canon 710 provides expressly for "the participation of infants in the Divine Eucharist," deferring to the liturgical books of each Church — books that, following the Byzantine rite, communicate baptized infants by the spoon. Where Latin law makes the age of reason the gate, Eastern Catholic law makes it no gate at all.

Notes

  • The two codes make the East–West divergence a live, current legal fact rather than a matter of history: within one church, Latin and Eastern law reach opposite answers on whether a baptized infant is communicated. This is not a contradiction of doctrine — Trent defined only that infant communion is under no necessity, leaving the practice permissible — but a codified difference of discipline.
  • Canon 710's phrase "with the suitable due precautions" (opportunis cautelis adhibitis) is the Eastern counterpart to the old Western worry about an infant ejecting the consecrated Host: it presumes, rather than forbids, the communicating of infants, and simply asks that it be done carefully.
  • Whether the Latin discipline codified in c. 913 is a legitimate development or a rupture with the ancient and Eastern practice preserved in cc. 697 and 710 is weighed at Was the Western lapse of infant communion a legitimate development of discipline, or a rupture with apostolic tradition? (rated plausible). The codes do not settle that question; they are its two standing witnesses.
