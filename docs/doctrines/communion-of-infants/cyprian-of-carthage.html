Cyprian of Carthage on the communion of infants

Dates: c. 251 (bishop of Carthage c. 249–258) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian gives the earliest concrete account of an infant being communicated — an unremarked detail assuming that infants took their turn at the chalice "as the rest received it," in the characteristic early form of infant communion under the consecrated wine.

Claims & evidence

An infant is communicated under the cup as a matter of course

When, however, the solemnities were finished, and the deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child, by the instinct of the divine majesty, turned away its face, compressed its mouth with resisting lips, and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup. Then there followed a sobbing and vomiting.

— On the Lapsed 25, source (cf. New Advent). The story is told to show the sacrament's power over one defiled by the pagan sacrifices — the infant, taken by her nurse to eat the idol-food during the persecution, cannot retain the Eucharist. But the incidental machinery of the narrative is the historical gold: the deacon carries the cup down the ranks, the infant's "turn" comes "as the rest received it," and no one finds the communion of a baby remarkable. Infant communion is simply the church's practice at Carthage c. 251, and its form is reception under the consecrated wine — the natural mode for one too young to swallow the bread.

Notes

  • The characteristic early form — infants communicated under the wine, not the host — is why the later Western withdrawal of the chalice from the laity carried infant communion away with it: the practice depended on the cup the medieval West took back.
  • Cyprian appears on this wiki also on the communion under one kind timeline, where the same treatise supplies the earliest one-kind exception (the host reserved at home in the arca).
