The Council of Trent on the communion of little children

Dates: Session 21, 16 July 1562 · Wikipedia
Summary: Trent defined that little children who have not the use of reason are under no necessity of sacramental communion, and anathematized the contrary — answering the Augustinian-African reading of John 6:53 head-on.

Claims & evidence

Little children are under no necessity of communion

Finally, this same holy Synod teaches, that little children, who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion of the Eucharist: forasmuch as, having been regenerated by the laver of baptism, and being incorporated with Christ, they cannot, at that age, lose the grace which they have already acquired of being the sons of God.

— Council of Trent, Session 21, ch. IV, source. The council does not condemn the ancient practice — it adds that "not therefore, however, is antiquity to be condemned, if, in some places, it, at one time, observed that custom" — but it denies the necessity: baptism has already made the child a son of God, and the grace so given is not lost for want of the Eucharist.

The anathema against requiring it

If any one saith, that the communion of the Eucharist is necessary for little children, before they have arrived at years of discretion; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 21, canon 4, source. This is the definitive Catholic answer to the necessity reading. Trent read the very verse (John 6:53) that Augustine had pressed for infants, and declined to make the cup — or the sacrament in either kind — necessary to the salvation of the not-yet-rational child.

Notes

  • The same twenty-first session defined communion under one kind; the ruling on little children is the fourth chapter of that decree. Both turn on the same verse: the strong necessity reading of John 6:53, pressed by the Utraquists for the lay cup and by the Augustinians for infant communion, is the reading Trent set aside — and which the wiki rates weak.
  • Trent settles the necessity question but not the question of fittingness: whether infant communion, though not required, was a good the West lost — the live issue between the Latin and Eastern practices. To be weighed on a dedicated argument page.
