The Fourth Lateran Council on the age of first communion

Dates: 1215 · Wikipedia
Summary: Lateran IV bound every believer "of either sex" to confess yearly and receive at least at Easter once come to the "age of discretion" — tying communion to the capacity to discern the sacrament, and so formalizing the exclusion of the very young that the Western lapse had produced.

Claims & evidence

Reception is bound to the "age of discretion"

All the faithful of either sex, after they have reached the age of discretion, should faithfully confess all their sins at least once a year to their own priest … and let them reverently receive the sacrament of the eucharist at least at Easter, unless they think, for a good reason and on the advice of their own priest, that they should abstain from receiving it for a time.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, source. The canon does not legislate against infant communion in so many words; it presupposes its disappearance. By making the annual obligation begin at "the age of discretion" — the capacity to discern the sacrament — it fixed the frame in which first communion would henceforth be reckoned, and left the pre-rational infant outside the ordinary discipline of the table.

Notes

  • The Western lapse this canon presupposes ran in step with the withdrawal of the chalice over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries: since infants had been communicated under the consecrated wine, the loss of the lay cup made infant communion impractical, and it fell away.
  • "The age of discretion" was long taken as around seven, but in practice first communion drifted much later — to twelve or fourteen — the very deferral Pius X's Quam singulari (1910) would move to correct by fixing reception at the age of reason.
