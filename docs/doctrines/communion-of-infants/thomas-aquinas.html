Thomas Aquinas on the communion of infants

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Between Lateran IV's bare "age of discretion" and Trent's non-necessity ruling stands the scholastic rationale for both. Aquinas holds that the Eucharist, unlike baptism, is not necessary for the salvation of baptized children — they already have its reality (res) through the Church's intention — and that the sacrament is therefore not to be given to those who have never had the use of reason, including newborn infants, since a preceding devotion toward the sacrament is required. This is the theological articulation of the discipline the Latin West had drifted into, and it anticipates Trent's answer to the Augustinian reading of John 6:53.

Claims & evidence

The Eucharist is not necessary for salvation as baptism is — baptized children have its reality by desire through the Church

Aquinas raises the Augustinian objection directly: "Nor are you to suppose that children cannot possess life, who are deprived of the body and blood of Christ" — the very sentence Augustine had pressed. His answer distinguishes the sacrament's outward reception from its inward reality:

… by Baptism a man is ordained to the Eucharist, and therefore from the fact of children being baptized, they are destined by the Church to the Eucharist; and just as they believe through the Church's faith, so they desire the Eucharist through the Church's intention, and, as a result, receive its reality.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 73, a. 3, reply to obj. 3, source. The "reality" (res) of the Eucharist is the unity of the mystical body; a baptized infant already shares it, so actual reception is not required for salvation. This is the pivot on which the whole Western discipline turns: if the child is not endangered by delay, the practice of communicating infants — however ancient — is not a matter of necessity, and may lapse without loss. Trent would define exactly this three centuries later.

Those who have never had the use of reason, including newborn infants, are not to be communicated

Men are said to be devoid of reason in two ways. First, when they are feeble-minded … and since such persons can conceive some devotion towards this sacrament, it is not to be denied them. In another way men are said not to possess fully the use of reason. Either, then, they never had the use of reason, and have remained so from birth; and in that case this sacrament is not to be given to them, because in no way has there been any preceding devotion towards the sacrament …

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 9, respondeo, source. The governing criterion is a preceding devotion toward the sacrament — a capacity to conceive some reverence for what is received. The feeble-minded who can conceive it may receive; those who never could (from birth) may not.

Newborn infants stand with those who never had reason — and John 6:53 does not condemn them

The same reason holds good of newly born children as of the insane who never have had the use of reason: consequently, the sacred mysteries are not to be given to them. Although certain Greeks do the contrary, because Dionysius says … that Holy Communion is to be given to them who are baptized; not understanding that Dionysius is speaking there of the Baptism of adults. Nor do they suffer any loss of life from the fact of our Lord saying (John 6:54), "Except you eat the flesh of the Son of Man, and drink His blood, you shall not have life in you"; because … "then every one of the faithful becomes a partaker," i.e. spiritually, "of the body and blood of the Lord, when he is made a member of Christ's body in Baptism."

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 9, reply to obj. 3, source. Three things are settled here at once: newborns are classed with those lacking reason and so are not ordinarily communicated; the Greek practice of communicating infants is met head-on and set aside (as resting, Aquinas argues, on a misread Dionysius); and the necessity-text of John 6 is answered by referring the "eating" of the infant to the spiritual partaking already had in baptism — the reading Trent would ratify against the strong Augustinian necessity thesis.

Notes

  • Aquinas is the scholastic hinge the timeline otherwise lacks: Lateran IV (1215) had bound reception to the "age of discretion" without explaining why the pre-rational infant is left outside, and Trent (1562) would define that infants are under "no necessity" of communion without spelling out the mechanism. Aquinas supplies both — the criterion (a preceding devotion the infant cannot have) and the reason it costs the child nothing (the res is already had through baptism and the Church's intention).
  • St. Antoninus of Florence (1389–1459), the Dominican archbishop, is the other scholastic authority Pius X invokes on the same point: a child is bound to confession, and so to communion, only "when a child is capable of doing wrong, that is of committing a mortal sin." Both Aquinas and Antoninus are named in Quam singulari as the "principal interpreters" who tie first communion to the dawning use of reason — the seventh year "more or less" — rather than to full doctrinal knowledge.1
  • Aquinas already appears on this wiki as a witness on the communion under one kind timeline, on concomitance (the whole Christ present under either species) — a distinct text and question from the one treated here.
  • The strong Augustinian reading of John 6:53 that Aquinas answers is weighed at Does John 6:53 make communion necessary for infants? (rated weak); whether the Western abandonment of infant communion was a legitimate development or a rupture is weighed at Was the Western lapse … a legitimate development of discipline, or a rupture with apostolic tradition? (rated plausible).
