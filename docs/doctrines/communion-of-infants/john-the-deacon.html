John the Deacon and the Roman ordines: the West keeps infant communion (c. 500–12th c.)

Dates: c. 500 (John the Deacon of Rome, fl. c. 500–520) · Wikipedia
Summary: Between Augustine (c. 412) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) the Latin West leaves a long documentary gap, and the summary timeline can look as if it leaps from the African necessity-argument straight to the medieval "age of discretion." It did not: the West kept communicating the newly-baptized — infants included — for some seven centuries more. This page gathers the concrete carriers of that survival: John the Deacon's early-sixth-century description of the Roman baptismal rite, the baptismal Ordines Romani rubric that communicates the neophytes, and the scholarly consensus that infant communion remained the general Latin practice up to the twelfth century and faded only in the twelfth and thirteenth — as an incidental casualty of withdrawing the chalice from the laity, not by a ruling about the age of reason.

Claims & evidence

John the Deacon, Letter to Senarius (c. 500–520) — the whole rite is done "even to infants"

Answering a Roman nobleman's questions about the meaning of the baptismal ceremonies, John the Deacon (a Roman cleric, sometimes identified with the later Pope John I) describes the rite of initiation as the Roman Church then administered it — the scrutinies, exorcisms, the font, the chrism, and the communion that followed. Two of his statements together make plain that infants were carried through the whole of it, communion included.

First, he insists that everything he has described is done to babies no less than to adults, even though the baby understands nothing of it:

… ista omnia etiam parvulis fiant, qui adhuc pro ipsius aetatis primordio nihil intelligunt.

"… all these things are done even to little children, who as yet, by reason of the very beginning of their age, understand nothing" (§ VII). Second, in explaining the post-baptismal cup of milk and honey he identifies the newly-baptized as those who have become partakers of the Lord's body and blood:

… ipsi qui participes fiunt corporis et sanguinis Domini, terram repromissionis accipient …

"… those very ones who become partakers of the body and blood of the Lord shall receive the land of promise" (§ XII, on the milk and honey given in the sacred chalice). Read together, the two passages attest that in the Roman rite around the year 500 the person who "becomes a partaker of the body and blood of the Lord" at baptism includes the parvulus to whom "all these things are done" — the newly-baptized infant received the Eucharist as the last step of a single initiation.1

The Ordines Romani — "and all the infants themselves communicate"

The Roman service-books turn that testimony into an explicit rubric. In the baptismal order of the Ordines Romani (the directions for the Easter-vigil baptisms, of about the sixth-to-seventh century), after the newly-baptized have been signed and confirmed the rite directs, at the communion of the Mass:

… et communicant omnes ipsi infantes.

"… and all the infants themselves communicate."2 The rubric is worth reading carefully, and one honest caution belongs with it: the Latin infantes in the baptismal ordines can mean the newly-baptized neophytes generally (all the newly-reborn were "infants" in faith), not the nursing baby specifically, and Charles Crawford flags exactly this — the ordo gives the direction "without specifying what precisely is meant by infantes." But the ambiguity does not empty the evidence: whichever sense is primary, the rubric communicates the just-baptized at once, and the same books elsewhere direct that during the pre-baptismal scrutiny Masses "let all communicate except the infants themselves" (communicent omnes praeter ipsos infantes) — because the unbaptized may not yet receive. The contrast between the two moments is decisive: those excluded before baptism precisely because they are not yet baptized are then included the moment they are. Set beside John the Deacon's "all these things are done even to little children," the liturgical scholars are, as Crawford puts it, "in no doubt as to the practice of children's Communion from the very beginning of authentically verifiable Church history."3

The West kept it to the twelfth century — and lost it with the chalice

What John the Deacon and the ordines document at the start of the medieval West held for the length of it. The neophyte's communion at baptism was the standing discipline: "immediately after baptism the neophyte shares at Mass as a full member of the Church by receiving Communion," and — as the ancient ritual books "until the thirteenth century" prescribe — children were "admitted to Communion at Baptism," receiving "usually under the species of wine." Up to the twelfth century, Crawford writes, "it is true to say that … all the faithful, even in the Latin Church, communicated under both species."4

The practice then died — but not by a decision about infants. In the eleventh and twelfth centuries a "growing scrupulosity regarding the consecrated elements," the fruit of eucharistic realism, made churchmen uneasy about a baby's handling of the sacrament; the first response was to communicate infants under the wine only, and then the same realism turned the whole laity away from the chalice. As J. D. C. Fisher puts it, "the doctrine of Realism led in time also to a growing disinclination to permit any of the laity ever to communicate from the chalice, a development which … eventually brought to an end the practice of communicating newly baptized infants." Because infants had always received under the wine, withdrawing the lay cup withdrew their communion with it. Crawford states the mechanism bluntly: "it was because the Church had been accustomed to give Communion under the form of wine to infants that the law abolishing Communion for adults under the form of wine meant that infants would no longer receive Communion! The conclusion was simple: no wine, no Communion for infants." Liturgists agree "that infant Communion fell into disuse after the twelfth century."5 The Western lapse was thus a by-product of communion under one kind, not a considered abolition — the point that matters for whether the change was a development or a rupture.

Notes

  • Why this fills the gap. The summary's Western witnesses cluster at the two ends — the ancient African practice (Cyprian, Augustine) and its medieval disappearance (Tours, Lateran IV). John the Deacon and the ordines supply the missing middle: the Latin West did not merely inherit infant communion and then drop it, but kept it as its ordinary baptismal practice for seven centuries. The survival is what makes the eventual lapse a real change of practice rather than the fading of something the West had never firmly held.
