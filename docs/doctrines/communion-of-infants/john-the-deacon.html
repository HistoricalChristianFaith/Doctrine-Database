John the Deacon and the Roman ordines on the communion of the newly baptized (c. 500–7th c.)

Dates: c. 500 (John the Deacon of Rome, fl. c. 500–520) · Wikipedia
Summary: Western witnesses from the interval between Augustine (c. 412) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): John the Deacon's early-sixth-century description of the Roman baptismal rite, the baptismal Ordines Romani rubric that communicates the neophytes, and the account of Charles Crawford and J. D. C. Fisher, who hold that infant communion remained the general Latin practice up to the twelfth century and ended with the withdrawal of the chalice from the laity.

Claims & evidence

John the Deacon, Letter to Senarius (c. 500–520) — the whole rite is done "even to infants"

Answering a Roman nobleman's questions about the meaning of the baptismal ceremonies, John the Deacon (a deacon of the Roman Church) describes the rite of initiation as the Roman Church then administered it — the scrutinies, exorcisms, the font, the chrism, and the communion that followed. Two of his statements bear on whether infants received the communion.

First, he insists that everything he has described is done to babies no less than to adults, even though the baby understands nothing of it:

… ista omnia etiam parvulis fiant, qui adhuc pro ipsius aetatis primordio nihil intelligunt.

"… all these things are done even to little children, who as yet, by reason of the very beginning of their age, understand nothing" (§ VII). Second, in explaining the post-baptismal cup of milk and honey he identifies the newly-baptized as those who have become partakers of the Lord's body and blood:

… ipsi qui participes fiunt corporis et sanguinis Domini, terram repromissionis accipient …

"… those very ones who become partakers of the body and blood of the Lord shall receive the land of promise" (§ XII, on the milk and honey given in the sacred chalice). The first statement concerns the whole rite; the second names the newly baptized as partakers of the body and blood.1

The Ordines Romani — "and all the infants themselves communicate"

A Roman baptismal ordo directs the communion of the neophytes. In the baptismal order of the Ordines Romani (the directions for the Easter-vigil baptisms, of about the sixth-to-seventh century), after the newly-baptized have been signed and confirmed the rite directs, at the communion of the Mass:

… et communicant omnes ipsi infantes.

"… and all the infants themselves communicate."2 Charles Crawford notes that the Latin infantes in the baptismal ordines can mean the newly-baptized generally, "since all would-be Christians were spoken of as 'infants' from the point of view of their newly-found faith," and that the ordo gives the direction "without specifying what precisely is meant by infantes." Herbert Thurston, in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), writes of the baptismal ordo (Mabillon's Ordo VII) that it "speaks throughout of infantes as if they alone were likely to be subjects for baptism, and the whole ceremony is modified to suit the case of infants in arms." Mabillon's Ordo VII, in the Migne text, directs that at the pre-baptismal scrutiny Masses "let all communicate except the infants themselves" (communicent omnes praeter ipsos infantes). Crawford writes that "liturgical scholars are in no doubt as to the practice of children's Communion from the very beginning of authentically verifiable Church history."3

Crawford and Fisher on the Latin practice to the twelfth century and its end

On Crawford's account the neophyte's communion at baptism was the standing discipline: "immediately after baptism the neophyte shares at Mass as a full member of the Church by receiving Communion," and — as the ancient ritual books "until the thirteenth century" prescribe — children were "admitted to Communion at Baptism," receiving "usually under the species of wine." Up to the twelfth century, Crawford writes, "it is true to say that … all the faithful, even in the Latin Church, communicated under both species."4

Crawford and Fisher connect the end of the practice with eucharistic realism and the withdrawal of the cup. On their account, in the eleventh and twelfth centuries a "growing scrupulosity regarding the consecrated elements" made churchmen uneasy about a baby's handling of the sacrament; the first response was to communicate infants under the wine only, and then the same realism turned the whole laity away from the chalice. As J. D. C. Fisher puts it, "the doctrine of Realism led in time also to a growing disinclination to permit any of the laity ever to communicate from the chalice, a development which … eventually brought to an end the practice of communicating newly baptized infants." On their account, since infants by then received under the wine only, withdrawing the lay cup withdrew their communion with it. Crawford writes: "it was because the Church had been accustomed to give Communion under the form of wine to infants that the law abolishing Communion for adults under the form of wine meant that infants would no longer receive Communion! The conclusion was simple: no wine, no Communion for infants." Crawford reports that liturgists "are agreed that infant Communion fell into disuse after the twelfth century."5 See also communion under one kind.
