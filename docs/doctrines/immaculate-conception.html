The Immaculate Conception of Mary

The Immaculate Conception is the doctrine defined by Pope Pius IX in the bull Ineffabilis Deus (8 December 1854), “that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege granted by Almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin.” The bull declares it “a doctrine revealed by God”; it is a dogma of the Catholic Church.1

The sources reported below speak to two distinct claims: (a) that Mary committed no actual sin, and (b) that she was preserved from original sin from the first instant of her conception, the content of the 1854 definition. Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church) draws the same line: “all Orthodox are agreed in believing that Our Lady was free from actual sin. But was she also free from original sin?” He writes that the Orthodox Church “has never in fact made any formal and definitive pronouncement on the matter,” and that “since 1854 the great majority of Orthodox have rejected the doctrine.”2

How the early texts bear on claim (b) is read differently by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant writers. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910), surveying the patristic evidence, concluded that “the belief in Mary’s immunity from sin in her conception was prevalent amongst the Fathers, especially those of the Greek Church,” adding that “the rhetorical character, however, of many of these and similar passages prevents us from laying too much stress on them, and interpreting them in a strictly literal sense,” and that “the Greek Fathers never formally or explicitly discussed the question of the Immaculate Conception.”3 Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, 1974) holds that Mary, “like other human beings… was born under the law of original sin,” and that “sin never could become actual in her person.”4 Philip Schaff (Protestant, The Creeds of Christendom, 1877) found, instead of a “unanimous consent” of the Fathers in its favour, “a unanimous silence, or even protest, of the Fathers against it.”5

The timeline below runs earliest first: the Syriac, Greek and Latin fathers who wrote of Mary’s purity; the Eastern feast of her conception; Anselm and Eadmer at Canterbury; the scholastic debate between those who held that Mary was sanctified after her conception (Bernard, Bonaventure, Aquinas, Capreolus, Cajetan) and those who held that she was preserved at it (William of Ware, Llull, Scotus, Auriol, Mayronis, and the Dominican Durandus); the institutional episodes of the dispute between the Franciscan and Dominican orders (John of Montson’s condemnation at Paris, the Paris oath, the Jetzer affair at Bern); the Swiss Reformers and Luther; the acts of the Council of Basel, Sixtus IV, the Council of Trent and the popes from Paul V to Clement XI; Pius IX’s consultation of the bishops and his definition; and the Eastern Orthodox response to it.

Timeline

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 361)

Ephrem the Syrian, in a stanza of his Nisibene Hymns, praises Christ and his mother together: “Thou and Thy Mother are the only ones who are totally beautiful in every respect; for in Thee, O Lord, there is no spot, and in Thy Mother no stain.”6 Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that “the Syrian Fathers never tire of extolling the sinlessness of Mary,” and, citing the Nisibene Hymns, that “to St. Ephraem she was as innocent as Eve before her fall, a virgin most estranged from every stain of sin.”7 In his Commentary on the Diatessaron, as reported by the ninth-century Syriac commentator Isho‘dad of Merv, Ephrem reads Christ’s words to Mary at Cana (John 2:4) as a reproof of her.8

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380)

Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching Christ’s nativity, says that Christ was “conceived by the Virgin, who first in body and soul was purified by the Holy Ghost” — in Greek a single participle, prokathartheisa (“prepurified”), which he repeats in his Easter oration and which recurs in later Greek writers on Mary. Christiaan Kappes (Catholic, 2014) reads the “purification” on the analogy of Christ’s being “purified” in the Jordan though sinless, as a special preparation and outpouring of grace, and holds that the Greek Fathers “in the line of the Nazianzen” never vacillated about Mary’s “all-immaculate status… from the first moment of her existence”; Craig Truglia (writing at Orthodox Christian Theology, 2020) reads the word as a purification of a womb “subject to impurity” at the conceiving of Christ; Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 2007), following Nicholas Cabasilas, takes the purification as “an addition of the gifts of grace” to a Virgin sinless in her own acts who “inherited fallen human nature,” and Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, 1974) places the Spirit’s preparation of her at the Annunciation.9 The readings are set out on the disputed-reading page.

Ambrose of Milan (c. 389)

Ambrose of Milan praises Mary as “an incorrupt virgin — but a virgin through grace, free from every stain of sin” (virgo per gratiam ab omni integra labe peccati), and hails her as uniquely “full of grace… which no other deserved.” The same Exposition of Luke says that to be immaculate “from the beginning… is impossible for human nature” and speaks of the “stain of original sin” in the line from which Christ took flesh; elsewhere he writes that Christ “alone is without sin.” Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) lists Ambrose’s description of Mary as “a virgin immune through grace from every stain of sin” among the patristic texts on “the absolute purity of Mary”; Hilda Graef (Mary: A History of Doctrine and Devotion, 1963) judged that Ambrose “never stretched his claim… as far as the Immaculate Conception.”10

Augustine of Hippo (c. 415)

Augustine, answering Pelagius’s list of the sinless, sets Mary apart: “We must except the holy Virgin Mary, concerning whom I wish to raise no question when it touches the subject of sins, out of honour to the Lord.”11

Proclus of Constantinople (c. 430)

Proclus of Constantinople, in his Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos — the sermon at the start of the Nestorian controversy — hymns Mary as “the spotless flesh of the Virgin,” “the awesome loom of the divine economy,” “the purest fleece.” Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) cites a further phrase among his patristic texts on Mary’s purity, rendering Proclus as saying she “was formed without any stain.” The homily’s imagery praises Mary’s flesh as the material from which the Word took his humanity.12

Theodotus of Ancyra (c. 431)

Theodotus of Ancyra, an anti-Nestorian theologian at the Council of Ephesus, praises Mary in his Oration on the Mother of God as “a virgin innocent, without spot… holy in body and in soul… untaught the ills of Eve,” and adds that she, “when not yet born, was consecrated to God.” Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) quotes the passage among his texts on “the absolute purity of Mary.”13

Sophronius of Jerusalem (c. 634)

Sophronius of Jerusalem, preaching the Annunciation, greets Mary as “the Immaculate,” on whom the Spirit descends “to make you purer,” filled with a grace “granted to you alone” and surpassing every saint and rank of angels; his Synodical Letter of 634, read into the acts of the Sixth Ecumenical Council, confesses her “prepurified (prokathartheisēs) in body and soul.” Kappes’s reading of the Greek “prepurified” vocabulary extends to the Fathers in Gregory’s line (see Gregory, above); Truglia quotes the Synodical Letter in arguing that Mary’s womb, “subject to impurity,” was purified at the Annunciation.14 The readings are set out with Gregory’s word on the disputed-reading page.

The Life of the Virgin — attributed to Maximus the Confessor (7th or 10th c.)

The Life of the Virgin, a full biography of Mary, calls her the “all-holy, immaculate, and most blessed Theotokos,” “ever immaculate: before birth, in birth, and after birth.” Its one passage reasoning about a conception free of “the original sin of Adam and Eve” concerns Christ, and its account of Mary’s own conception is the miracle of the barren Anne. Its translator, Stephen Shoemaker, cautions against reading later Latin Marian doctrine into it. Its authorship and date are disputed: Shoemaker assigns it to Maximus in the early seventh century; Phil Booth (2015) argues that Maximus cannot be its author and that it cannot date to the seventh century.15

The Eastern feast of Mary’s conception — Andrew of Crete and Germanus (7th–8th c.)

A feast of the conception of Mary by her mother Anne was kept in the Christian East from the seventh century; its oldest surviving witness is the liturgical Canon of Andrew of Crete. Andrew and Germanus of Constantinople hymn Mary as “undefiled… most pure” and “wholly immaculate.” Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) writes that this older feast and “the modern feast of the Immaculate Conception are not identical in their object”: originally the Church “celebrated only the Feast of the Conception of Mary, as she kept the Feast of St. John’s conception, not discussing the sinlessness,” and the feast “in the course of centuries became the Feast of the Immaculate Conception.”16

John of Damascus (c. 730)

John of Damascus, preaching on Mary’s own nativity, praises her conception and her parents: “O most all-blessed loins of Joachim, from which a wholly unblemished seed was sent forth! O renowned womb of Anna, in which… an all-holy infant grew,” a birth at which “nature did not dare to anticipate the germ of grace.” He frames the homily in terms of God’s foreknowledge and predestination of Mary: God, “having known in advance your worth… predestined you.” Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) cites the “germ of grace” line among the patristic texts on Mary’s purity, and reads the homily as extending God’s sanctifying influence to her parents, so that “the material of which she was formed, was pure and holy” — an opinion of “immaculate active generation” that, he notes, some Western authors took up; Hilda Graef discusses the homily within the Greek “all-holy” vocabulary.17 (John’s account of Joachim also gives Mary a Davidic pedigree, discussed on the genealogies of Jesus timeline.)

Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098)

Anselm of Canterbury writes in Cur Deus Homo that Mary “was of the number of those who were cleansed from their sins before” Christ’s birth. In De conceptu virginali he argues that it was fitting that the Virgin shine with “a purity than which, under God, none greater can be conceived,” and gives an account of original sin as transmitted to every descendant of Adam; his disciple Eadmer drew on the argument from fittingness (next entry).18

Eadmer of Canterbury (c. 1125)

Eadmer of Canterbury, Anselm’s disciple and biographer, wrote a treatise on the conception of Mary arguing that she was preserved at it. Taking up his master’s argument from fittingness, he reasoned that God “clearly could and willed it; if therefore he willed it, he did it” (Potuit plane et voluit; si igitur voluit, fecit), illustrating the point with a chestnut grown unharmed amid its thorns.19

Bernard of Clairvaux (c. 1140)

Bernard of Clairvaux wrote to the canons of Lyons to rebuke their newly adopted Feast of the Conception. Holiness presupposes existence, he argues, so Mary could not have been sanctified before she was conceived, nor in the conception itself; rather “she received sanctification when existing in the womb after conception, which, by excluding sin, made her birth holy, but not her conception” — sanctified in the womb like Jeremiah and John the Baptist.20

Peter Lombard (c. 1155)

Peter Lombard’s Sentences became the standard theology textbook of the medieval schools. At Book III, distinction 3, he teaches that the flesh from which Christ’s body was formed was “subject to sin” (obnoxia peccato) “like the rest of the Virgin’s flesh,” and that the Holy Spirit “wholly purged” Mary from sin and freed her from its “tinder” (fomes peccati) at Christ’s conception.21 The commentaries on the Sentences by Bonaventure, Scotus and Capreolus below treat the question of Mary’s conception at this distinction.

Bonaventure (c. 1255)

Bonaventure, in his commentary on the Sentences, holds what he calls the “more common” opinion of his day: Mary’s soul contracted original sin and was sanctified shortly after its infusion (animation), not preserved from it at conception. He judged the sanctification-after-contraction view the safer and more reasonable, and more consonant with Christ’s office as universal Redeemer.22

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Thomas Aquinas writes that Mary “was sanctified after animation,” her soul having “contracted original sin” but being “cleansed therefrom before her birth from the womb.”23 A conception wholly free of the stain, he reasoned, would be “derogatory to the dignity of Christ, by reason of His being the universal Saviour of all,” for then Mary alone would not have needed the redemption Christ won for every human being.24 How far his denial reaches is the subject of a disputed-reading page.

William of Ware (c. 1290)

William of Ware, an English Franciscan and Scotus’s master, asks in a disputed question “whether the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original sin,” and answers that since her Son “is purity itself, it was fitting that he should make his Mother as pure as he could” — and so “not merely cleanse her but preserve her from all impurity” (ab omni immunditia praeservaret); “what he could do, it was fitting that he did, and… it follows that he did so.” The slogan potuit, decuit, ergo fecit and the maxim that “the more excellent is to be attributed to Mary” are attributed to Scotus; how far Ware’s question shaped his pupil’s is discussed in the study cited.25

Ramon Llull (c. 1298)

Ramon Llull, the Majorcan lay Franciscan and philosopher, argued for Mary’s freedom from original sin at Paris in the autumn of 1298. Reasoning in the demonstrative style of his “Art,” he held that God and sin cannot be united in one and the same subject, so the mother of the incarnate Son did not contract the stain but was sanctified in the very matter from which she was formed. Luigi Gambero credits Llull with the earliest use of the phrase “Immaculate Conception”; the older reference literature, including the Catholic Encyclopedia, does not.26

John Duns Scotus (c. 1300)

John Duns Scotus answered the objection from Christ’s universal redemption. The most perfect Mediator, he argued, performs the most perfect act of mediation, and “he who prevents lest one offend, is a more perfect mediator than he who merely reconciles after the offense”; so Mary was redeemed by being preserved from ever contracting original sin — “she would have contracted original sin… unless this had been prevented by the grace of a Mediator.” The argument is known as “preservative redemption.”27

Peter Auriol (1314)

Peter Auriol, a Franciscan lector at Toulouse, wrote a monograph on the question. He defended the doctrine in a public disputation at Toulouse in 1314, finished his Tractatus de conceptione beatae Mariae Virginis on 20 December 1314, and answered his critics the next year in the Repercussorium. His case runs in the Scotist order — God could preserve Mary from contracting original sin, it was of the “highest fittingness” that he do so, and it “can be held without danger to faith… that God did in fact preserve her” — while leaving the matter open “until the Church shall have determined” it. William Duba (2000) analyses his treatment of the debitum peccati: whether Mary was ever under any debt or obligation to contract the stain, original sin construed as an inherited legal liability from which a singular exemption might be granted.28

Francis of Mayronis (c. 1320)

Francis of Mayronis, called princeps Scotistarum, is named with Auriol by Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia) as one of the doctrine’s “most fervent champions” in the generation after Scotus. In Christiaan Kappes’s account (2019), where Scotus advanced preservative redemption tentatively, Mayronis “threw Duns’ caution into the wind… arguing forcefully for Mary’s divine ‘privilege’ or exemption from original sin.” He extended Scotus’s doctrine of Christ’s absolute predestination to Mary, and read original sin through the categories of Roman law as an inherited debt on which her exemption is a juridically granted privilegium.29

Durandus of Saint-Pourçain (c. 1320)

Durandus of Saint-Pourçain, a Dominican, held that Mary was conceived without original sin, reasoning that the union of her soul with her body and the gift of sanctifying grace fell at one and the same instant, leaving no interval in which the stain could be contracted. Bogusław Kochaniewicz (2024), on whose study the attribution rests, notes that Durandus does not cite Scotus but relies on Augustine, Anselm and Richard of St-Victor, and sets him against the Dominicans of his time who denied the doctrine, among them Herveus Natalis, the order’s Master General (1318–23).30

John of Montson condemned at Paris (1387)

John of Montson, an Aragonese Dominican and Paris master, taught that Mary was conceived in original sin like all Adam’s descendants and that the contrary opinion was heretical; the Paris theology faculty condemned fourteen of his propositions, and the Bishop of Paris excommunicated any who should defend them. Montson appealed to Avignon and then fled, and the Order of Preachers was expelled from the University; according to the 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, the delegates against him, among them Pierre d’Ailly and Jean Gerson, rested their case on the university’s right to police its teachers.31

John Capreolus (c. 1430)

John Capreolus, called the “Prince of Thomists” (princeps thomistarum), defended Aquinas point by point against Scotus and Auriol in his Defensiones, concluding at Book III that “the Blessed Mary was conceived in original sin” (beata Maria fuit concepta in peccato originali), a thesis he credits to Aquinas and supports with Bernard’s letter to Lyons.32

The Council of Basel (1439)

The Council of Basel, on 17 September 1439, declared the doctrine “pious, and consonant to the Catholic faith,” and forbade preaching against it. By then the council had broken with Pope Eugene IV, who had transferred it to Ferrara; on 25 June 1439 it purported to depose him and later elected Felix V. Its sessions from this period are not counted as those of an ecumenical council in the Catholic reckoning given by Papal Encyclicals Online.33

Pope Sixtus IV (1476–1483)

Pope Sixtus IV, in Cum praeexcelsa (1476), approved the feast and its Office, calling the conception “miraculous” and defining nothing about original sin. His Grave nimis (1483) forbade either party to brand the other heretical, on the ground that the point had “not yet [been] decided by the Roman Church and the Apostolic See.”34

The University of Paris oath (1497)

In 1497 the University of Paris decreed that no one could be “admitted a member of the university, who did not swear that he would do the utmost to defend and assert the Immaculate Conception of Mary”; McClintock and Strong’s Cyclopædia traces the oath to the Franciscan–Dominican disputes. Cologne (1499), Vienna (1501) and universities from Oxford and Cambridge to Salamanca and Louvain adopted similar oaths.35

The Jetzer affair at Bern (1507–1509)

In the Dominican convent at Bern, apparitions of the Virgin were staged before a lay brother, Hans Jetzer, in which “Mary” declared she had been conceived in original sin. After trials that relied on confessions extracted under torture, four Dominicans were burned at Bern on 31 May 1509. Who devised the apparitions is disputed. The Bern verdict held the four friars responsible; Nikolaus Paulus (1897) presented Jetzer as the sole guilty party and the friars as victims of “judicial murder,” and Rudolf Steck (1904), publishing the trial records, also called the verdict judicial murder, laying responsibility on the pope.36 Kathrin Utz Tremp (2022) is reported as reading Jetzer as the dupe rather than the mastermind ⚠ unverified.37

Cardinal Cajetan (1515)

Cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio), commentator on Aquinas and Master General of the Dominicans, addressed a treatise to Leo X while the Fifth Lateran Council sat. He framed the question as whether God “preserved” Mary from original sin or “cleansed” her from it, ranked the cleansing as “probable” and the preservation as “tolerable,” called the strong form — a total exemption that would place Mary outside those for whom Christ died — “manifest heresy,” and dismissed the Council of Basel’s decree as the act of a schism, “the synagogue of Satan.” The treatise drew Dominican replies, among them Ambrosius Catharinus’s.38 Bogusław Kochaniewicz (2024) reads Cajetan, who “for the first time accepted the immaculistic interpretation” as tolerable, as “a turning point in the Franciscan-Dominican debate, although he remained faithful to the traditional position of the Dominicans.”39

The Swiss Reformers — Zwingli and Bullinger (c. 1525–1566)

Huldrych Zwingli calls Mary “the immaculate and perpetual Virgin,” says she alone conceived her Son without transmitting “the human weakness that we all bring with us from Adam,” and (in his Commentary on True and False Religion) that she bore not “the smallest trace of a stain”; his successor Heinrich Bullinger hymns her most holy body as “the most pure bed and temple of the Holy Spirit.” Zwingli described original sin as an inherited disease or defect, “not a sin, because sin implies guilt.” Gottfried Locher writes that there is “no trace in Zwingli of the doctrine” of the immaculate conception, and in a footnote that “Mary was, at least at the moment of conception, wholly pure and free from original sin”; James Swan (Reformed, 2013) asks which conception, Mary’s or Christ’s, is meant.40 See the Swiss Reformers detail page.

Martin Luther (c. 1527–1540)

Martin Luther, in a 1527 sermon, called it “a sweet and pious belief that the infusion of Mary’s soul was effected without original sin… thus from the first moment she began to live she was free from all sin,” placing the exemption at ensoulment (his “second conception”) and offering it as a pious opinion, “no article of faith”; the passage is absent from the reprints after 1527. In his 1540 Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ he wrote that “in conception the flesh and blood of Mary were entirely purged, so that nothing of sin remained,” a conception James Swan (Reformed, 2007) reads as Christ’s; and in sermons of 1539–40, as Julius Köstlin reports them, he taught that Mary, like every believer, was conceived and born in sin and made free from it by faith.41

The Council of Trent (1546)

When the Council of Trent defined the doctrine of original sin, it “declare[d], that it is not its intention to include in this decree, where original sin is treated of, the blessed and immaculate Virgin Mary,” and ordered Sixtus IV’s constitutions observed.42 What the clause implies about the doctrine is the subject of a disputed-reading page.

Pope Alexander VII — Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum (1661)

Pope Alexander VII’s bull Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum describes the long-standing devotion of the faithful who believe that Mary’s soul, “from the first instant of its creation and infusion into her body, was preserved immune by a special grace and privilege of God from the stain of original sin, in view of the merits of her Son, Jesus Christ, the Redeemer of our human race”; it renews the constitutions of Sixtus IV (with Paul V and Gregory XV) issued “in favor of the opinion” that she was preserved from original sin, and bars further attack on that opinion. It does not use the formula of the 1854 definition, “a doctrine revealed by God.”43

Papal acts from Paul V to Clement XI (1616–1708)

Between Trent and the nineteenth century a series of papal acts restricted public denial of the doctrine and extended its feast. Paul V renewed his predecessors’ ban on either party calling the other heretical (1616), then in the Apostolic Constitution Sanctissimus (12 September 1617) forbade anyone to teach publicly that Mary was conceived in original sin; Gregory XV extended the silence in 1622 to writings and sermons “even private” (in scriptis et sermonibus etiam privatis) until the Holy See should define the question, and abolished the practice of naming the feast the “Sanctification” rather than the “Conception” of Mary; Innocent XII raised the feast to a double of the second class with an octave for the universal Church (1693); and Clement XI’s Commissi Nobis Divinitus (6 December 1708) made 8 December a holy day of obligation. Ineffabilis Deus (1854) renews Paul V’s and Gregory XV’s constitutions by name.44

Pope Pius IX consults the bishops — Ubi primum (1849)

Pius IX, in exile at Gaeta after the Roman revolution of 1848, issued the encyclical Ubi primum on 2 February 1849, asking every bishop how great the devotion of their clergy and people to the Immaculate Conception was and whether they desired its definition, and — “especially” — “what you yourselves, in your wise judgment, think and desire on this matter.” Of the roughly six hundred bishops who replied, some 536 favoured the definition; four or five (among them the Archbishop of Paris) opposed it, and about three dozen, mostly from Protestant countries, judged the moment inopportune.45

Pope Pius IX — Ineffabilis Deus (1854)

Pope Pius IX defined the doctrine on 8 December 1854, declaring “that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege granted by Almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin, is a doctrine revealed by God.” Among its grounds the bull reads Mary as the woman set at enmity with the serpent in Genesis 3:15, a reading traced on the Mary as the New Eve timeline.46

The Eastern Orthodox response (modern)

The Eastern Orthodox Church venerates Mary as Panagia (“all-holy”), and its modern theologians have declined the 1854 definition. Vladimir Lossky (1974) writes that “the Orthodox Church does not admit the idea that the Holy Virgin was thus exempted from the lot of the rest of fallen humanity.” Kallistos Ware writes that “most Orthodox theologians reject the idea of ‘original guilt,’” holding that people “automatically inherit Adam’s corruption and mortality, but not his guilt.”47 Ware gives the Orthodox reasons for rejecting the doctrine as that it is “unnecessary,” that “as defined by the Roman Catholic Church, it implies a false understanding of original sin,” and that “it seems to separate Mary from the rest of the descendants of Adam”; he adds that “from the Orthodox point of view… the whole question belongs to the realm of theological opinion.”48

Disputed readings

How particular texts and acts in the history of the doctrine have been read.

The Greek vocabulary of Mary’s purity

Two readings concern Greek words for Mary’s purity: one participle of Gregory of Nazianzus, and the body of titles and feast-hymns that use the same vocabulary.

  • Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception? — on what the prefix pro- marks and when the purification takes place.49
  • Does the Eastern “all-holy” vocabulary (Panagia) testify to the Immaculate Conception? — on the titles and hymns of the feast of Mary’s conception, read by the rule that the law of prayer is the law of belief.50

Other readings

  • Did Thomas Aquinas deny the Immaculate Conception? — on what his texts deny, and how Thomists have related them to the later definition.51
  • How does the first-millennium record bear on the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception? — on how the first-millennium record and the scholastic denials bear on the 1854 definition.52
  • Does Trent’s exemption clause tacitly affirm the Immaculate Conception? — on the 1546 clause leaving Mary out of the decree on original sin.53
