Who Was Nebuchadnezzar?

Nebuchadnezzar II is the Babylonian king of the Hebrew Bible who burned Jerusalem and carried Judah into exile, and in the book of Daniel the subject of a narrative of madness and recovery: driven out to eat grass like an ox, and brought back confessing the God of heaven. In Daniel he dreams of the great image whose head of gold is his own kingdom, and on its interpretation confesses that Daniel’s God is “a God of gods, and a Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets” (Daniel 2:47). When the three young men walk unburnt in his furnace he blesses their God and forbids any word against him (Daniel 3:28–29). In chapter 4, for his pride, he is “driven from men… and did eat grass as oxen,” his hair grown like eagles’ feathers and his nails like birds’ claws, until “seven times” pass over him (Daniel 4:33).

The account ends with his recovery. At the end of the days he lifts up his eyes to heaven, his reason returns, and he blesses “the most High”: “all whose works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase” (Daniel 4:34–37). Commentators have read that confession differently: Hippolytus, Ephrem and Theodoret as a genuine repentance, Jerome as words spoken “like a worldling” (see their entries below). A Dead Sea Scroll, the Prayer of Nabonidus, tells a similar story of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, and how the two texts are related is disputed (see Disputed readings).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Nebuchadnezzar but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out by earliest extant source. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the narrative of Daniel and the prophetic “my servant Nebuchadnezzar” of Jeremiah; the Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus; the divergent Old Greek edition of Daniel 4; Josephus with his citations of the Babylonian historian Berossus; the Fathers who commented on the madness and the doxology (Hippolytus, Ephrem, Chrysostom, Jerome, Theodoret); and the Babylonian Talmud, which calls him “that wicked person” and says an angel cut short his praise. Other Gentile figures who confess the God of Israel have their own timelines: the Syrian commander Naaman (“there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel”) and the Queen of Sheba.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Daniel 1–4; Jeremiah)

In the Hebrew Bible he appears chiefly in Jeremiah and Daniel. In Jeremiah he is the rod of God’s anger, and God’s own servant: “I will send and take… Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant”; “I have given all these lands unto the hand of Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant” (Jeremiah 25:9; 27:6). The prophet who saw him burn Jerusalem names him the instrument by whom the LORD works his purpose.1

In Daniel the same king dreams the great image whose head of gold is his own reign, and confesses at its unveiling that Daniel’s God is “a God of gods, and a Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets”; he sees the three young men walk unburnt and blesses “the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego.” In chapter 4, after his boast over Babylon, a voice from heaven drives him out: “they shall make thee to eat grass as oxen… and seven times shall pass over thee, till thou know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men.” And so it is — “he was driven from men, and did eat grass as oxen… till his hairs were grown like eagles’ feathers, and his nails like birds’ claws.”2

The account ends with his recovery. “At the end of the days I Nebuchadnezzar lifted up mine eyes unto heaven, and mine understanding returned unto me, and I blessed the most High… whose dominion is an everlasting dominion”; and the closing verse reads: “Now I Nebuchadnezzar praise and extol and honour the King of heaven, all whose works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase.” The commentators below read this confession in different ways.3

The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242, c. 2nd–1st century BC) — a seven-year affliction told of Nabonidus

Among the Qumran scrolls is a short Aramaic text with a story like that of Daniel 4, told of a different king. In the Prayer of Nabonidus it is Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, who “was stricken with an evil disease by the decree of God in Teiman” (Tayma, in Arabia) “for seven years,” who prayed to “gods of silver and gold, bronze, iron, wood, stone and clay,” and who was pardoned and healed after “a diviner — who was a Jew of the Exiles” told him to “give honour and greatness to the name of the God Most High.” Both texts have a Babylonian king, a seven-year affliction, a Jewish adviser and a turn to the Most High; the Babylonian sources place Nabonidus at Tayma for about a decade. Collins and Newsom read Daniel 4 as “ultimately based on traditions about Nabonidus”; Steinmann argues that the Prayer depends on Daniel; see Disputed readings.4,5 Full detail »

The Old Greek and Theodotion (the Greek editions of Daniel 4, c. 100 BC and later) — the confession expanded

Daniel survives in two ancient Greek versions, and in chapter 4 they part company. For most of the book the Old Greek and the later Theodotion track a Semitic text near the Hebrew-Aramaic; but chapters 4–6 are the exception — there the Old Greek is a substantially different edition. Its best witness, papyrus 967, sets chapters 7–8 between 4 and 5; the king’s confession and decree stand, in a much expanded form, at the end of the chapter; and the Old Greek “expands on the hubris of the king,” adding notes of temple-desecration absent from the other texts. The Greek evidence thus preserves the king’s confession in two different forms.6 Full detail »

Josephus (Antiquities 10 and Against Apion 1, c. AD 93) — the great builder

Josephus retells Daniel at length — the dreams, the image, the furnace — and reports the second dream briefly: the king was to “feed among the wild beasts” and live “in the desert for seven years,” after which “he prayed to God that he might recover his Kingdom, and he returned to it.” He then adds the external record: quoting the Babylonian priest-historian Berossus, he reports how Nebuchadnezzar walled Babylon three times about and raised “a pensile paradise” for his Median queen, and notes that “Megasthenes… endeavors to show that this king exceeded Hercules in fortitude.” He gives the reign its length — “when king Nebuchadnezzar had reigned forty-three years, he ended his life” — and calls him “an active man, and more fortunate than the kings that were before him.”7 Full detail »

Hippolytus of Rome (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 204) — the beast-king who confessed and was restored

The earliest surviving Christian commentary on Daniel reads the madness and the recovery as a single lesson in humbling. Hippolytus paints the fall in full: “no longer did the great king and ruler live as a man… but being alone in the desert he ate grass and herbs until his heart was changed and became as a beast… his hair grew as a lion’s and his nails grew large as a bird’s.” And he reads the outcome as genuine repentance: Nebuchadnezzar, “having humbled himself and recognized that he is a man lying under the authority of God… after begging the Lord, obtained from him a good heart, and again his kingdom and glory were reestablished.” The whole ordeal was “an education for the king,” so that he could say at last, “all who walk in arrogance are able to be humbled.”8 Full detail »

Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 363) — the ordeal that led him to repentance

Ephrem frames the whole story as God’s patient work toward a genuine turn. The king’s boast on the palace terrace comes just when “God was patient and had shown magnanimity by endeavoring to lead him to repentance”; in his reading the madness is the means by which God reaches him. And Ephrem reads the doxology as real restored piety: “‘My reason returned to me’… I blessed the Most High, who does what he wills” — the God who rules “not only the affairs of human beings… but also those of the spiritual powers.” Through the punishment God “showed that he gives back to each one his properties with justice… and humiliates those who walk in arrogance.” For Ephrem the confession is the point the ordeal was for.9 Full detail »

John Chrysostom, with Cyprian and Augustine (homilies and treatises, c. AD 253–420) — the sins redeemed by almsgiving

A whole stream of the reception fastened not on the madness but on Daniel’s counsel before it — “break off thy sins by almsdeeds, and thine iniquities by showing mercy to the poor” (Daniel 4:27) — and made Nebuchadnezzar an example of mercy to the poor cancelling sin. Chrysostom marvels that “after countless sins, after so many transgressions, he is promised that he will be reconciled” if he will give alms, and that “nothing is so strong and powerful to extinguish the fire of our sins as almsgiving.” Cyprian had already drawn the same lesson — the king “suffered the misfortunes… which he might have escaped… if he had redeemed his sins by almsgiving” — and Augustine preached it too (“you redeemed your sins with alms deeds”). In these writers the passage serves as an exhortation to almsgiving.10 Full detail »

Jerome (Commentary on Daniel and the letters, c. AD 407) — the doxology of a worldling, and the servant of God

Jerome comments on the figure in Latin, and he doubts the king’s piety. He defends the historicity of the madness and restoration — defending it against Origenist readers who used it to argue that the devil too, “through repentance… restored to favor,” will one day be reinstated over the angels — but he doubts the doxology is devout: Nebuchadnezzar “expresses [it] like a worldling,” even seeming “to impugn God’s justice, on the ground that he had suffered unmerited punishment.” On Jeremiah he comments on the pagan king’s being called God’s servant: “Miserable must those Israelites have been compared with whom Nebuchadnezzar was called God’s servant… if we wish to be lifted up, we must first prostrate ourselves.”11 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 433) — the reason lost, and grace restored

Theodoret reads the chapter as ending in a genuine restoration. For him the madness is a justice fitted to the sin: “in his wish to snatch heaven he was driven also from earth”; stripped of reason he “like a wild animal… kept frequenting uninhabited places, filling his stomach with the food of brute beasts.” And he ascribes the recovery to grace: “thanks to divine grace he was recognized as the person who previously had administered the mighty kingdom.” Theodoret puts the closing praise in the king’s own grateful mouth — “I sing the praises of the one who to my advantage brought on me one condition and the other… capable of humbling those behaving haughtily.”12 Full detail »

The rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted by c. 500) — the blasphemer and the interrupted song

The Babylonian Talmud makes Nebuchadnezzar a blasphemer: “the disciple in corruption of Nimrod the wicked,” the “king of Babylon” of Isaiah 14 who said “I will ascend above the heights… I will be like the Most High,” and was answered “yet you shall be brought down to the netherworld.” The same corpus preserves a tradition about his praise: had an angel not intervened, his doxology would have rivalled the Psalter. “If an angel had not come and struck him on his mouth… he would have sought to overshadow all the songs and praises that David recited in the book of Psalms.” In Sanhedrin 92b the passage that calls him “that wicked person” also records that his praise was cut short by the angel.13 Full detail »

Disputed readings

How the origin of the Daniel 4 account of the king’s madness has been read, in the light of the Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus.
