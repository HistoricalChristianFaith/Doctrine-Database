Who Was Nebuchadnezzar?

Nebuchadnezzar II is the great pagan world-emperor of the Hebrew Bible — the Babylonian king who burned Jerusalem and carried Judah into exile, and whom the book of Daniel makes the stage for a drama no other Gentile king receives: the master of the earth driven mad, sent out to eat grass like an ox, and brought back confessing the God of heaven. He enters Daniel as the terror before whom the wise men tremble, dreams the dream of the great image whose head of gold is his own kingdom, and on its interpretation confesses that Daniel’s God is “a God of gods, and a Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets” (Daniel 2:47). When the three young men walk unburnt in his furnace he blesses their God and forbids any word against him (Daniel 3:28–29) — yet each confession is followed by relapse, until the last and strangest judgment falls: for his pride he is “driven from men… and did eat grass as oxen,” his hair grown like eagles’ feathers and his nails like birds’ claws, until “seven times” pass over him (Daniel 4:33).

The arc closes in the one passage that made Nebuchadnezzar a figure the church could not leave alone. At the end of the days he lifts up his eyes to heaven, his reason returns, and he blesses “the most High” in words as high as any psalm: “all whose works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase” (Daniel 4:34–37). The destroyer of the Temple ends as a doxologist of Israel’s God. Around that reversal a long argument grew — was the confession a true conversion, or the cry of a broken tyrant? — and around the madness itself a still-live historical crux: a Dead Sea Scroll, the Prayer of Nabonidus, tells almost the same story of a different Babylonian king, and many read Daniel 4 as the reworking of a tradition that once belonged to Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Nebuchadnezzar but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the narrative of Daniel and the prophetic “my servant Nebuchadnezzar” of Jeremiah; the Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus; the divergent Old Greek edition of Daniel 4; Josephus with his citations of the Babylonian historian Berossus; the Fathers who read the madness and the doxology (Hippolytus, Ephrem, Chrysostom, Jerome, Theodoret); and the rabbis, who made him the arch-blasphemer whose praise nearly outsang David’s until an angel struck him silent. The genuinely contested questions — does Daniel 4 rework a Nabonidus tradition? is the ox-madness a real malady? was the final confession a true conversion? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. Nebuchadnezzar belongs to a recurring biblical type — the Gentile ruler who is brought to confess Israel’s God — whose nearest kin are the Syrian commander Naaman (“there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel”) and the Queen of Sheba, though he is the darkest of them: the one who must first be unmade before he will bless.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Daniel 1–4; Jeremiah)

Everything the later tradition made of Nebuchadnezzar grows from two books read together. In Jeremiah he is the rod of God’s anger, and — astonishingly for a pagan destroyer of the Temple — God’s own servant: “I will send and take… Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant”; “I have given all these lands unto the hand of Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant” (Jeremiah 25:9; 27:6). The prophet who watched him burn Jerusalem still names him the instrument by whom the LORD works his purpose — a title the Fathers would find as scandalous as it is exact.1

In Daniel the same king becomes a stage for the God he does not know. He dreams the great image whose head of gold is his own reign, and confesses at its unveiling that Daniel’s God is “a God of gods, and a Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets”; he sees the three young men walk unburnt and blesses “the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego” — yet after each confession he hardens again, until the last judgment falls on his pride. A voice from heaven drives him out: “they shall make thee to eat grass as oxen… and seven times shall pass over thee, till thou know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men.” And so it is — “he was driven from men, and did eat grass as oxen… till his hairs were grown like eagles’ feathers, and his nails like birds’ claws.”2

Then the reversal that made him a figure the church could not forget. “At the end of the days I Nebuchadnezzar lifted up mine eyes unto heaven, and mine understanding returned unto me, and I blessed the most High… whose dominion is an everlasting dominion”; and the closing verse of the account gives him a confession as high as any in the Psalter: “Now I Nebuchadnezzar praise and extol and honour the King of heaven, all whose works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase.” The destroyer of the Temple ends as a doxologist of Israel’s God — whether as a true convert or a broken tyrant is the question the tradition would never quite close.3

The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242, c. 2nd–1st century BC) — the same story told of a different king

Among the Qumran scrolls is a short Aramaic text that reads like Daniel 4 with the names changed. In the Prayer of Nabonidus it is Nabonidus — the last king of Babylon, not Nebuchadnezzar — who “was stricken with an evil disease by the decree of God in Teiman” (Tayma, in Arabia) “for seven years,” who prayed uselessly to “gods of silver and gold, bronze, iron, wood, stone and clay,” and who was pardoned and healed after “a diviner — who was a Jew of the Exiles” told him to “give honour and greatness to the name of the God Most High.” The parallels are dense — a Babylonian king, a seven-year affliction, a Jewish adviser, a turn from idols to the Most High — and the setting fits history: Nabonidus really did spend a decade at Tayma. Many scholars since its 1956 publication have read Daniel 4 as the reworking of a tradition that first attached to Nabonidus and was transferred to the more famous Nebuchadnezzar. How far that dependence reaches — a shared tradition, or a direct literary source — is weighed on its own argument page.4 Full detail »

The Old Greek and Theodotion (the Greek editions of Daniel 4, c. 100 BC and later) — the confession expanded

Daniel survives in two ancient Greek versions, and in chapter 4 they part company. For most of the book the Old Greek and the later Theodotion track a Semitic text near the Hebrew-Aramaic; but chapters 4–6 are the exception — there the Old Greek is a substantially different edition. Its best witness, papyrus 967, even sets chapters 7–8 between 4 and 5; the king’s confession and decree are moved to a greatly expanded form at the end of the chapter; and the Old Greek “expands on the hubris of the king,” adding notes of temple-desecration absent from the other texts. Where the tradition would later ask whether Nebuchadnezzar’s doxology was sincere, the oldest Greek edition had already heightened it — the first sign of how far a scribe would go in shaping the proud king’s repentance.5 Full detail »

Josephus (Antiquities 10 and Against Apion 1, c. AD 93) — the great builder, and a silence

The earliest datable treatment of Nebuchadnezzar after the Bible is also the most revealing for what it leaves out. Josephus retells Daniel at length — the dreams, the image, the furnace — but passes over the seven-year madness in silence, moving straight from the king’s dream-interpretations to his death and his monuments. What he adds instead is the external record: quoting the Babylonian priest-historian Berossus, he reports how Nebuchadnezzar walled Babylon three times about and raised “a pensile paradise” for his Median queen, and notes that “Megasthenes… endeavors to show that this king exceeded Hercules in fortitude.” He gives the reign its length — “when king Nebuchadnezzar had reigned forty-three years, he ended his life” — and calls him “an active man, and more fortunate than the kings that were before him.” A Jewish historian writing for Rome kept the builder and quietly dropped the humiliation.6 Full detail »

Hippolytus of Rome (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 204) — the beast-king who confessed and was restored

The earliest surviving Christian commentary on Daniel reads the madness and the recovery as a single lesson in humbling. Hippolytus paints the fall in full: “no longer did the great king and ruler live as a man… but being alone in the desert he ate grass and herbs until his heart was changed and became as a beast… his hair grew as a lion’s and his nails grew large as a bird’s.” And he reads the outcome as genuine repentance: Nebuchadnezzar, “having humbled himself and recognized that he is a man lying under the authority of God… after begging the Lord, obtained from him a good heart, and again his kingdom and glory were reestablished.” The whole ordeal was “an education for the king,” so that he could say at last, “all who walk in arrogance are able to be humbled.”7 Full detail »

Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 363) — the ordeal that led him to repentance

Ephrem frames the whole story as God’s patient work toward a genuine turn. The king’s boast on the palace terrace comes just when “God was patient and had shown magnanimity by endeavoring to lead him to repentance”; the madness is the mercy that finally reaches him. And Ephrem reads the doxology as real restored piety: “‘My reason returned to me’… I blessed the Most High, who does what he wills” — the God who rules “not only the affairs of human beings… but also those of the spiritual powers.” Through the punishment God “showed that he gives back to each one his properties with justice… and humiliates those who walk in arrogance.” For Ephrem the confession is the point the ordeal was for.8 Full detail »

John Chrysostom, with Cyprian and Augustine (homilies and treatises, c. AD 253–420) — the sins redeemed by almsgiving

A whole stream of the reception fastened not on the madness but on Daniel’s counsel before it — “break off thy sins by almsdeeds, and thine iniquities by showing mercy to the poor” (Daniel 4:27) — and made Nebuchadnezzar the great proof that mercy to the poor cancels sin. Chrysostom marvels that “after countless sins, after so many transgressions, he is promised that he will be reconciled” if he will give alms, and that “nothing is so strong and powerful to extinguish the fire of our sins as almsgiving.” Cyprian had already drawn the same lesson — the king “suffered the misfortunes… which he might have escaped… if he had redeemed his sins by almsgiving” — and Augustine preached it too (“you redeemed your sins with alms deeds”). Here Nebuchadnezzar is less a convert than a case study in the power of penance.9 Full detail »

Jerome (Commentary on Daniel and the letters, c. AD 407) — the doxology of a worldling, and the servant of God

Jerome carries the whole figure into Latin, and he is the tradition’s sharpest skeptic of the king’s piety. He grants the historicity of the madness and restoration — defending it against Origenist readers who used it to argue that even the devil, “through repentance… restored to favor,” will one day be reinstated over the angels — but he doubts the doxology is devout: Nebuchadnezzar “expresses [it] like a worldling,” even seeming “to impugn God’s justice, on the ground that he had suffered unmerited punishment.” On Jeremiah he presses the other scandal — the pagan called God’s servant: “Miserable must those Israelites have been compared with whom Nebuchadnezzar was called God’s servant… if we wish to be lifted up, we must first prostrate ourselves.”10 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (Commentary on Daniel, c. AD 433) — the reason lost, and grace restored

Theodoret gives the fullest Greek reading of the chapter and lands, against Jerome, on a genuine restoration. The madness is a tailored justice: “in his wish to snatch heaven he was driven also from earth”; stripped of reason he “like a wild animal… kept frequenting uninhabited places, filling his stomach with the food of brute beasts.” And the recovery is grace: “thanks to divine grace he was recognized as the person who previously had administered the mighty kingdom.” Theodoret puts the closing praise in the king’s own grateful mouth — “I sing the praises of the one who to my advantage brought on me one condition and the other… capable of humbling those behaving haughtily.”11 Full detail »

The rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted by c. 500) — the blasphemer whose song outsang David

Jewish tradition ran the other way from the Christian and made Nebuchadnezzar the arch-blasphemer: the disciple “of Nimrod the wicked,” the “king of Babylon” of Isaiah 14 who said “I will ascend above the heights… I will be like the Most High,” and was answered “yet thou shalt be brought down to hell.” And yet the very same tradition preserves the most extravagant estimate of his praise anywhere in the sources: had an angel not intervened, his doxology would have eclipsed the Psalter. “If an angel had not come and struck him on his mouth… he would have sought to overshadow all the songs and praises that David recited in the book of Psalms.” The blasphemer and the singer are held together — his praise is genuine, and dangerous, and must be silenced.12 Full detail »

Arguments

Related arguments — the madness of Daniel 4

Two questions cluster around the strangest thing the Bible says of Nebuchadnezzar — that for “seven times” he was driven out to graze like an ox (Daniel 4:25–33). One asks where the story came from: a Dead Sea Scroll tells almost the same tale of a different king. The other asks what the story is: a clinical malady, a legend, or a theological sign. They share the same proof-text and are best read together.

Does Daniel 4 rework a tradition about Nabonidus? (The Prayer of Nabonidus, 4Q242) — plausible that it is rooted in a Nabonidus tradition / contested that it directly reworks 4Q242

The Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus tells of Nabonidus — Babylon’s last king — struck with a seven-year affliction at Tayma and healed after a Jewish diviner bade him honour the Most High. That Daniel 4 is ultimately rooted in a Nabonidus tradition is well-grounded and close to the critical consensus: the seven years, the Jewish adviser, the turn from idols, and above all the Tayma setting (Nabonidus’s real decade in Arabia, and his son Belshazzar’s regency behind Daniel 5) converge too tightly for coincidence — plausible, arguably sound. But the sharper claim that Daniel directly reworks this fragment is contested: the king is different (Nabonidus vs. Nebuchadnezzar), the affliction is different (a skin inflammation vs. ox-madness), and the adviser is anonymous, not Daniel — so Collins and others read the two as “different developments of a common tradition,” not one copied from the other, while a minority (Steinmann) argues the scroll depends on Daniel. The single conjectural word “like a beast,” restored into a gap in the scroll, is the weakest link in the case for direct dependence.

The ox-madness of Daniel 4 — a real malady, a legend, or a theological sign? — contested

Daniel 4 describes a king who believes himself an ox, grazes on grass, and grows hair and nails like an animal (Daniel 4:33). That this pictures a real psychiatric condition — the rare presentation of clinical lycanthropy sometimes called boanthropy — is plausible: the syndrome is medically attested, and the symptoms map onto it closely, so the account is not fantastical. But that the specific episode is a historically attested event in Nebuchadnezzar’s reign is contested: no contemporary Babylonian record and no independent source reports it (Josephus omits it entirely), the defence rests on an argument from a documented lull in his late inscriptions, and the narrative is plainly shaped for a theological end — the humbling of pride until “the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men.” The malady is real as a type; the historicity of the episode is under-attested, and the medical, legendary, and theological readings are not mutually exclusive.

Was Nebuchadnezzar’s final confession a true conversion? — contested

The doxology of Daniel 4:34–37 is the highest praise any Gentile king gives in the Hebrew Bible — “those that walk in pride he is able to abase.” Whether it marks a genuine conversion, as it does for Naaman, is contested, and the sources split cleanly. For a real turn: the confession is public and self-abasing, gives God the glory at the king’s own expense, and was read as sincere repentance by Hippolytus, Ephrem, and Theodoret — while the rabbis went further still, making his praise nearly outshine David’s. Against it: Nebuchadnezzar had already confessed twice (Daniel 2 and 3) and relapsed each time; the confession lacks the exclusivity that marks Naaman’s (no vow to sacrifice to the LORD alone); and Jerome and, later, Calvin heard in it not faith but the cry of a broken man — a doxology spoken “like a worldling,” even seeming to “impugn God’s justice.” Unlike Naaman’s exclusive vow, the text gives no decisive tell either way — which is why the question stays open: an acknowledgment of God’s sovereignty beyond doubt, a saving conversion beyond proof.
