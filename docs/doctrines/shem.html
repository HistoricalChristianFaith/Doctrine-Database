Who Was Shem?

Shem is the son of Noah named in Noah’s blessing. When Noah wakes from his wine and pronounces on his three sons, Ham’s son Canaan is cursed, and Shem is blessed in the name of the LORD: “Blessed be the LORD God of Shem; and Canaan shall be his servant. God shall enlarge Japheth, and he shall dwell in the tents of Shem” (Genesis 9:26–27). Of the three brothers’ blessings only Shem’s invokes the LORD. The Jewish and Christian sources set out below read it as marking out a line: of true worship, of the covenant, and, in the Christian sources, of the Messiah. Before the blessing, Shem and Japheth, refusing to look, had walked backward to cover their father’s nakedness (Genesis 9:23).

Genesis traces its main genealogy through Shem. He is “the father of all the children of Eber” (Genesis 10:21), the Eber from whom Josephus (below) derives the name of the Hebrews (ʿibrî); his sons head one branch of the Table of Nations (Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, Aram); and the genealogy of Genesis 11:10–26 runs Shem → Arphaxad → … → Terah → Abram. He is also the longest-lived of the fathers listed after the Flood — “Shem lived after he begat Arphaxad five hundred years,” six hundred in all (Genesis 11:10–11). On the received Hebrew numbers his lifespan overlaps Abraham’s, and rabbinic and Syriac sources below have Shem still living, teaching, and reigning in the patriarchs’ day. His name also gave modern linguistics the label “Semitic,” coined from Sem.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Shem but a record of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, earliest extant source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Genesis portrait, the Book of Jubilees that gives Shem the navel of the earth, the Septuagint and the two chronologies of Genesis 11 (on one of which Shem lives into Abraham’s day, and on the other does not), the New Testament genealogy that runs Jesus’ line through “Sem,” Josephus, the rabbinic sources that identify Shem with Melchizedek and make him master of a house of study, Ephrem, who also takes Shem for Melchizedek, and the Fathers who read “the tents of Shem” of the Church the Gentiles enter (Justin, Jerome, Augustine, Bede). Two disputed questions — whether Melchizedek was Shem, and which text of the Genesis 11 chronology is original — are treated on the Melchizedek and Flood-date timelines (see Disputed readings).

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 5; 9–11) — the blessed son and the line to Abraham

Everything later sources say of Shem starts from Genesis. He is the first-named of Noah’s three sons (Genesis 5:32), and with Japheth he covers his father’s nakedness “backward… and they saw not their father’s nakedness” (Genesis 9:23). Of the two blessings Noah then pronounces, only Shem’s names the LORD: “Blessed be the LORD God of Shem… God shall enlarge Japheth, and he shall dwell in the tents of Shem” (Genesis 9:26–27). Ham is not blessed, and Japheth’s blessing ends “he shall dwell in the tents of Shem” — the “he” read of Japheth by Justin, Jerome, Augustine and Bede, and of the Divine Presence in Genesis Rabbah (below). The later sources take the blessing as marking out Shem’s line.1

From there the genealogy runs through Shem. He is “the father of all the children of Eber, the brother of Japheth the elder” (Genesis 10:21), and his sons — “Elam, and Asshur, and Arphaxad, and Lud, and Aram” — head the branch of the Table of Nations from which Israel descends. The generations of Shem then run in a straight line to the patriarch: Shem → Arphaxad → Salah → Eber → … → Terah → Abram. And Shem outlives the fathers after him: “Shem was an hundred years old, and begat Arphaxad two years after the flood: and Shem lived after he begat Arphaxad five hundred years” — six hundred years in all, longer than any patriarch after him, and (on the received Hebrew numbers) long enough to be still living in Abraham’s day.2

The Book of Jubilees (2nd century BC) — Shem given the navel of the earth

The earliest post-biblical expansion on this timeline turns Shem’s blessing into an inheritance of land. Retelling the flood-story, Jubilees has Noah divide the earth among his sons by lot, and “there came forth on the writing as Shem’s lot the middle of the earth which he should take as an inheritance for himself and for his sons for the generations of eternity.” That middle is the holy centre: Noah “knew that the Garden of Eden is the holy of holies, and the dwelling of the Lord, and Mount Sinai the centre of the desert, and Mount Zion—the centre of the navel of the earth,” all falling within Shem’s portion, “a blessed and spacious land.” And the Genesis blessing is recalled with it: “Blessed be the Lord God of Shem, and may the Lord dwell in the dwelling of Shem.” Where Genesis blesses Shem, Jubilees places Zion and Eden in his inheritance.3

The Septuagint and the two chronologies (3rd–2nd century BC) — does a living Shem reach Abraham?

The genealogy of Genesis 11 comes down in two forms, and the difference bears directly on Shem. On the received Hebrew (Masoretic) numbers the postdiluvian fathers beget early, so that Shem’s six hundred years reach down past Abraham — the calculation behind the Hebrews’ view, reported by Jerome, that Shem lived until Isaac, and Ephrem’s, that he lived to the time of Jacob and Esau (both below, in connection with Melchizedek). On the Septuagint numbers each begetting-age is about a century higher and an extra generation (Kainan) is inserted, stretching the span from Shem to Abram by roughly eight centuries — on which reckoning Shem dies long before Abram is born. Which numbers are original is a text-critical question set out on the Flood-date timeline; its bearing on the Melchizedek question is set out on that interpretation page.4

The New Testament (Luke’s Gospel, 1st century AD) — the line of Christ runs through “Sem”

The one New Testament notice of Shem is a link in a chain: Luke’s genealogy carries the descent of Jesus back past Abraham and Noah — “which was the son of Sala, which was the son of Cainan, which was the son of Arphaxad, which was the son of Sem, which was the son of Noe” (Luke 3:35–36). The evangelist runs Jesus’ pedigree through Shem’s line, which the Fathers below connect with the blessing of “the LORD God of Shem.” (The verse also carries the Septuagint’s extra generation, “Cainan,” the datum behind the chronology fork above.)5

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 1.6.4, c. AD 93) — Shem’s sons and the name of the Hebrews

Writing the biblical history for a Greco-Roman readership, Josephus turns the Table of Nations into an ethnography. “Shem, the third son of Noah, had five sons, who inhabited the land that began at Euphrates, and reached to the Indian Ocean”: Elam fathered the Persians, Asshur the Assyrians (“the most fortunate nation”), Arphaxad “the Arphaxadites, who are now called Chaldeans,” Aram the Syrians, Laud the Lydians. And he gives the etymology that links Shem’s line with the Jewish people: “Sala was the son of Arphaxad; and his son was Heber, from whom they originally called the Jews Hebrews.” From Shem’s line Josephus then passes directly to Abraham, “the tenth from Noah.” In Josephus, Shem is the ancestor of the peoples from the Euphrates to the Indian Ocean and, through Heber, of the Hebrews.6

Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 139, c. AD 155) — the two blessed peoples, and the Gentiles in Shem’s tents

Justin’s is the earliest Christian reading of Shem on this timeline. He finds in Noah’s blessing “another mystery… accomplished and predicted”: that Shem’s descendants would hold Canaan’s land, and that Japheth’s would in turn “dwell in the houses of Shem.” He reads it redemptively — “as two peoples were blessed, those from Shem, and those from Japheth… so Christ has come” to bless both, calling all men “whether bond or free” into the inheritance. In Justin’s reading the nations (Japheth) come into the blessing first given to Shem.7

The rabbinic tradition (Targum, Talmud, and Genesis Rabbah; traditions c. AD 200–500) — Shem the priest-king and the first teacher

Rabbinic sources give Shem a much larger role than Genesis does. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan identifies him with the priest-king of Genesis 14:18: “Malka Zadika — who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem — came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine”, and the Talmud assumes it — God “wanted the priesthood to emerge from Shem” (Nedarim 32b; the Steinsaltz translation on Sefaria glosses the Melchizedek of the next sentence as “traditionally identified as Shem”), a strand deployed there, on Psalm 110:4, to have Melchizedek forfeit the priesthood to Abram. Whether Melchizedek was Shem is treated as a disputed reading on the Melchizedek timeline (see Disputed readings).8

Beside it stands the tradition of “the tents of Shem” as a house of study. The rabbis read Jacob “a plain man, dwelling in tents” as sitting in “two tents; the study hall of Shem, and the study hall of Ever” (Genesis Rabbah 63:10). And on Noah’s own words Genesis Rabbah reads the phrase two ways: the Divine Presence “rested only in the tents of Shem,” and, in Bar Kappara’s reading, “The words of the Torah will be said in the Greek language in the tents of Shem” (Greece being reckoned to Japheth). In these sources Shem is priest, king, and master of a house of study.9

Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Genesis XI, c. AD 370) — a Church Father takes Shem for Melchizedek

The identification of Shem with the priest-king Melchizedek appears in Christian exegesis in Ephrem the Syrian. Jerome (below) reports the equation as the Hebrews’ without deciding for it; Ephrem states it outright in his Commentary on Genesis: “This Melchizedek is Shem, who became a king due to his greatness; he was the head of fourteen nations. In addition, he was a priest. He received this from Noah, his father, through the rights of succession.” He leans on Shem’s longevity — “because of the length of Melchizedek’s life extended to the time of Jacob and Esau… he was Shem” — the same six-hundred-year lifespan the Genesis genealogy gives him, here reaching not just Abraham but his grandsons; and he pictures Shem settled at Salem as a “partition” between the sons of Ham and his own line, guarding it from idolatry. Whether Melchizedek was Shem is treated as a disputed reading on the Melchizedek timeline (see Disputed readings).10

Jerome (Hebrew Questions on Genesis; Letter 73, c. AD 391–398) — the tents of Shem are “about us,” and the Hebrews’ Shem–Melchizedek reported

Jerome treats Shem in two ways. On Genesis 9:27 he reads the tents of Shem of the Church: “From Shem come the Hebrews, and from Japheth, the people of the nations… what he says, ‘And may he dwell in the tents of Shem,’ is prophesying about us, who are engaged in the teaching and knowledge of the Scriptures, with Israel being cast out.” On the Melchizedek question he reports the Hebrews’ view: “They say that this man is Shem, the son of Noah, and by reckoning up the years of his life they show that he lived until Isaac” (Hebrew Questions on Genesis 14:18). In Letter 73 he sets out two answers side by side: the Greek commentators’ view, from Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius and others, that Melchizedek was “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem,” and then “the opinion of the Hebrews,” that he was Shem, with their chronology in full. He then leaves the choice to his correspondent: “It was my responsibility to cite (or repeat) witnesses; it is your responsibility to judge the credibility of the witness.”11

Augustine (City of God 16, c. AD 420) — Shem “named,” of whom Christ was born

In the City of God Augustine passes from the flood to Abraham through Shem. He reads the name itself: “Shem, of whom Christ was born in the flesh, means ‘named.’ And what is of greater name than Christ?” The enlargement of Japheth he reads of the nations coming into the Church: “Is it not also in the houses of Christ, that is, in the churches, that the ‘enlargement’ of the nations dwells? For Japheth means ‘enlargement’… now that Japheth already dwells in the habitations of Shem.” And he states the rule that governs the whole genealogy: “It is necessary… to preserve the series of generations descending from Shem, for the sake of exhibiting the city of God after the flood… revert to the patriarch Shem and recapitulate the generations from him to Abraham.” In Augustine’s account the line of Shem carries the city of God from the flood to Abraham.12

The Venerable Bede (Commentary on Genesis, c. AD 720) — “Sem, the son of blessing”

Bede’s Commentary on Genesis reads Shem as the Church of the circumcision and Japheth as the Church of the nations: “In Shem, the firstborn son of Noah, we have indicated the primitive Church, gathered from the Israelite people, and in Japheth… the election of the Gentiles which followed.” “Japheth dwells in the tents of Shem because in the faith of the patriarchs and prophets… the Church sojourns on earth.” He traces Shem’s piety through the line: his progeny “specifically followed his faith and piety, which through Heber descended to Abraham and the Hebrew people,” keeping itself “immune from the conspiracy of the arrogant work” at Babel. And he calls Shem by an epithet: “fittingly Sem, the son of blessing… whose lineage would… reach to Abraham.”13

Modern scholarship — Shem the eponym of the Semites

In the eighteenth century Shem’s name entered the study of languages. When comparative philology grouped Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, and Babylonian as one family, that family was named after Shem: according to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, the term “Semitic” was coined from Sem in 1781 by August Ludwig von Schlözer, in Eichhorn’s Repertorium für biblische und morgenländische Literatur. The choice rested on Genesis 10, which descends from Shem the peoples who spoke those languages. So the name of a figure of the primeval genealogy became a category of linguistics and ethnography — “Semitic,” “Semite,” and, by derivation, “anti-Semitism,” all formed from the name of Noah’s son.14

Disputed readings

Two disputed questions about Shem are treated on interpretation pages of the timelines where they arise.

  • Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? — the identification made in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, the Babylonian Talmud and Ephrem, and reported by Jerome.
  • Does the Samaritan Pentateuch preserve the original chronology of Genesis 5 and 11? — one of the text-critical questions about Genesis 11 on which the overlap of Shem’s and Abraham’s lifetimes depends (the reckonings themselves are set out on the Flood-date timeline).
