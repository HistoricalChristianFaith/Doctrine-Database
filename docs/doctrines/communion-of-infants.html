The communion of infants (and the age of first communion)

For much of the early Church the newly baptized received the Eucharist at once — infants and nursing children included. Baptism, communion, and (in the East) chrismation were a single initiation, and the baby carried from the font was carried to the table. Yet the Latin West would later set infant communion aside, and the question of when a child should first receive the sacrament became a discipline in its own right — settled, in the end, not at the cradle but at the "age of reason." This page traces how the practice arose, how the East kept it and the West let it lapse, and how the age of first communion came to be fixed where it now stands.

The arc has a recognizable shape:

  • Ancient infant communion — from the third century the church communicated infants and small children, in the West typically under the consecrated wine alone (a drop given by the deacon or from the spoon, since a baby cannot swallow the bread), witnessed by Cyprian, the Apostolic Constitutions, and Augustine.
  • The necessity argument — in the Pelagian controversy Augustine made infant communion a matter of necessity, pressing "except ye eat the flesh … and drink the blood … ye have no life in you" (John 6:53) to argue that infants too must feed on Christ to have life — the high-water mark of the practice's theology, and the reading Trent would later decline.
  • The Eastern retention — the Eastern churches never stopped: they communicate baptized infants to this day, under the consecrated wine, by the communion-spoon (the labis), the sacrament of initiation kept whole from font to chalice.
  • The Western lapse — over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, as the Latin Church withdrew the chalice from the laity, infant communion — which depended on the cup — fell away with it, until children were no longer brought to the table until they could reason.
  • The "years of discretion" — the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) bound every believer "of either sex" to receive at least at Easter once come to the age of discretion, fixing communion to those old enough to discern the sacrament and formalizing the exclusion of the very young.
  • Trent's non-necessity ruling — the Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562) defined that little children who have not the use of reason are under no necessity of sacramental communion, and anathematized the contrary — answering the Augustinian-African reading of John 6:53 head-on.
  • The age of reason fixed — Pope Pius X's decree Quam singulari (1910) set first communion at the age of discretion, "about the seventh year," against a drift that had delayed it to twelve or fourteen — presenting the change as a restoration of ancient access, not an innovation.
  • The codified bookend — the two current codes of the Catholic communion legislate opposite answers: the Latin 1983 Code of Canon Law (c. 913) makes first communion wait on the age of reason, while the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990, cc. 697, 710) keeps the ancient order in which the baptized infant is communicated at once — the East/West split still standing in law.

Timeline

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

The earliest concrete witness to infant communion comes from Carthage. In On the Lapsed Cyprian tells of a baby girl, carried by her nurse to the pagan sacrifices during the persecution, who was afterwards brought to the Christian assembly: "when the deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child … turned away its face … and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup" (On the Lapsed 25).1 The passing detail — that an infant took her "turn" at the chalice "as the rest received it" — shows infant communion as the settled, unremarked practice of the mid-third-century African church, and shows its characteristic form: the infant received under the consecrated wine, given by the deacon, not the bread.

The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380)

The fullest early order of service, the Syrian compilation known as the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380), lists children among the ranks brought to communion in order. Book VIII directs that after the clergy come "the women," then "the children," then "all the people in order," each receiving the Body from the bishop and the Cup from the deacon (Constitutions VIII.13).2 The rubric places the young among the communicants as a matter of course — direct liturgical evidence that the fourth-century Eastern church communicated children as part of the assembly, the practice the East would never abandon.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 412)

With Augustine infant communion becomes not merely a custom but a necessity. Arguing against the Pelagians that infants too need Christ's saving grace, he presses the Bread-of-Life discourse: "let us … hear what the Lord says … 'Except ye eat my flesh and drink my blood, ye shall have no life in you.'" To the objection that Christ addressed adults who could "hear and understand," Augustine answers that "unless all are embraced in the statement, that without the body and the blood of the Son of man men cannot have life, it is to no purpose that even the ages of infancy" are included — infants must feed on Christ (On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins I.20–27).3 He grounds the practice in the very speech of his people: "the Christians of Carthage have an excellent name for the sacraments, when they say that baptism is nothing else than 'salvation,' and the sacrament of the body of Christ nothing else than 'life,'" from "that primitive … and apostolic tradition" that "without baptism and partaking of the supper of the Lord it is impossible for any man to attain … everlasting life" (I.34).4 This necessity reading — John 6:53 applied to infants — is the high-water mark of the practice's theology, and the very reading Trent would answer by defining infants under no necessity of communion.

John the Deacon and the Roman ordines (c. 500)

Between Augustine and the medieval "age of discretion" the Latin West did not abandon infant communion — it kept it as ordinary practice for seven more centuries, a survival this timeline would otherwise leap. Around the year 500 John the Deacon, describing the Roman baptismal rite, insists that "all these things are done even to little children, who as yet … understand nothing" (ista omnia etiam parvulis fiant), and names the newly-baptized as "those who become partakers of the body and blood of the Lord" — the infant carried through the whole initiation, communion included.5 The Roman service-books make it an explicit rubric: at the Easter-vigil baptisms, after the neophytes are signed and confirmed, "and all the infants themselves communicate" (et communicant omnes ipsi infantes).6 This remained the general Latin practice up to the twelfth century — when a growing scrupulosity about the consecrated elements withdrew the chalice from the laity, and infant communion, which had always been given under the wine, fell away with it. The Western lapse was thus an incidental casualty of communion under one kind, not a considered ruling that infants must first reach the age of reason.7

The Eastern retention (the infant and the spoon) (692 onward)

What the West would let lapse, the East never surrendered. The Byzantine rite kept the communion of infants as part of the single initiation — baptism, chrismation, and Eucharist given together — and re-engineered its delivery for the very young by the same instrument it adopted for all the laity: the consecrated Bread placed in the chalice and administered with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), from which a baptized infant receives a drop of the consecrated wine.8 To this day the Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches communicate baptized infants at every Liturgy — the standing counter-case that the Western delay of first communion was a Latin development, not the church's universal mind.

The Council of Tours (813)

Between Augustine and Lateran IV the Latin West leaves a long documentary gap, into which the Council of Tours (813) — one of the Carolingian reform councils under Charlemagne — is often set as the first Western restriction of children's communion. The actual canon is narrower than that reputation. Its canon 19 tells priests, having finished Mass and communicated, "not to give the Body of the Lord indiscriminately to boys or to any other persons standing by" (pueris aut aliis quibuslibet personis adstantibus corpus Domini indiscrete non tribuant), lest any "entangled in graver sins" should "take damnation upon themselves rather than a remedy," on the Pauline ground that a man must "examine himself" before he eats and drinks (1 Corinthians 11:27–28).9 This is a rule about worthy reception and the manner of distribution — not a categorical bar on pre-rational infants, and with no "danger of death" exception (the categorical form, softened only by a death clause, is a later reading imported by the reference literature that made this council famous). Its worthiness logic — self-examination as the filter on who receives — is nonetheless the same rationale that would later underwrite the "age of discretion," so the canon marks the Carolingian tightening of eucharistic discipline within which the Western lapse of infant communion would unfold, rather than a formal abolition of it.

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

In the Latin West the practice fell away over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, in step with the withdrawal of the chalice from the laity: since infants had always been communicated under the consecrated wine, the loss of the lay cup carried infant communion away with it. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) fixed the new discipline. Its canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, bound "all the faithful of either sex, after they have reached the age of discretion," to confess yearly and to "reverently receive the sacrament of the Eucharist at least at Easter" — tying reception to the capacity to discern the sacrament, and so formalizing the exclusion of the very young.10 The "age of discretion" it named — long taken as around seven, but in practice often deferred much later — became the frame within which the whole later question of first communion would be argued.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

The rationale Lateran IV had only presupposed was supplied by Thomas Aquinas. Unlike baptism, he argues, the Eucharist is not necessary for a baptized child's salvation: "from the fact of children being baptized, they are destined by the Church to the Eucharist; and just as they believe through the Church's faith, so they desire the Eucharist through the Church's intention, and, as a result, receive its reality" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 73, a. 3). The sacrament is therefore not to be given to those who have never had the use of reason, "because in no way has there been any preceding devotion towards the sacrament"; and "the same reason holds good of newly born children," who suffer no "loss of life" from want of it, since John 6's eating is had spiritually by the infant "when he is made a member of Christ's body in Baptism" (ST III, q. 80, a. 9).11 This is the scholastic hinge between the "age of discretion" and Trent's non-necessity ruling: it answers the Augustinian reading of John 6:53 and grounds the Western reservation of the Eucharist to the age of reason — the same authority (with St. Antoninus of Florence) that Pius X's Quam singulari would later cite to fix first communion at the seventh year.

The Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562)

The Council of Trent, in the same twenty-first session (16 July 1562) that defined communion under one kind, turned to "the Communion of Little Children" and settled the necessity question against the Augustinian reading. It taught "that little children, who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion of the Eucharist: forasmuch as, having been regenerated by the laver of baptism, and being incorporated with Christ, they cannot, at that age, lose the grace which they have already acquired" (ch. IV), and its fourth canon anathematized any who "saith, that the communion of the Eucharist is necessary for little children, before they have arrived at years of discretion."12 Reading the very verse (John 6:53) that Augustine had pressed, Trent declined to require infant communion — the definitive Catholic answer, and the reason the strong necessity form of that argument is judged weak on the sibling timeline.

Pope Pius X, Quam singulari (1910)

The age of first communion was fixed in its modern form by Pope Pius X. His decree Quam singulari (8 August 1910) opened with the memory of the ancient practice — "the Catholic Church … took care even from the beginning to bring the little ones to Christ through Eucharistic Communion, which was administered even to nursing infants," under the wine "to remove the danger that infants might eject the Consecrated Host" — noting that "this practice later died out in the Latin Church."13 Against a widespread drift that had delayed first communion to twelve or fourteen, it decreed that "the age of discretion, both for Confession and for Holy Communion, is the time when a child begins to reason, that is about the seventh year, more or less," and that "a full and perfect knowledge of Christian doctrine is not necessary" for first communion.14 Pius X presented this not as a new discipline but as a restoration of the church's ancient and universal law of early access — the Latin West returning, as far as it could within the frame of the age of reason, toward the childhood communion it had once shared with the East.

The modern codes: Latin and Eastern canon law (1983 / 1990)

What the timeline narrates as history is, today, a codified legal fact: the two halves of the Catholic communion legislate opposite answers on infant communion. The Latin Church's 1983 Code of Canon Law makes first communion wait on the age of reason — canon 913 §1 requires that children "have sufficient knowledge and careful preparation so that they understand the mystery of Christ according to their capacity and are able to receive the body of Christ with faith and devotion," while §2 admits a child in danger of death only "if they can distinguish the body of Christ from ordinary food."15 The Eastern Catholic Churches' 1990 Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches keeps the ancient order: canon 697 makes the Eucharist the completion of initiation, "administered after baptism and chrismation with holy myron as soon as possible," and canon 710 provides expressly for "the participation of infants in the Divine Eucharist," deferring to "the liturgical books of each Church sui iuris" — books that, on the Byzantine pattern, communicate baptized infants.16 The two codes are the clean legislative bookend of the whole arc: within one church, Latin law fixes first communion at the age of reason while Eastern law preserves the infant at the chalice — the East/West split this timeline follows, still standing in current law.

Arguments

  • Does John 6:53 make communion necessary for infants? (the Augustinian–African necessity thesis) — weak (the practice is ancient and fitting, but the strict necessity outruns the text and was answered by Trent)
  • Was infant communion apostolic and universal from the beginning, or an early-but-local custom? — weak (the practice is genuinely ancient and became near-universal in the East, but the proponents' stronger claim — apostolic in origin, universal from the start — outruns the evidence: the attestation opens only at Cyprian c. 251, with no NT or sub-apostolic witness, and even friendly sources concede the West "was not so general")
  • Was the Western lapse of infant communion a legitimate development of discipline, or a rupture with apostolic tradition? — plausible (the development reading holds on the doctrinal core — no necessity was violated, no command disobeyed — but the practice set aside was ancient and near-universal and the East retained it on principle, so a real break with ancient practice survives even where a rupture with doctrine does not)
