The communion of infants (and the age of first communion)

The earliest extant attestation of an infant receiving the Eucharist is Cyprian of Carthage's account of c. 251, and fourth- to seventh-century church orders in East and West list children and the newly baptized among the communicants. The Orthodox churches communicate baptized infants to the present. In the Latin West the practice lapsed in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and the age at which a child should first receive became a question of discipline in its own right, set in current Latin law at the age of reason. This page records the witnesses to the practice, its Eastern retention and Western lapse, and the fixing of the age of first communion.

The record falls into these stages:

  • Early infant communion — third- to fifth-century witnesses (Cyprian, the Apostolic Constitutions, Augustine) place infants and children among the communicants; in the West infants received the consecrated wine, given by the deacon, which Quam singulari (1910) explains as a way "to remove the danger that infants might eject the Consecrated Host."
  • The necessity argument — in the Pelagian controversy Augustine argued from "except ye eat the flesh … and drink the blood … ye have no life in you" (John 6:53) that infants too must receive the Eucharist to have life.
  • The Eastern retention — the Orthodox churches communicate baptized infants to this day, giving baptism, chrismation and the Eucharist together and administering the consecrated wine by the communion-spoon (the labis); among the Eastern Catholic Churches the practice is kept or being restored unevenly.
  • The Western lapse — over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries infant communion ceased in the Latin Church; Charles Crawford and J. D. C. Fisher connect its end with the withdrawal of the chalice from the laity.
  • The "years of discretion" — the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) bound every believer "of either sex" to receive at least at Easter once come to the age of discretion.
  • Trent on necessity — the Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562) defined that little children who have not the use of reason are under no necessity of sacramental communion, and anathematized the contrary.
  • The age of reason — the decree Quam singulari (1910), under Pope Pius X, set first communion at the age of discretion, "about the seventh year," where local customs had placed it at ten, twelve or fourteen.
  • The current codes — the Latin 1983 Code of Canon Law (c. 913) makes first communion depend on the child's knowledge and preparation, while the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990, cc. 697, 710) provides for the Eucharist "as soon as possible" after baptism and chrismation, infants included.

Timeline

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

The earliest extant attestation of infant communion comes from Carthage. In On the Lapsed Cyprian tells of a baby girl, carried by her nurse to the pagan sacrifices during the persecution, who was afterwards brought to the Christian assembly: "when the deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child … turned away its face … and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup" (On the Lapsed 25).1 In the account the infant receives the consecrated wine, offered by the deacon, in her "turn" "as the rest received it." Whether notices like this attest a practice received from the apostles or a custom of the third-century church is disputed; see Was the communion of infants a practice received from the apostles, or a custom that arose after them?

The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380)

The Syrian church order known as the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) lists children among the ranks brought to communion in order. Book VIII directs that after the clergy come "the women," then "the children," then "all the people in order," each receiving the Body from the bishop and the Cup from the deacon (Constitutions VIII.13).2 The rubric gives the children a fixed place in the order of communicants.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 412)

Arguing against the Pelagians that infants too need Christ's saving grace, Augustine presses the Bread-of-Life discourse: "let us … hear what the Lord says … Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you shall have no life in you." To the objection that Christ addressed those "able to hear and to understand, which of course infants cannot do," Augustine answers that "unless all are embraced in the statement, that without the body and the blood of the Son of man men cannot have life, it is to no purpose that even the elder age is solicitous of it" — a chapter headed, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation, "Infants Must Feed on Christ" (On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins I.26–27).3 He appeals to the usage of his own region: "the Christians of Carthage have an excellent name for the sacraments, when they say that baptism is nothing else than 'salvation,' and the sacrament of the body of Christ nothing else than 'life,'" from "that primitive … and apostolic tradition" that "without baptism and partaking of the supper of the Lord it is impossible for any man to attain … everlasting life" (I.34).4

John the Deacon and the Roman ordines (c. 500)

Around the year 500 John the Deacon, describing the Roman baptismal rite, writes that "all these things are done even to little children, who as yet … understand nothing" (ista omnia etiam parvulis fiant), and speaks of the newly baptized as "those who become partakers of the body and blood of the Lord."5 A Roman ordo for the Easter-vigil baptisms directs, after the neophytes are signed and confirmed, "and all the infants themselves communicate" (et communicant omnes ipsi infantes); Charles Crawford, who quotes it, notes that the ordo does not specify "what precisely is meant by 'infantes.'"6 Crawford (1970) and J. D. C. Fisher (1965) hold that infant communion remained the general Latin practice up to the twelfth century and ended as the chalice was withdrawn from the laity; in Crawford's words, "no wine, no Communion for infants."7

The Eastern retention (the infant and the spoon) (692 onward)

The Byzantine rite kept the communion of infants within a single initiation — baptism, chrismation and the Eucharist given together — and administers it with the instrument it uses for all the laity: the consecrated Bread is placed in the chalice and given with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), from which a baptized infant receives a drop of the consecrated wine. The Byzantine liturgical writers Nicholas Cabasilas and Symeon of Thessalonica describe Communion as the completion of that initiation.8 The Orthodox churches communicate baptized infants at the Divine Liturgy today. Among the Eastern Catholic Churches the practice is kept or being restored unevenly: Mark M. Morozowich (Catholic, 2008) writes that "the effects of Latinization continue in Eastern Catholic parishes today" and that "chief among the varied contemporary difficulties remains the question of whether or not to reintroduce the practice of infant reception of Holy Communion."9

The Council of Tours (813)

The Council of Tours (813), one of the Carolingian reform councils held under Charlemagne, is described in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1908) as "prohibiting the reception by young children of Communion unless they were in danger of death." Its canon 19 tells priests, having finished Mass and communicated, "not to give the Body of the Lord indiscriminately to boys or to any other persons standing by" (pueris aut aliis quibuslibet personis adstantibus corpus Domini indiscrete non tribuant), lest any "entangled in graver sins" should "take damnation upon themselves rather than a remedy," on the Pauline ground that a man must "examine himself" before he eats and drinks (1 Corinthians 11:27–28).10 Werminghoff's edition of canon 19 has no danger-of-death clause; Morrisroe cites Zaccaria, Bibliotheca ritualis II, 161, for his description.

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215), in its canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, bound "all the faithful of either sex, after they have reached the age of discretion," to confess yearly and to "reverently receive the sacrament of the Eucharist at least at Easter," tying the annual obligation to the age of discretion.11 The canon does not mention infants.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Thomas Aquinas argues that, unlike baptism, the Eucharist is not necessary for a baptized child's salvation: "from the fact of children being baptized, they are destined by the Church to the Eucharist; and just as they believe through the Church's faith, so they desire the Eucharist through the Church's intention, and, as a result, receive its reality" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 73, a. 3). The sacrament is therefore not to be given to those who have never had the use of reason, "because in no way has there been any preceding devotion towards the sacrament"; and "the same reason holds good of newly born children," who suffer no "loss of life" from want of it, since John 6's eating is had spiritually by the infant "when he is made a member of Christ's body in Baptism" (ST III, q. 80, a. 9).12 In the same article he notes that "certain Greeks do the contrary."

The Council of Trent (Session 21, 1562)

The Council of Trent, in the same twenty-first session (16 July 1562) that defined communion under one kind, turned to "the Communion of Little Children." It taught "that little children, who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion of the Eucharist: forasmuch as, having been regenerated by the laver of baptism, and being incorporated with Christ, they cannot, at that age, lose the grace which they have already acquired" (ch. IV), adding that "not therefore, however, is antiquity to be condemned, if, in some places, it, at one time, observed that custom"; and its fourth canon anathematized any who "saith, that the communion of the Eucharist is necessary for little children, before they have arrived at years of discretion."13

Pope Pius X, Quam singulari (1910)

The decree Quam singulari (8 August 1910), issued under Pope Pius X, opened with the memory of the ancient practice — "the Catholic Church … took care even from the beginning to bring the little ones to Christ through Eucharistic Communion, which was administered even to nursing infants," under the wine "to remove the danger that infants might eject the Consecrated Host" — noting that "this practice later died out in the Latin Church."14 Observing that local customs had placed first communion "at ten years or twelve, and in places fourteen years or even more," and citing Thomas Aquinas and St. Antoninus of Florence among others, it decreed that "the age of discretion, both for Confession and for Holy Communion, is the time when a child begins to reason, that is about the seventh year, more or less," and that "a full and perfect knowledge of Christian doctrine is not necessary" for first communion.15 The Catholic Encyclopedia (J. McNicholas, 1911), citing a letter of Pius X to the Archbishop of Cologne (31 December 1910), describes the decree as "not a decree inaugurating a new discipline, but one restoring the ancient and universal law of the Church, wherever it has not been observed."16

The modern codes: Latin and Eastern canon law (1983 / 1990)

The two current codes of the Catholic Church legislate differently on infant communion. The Latin Church's 1983 Code of Canon Law makes first communion depend on the child's knowledge and preparation — canon 913 §1 requires that children "have sufficient knowledge and careful preparation so that they understand the mystery of Christ according to their capacity and are able to receive the body of Christ with faith and devotion," while §2 admits a child in danger of death "if they can distinguish the body of Christ from ordinary food."17 The Eastern Catholic Churches' 1990 Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches provides in canon 697 that the Eucharist completes initiation and is "administered after baptism and chrismation with holy myron as soon as possible," and in canon 710 for "the participation of infants in the Divine Eucharist," deferring to "the liturgical books of each Church sui iuris"; in the Byzantine books the baptized infant is communicated.18
