How did Bartholomew die: crucified, flayed, or beheaded?

Disputed reading · on Bartholomew · witness: the Golden Legend

Related readings: Where did Bartholomew preach: in “India”, in Armenia, or in both?

The passage

…commanded that the apostle should be beaten with staves, and that he should be flayed quick, and so it was done.

There be divers opinions of the manner of his passion. For the blessed Dorotheus saith that he was crucified, and saith also: Bartholomew preached to men of India, and delivered to them the gospel after Matthew in their proper tongue. He died in Alban, a city of great Armenia, crucified the head downward. S. Theoderus saith that he was flayed, and it is read in many books that he was beheaded only.

— Jacobus de Voragine, the Golden Legend (c. 1260), tr. William Caxton, life of St Bartholomew.1

The question

The New Testament does not report Bartholomew’s death. The later sources that do report it give it in different forms: crucifixion head downward, flaying, beheading, or a combination of these, most often at Albanopolis (Alban, Allanum) in Armenia. The question is which account, if any, preserves the manner of his death. The Greek passion names the king whose brother ordered it Polymius; Wikipedia’s article on Bartholomew states that no Arsacid king of Armenia of that name is recorded, and that other versions name the king Agrippa (identified with Tigranes VI) or Sanatruk.2

Crucified head downward, and buried in Armenia

In this reading Bartholomew died by crucifixion, head downward, and was buried at Allanum or Alban in Armenia.

Held by: Pseudo-Hippolytus (On the Twelve Apostles 6, date uncertain); Dorotheus, as quoted by Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260).3

The apostle-list that goes under the name of Hippolytus gives the earliest extant notice of how Bartholomew died, in a single sentence that joins his mission to his end: he “preached to the Indians, to whom he also gave the Gospel according to Matthew, and was crucified with his head downward, and was buried in Allanum, a town of the great Armenia.” Dorotheus, as Jacobus quotes him, gives the same death and the same country: “He died in Alban, a city of great Armenia, crucified the head downward.” Some of the related apostle-lists keep the crucifixion. In this reading the apostle-list tradition, the earliest extant layer to speak of his death, remembers Bartholomew as crucified head downward, and gives a named town in Greater Armenia for his grave; and Jacobus, when he sets out the place of Bartholomew’s death, follows Dorotheus.4

Flayed alive at the order of Astyages

In this reading Bartholomew was beaten and flayed alive at the order of Astyages, brother of the king he had converted.

Held by: Theoderus, as quoted by Jacobus de Voragine; Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260), who combines the flaying with crucifixion and beheading.5

The apocryphal Martyrdom of Bartholomew sets his passion at the court of King Polymius and his brother Astyages, and a strand of the apostle-lists reports that he was flayed and then beheaded. The Golden Legend tells the passion in full: after Bartholomew converts King Polemius and his idol is thrown down, the king’s brother Astyages “commanded that the apostle should be beaten with staves, and that he should be flayed quick, and so it was done,” and the Christians took away the body and buried it. Jacobus cites Theoderus for the flaying and joins it to the other accounts: “some say that he was crucified and was taken down ere he died, and for to have greater torment he was flayed and at the last beheaded.” J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reports the same tradition, that Bartholomew was “flayed alive and crucified, head downward, by order of Astyages, for having converted his brother, Polymius, King of Armenia.” Fenlon adds: “On account of this latter legend, he is often represented in art (e.g. in Michelangelo’s Last Judgment) as flayed and holding in his hand his own skin.” Marco d’Agrate’s St Bartholomew Flayed (1562) in Milan Cathedral shows him wrapped in his own skin.6

The manner of his death is not known from early testimony

In this reading no source close to Bartholomew’s lifetime reports his death, and the later accounts do not settle how he died.

Held by: J. F. Fenlon (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015).7

Fenlon writes: “No mention of St. Bartholomew occurs in ecclesiastical literature before Eusebius.” Of the manner of his death, “said to have occurred at Albanopolis in Armenia,” he writes that it “is equally uncertain; according to some, he was beheaded, according to others, flayed alive and crucified, head downward.” McDowell, assessing the martyrdom accounts of each of the Twelve, places the evidence for Bartholomew’s among the least secure ⚠ unverified. The sources that name a manner of death are of uncertain date and depend on one another (⚠ unverified), and they give crucifixion, flaying and beheading; the Golden Legend records the same range of reports (“there be divers opinions of the manner of his passion”) before combining them. In this reading what the sources attest is the church’s memory of Bartholomew as a martyr of Armenia, commemorated in several forms, while the manner of his death is not established by early testimony.8
