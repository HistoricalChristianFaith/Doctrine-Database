Where did Bartholomew preach: in “India”, in Armenia, or in both?

Disputed reading · on Bartholomew · witness: Pantaenus (in Eusebius)

Related readings: How did Bartholomew die: crucified, flayed, or beheaded?

See also: the same report of Pantaenus bears on Was Matthew’s Gospel first written in Hebrew? · Did Thomas evangelize India?

The passage

…Bartholomew, one of the apostles, had preached to them, and left with them the writing of Matthew in the Hebrew language, which they had preserved till that time.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 5.10, 3 (reporting Pantaenus).1

The question

Eusebius reports that Pantaenus of Alexandria, about 180, was sent “as far as India” and found there a Hebrew Matthew left by Bartholomew; Pseudo-Hippolytus’ On the Twelve Apostles has Bartholomew preach “to the Indians” and be buried “in Allanum, a town of the great Armenia”; and the Armenian Apostolic Church honours him, with Thaddaeus, as its patron saint. The writers below differ on where Pantaenus’s “India” lay and on whether Armenia was the field of Bartholomew’s preaching or the place of his death. A further proposal, reported without names in the Nasrani.net survey, is that Pantaenus heard the Thomas Christians’ “Mar Thoma” as “Bar Tolmai” (Bartholomew).2

A mission to India itself, on its western coast

In this reading the “India” of Pantaenus’s report is the Indian subcontinent, and Bartholomew’s field was the Konkan coast around Kalyān.

Held by: A. C. Perumalil SJ (The Apostles in India); G. M. Moraes (A History of Christianity in India AD 52–1542), reading the “India” of Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History 5.10, c. 325) and Jerome (On Illustrious Men 36, late 4th c.) as the subcontinent.3

Eusebius and Jerome both report that Pantaenus found in India a Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew which the Christians there said Bartholomew had preached and left with them, and Jerome adds that Pantaenus brought it back to Alexandria. A Greek strand locates the mission more closely: Pseudo-Sophronius (7th c.) has Bartholomew preach to “the Indians who are called Happy,” and the Greek tradition sends him to “India Felix.” Perumalil and Moraes read this as the region named after the ancient port of Kalyān, near Bombay, whose name means “felix” or “happy,” and which they take to be the Kalliana that Cosmas Indicopleustes visited in the sixth century. Perumalil reads the “India Citerior” of the Hieronymian Martyrology as western India and the “India” of the Passio Bartholomaei as the Maratha country, and holds that the Konkan coast was the field of both Bartholomew’s mission and his martyrdom. The survey that summarises them adds that Pantaenus’s pupils and successors, Clement and Origen, “write about India as if they know more of that land than passing myths and in no way confused it with Arabia and Persia,” speaking of Brahmans and gymnosophists, knowledge they may have had from Pantaenus himself. Moraes accounts for the absence of a local tradition of the mission: the history of Bartholomew’s Christians became intermingled with that of the Thomas Christians. Perumalil holds that the Bartholomew Christians continued as a separate community until the coming of the Portuguese, and then merged with the Christians of Bombay.4

A mission among the Indians, and a death and burial in Armenia

In this reading the two places name two stages of one life: Bartholomew preached in India and died in Armenia.

Held by: Pseudo-Hippolytus (On the Twelve Apostles 6, date uncertain); Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260), following Dorotheus.5

The apostle-list that goes under the name of Hippolytus joins the two places in a single sentence: “Bartholomew, again, preached to the Indians, to whom he also gave the Gospel according to Matthew, and was crucified with his head downward, and was buried in Allanum, a town of the great Armenia.” The Gospel he gives the Indians is the Matthew of Pantaenus’s report, so the list keeps the early notice of the mission and adds to it a place of death and burial. The Golden Legend keeps the same order. It opens, “S. Bartholomew, the apostle, went into India, which is in the end of the world,” tells of his preaching and his healing of the king’s daughter there, and, in its account of where he died, follows Dorotheus: “He died in Alban, a city of great Armenia, crucified the head downward.” Read this way, the India and Armenia traditions report a mission field and a death-place, which an apostle travelling east could both have had, and the sources that name both places set them side by side without treating them as alternatives.6

Armenia, the field of his preaching and martyrdom

In this reading Bartholomew, with Thaddaeus, carried the gospel to Armenia, and died there as its apostle.

Held by: the Armenian Apostolic Church, which honours Bartholomew and Thaddaeus as its patron saints; Movses Khorenatsi (History of Armenia, 5th c.) ⚠ unverified; the Syriac tradition, as reported by Moraes.7

The Armenian tradition holds that Bartholomew, with his fellow apostle Jude Thaddaeus, brought Christianity to Armenia in the first century, and it connects that preaching with the Armenian kingdom’s official adoption of Christianity in 301. Both apostles are honoured by the Armenian Apostolic Church as its patron saints. The memory is tied to a place: the thirteenth-century Saint Bartholomew Monastery in Vaspurakan, Greater Armenia, was built at the presumed site of his martyrdom. The Greek passion sets his death at Albanopolis in Armenia, at the order of Astyages, brother of the king Polymius whom Bartholomew had converted; other versions name the king Agrippa (identified with Tigranes VI) or Sanatruk, king of Armenia. J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) lists Armenia among the lands in which traditions represent Bartholomew as preaching, beside Mesopotamia, Persia, Egypt, Lycaonia, Phrygia and the shores of the Black Sea, and gives Albanopolis in Armenia as the reported place of his death. Moraes reports that the Syriac tradition places Bartholomew’s preaching in Armenia. In this reading a national church’s memory of its founders, a fixed site of martyrdom and burial, and versions of the passion in Greek and Armenian locate both the mission and the death in Armenia.8

The “India” of the report is Arabia Felix or Ethiopia

In this reading Pantaenus’s “India” was a land nearer to Alexandria, South Arabia or Ethiopia, which ancient writers also called India.

Held by: Sollerius (1669); Adolf von Harnack (1903); A. E. Medlycott (India and the Apostle Thomas, 1905); Alphonse Mingana (1926).9

These writers do not accept an apostolate of Bartholomew in the Indian subcontinent; their main argument, in the Nasrani.net survey’s summary, is that the India to which Eusebius and Jerome refer “should be Ethiopia or Arabia Felix.” Fenlon notes that “India” was “a name covering a very wide area, including even Arabia Felix,” and the survey records the view that Eusebius and Jerome took Arabia or Persia for India, as some other classical writers did. Eusebius reports the episode at second hand: Pantaenus “is said” to have gone among the Indians, where “a report is” that he found the Gospel. Jerome, writing to Marcella, places Thomas in India (“with Thomas in India, with Peter in Rome”), and the same survey states that there is no local tradition in India of the mission of Pantaenus or of an apostolate of Bartholomew. On this reading the community that showed Pantaenus a Hebrew Matthew lived in Arabia Felix or Ethiopia, lands that ancient writers also called India.10
