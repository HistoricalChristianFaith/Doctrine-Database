The New Testament on Bartholomew

Dates: c. 30–90 (the events, and the writing of the Gospels and Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant sources. Bartholomew is named only in the four lists of the Twelve — three times beside Philip — and no saying, deed, mission, or death of his is recorded. His name is a patronymic, “son of Tolmai.” John’s Gospel, which never names a Bartholomew, tells of Nathanael, whom Philip brings to Jesus; whether the two are one man is disputed (see Reception and debate).

Claims & evidence

Named in all four lists of the Twelve — three times beside Philip

Bartholomew appears in every list of the apostles and nowhere else in the New Testament. In Matthew, Mark, and Luke he stands immediately after Philip; only in Acts, which lists the Eleven after the resurrection, is he separated from Philip by Thomas. Those who identify Bartholomew with Nathanael draw on this pairing (see below).

Philip, and Bartholomew; Thomas, and Matthew the publican; James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus…

— Matthew 10:3 (cf. Mark 3:18; Luke 6:14). In Acts the order shifts: “Philip, and Thomas, Bartholomew, and Matthew” (Acts 1:13).

The name “Bartholomew” (bar-Tolmai, “son of Tolmai”)

“Bartholomew” is the Aramaic bar-Tolmai, “son of Tolmai” (Tolmai/Talmai being an old Hebrew name). It identifies a man by his father, as “Bar-jona” does Simon Peter or “Barnabas” and “Barabbas” do others. J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) writes that it “may have been his genuine proper name or simply added to distinguish him as the son of Talmai.” Those who identify him with Nathanael take it as a patronymic borne beside a personal name that the apostle-lists do not give.

Nathanael in John’s Gospel

John’s Gospel never mentions a Bartholomew. It tells instead of Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, whom Philip — the apostle the Synoptics pair with Bartholomew — fetches to Jesus, and whom Jesus greets as “an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile.”

Philip findeth Nathanael, and saith unto him, We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph. And Nathanael said unto him, Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth? Philip saith unto him, Come and see. Jesus saw Nathanael coming to him, and saith of him, Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile!

— John 1:45–47. Nathanael appears once more, with Simon Peter, Thomas, the sons of Zebedee and two unnamed disciples, at the last miraculous draught of fishes: “There were together Simon Peter, and Thomas called Didymus, and Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, and the sons of Zebedee” (John 21:2).

Reception and debate

  • What the New Testament records. It records no saying, act, mission, or death of Bartholomew. The traditions of his mission and death on the summary timeline come from later sources.
  • The identification with Nathanael. J. F. Fenlon (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reports that “many scholars” identify Bartholomew with Nathanael, on the grounds that the Synoptics list Bartholomew but never a Nathanael, John names Nathanael but never a Bartholomew, Philip is linked to both, and Nathanael appears among apostles in John 21. He calls the chain “ingenious and pretty strong,” adding that “Nathaniel may have been another personage” in whom the Fourth Evangelist was particularly interested, as he was in Nicodemus. Augustine (Tractates on the Gospel of John 7) held that Nathanael was not one of the Twelve: “Nathanael was learned and skilled in the law and for that reason was the Lord unwilling to place him among His disciples, because He chose unlearned persons, that He might by them confound the world.”
  • The fishing scene. Nathanael fishes in John 21 with Peter, Thomas, the sons of Zebedee, and “two other of his disciples.” John never lists the Twelve and never calls Nathanael one of them. Fenlon reads the two unnamed disciples as “almost certainly, likewise Apostles,” so that Nathanael “presumably, was one of the Twelve.”
