Bede on Gethsemane “shown to this day”

Dates: c. 672/673 – 735 (the Commentary on Mark, c. 720s–730) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Northumbrian monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, who never saw the Holy Land, transmits the pilgrim reports of his day and states that the Gethsemane site — with a church over it — was still shown in the eighth century.

Claim & evidence

“The place of Gethsemane is shown to this day… now a church built above it” (c. 720s)

And they come to a place called Gethsemane. The place of Gethsemane is shown to this day, where the Lord prayed, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, now a church built above it. However, Gethsemane is interpreted as the valley of the fat ones or the fatness.

— Bede, Commentary on Mark 14:32 (the abridged form is also carried in Aquinas's Catena Aurea on Mark 14). Bede goes on to read the “valley of fatness” allegorically, as a figure of humility and of “the internal fatness of love” to be kept in prayer.

Placement on the timeline

Bede is not an eyewitness — he compiled his Holy-Land notices from earlier pilgrim accounts (chiefly Adomnán's De locis sanctis, itself reporting the Gaulish bishop Arculf). His sentence reports three things also found in earlier witnesses on this timeline: the site is shown (a pointed-out place), it is at the foot of the Mount of Olives (the placement of Jerome's Latin Onomasticon), and a church stands over it (as in Egeria's account and Jerome's Latin Onomasticon). The second half of the passage turns to the meaning of the name.

Notes

  • A century later Rabanus Maurus, as excerpted in Aquinas's Catena Aurea, makes the same report — identifying Luke's mount, John's Kidron garden, and Matthew's Gethsemane, and reporting “a Church now built.”
  • Bede is also a witness on the perpetual-virginity timeline; here he reports the Gethsemane site as shown in the eighth century.
