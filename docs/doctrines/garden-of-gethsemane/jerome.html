Jerome on the name of Gethsemane

Dates: c. 347 – 420 (the Commentary on Matthew, 398) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Latin translator and biblical scholar, resident in Palestine, who rendered Eusebius's gazetteer into Latin and, in his Commentary on Matthew, interpreted the name Gethsemane as “most fertile valley.”

Claim & evidence

“Gethsemane is interpreted most fertile valley” (398)

Then Jesus came with them to an estate which is called Gethsemane, and he said to his disciples: Sit here while I go over there and pray. Gethsemane is interpreted most fertile valley; there he ordered the disciples to sit for a little while and to wait for his return, while the Lord alone prayed for all.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew 26:36. The same rendering — “Gethsemane is interpreted, ‘The rich valley’” — is preserved in Aquinas's Catena Aurea on Matthew 26.

Placement on the timeline

Jerome's gloss is an etymology, not a topographical notice. He does not locate the site in this passage; when he translated the Onomasticon he had carried Eusebius's placement into Latin, adding “the foot of” (“(the foot of) the Mt. of Olives” in Wolf's translation, whose parentheses mark the Latin's variants). The reference literature cited on the timeline derives the name from a Semitic gat shemanim, “oil-press.” Aquinas's Catena Aurea carries Jerome's rendering as “The rich valley”; in the eighth century Bede gives the name as “the valley of the fat ones or the fatness” and reads the valley as a figure of humility and of “the internal fatness of love” to be kept in prayer.

Notes

  • Jerome is also a Passion-topography witness on the timeline of the tomb of Jesus, where he reports Hadrian's suppression of the site and is cited on Golgotha's place “without the gate.”
  • His Latin recension of Eusebius's Onomasticon (the Liber locorum) is how the earliest gazetteer placement of Gethsemane reached the Latin West.
