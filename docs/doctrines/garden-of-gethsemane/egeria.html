Egeria on the church at Gethsemane

Dates: c. 384 (the Itinerarium Egeriae, c. 381–384) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Western nun whose pilgrimage diary gives the fullest firsthand account of 4th-century Holy-Land liturgy — and the first firm evidence of an actual church on the Gethsemane prayer-spot, embedded in the Maundy-Thursday night procession.

Claim & evidence

The Holy-Thursday procession, the “graceful church,” and two stations (c. 384)

And at the first cockcrow they come down from the Imbomon with hymns, and arrive at the place where the Lord prayed, as it is written in the Gospel: and He was withdrawn (from them) about a stone's cast, and prayed, and the rest. There is in that place a graceful church. The bishop and all the people enter, a prayer suitable to the place and to the day is said, with one suitable hymn, and the passage from the Gospel is read where He said to His disciples: Watch, that ye enter not into temptation; the whole passage is read through and prayer is made. And then all, even to the smallest child, go down with the Bishop, on foot, with hymns to Gethsemane; where, on account of the great number of people in the crowd, who are wearied owing to the vigils and weak through the daily fasts, and because they have so great a hill to descend, they come very slowly with hymns to Gethsemane.

— The Pilgrimage of Egeria, ch. XXXVI, trans. M. L. McClure & C. L. Feltoe. The Latin behind “graceful church” is the standard label ecclesia elegans.

What it attests — and what it does not

Two things enter the record with Egeria. First, a building: by the 380s the prayer-spot Eusebius knew as an open station of devotion had a church on it — the ecclesia elegans whose foundations the 1919 excavation would find beneath the modern Basilica of the Agony. Her diary is the written end of a chain the spade later confirms. Second, and easy to miss, she describes two distinct stations on the descent: “the place where the Lord prayed” (higher, with the church) and “Gethsemane” itself (lower, reached after a further descent). That two-station shape is the earliest hint of the grotto-versus-garden split — the prayer-church above, the garden (or the disciples' cave) below — that runs through the whole later tradition. What she does not give is an exact plot boundary or any hint of the later denominational division; she witnesses one liturgical site, richly used.

Notes

  • Egeria also appears on this wiki as a witness to the 4th-century cult at the tomb of Jesus and (with a caution — her Bethlehem station is lost in the sole manuscript) at the birthplace of Christ.
  • The “graceful church” she enters is the direct ancestor of the present Church of All Nations, which stands on its excavated foundations.
