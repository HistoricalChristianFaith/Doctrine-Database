Eusebius of Caesarea on the location of Gethsemane

Dates: c. 260 – 339 (the Onomasticon c. 290–325) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Caesarea's Onomasticon — a gazetteer of biblical place-names — carries the earliest surviving topographical notice of Gethsemane, placing it at the foot of the Mount of Olives and reporting that the faithful of his own day went there to pray.

Claim & evidence

The earliest gazetteer entry: a place already visited for prayer (c. 325)

Gethsimanē (Gethsimani). Place where the Christ (the Savior) prayed before the passion. It is located at (the foot of) the Mt. of Olives where even now the faithful fervently utter prayers (where now a church has been built over it).

— Eusebius, Onomasticon, the Gethsemane entry (Klostermann's critical Greek text, GCS 11.1, 1904, p. 74, no. 364), in the first-hand English translation of C. Umhau Wolf (1971). The entry itself preserves two stages: the clause that “even now the faithful fervently utter prayers” is Eusebius's Greek (early 4th c.), while the parenthetical “where now a church has been built over it” is the addition of Jerome's Latin recension (c. 390) — so the wording tracks the two-stage growth of the site, an open place of prayer under Eusebius that had acquired a church by Jerome's day.

What it attests — and what it does not

The Onomasticon is the hinge between the Gospel descriptions and the built site. It confirms two things a full generation before any church is attested on the spot: that the church of Eusebius's day already located Gethsemane specifically at the foot of the Mount of Olives (harmonizing Luke's mount with John's garden across the Kidron), and that the place was already a station of active devotion — people went there to pray. What it does not give is an exact plot or a building: Eusebius fixes the general ground, not the precise yards that later centuries would divide among four churches. Jerome, who translated the Onomasticon into Latin (the Liber locorum), carried the entry westward, and it is Jerome who later supplies the name's disputed etymology.

Notes

  • Eusebius also appears on this wiki as the fullest ancient witness to the recovery of the tomb of Jesus under Constantine — the two Passion sites run through his pen a decade apart.
  • The two-clause structure of the entry — open prayer-place in the Greek, church “now built over it” in Jerome's Latin — independently frames the moment the site went from station to sanctuary, between Eusebius (early 4th c.) and Jerome (c. 390); the Bordeaux Pilgrim's silence in 333 and Egeria's “graceful church” c. 384 bracket the same interval on this timeline.
