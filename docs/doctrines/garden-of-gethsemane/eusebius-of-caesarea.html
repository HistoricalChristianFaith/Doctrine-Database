Eusebius of Caesarea on the location of Gethsemane

Dates: c. 260 – 339 (the Onomasticon c. 290–325) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Caesarea's Onomasticon — a gazetteer of biblical place-names — carries the earliest surviving topographical notice of Gethsemane, placing it at the foot of the Mount of Olives and reporting that the faithful of his own day went there to pray.

Claim & evidence

The earliest gazetteer entry: a place already visited for prayer (c. 325)

[Gethsemane is] at the foot of the Mount of Olives… the faithful were accustomed to go there to pray.

— Eusebius, Onomasticon (ed. Klostermann, 1904, p. 74), as reported in the Catholic Encyclopedia's entry “Gethsemani.” The wording above is quoted from that secondary citation of Klostermann's critical edition, not verified here against the Greek text or a first-hand English edition (Notley & Safrai, The Onomasticon of Eusebius Pamphili, Brill 2005), so the exact phrasing should be treated as reported rather than as a checked verbatim.

What it attests — and what it does not

The Onomasticon is the hinge between the Gospel descriptions and the built site. It confirms two things a full generation before any church is attested on the spot: that the church of Eusebius's day already located Gethsemane specifically at the foot of the Mount of Olives (harmonizing Luke's mount with John's garden across the Kidron), and that the place was already a station of active devotion — people went there to pray. What it does not give is an exact plot or a building: Eusebius fixes the general ground, not the precise yards that later centuries would divide among four churches. Jerome, who translated the Onomasticon into Latin (the Liber locorum), carried the entry westward, and it is Jerome who later supplies the name's disputed etymology.
