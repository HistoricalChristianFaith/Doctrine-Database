Eusebius of Caesarea on the location of Gethsemane

Dates: c. 260 – 339 (the Onomasticon c. 290–325) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Caesarea's Onomasticon — a gazetteer of biblical place-names — carries the earliest extant topographical notice of Gethsemane, placing it at the Mount of Olives and reporting that the faithful of his own day went there to pray.

Claim & evidence

The Gethsemane entry: a place visited for prayer (c. 325)

Gethsimanē (Gethsimani). Place where the Christ (the Savior) prayed before the passion. It is located at (the foot of) the Mt. of Olives where even now the faithful fervently utter prayers (where now a church has been built over it).

— Eusebius, Onomasticon, the Gethsemane entry (Klostermann's critical Greek text, GCS 11.1, 1904, p. 74, no. 364), in the first-hand English translation of C. Umhau Wolf (1971). The entry itself preserves two stages: the clause that “even now the faithful fervently utter prayers” is Eusebius's Greek (early 4th c.), while the parenthetical “where now a church has been built over it” is the addition of Jerome's Latin recension (c. 390) — so the entry in its two forms reports an open place of prayer in Eusebius's Greek and a church in Jerome's Latin.

Placement on the timeline

On this timeline the Onomasticon entry stands between the Gospel descriptions and the later reports of a church on the site. Its Greek locates Gethsemane at the Mount of Olives (the Latin has “the foot of”, set in parentheses by Wolf, who uses them “to indicate minor variations in the Latin from the presumed earlier Greek text”) and reports that people of Eusebius's day went there to pray; it gives no exact plot and no building. Jerome, who translated the Onomasticon into Latin (the Liber locorum), carried the entry westward with its church-clause, and his Commentary on Matthew gives an etymology of the name.

Notes

  • Eusebius is also a witness on the timeline of the tomb of Jesus, recording its recovery under Constantine.
  • The entry's Greek (early 4th c.) reports an open place of prayer, and Jerome's Latin (c. 390) a church “now built over it”; on this timeline the Bordeaux Pilgrim (333) names no Gethsemane, and Egeria (c. 384) reports a “graceful church” on the prayer-spot.
