Origen on Gethsemane as a named estate

Dates: c. 185 – c. 253 (the Commentary on Matthew, c. 246) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Alexandrian commentator whose Commentary on Matthew is the earliest extant comment on this timeline to treat Gethsemane as a named place whose Hebrew name is worth interpreting; in the same passage he discusses whether the place of a prayer makes it different.

Claim & evidence

“The name of this estate… is of use for making this place clear” (c. 246)

Then Jesus came with them to an estate called Gethsemane.

To one who knows the interpretation of Hebrew names, even the name of this estate which is called Gethsemane is of use for making this place clear, since it is not without reason that the name of the estate too is set down in Matthew and in Mark. But we, passing that over, suggest only this: how Jesus came with his disciples to this estate which is called Gethsemane, taking them out of that place in which he had eaten the Passover with them… But it was fitting also that, before he was betrayed, he should pray and, for praying, should choose a clean place for the prayer; for he knew that, just as air differs from purer air, so does the earth of a holy place differ from that of a holier one.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew 89 (on Matthew 26:36). A condensed form — “He must first pray, and choose a place pure for prayer” — is preserved in Aquinas's Catena Aurea on Matthew 26.

Placement on the timeline

Origen writes about a century before Eusebius's gazetteer, and his is the earliest extant comment on this timeline that treats Gethsemane as a named place. The Greek he is glossing calls it a chōrion — a farm or landed estate — and Origen writes that the evangelists did not name the estate “without reason” and that its Hebrew name is “of use for making this place clear.”

Origen does not give the interpretation of the name himself (“we, passing that over”), and he gives no placement; the earliest extant placement at the Mount of Olives on this timeline is Eusebius's. Having written that the earth of a holy place differs from that of a holier one, he continues that one who has withdrawn from “Jewish fables” will say that a prayer “does not become different from another prayer on account of the place, but rather because it is better to pray with no one than to pray with wicked men.”

Notes

  • Origen also figures in the Passion-topography record as a reporter of the Adam's-skull tradition at Golgotha.
  • The chōrion (“estate/farm”) is the Greek word Origen glosses; the derivation of the name Gethsemane from a Semitic word for “oil-press,” and the oil-press found in the nearby grotto, are reported on the timeline.
