Origen on Gethsemane as a named estate

Dates: c. 185 – c. 253 (the Commentary on Matthew, c. 246) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving commentator to treat Gethsemane as a real, named place whose Hebrew name is worth interpreting — while, characteristically, drawing back from the idea that the ground itself is holier than any other.

Claim & evidence

“The name of this estate… is of use for making this place clear” (c. 246)

Then Jesus came with them to an estate called Gethsemane.

To one who knows the interpretation of Hebrew names, even the name of this estate which is called Gethsemane is of use for making this place clear, since it is not without reason that the name of the estate too is set down in Matthew and in Mark. But we, passing that over, suggest only this: how Jesus came with his disciples to this estate which is called Gethsemane, taking them out of that place in which he had eaten the Passover with them… But it was fitting also that, before he was betrayed, he should pray and, for praying, should choose a clean place for the prayer; for he knew that, just as air differs from purer air, so does the earth of a holy place differ from that of a holier one.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew 89 (on Matthew 26:36). A condensed form — “He must first pray, and choose a place pure for prayer” — is preserved in Aquinas's Catena Aurea on Matthew 26.

What it attests — and what it does not

Origen is writing a full century before Eusebius's gazetteer, and he is the earliest father to register Gethsemane as a genuine place rather than a mere scene. The Greek he is glossing calls it a chōrion — a farm or landed estate — and Origen takes the detail seriously: the evangelists did not name the estate “without reason,” and its Hebrew name (the gat shemanim, “oil-press,” that the later tradition and the archaeology of the grotto recover) is “of use for making this place clear.” That an Alexandrian reading in the mid-third century already treats Gethsemane as a real, interpretable farm-estate is the earliest textual anchor for the site's identity as an oil-press installation at the foot of the mount.

Two honest limits. First, Origen declines to unpack the etymology himself (“we, passing that over”), so he attests the estate's reality and topographical significance without supplying the placement — the first gazetteer fix (foot of the Mount of Olives) is still Eusebius's. Second, and characteristically, Origen half-retracts his own opening: having said the earth of a holy place differs from that of a holier one, he turns and argues that for the Christian a prayer “does not become different from another prayer on account of the place, but rather because it is better to pray with no one than to pray with wicked men.” So Origen is a witness to Gethsemane as a real named estate, not to a doctrine of its sacred ground — a distinction that fits the character of the man who elsewhere reads the topography of the Passion typologically.

Notes

  • Origen recurs across the Passion-topography pages: he is the earliest witness to the Adam's-skull tradition at Golgotha (which he reports rather than asserts), and here the earliest to treat Gethsemane as a real estate.
  • The chōrion (“estate/farm”) Origen reads is the same word behind the modern identification of the site as an ancient oil-press — the installation the grotto excavation uncovered.
