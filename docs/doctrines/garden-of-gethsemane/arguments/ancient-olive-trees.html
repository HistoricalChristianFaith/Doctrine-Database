Did the garden's ancient olive trees witness Jesus' agony?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — for the popular devotional claim in its strong form, that the gnarled olives standing in today's garden are the very trees that stood over Jesus as he prayed. Two independent lines of evidence trim it: Josephus records that the Roman siege of AD 70 felled every tree for miles around Jerusalem, so the standing timber of Jesus' day was destroyed; and a 2015 radiocarbon study dated the oldest datable trunks to the twelfth century, the Crusader era. The relic-claim fails on the evidence. What survives is a weaker, defensible form — that the site has borne olives across the centuries, and that olive rootstock can regenerate for ages from a felled stump, so medieval wood does not prove the ground was ever bare of olives — but that is a claim about continuity of cultivation, not about relics that saw the agony.

Related arguments: this stands apart from the where exactly pair (grotto-or-garden and the four rival plots): those weigh which ground is authentic; this weighs the antiquity of a relic that grows on it.

The claim

The Garden of Gethsemane's most venerated feature is its cluster of eight enormous, gnarled olive trees. Pilgrims are shown them as living witnesses of the Passion — trees that stood, or grew directly from trees that stood, in the grove where Jesus “ofttimes resorted” with his disciples and where he prayed on the night of his arrest.1 The devotion is powerful and old-feeling, but it is not itself ancient: the trees are first singled out for veneration only in the sixteenth century, long after the enclosure had acquired its medieval name Campus Florum (“field of flowers”) in the thirteenth.2

The steelman: olives are famously long-lived

The claim is not absurd on its face, and it deserves its strongest form before being weighed. Olive trees (Olea europaea) are among the longest-lived cultivated plants on earth. A single tree can persist for many centuries, and — crucially — even when the visible trunk dies, is burned, or is cut to the ground, the plant regularly regenerates from suckers off its surviving root-crown, so that a “new” trunk can rise on the same ancient rootstock.3 On that biology, a claim of botanical continuity from the first century is at least conceivable: one need not suppose the identical wood survived, only that the same living root system did, throwing up successive trunks on the same spot for two thousand years. That is the honest strong form of the tradition, and it is the part that survives the evidence. The strong form that does not survive is the one pilgrims are usually told: that this very timber is apostolic.

The historical defeater: Josephus and the siege

The first difficulty is documentary. Josephus, an eyewitness to the Roman siege of Jerusalem in AD 70, records that Titus's army stripped the whole environs of timber for its siege-works and embankments: “all the trees that were about the city had been already cut down,” and the countryside “before adorned with trees and pleasant gardens” was reduced to “a desolate country every way, and its trees… all cut down,” the devastation reaching some ninety furlongs (about eighteen kilometres) around.4 The Mount of Olives, immediately across the Kidron and a natural staging-ground for the northern assault, lay well inside that ring. Whatever olives grew at Gethsemane in Jesus' day were, on Josephus's own account, felled forty years later. Any surviving trunk therefore has to postdate AD 70 — which by itself defeats the strong relic-claim, and leaves only the root-regeneration version standing.

The scientific defeater: the trunks are medieval

The second difficulty is physical. In a 2015 study led by Mauro Bernabei of Italy's National Research Council, researchers radiocarbon-dated wood from the garden's eight great trees. The work was hard: every trunk is hollow, its original central (oldest) wood long gone, and several have split into separate stumps around the void — so only three of the eight could be dated at all. Those three returned dates in the twelfth century (AD 1092, 1166, and 1198), the era of the Crusader Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem, when Western Christians were actively restoring the holy sites. The remaining five giant trunks are undatable, and the study was candid that their age cannot be inferred from the three.5

A companion genetic study of all eight trees (Petruccelli et al., 2014) found that they share a single genotype — identical profiles at all fourteen microsatellite loci tested — meaning every tree in the garden was propagated from one common parent plant, the small morphological differences being mutations accumulated over roughly nine centuries or simple effects of position. That result fits the radiocarbon picture exactly: a deliberate twelfth-century replanting of the enclosure from a single stock, not a stand of independently ancient trees.6

The nuance to preserve

Honesty cuts both ways here, and the medieval dates must not be over-read. “The datable trunks began in the twelfth century” is not the same as “these trees are not on the ancestral spot” or “no olive ever grew here before the Crusaders.” Because olives regenerate from the root-crown, the living wood being medieval is entirely compatible with continuous cultivation on ground whose olives' ancestors may well have stood there in antiquity — a felled or burned grove replaced by suckers, by replanting, or by both, on the same devotional plot. No test has reached the root systems, and the single shared genotype tells us the trees were propagated from one stock, not when that stock's line first grew on this ground. So the site's continuity as an olive-plot is untouched by the dating; what the dating removes is the specific relic-claim hung on the visible timber.

Adversarial assessment

What fails. The strong, popular claim — that the trees now shown to pilgrims are the very trees (or the very wood) that witnessed Jesus' agony — is a factual overreach, defeated twice over: by Josephus's record that the siege of AD 70 felled every tree around the city, and by radiocarbon dates placing the oldest datable trunks in the twelfth century. The veneration of these specific trees is itself a late devotional growth, first attested only in the sixteenth century. On the material evidence, the visible olives are medieval, not apostolic.

What holds. The weaker form is defensible and should not be swept away with the strong one. Olives regenerate from rootstock for ages; medieval trunks do not prove the ground was ever without olives; the roots are untested; and the site has plausibly borne olives across the centuries on the same enclosure. That is a claim about continuity of cultivation, not about relics — and it is compatible with the evidence, not established by it (possibility is not proof; no root-dating has been done). The tradition is not a fraud; it is a devotion that attached the right idea (an olive-plot with a long memory) to the wrong object (this particular wood).

Assessment

Weak — for the strong relic-claim that the garden's ancient olives witnessed the agony. It is a beloved tradition that the evidence trims: Josephus felled the first-century trees, radiocarbon dates the oldest surviving trunks to the Crusader twelfth century, and a shared genotype marks the eight as a single medieval replanting. The defensible residue is a different and much more modest claim — that Gethsemane has borne olives across the centuries and that regenerating rootstock could in principle carry a line of trees from antiquity on this ground — but that continuity has not been demonstrated either, and it is not what the relic-devotion asserts. The site's authenticity does not stand or fall with the age of its trees; the trees simply are not the witnesses they are shown as.
