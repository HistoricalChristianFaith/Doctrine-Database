Which of the four rival Gethsemane sites is the authentic garden?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as a claim to identify one authentic plot. The four denominational gardens sit within a few hundred metres of one another at the western foot of the Mount of Olives, and no one of them carries decisive evidence over its neighbours: the earliest notices (Eusebius, the Bordeaux Pilgrim, Egeria) fix the region, not a precise plot, and the denominational boundaries hardened only from the medieval and modern centuries as property and pilgrimage interests did. The honest headline is a two-level verdict — the general location is sound (the western foot of the mount, across the Kidron, is well-attested from the fourth century and cannot be far wrong), while any single plot is at best plausible: prominence is not proof, and the multiplicity is not a debunking — convergence on the region is itself meaningful.

Related arguments: where exactly — see also Was the agony in the grotto or the open garden?

The question

Every claimant honours the same Gospel data — Luke's Mount of Olives, John's garden across the brook Cedron, Matthew and Mark's “place called Gethsemane”1 — and every claimant sits on or at the western foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron from the Old City, exactly where those texts put it. The dispute is therefore not over miles but over metres: four churches maintain four separate walled plots within a short walk of one another, and each is shown to pilgrims as “the” garden of the agony. The question weighed here is whether the evidence lets us pick one of them out as the authentic ground — or whether the most that can honestly be said is that the true spot lay somewhere in that small compass.

The four claimants

All four lie at or near the western foot of the mount, and the traditions attached to them reach back no earlier than the fourth century:2

  • Roman Catholic (Franciscan) — the dominant modern site: the walled garden beside the Church of All Nations (Basilica of the Agony), enclosing the bedrock “Rock of the Agony” and the gnarled olive trees. Its enclosure appears from the thirteenth century as the Campus Florum; the plot was bought by Croatian knights in 1681 and given to the Franciscans, who hold it still.3 It is the one built directly over the fourth-century Byzantine basilica — Egeria's ecclesia elegans — and a twelfth-century Crusader chapel, so it has the strongest claim to architectural continuity with the earliest attested church.4
  • Greek Orthodox — a walled garden and church of Gethsemane a short walk to the north/east, with ancient olives of its own.2
  • Russian Orthodox — the olive orchard higher up the slope beside the Church of St Mary Magdalene, kept by the Russian convent there.2
  • Armenian — the fourth tradition, maintained to the north near the Tomb of the Virgin (the same quarter as the venerated grotto).2

To these the Eastern tradition adds the grotto by the Tomb of the Virgin — the cave venerated from the fourth century as the place the disciples waited and where Judas came — which is a distinct kind of claim (a sheltered station, not an open garden) and is weighed as its own question of grotto-versus-garden priority.

The common ground: the region is secure

The strongest thing to say for the tradition is that the area was never seriously in doubt. The earliest topographical notice, Eusebius's Onomasticon (c. 325), places Gethsemane “at the foot of the Mount of Olives” and records that the faithful of his day already resorted there to pray.5 By the 380s Egeria walks into an actual church on the prayer-spot,6 and the 1919 excavation beneath the modern basilica found that very fourth-century church, and a Crusader chapel above it, on the same ground.4 The Gospel constraints — foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron, an enclosed olive-grove the disciples knew — are met by the whole compass in which the four plots sit. Whatever the disagreement over yards, all four candidates fall inside the ground the Gospels and the fourth-century sources describe, and none lies anywhere it could not have been.

The adjudication problem

What the early sources do not supply is a plot. Eusebius fixes an area and a devotional habit; the Bordeaux Pilgrim in 333 climbs the mount, notes the monuments at its summit, and names no Gethsemane at all — the agony-garden was not yet a marked pilgrim station with a church of its own.7 Egeria's church anchors the Franciscan ground to the fourth century, but it anchors a church, not a demonstrated identification of the exact spot of the prayer; and the other three denominational plots crystallized later still, as the single “place where the Lord prayed” hardened into separately-owned enclosures through the medieval and Ottoman centuries.2 There is no first-century marker, no inscription, and no decisive archaeology that favours one plot over its neighbour a hundred metres away. The boundaries between the four are an inheritance of property and pilgrimage — the same Status-Quo pattern of divided custody that governs the shared Tomb of the Virgin and the Holy Sepulchre — not an ancient datum handed down about which yards Jesus knelt in.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The general location is as secure as topographical tradition gets: attested from Eusebius forward, matching every Gospel constraint, and physically confirmed at the Franciscan plot by a fourth-century church under the modern basilica. The four sites are not four contradictory locations but four neighbours within one small, correct compass; their very convergence on that ground is evidence for the tradition, not against it. And the Franciscan site does carry one asymmetric advantage — it alone stands on the earliest attested church, so if one wanted the plot most likely to preserve the ancient devotional focus, it is the reasonable default.

What is overstated. That advantage is prominence and architectural continuity, not proof that Jesus prayed on that rock rather than in the Greek or Russian olives a short walk off. The fourth-century church marks where fourth-century Christians located the memory; it does not reach behind them to the event, and the localized devotion of the earliest centuries may in any case have clustered on the grotto rather than any open garden. To read the Franciscan plot's fame as settling the metres is to let prominence stand in for evidence. Equally, the popular move in the other direction — that four rival gardens expose the whole site as a pious fiction — overshoots: rival claims within a hundred metres are a dispute over a plot, not over whether the region is authentic, and the region is well-founded.

Assessment

Contested — with a two-level verdict that should be kept distinct. The general location (the western foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron) is sound: attested from the fourth century, matching the Gospel topography, and archaeologically anchored. But identifying one of the four denominational plots as the authentic garden is at best plausible and cannot be adjudicated on present evidence — there is no first-century marker and no decisive dig favouring one plot over its neighbours, and the boundaries reflect later property and pilgrimage history. The Franciscan site is the reasonable default only because it stands on the earliest attested church, not because the metres are proven. Prominence is not proof; multiplicity is not refutation. The true spot lay somewhere in that small compass, and that is as far as the evidence honestly reaches.
