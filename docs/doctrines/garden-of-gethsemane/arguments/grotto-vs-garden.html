Was the agony in the grotto or the open garden?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — as the claim that the earliest localized Gethsemane devotion attached to the Grotto of Gethsemane (the natural cave ~100 m north of the modern garden, beside the Tomb of the Virgin) rather than to the open-air olive-plot venerated today. The grotto is a credible — perhaps the earliest datable — station: it is sanctified from the fourth century as the place the disciples waited and slept, it is itself an ancient oil-press (the very kind of installation that named Gethsemane), and it has survived largely unchanged where the open garden's features are late devotional overlays. But two things keep this short of probable: the attestation, though early, is still centuries after the event and topographical before it is architectural; and the strongest form of the reading is not “the cave instead of the garden” but “the cave (the disciples' lodging) and the garden (Jesus' prayer) as adjacent, complementary spots” — which the Gospels themselves invite.

The question

The Gospels fix a general ground — the Mount of Olives, across the brook Cedron, a garden the disciples knew1 — but they do not fix a single point within it. Modern pilgrimage puts the whole memory in one place: the walled open-air garden beside the Church of All Nations, with its gnarled olives. Yet a hundred metres to the north, beside the Tomb of the Virgin, is a natural cave — the Grotto of Gethsemane — venerated from the fourth century as the spot where the disciples waited and slept while Jesus withdrew to pray, and where Judas came to betray him.2 The question weighed here is which claimant carries the older devotion — and whether the tradition's earliest localized focus was the cave, later eclipsed by the open plot, or whether both spots belong together.

The steelman: why the cave may be the older locus

Four strands make the grotto a serious — arguably the earliest — candidate.

It fits the Gospel picture of an overnight resort. Luke has Jesus go to the mount “as he was wont,” and John calls the garden “a place where Jesus ofttimes resorted… with his disciples.”1 A sheltered cave at the foot of the olive groves, close to the Kidron, is exactly where a party lodging on the Mount of Olives would shelter for the night — and the incidental details of the arrest fit a cold night's camping in a cave: Peter later warms himself at a fire, and a young man flees the scene in nothing but a linen cloth.1

The cave is itself an oil-press. Gethsemane transliterates gat shemanim, “oil-press” — and the grotto preserves the cisterns, drainage channels, and press-fittings of a working agricultural installation. Byzantine pilgrims who were shown “round stone beds” on which the disciples were said to have slept were most likely looking at the components of that ancient press. If any single point on the hillside literally was the oil-press that named the place, the cave is the strongest claimant.2

Its Christian veneration is early and materially attested. From the fourth century the cave was sanctified as the disciples' resting-place and converted from an agricultural space into a rock-cut church used for burials; forty-odd tombs of the Byzantine and Crusader periods were later cut into it, and it carried Crusader-era ceiling paintings of stars and Gospel scenes. Fr. Virgilio Corbo's excavation after the 1955 flood documented forty-two tombs across those two periods and the pilgrims' graffiti that accompanied them.3 That people sought burial hard against the cave is a strong index of a holy place, and the fourth-century date sets the grotto's devotion alongside — not after — the earliest church on the prayer-spot up the slope.

Egeria already distinguishes two stations. When she describes the Holy-Thursday procession of the 380s, the pilgrim Egeria names two places in sequence: “the place where the Lord prayed” higher up, where the Gospel of the agony is read, and then “Gethsemane” lower down, to which the company descends with hymns.4 The earliest liturgy of the site therefore already treats the prayer-spot and “Gethsemane” proper as distinct stations — precisely the grotto-and-garden pairing this argument describes, present at the very first moment we can watch the devotion in motion.

The cave is stable; the open garden's relics are late. The grotto, about 190 square metres, is basically unchanged from antiquity. By contrast the venerated features of the open plot are demonstrably later overlays: the enclosure is first named Campus Florum only from the thirteenth century, and the pilgrim veneration of the “ancient” olive trees is first attested only in the sixteenth.5 So the most tangible focus of the modern garden is a late devotional growth, while the cave's claim is anchored in the fourth century and in unchanged stone.

The distinction to keep sharp: not rivals but neighbours

The reading is at its strongest, and its most honest, when it is not pressed into “the cave instead of the garden.” The Gospels themselves separate two acts a short distance apart: Jesus leaves the main body of disciples and withdraws “about a stone's cast” to pray alone, while they wait (and sleep).1 Read that way, the grotto and the garden are not competing identifications of one point but the two ends of one scene: the cave is where the disciples lodged and were found sleeping (and where the arrest party closed in), and the open ground a little off is where Jesus prayed his agony. Both can be authentic at adjacent spots — which is why the tradition, from Egeria onward, keeps both and processes between them. On this steelman the grotto's claim is strong for the lodging and the betrayal and correspondingly weaker for the agony itself, which the Gospels place a little apart from where the disciples lay.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The grotto is a genuinely early, materially anchored, and internally coherent station — fourth-century in its Christian use, an actual oil-press by its fabric, sheltered exactly where an overnight party would lodge, and unchanged where the garden's relics are late. As the earliest localized focus of Gethsemane devotion it is at least the equal of the open plot, and its distinctness from the prayer-spot is written into the liturgy already in the 380s. The pairing reading — cave for the disciples, garden for the prayer — is more than defensible; it may be the natural sense of the Gospel scene.

What is overstated. Three cautions keep this from probable. First, “earliest” is a claim about the earliest surviving devotion, not about an unbroken memory from the event: the oldest notices (Eusebius's gazetteer) fix a region and a habit of prayer, not the cave, and the specifically localized cave-tradition, though fourth-century, is still some three centuries downstream of the arrest — topographical piety, not eyewitness testimony. Second, the “this cave was the very oil-press that named the place” step is attractive but unproven: the “round stone beds” are read as press-parts retrospectively, and the cave being an oil-press does not make it the one the evangelists meant. Third, the inference from “the open garden's relics are late” to “the garden devotion drifted away from an original grotto focus” overreaches: the press/garden and the cave sat adjacent all along, so the late naming of Campus Florum and the sixteenth-century olive cult may simply reflect the open plot always having been the complementary half of the pair, not a migration from the cave.

Assessment

Plausible. The grotto is a credible — perhaps the earliest datable — locus of Gethsemane devotion, and the strongest form of the claim is not “the cave, not the garden” but a grotto-and-garden pair, both authentic at adjacent spots: the cave for the disciples' lodging and the betrayal, the open ground a stone's throw off for Jesus' prayer. That reading fits the Gospel scene, the cave's fourth-century veneration, its character as an oil-press, and Egeria's two-station liturgy. It falls short of probable because the attestation, while early, is still centuries after the event and begins as a fix on the region rather than the cave, and because the sharper form of the thesis — that the agony itself belongs in the grotto, or that the open-garden devotion drifted away from an original cave-focus — presses the thin evidence further than it will bear. The modest, defensible finding: the cave is very possibly the older localized station, and grotto and garden are best held together rather than set against each other — the reading the standard archaeological guides to the site carry.6
