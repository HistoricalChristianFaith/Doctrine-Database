Eusebius of Caesarea on the Date of Revelation

Dates: c. 324 (Church History) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Caesarea and church historian sets the earlier testimonies in a single chronology: John was banished to Patmos under Domitian, released after Domitian's death under Nerva, and returned to Ephesus, where he lived until Trajan. Eusebius quotes Irenaeus's "almost in our own generation, at the end of the reign of Domitian" in support.

Claims & evidence

John was banished to Patmos under Domitian

"It is said that in this persecution the apostle and evangelist John, who was still alive, was condemned to dwell on the island of Patmos in consequence of his testimony to the divine word."

— Church History 3.18.1, trans. NPNF (McGiffert), source. "This persecution" is the one Eusebius has just assigned to Domitian's later years.

Eusebius quotes Irenaeus

"If it were necessary for his name to be proclaimed openly at the present time, it would have been declared by him who saw the revelation. For it was seen not long ago, but almost in our own generation, at the end of the reign of Domitian."

— Church History 3.18.3, quoting Irenaeus, Against Heresies 5.30.3, source. Eusebius reproduces Irenaeus's sentence as the authority for his own dating.

Release under Nerva and residence at Ephesus to Trajan

"It was at this time that the apostle John returned from his banishment in the island and took up his abode at Ephesus, according to an ancient Christian tradition."

— Church History 3.20.9, source (the return follows Domitian's death and the annulling of his acts under Nerva). Eusebius adds that at Ephesus John "remained among them until the time of Trajan" (Church History 3.23.4).

Reception and debate

  • A continuous chronology. Eusebius joins the earlier notices — Irenaeus's dating, Clement's exile-and-return tale, the memory of John at Ephesus — into one sequence: banished under Domitian, freed under Nerva, alive to Trajan. Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men (c. 393) gives the same sequence in Latin; see Jerome.
  • Eusebius and Irenaeus. Eusebius names Irenaeus, whom he quotes, as the authority for his date. Moses Stuart, Thomas Randell, Milton Terry and Kenneth Gentry read Eusebius as a transmitter of Irenaeus rather than an independent source, and hold that the later Domitianic notices trace back to Irenaeus's one sentence; G. K. Beale and Mark Hitchcock read the external tradition as broader than one source, with a reception through Eusebius and Jerome that carried it forward.
