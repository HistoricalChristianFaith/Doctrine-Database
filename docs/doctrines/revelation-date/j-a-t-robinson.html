J. A. T. Robinson on the Date of Revelation

Dates: 1976 (Redating the New Testament) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, not a preterist, who argued that the whole New Testament, Revelation included, was written before AD 70. Robinson dated the Apocalypse to c. AD 68 and held the Domitianic persecution behind the late date to be historically insubstantial.

Claims & evidence

The New Testament's silence on the fall of Jerusalem

"One of the oddest facts about the New Testament is that what on any showing would appear to be the single most datable and climactic event of the period—the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, and with it the collapse of institutional Judaism based on the temple—is never once mentioned as a past fact."

— J. A. T. Robinson, Redating the New Testament (London: SCM Press, 1976), 13, source. The observation frames Robinson's whole project and is not a claim about Revelation in particular; he applied it across the canon, Revelation among the rest, to argue a pre-70 date.

Revelation dated before AD 70

In his chapter on the Apocalypse (ch. VIII), Robinson placed the book c. AD 68 and read the seven-kings sequence of Revelation 17:9–11 against the emperors of the year of the four emperors (68–69). He regarded the Domitianic persecution behind the late date as historically insubstantial — there is, he urged, no good evidence of a systematic Domitianic persecution of Christians for Revelation to answer.
— Robinson, Redating the New Testament, ch. VIII.

Reception and debate

  • Not a preterist. Robinson was an Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, not a confessional preterist; his early dating of Revelation is part of a general historical argument applied across the New Testament.
  • Robinson and Irenaeus. Robinson takes Irenaeus's sentence to date the vision to Domitian's reign, and weighs it as evidence rather than re-construing it, while dating the book c. 68; see Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign?
  • The persecution question. Adela Yarbro Collins (1984) and Leonard Thompson (1990) also argue against the picture of a Domitianic "reign of terror," while keeping the late date. For Robinson the lack of evidence for a systematic Domitianic persecution tells against a Domitianic date; for Thompson the Domitianic setting stands, and the book answers the tensions of life under the imperial cult. See Yarbro Collins and Thompson.
