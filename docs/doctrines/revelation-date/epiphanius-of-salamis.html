Epiphanius of Salamis on the Date of Revelation

Dates: c. 375 (Panarion) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing against the Alogi (who rejected John's writings), Epiphanius twice dates John's prophecy on Patmos to the reign of the emperor Claudius (41–54), where Irenaeus, Victorinus, Eusebius and Jerome name Domitian. Whether the notice witnesses an early date, a separate Claudian tradition, or a chronological slip is disputed.

Claims & evidence

John returned from Patmos "under Claudius Caesar"

"the Holy Spirit compelled John to issue the Gospel in his old age when he was past ninety, after his return from Patmos under Claudius Caesar, and several years of his residence in Asia."

— Panarion 51.12,2, trans. Frank Williams (Brill), source.

John prophesied on Patmos "during the time of Claudius Caesar"

"He foretold it prophetically by the mouth of St. John, who prophesied before his falling asleep, during the time of Claudius Caesar and earlier, when he was on the isle of Patmos."

— Panarion 51.33,9, trans. Frank Williams (Brill), source.

Reception and debate

  • The chronology of the passage. Epiphanius pairs "under Claudius" (who died in AD 54) with John being "past ninety" when he then wrote the Gospel after returning, which would place the Fourth Gospel very early. A reading of the notice as a slip or a garbled tradition, rather than a considered rival to the Domitianic date, rests partly on this pairing (⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet).
  • Nero Claudius Caesar? Kenneth Gentry, with other early-date writers, notes that Nero's full imperial name was Nero Claudius Caesar, and proposes that Epiphanius (or his source) meant Nero, which would make the notice a witness to the early date. Frank Williams's translation reads "Claudius Caesar."
  • A Claudian tradition. The sixth-century Latin commentator Apringius of Beja likewise places John's exile under Claudius, presenting it as the teaching of earlier church writers; on one reading a Claudian strand of tradition persisted beside the Domitianic one, with Epiphanius as one of its witnesses.
