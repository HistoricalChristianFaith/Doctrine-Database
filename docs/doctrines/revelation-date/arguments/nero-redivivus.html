Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 13 and 17; see also Kenneth Gentry

Related readings: Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign? · Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign? · Does Revelation's emperor-worship setting fit Domitian's imperial cult, dating the book to the 90s? · Does the settled condition of the seven churches (Laodicea's wealth) require a late date?

The passage

"And I saw one of his heads as it were wounded to death; and his deadly wound was healed: and all the world wondered after the beast… The beast that thou sawest was, and is not; and shall ascend out of the bottomless pit… And the beast that was, and is not, even he is the eighth, and is of the seven, and goeth into perdition."

— Revelation 13:3; 17:8, 11 (KJV); cf. 13:14, the image made "to the beast, which had the wound by a sword, and did live."1

The question

Whether the beast's death-wound and healing, and the beast that "was, and is not," allude to the first-century legend that Nero (who died by his own hand in June 68) would return, and if so, whether that allusion places the book's writing after Nero's death, in Domitian's reign. The answer bears on the internal case for the late date.

Background: the returning-Nero expectation in ancient sources

Suetonius reports that after Nero's death some "now produced his statues on the rostra in the fringed toga, and now his edicts, as if he were still alive and would shortly return and deal destruction to his enemies," and that twenty years later a pretender won Parthian backing. Tacitus, writing of 69, records: "About this time Achaia and Asia Minor were terrified by a false report that Nero was at hand. Various rumours were current about his death; and so there were many who pretended and believed that he was still alive." Cassius Dio records a second pretender, the Asiatic Terentius Maximus, under Titus (c. 79/80), received by the Parthian leader Artabanus. The Sibylline Oracles carry an eschatological form of the expectation (4.119–124, 137–139, c. AD 80; 5.28–34, 361–385, later), and Juvenal calls Domitian "the bald Nero."2 Augustine, c. 425, still reports both branches of the expectation. Of Paul's "man of sin" some held that "he alluded to Nero, whose deeds already seemed to be as the deeds of Antichrist. And hence some suppose that he shall rise again and be Antichrist. Others, again, suppose that he is not even dead, but that he was concealed that he might be supposed to have been killed, and that he now lives in concealment in the vigor of that same age which he had reached when he was believed to have perished, and will live until he is revealed in his own time and restored to his kingdom. But I wonder that men can be so audacious in their conjectures."3

The returning Nero, seen from a vantage after his death

The beast's death and return are Nero's, drawn from the developed returning-Nero myth, which places the book well after 68, in Domitian's reign.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy, 1993); David E. Aune (Word Biblical Commentary, 1997–98); Craig R. Koester (Anchor Yale Bible, 2014), each dating the book c. 95.4

Bauckham gives an extended treatment of the Nero material in Revelation, tracing the "was, and is not, and is to come" and the "eighth… of the seven" to the returning-Nero expectation documented in the Sibylline Oracles. The phrase "was, and is not, and is to come" is a demonic parody of the divine title "which was, and is, and is to come" (1:4; 4:8), casting the returning Nero as a counterfeit, resurrected antagonist. The historical Nero was the persecutor under whom tradition places the deaths of Paul and Peter at Rome, and the legend mythologizes him from the far side of his death. A legend takes time to mature, and the form Revelation uses, in which the returning Nero is fused with eschatological, quasi-resurrection expectation, is the form of the later Sibylline Oracles. By Domitian's reign the legend had grown a long tail: a third pretender with Parthian backing c. 88, and a tradition that would remember Domitian himself as "a bald Nero." Aune and Koester read the death-wound and revival the same way. On this reading a Nero-saturated Revelation sits naturally on a Domitianic date, its beast drawn from the developed myth of the returning emperor.

The empire's wound and healing in the civil war of 68–69

The head wounded to death and healed is the Roman imperial house, convulsed in 68–69 and restored under Vespasian, so no matured legend is needed.

Held by: Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989).5

Gentry reads the mortally wounded head that "was healed" not of a resurrected Nero but of the Roman imperial house itself. In the chaos of 68–69, the "year of the four emperors," Nero's suicide ended the Julio-Claudian line, three emperors fell in quick succession, and the empire seemed to bleed to death; it was "healed" when Vespasian secured the throne and restored the state. On this reading the death-and-revival of the beast is the empire's, in real and recent events that "all the world wondered after." Gentry takes the "eighth… of the seven" (17:11) to be Vespasian. The text needs no developed Parthian-return myth, and so carries no requirement of a vantage decades after Nero's death. The reading fits Gentry's wider internal case for a date before Jerusalem's fall, in which the book's other markers, the sixth king who "is" and the temple still standing, set its writing in the Neronian years.

A parody of the slain and risen Lamb

The beast's death and return mimic Christ's death and resurrection, whether or not a Nero allusion is also present, without importing the full returning-Nero legend.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.6

Revelation presents the Lamb "as it had been slain" yet standing (5:6), and the beast with a head "as it were wounded to death" whose wound was healed (13:3). On this reading the beast's wound and revival are a counterfeit of the Lamb's death and resurrection, part of the book's pattern of demonic imitation: the beast that "was, and is not, and is to come" answers to God who "was, and is, and is to come." A Nero allusion may stand within this imagery, but the death-and-return motif belongs first to the book's own theology of the slain and risen Lamb, and so supplies no date for the writing.
