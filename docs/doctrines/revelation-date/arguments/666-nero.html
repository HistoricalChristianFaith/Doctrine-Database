Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 13:18; see also Irenaeus and Kenneth Gentry

Related readings: Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign? · Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date? · Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date?

The passage

"Here is wisdom. Let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man; and his number is Six hundred threescore and six."

— Revelation 13:18 (KJV).1

The question

Whether the number of the beast names a figure of John's own day, and if it names Nero, whether it fixes the book's writing in Nero's reign (AD 54–68) or is compatible with a later date. The answer bears on the internal case for an early (Neronian) date.

Background: the Nero solution and the 616 variant

The verse invites a decipherment ("count the number," "the number of a man"), and the solution most discussed in modern study reads the figure by gematria, the ancient practice (Hebrew and Greek both) of summing the numerical values of the letters of a name. Richard Bauckham (1993) and Bruce Metzger (1994) set it out: the imperial title Nero Caesar written in Hebrew consonants as נרון קסר (NRWN QSR, "Neron Qesar") totals 666: nun 50, resh 200, waw 6, nun 50 (= 306 for Neron), qoph 100, samekh 60, resh 200 (= 360 for Qesar). Some witnesses read not 666 but 616, which is what the same name yields when spelled the Latin way, Nero without the final nun (306 − 50 = 256; 256 + 360 = 616).2 The 616 reading was known to Irenaeus c. 180 and is attested in the papyrus P115 (3rd–4th c.) and the fifth-century Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (C).3 Rival solutions have been proposed since antiquity ("Lateinos," "Teitan," and later proposals); Bauckham holds that the Nero solution is set apart by its account of the 616 variant, and notes that the defective spelling קסר (qsr, without a yod) is attested in the Aramaic documents from Wadi Murabbaʿat.4

A name still to be revealed: the Antichrist to come

The number belongs to a future Antichrist whose name has not yet been made known, and should not be guessed.

Held by: Irenaeus (bishop of Lyons, Against Heresies 5.30, c. 180).5

Irenaeus, the earliest extant reader to comment on the verse at length, reports 666 as "found in all the most approved and ancient copies," attested also by "those men who saw John face to face." He explains the shorter number as a slip: "I am inclined to think that this occurred through the fault of the copyists," since numbers are written with letters, and he warns against those who "have ventured to seek out a name which should contain the erroneous and spurious number." The number, on his reading, is "capable of being fitted to many names": he lists Evanthas, Lateinos and Teitan as names that yield it, judging Lateinos "a very probable [solution]" and Teitan "rather worthy of credit," yet declines to fix any of them. Had the name needed to be known in his own time, he argues, "it would have been announced by him who beheld the apocalyptic vision." The number points forward, to a figure still to come.

A cipher of the reigning emperor, placing the book in Nero's reign

The beast is Nero, and the number names him as the ruler of John's present, so the book was written while he reigned.

Held by: F. W. Farrar (Anglican, The Early Days of Christianity, 1882); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989).6

The verse invites its readers to "count the number," and on this reading the count yields the emperor of John's own day. The Hebrew Neron Qesar gives 666 exactly, and the 616 variant gives the same name in its Latin form, so the identification carries a corroboration that rival names lack. If the beast is the reigning Nero, the emperor whose persecution of AD 64–68 fell on the Christians of Rome, the book was written in his reign. Farrar read the beast and its numbered name against Nero and the turmoil of 68–69. Gentry adduces 666 = Nero as one strand of a cumulative internal case, beside the "sixth king" of whom "one is" (17:10), the temple John is told to measure (11:1–2), and the book's language of imminent judgment, which together date it c. 65–66, before Jerusalem's fall.

A cipher of Nero that carries no date of its own

The beast is modelled on Nero, but the number names a person rather than a moment, and the book can look back on Nero from a later reign.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy, 1993); David E. Aune (Word Biblical Commentary, 1997–98); Craig R. Koester (Anchor Yale Bible, 2014), each dating the book c. 95.7

Bauckham argues at length that the beast is modelled on Nero throughout Revelation and that 666 ciphers "Nero Caesar," with the 616 variant explaining both numbers as one name; Aune and Koester take the Neronian gematria as the most probable reading of the number. On this reading, a name-cipher encodes an identity, and Revelation 13:18 carries no temporal index of its own: unlike 17:10, "five are fallen, and one is," it does not say the man it names is now on the throne. A writer of a later decade could cipher Nero as readily as a contemporary, to name the figure the audience already knew as the archetypal persecuting emperor, under whom tradition places the deaths of Paul and Peter at Rome. The beast that "was, and is not, and yet is" (17:8) and the head "wounded to death" and healed (13:3, 14) are read by these scholars as the Nero-redivivus legend, the expectation after Nero's suicide in 68 that he would return; a cipher that names Nero and depicts him as having died and come back is, on this reading, written from a vantage after his death. The number, read this way, names who the beast is, and the redivivus framing sets the book's vantage after Nero.8
