Does Revelation's emperor-worship setting fit Domitian's imperial cult, dating the book to the 90s?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 13; see also Adela Yarbro Collins and Leonard Thompson and J. A. T. Robinson

Related readings: Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date? · Does the settled condition of the seven churches (Laodicea's wealth) require a late date?

The passage

"And he had power to give life unto the image of the beast, that the image of the beast should both speak, and cause that as many as would not worship the image of the beast should be killed. And he causeth all… to receive a mark… and that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark, or the name of the beast, or the number of his name."

— Revelation 13:15–17 (KJV); cf. 13:4 ("they worshipped the beast"), and the third angel's warning that "if any man worship the beast and his image… the same shall drink of the wine of the wrath of God" (14:9–11).1

The question

Revelation depicts a cult of the beast and its image enforced by a "second beast," with death for refusal and exclusion from buying and selling. Whether this setting points specifically to the imperial cult of Roman Asia under Domitian (81–96), or fits ruler worship in Asia under earlier emperors as well, is disputed; the answer bears on the internal case for a late date.

Background: ruler worship in Roman Asia

The province of Asia dedicated a temple to Roma and Augustus at Pergamum in 29 BC, the first provincial cult of a living emperor in the Greek East; Pergamum is the city Revelation calls the place "where Satan's seat is" (2:13). In AD 40 Caligula ordered his statue set up in the Jerusalem temple, a crisis Josephus and Philo record. Under the Flavians a temple of the Sebastoi was dedicated at Ephesus c. AD 89/90, making the city a neokoros ("temple-warden") of the imperial cult, as Steven Friesen (2001) documents.2 Suetonius reports that Domitian "began as follows in issuing a circular letter in the name of his procurators, 'Our Master and our God bids that this be done'"; the Fourth Gospel gives Thomas the confession "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28). Brian Jones (1992) holds that the title appears chiefly in the court poets Martial and Statius and was not a decreed cult of the living emperor.3

A Flavian provincial cult, fitting Domitian's reign

The coercive cult of Revelation 13 matches the provincial imperial cult of Asia as it stood under the Flavians, and so places the book in Domitian's reign.

Held by: Adela Yarbro Collins (Crisis and Catharsis, 1984); Colin Hemer (The Letters to the Seven Churches, 1986); Leonard Thompson (The Book of Revelation, 1990); Steven Friesen (Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John, 2001).4

Hemer, having surveyed the seven letters against their local settings, rests the weight of his Domitianic date on the emperor cult rather than on the cities' economies. Friesen documents the seven cities saturated with imperial-cult institutions, priesthoods and festivals, and the Ephesian temple of the Sebastoi, dedicated in Domitian's reign, gives the seven-churches region a concrete, monumental cult focus of the kind Revelation presupposes. The shape of the pressure fits that setting: Nero's action of 64 was centred on Rome and brief, whereas Revelation's pressure is provincial, located in the cities of Asia, and driven by the cult. These scholars run the case without the older picture of an empire-wide Domitianic persecution. Thompson holds that the "reign of terror" is a construct of later, hostile senatorial writers, and that cult pressure, not a documented massacre, is what Revelation answers; Yarbro Collins grounds the book in a crisis perceived by its Christian community as the demands of the cult intensified. The major reference commentaries (Aune, Beale, Koester) likewise take the imperial cult as the book's primary social matrix. On this reading the book belongs to a maturing, cult-saturated Asia of the late first century, whose most concrete local marker, the Ephesian neokoria of c. 89/90, falls in Domitian's reign.

Long-established ruler worship, compatible with an earlier reign

Emperor worship in Asia was a century old by John's day and had turned coercive before, so the setting does not single out Domitian.

Held by: J. A. T. Robinson (Anglican, Redating the New Testament, 1976).5

Robinson, who dates Revelation c. 68, regards the Domitianic persecution behind the standard late date as historically insubstantial: there is, he urges, no good evidence of a systematic Domitianic persecution of Christians for Revelation to answer. On this reading the imperial-cult setting is compatible with several reigns from Augustus onward. The provincial cult of Asia was more than a century old by the 60s, established at Pergamum in 29 BC, and Revelation's "Satan's seat" at Pergamum (2:13) is read of that cult. Coercive ruler worship had a precedent in Caligula's demand of AD 40 that his statue be set up in the Jerusalem temple, an episode this reading sets beside the image of Revelation 13 that must be worshipped on pain of death. The beast material itself is shaped by Nero: the head wounded to death that lives again (13:3), and the number 666. An early-date reader can therefore grant a cult-pressured Asia in the 60s, with the cult already old, and read Revelation's beast and image against the Julio-Claudian emperors.
