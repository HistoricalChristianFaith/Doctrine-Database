Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 17; see also Kenneth Gentry and J. A. T. Robinson

Related readings: Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date? · Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign? · Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date?

The passage

"And here is the mind which hath wisdom. The seven heads are seven mountains, on which the woman sitteth. And there are seven kings: five are fallen, and one is, and the other is not yet come; and when he cometh, he must continue a short space. And the beast that was, and is not, even he is the eighth, and is of the seven, and goeth into perdition."

— Revelation 17:9–11 (KJV).1

The question

Whether verse 10's "five are fallen, and one is" names the emperor reigning as John wrote, and if so which one, depends on whether the seven kings are a count of Roman rulers and from which ruler the count begins. The answer bears on the internal case for a date under Nero or in the year of the four emperors (68–69).

Background: counting the Caesars

The seven heads are read on two registers: as "seven mountains, on which the woman sitteth," commonly taken of Rome on its seven hills, and as "seven kings." Ancient writers began the series of Caesars at different points. Suetonius opens The Lives of the Caesars with Julius, and Josephus reckons the Caesars from him; Tacitus opens the Annals with Augustus, the first princeps (Julius having been dictator, never Augustus). The eagle vision of 4 Ezra 11–12 and the fifth Sibylline Oracle number the succession in their own ways. Counted from Julius, the sixth ruler is Nero; counted from Augustus, Galba (June 68 – January 69); counted from Augustus with the three short reigns of 68–69 (Galba, Otho, Vitellius) left out, Vespasian (69–79), whose reign runs mostly after the temple fell in August 70.2

A literal succession of emperors, fixing a date in the late 60s

The seven kings are Roman emperors, and verse 10 marks the reign in which John wrote, before Jerusalem fell.

Held by: J. A. T. Robinson (Anglican, Redating the New Testament, 1976); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989).3

Verse 10 reads as a fixed point in a live succession: five rulers past, one reigning, one still to come for a short time. No other passage in the book offers so direct a chronological marker. Gentry puts the method simply: since the vision concerns Rome, "all we have to do is find a series of seven kings, five of whom 'have fallen,' the sixth of whom 'is' still ruling, and the last of whom was of but a brief reign." The Julio-Claudian line supplies exactly that when counted from Julius Caesar, as Suetonius and Josephus count: Julius, Augustus, Tiberius, Gaius, Claudius, and Nero, the sixth who "is." The seventh, who "must continue a short space," is Galba, whose reign of some seven months was the briefest to that point. On this count the starting point follows a recognized first-century convention rather than a choice made to fit the result, and John writes under Nero, with the temple in Jerusalem still standing. Gentry calls the eighth king of verse 11 "a difficulty" and identifies him with Vespasian, imperial power risen from the dead after the civil wars. Robinson, who argued that the whole New Testament was written before AD 70, reads the seven kings against the emperors of 68–69 and dates the book c. AD 68, in the convulsion of the year of the four emperors. On this reading Revelation 17 is one of the two internal anchors, with the temple of Revelation 11, of a date before the fall of Jerusalem.

A count the sources do not fix, with an "eighth" that looks back on Nero

The ancient lists diverge, and the eighth king who "was, and is not" presupposes Nero's death, so the passage can be about Nero without being written under him.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy, 1993).4

Bauckham sets out how the ancient enumerations of the Caesars differ among themselves: Suetonius, Josephus, the eagle vision of 4 Ezra, and the fifth Sibylline Oracle each number the succession differently, and none was written to settle Revelation 17. At least three defensible counts follow from the passage, giving Nero, Galba, or Vespasian as the reigning sixth, and the text itself does not say which to use. On this reading the key clue lies in verse 11 and its companion in 17:8: the beast "was, and is not, and shall ascend out of the bottomless pit," and "is the eighth, and is of the seven." Bauckham reads this as the Nero-redivivus legend, the expectation after Nero's suicide in 68 that he had not truly died or would return from the East. A beast that "was, and is not" is described from a vantage at which Nero is already dead, and the returning tyrant as a demonic eighth king belongs to the developed form of the legend. Bauckham argues that the beast is modelled on Nero throughout and that 666 ciphers "Nero Caesar" in Hebrew, and he dates the book under Domitian, because it looks back on Nero through the legend. On this reading the question whether Revelation is about Nero and the question whether it was written under Nero are distinct, and Revelation 17 answers the first.

Seven as the number of completeness, not a regnal census

The seven kings stand for the whole of Roman imperial power running to its appointed end, and verse 10 fixes no calendar date.

Held by: David Aune (Word Biblical Commentary, 1998); G. K. Beale (NIGTC, 1999); Craig Koester (Anchor Yale Bible, 2014).5

Seven structures the whole book as the number of completeness: seven churches, seals, trumpets, bowls, and spirits. The beast's heads and horns are drawn from the four beasts of Daniel 7, where heads and horns are figures of successive world-powers ("the ten horns out of this kingdom are ten kings," 7:24) rather than a ledger of named individuals. Read in that idiom, "five are fallen, and one is, and the other is not yet come" describes the fullness of the imperial succession: most of it past, part of it present, a brief remainder still to come before the end. The very next verse adds an eighth king who "is of the seven," a figure that does not fit a literal count of exactly seven rulers and that marks the arithmetic as symbolic rather than Suetonian. On this reading the passage interprets Rome's power theologically, as a complete and doomed succession, and the seven kings date nothing precisely; the book's date has to be settled on other grounds.
