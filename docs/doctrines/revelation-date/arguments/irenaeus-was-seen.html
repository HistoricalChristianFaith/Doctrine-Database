Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Irenaeus

Related readings: Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him? · Does Epiphanius's "Claudius Caesar" date witness an early (pre-Domitianic) date for Revelation?

The passage

"We will not, however, incur the risk of pronouncing positively as to the name of Antichrist; for if it were necessary that his name should be distinctly revealed in this present time, it would have been announced by him who beheld the apocalyptic vision. For that was seen no very long time since, but almost in our day, towards the end of Domitian's reign."

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 5.30.3 (c. 180), trans. ANF, New Advent.1

The question

Whether the subject of "was seen" is the apocalyptic vision, so that Irenaeus dates the seeing of Revelation to the end of Domitian's reign (c. 95–96), or John himself, seen alive in Domitian's day. Because the later Fathers who name Domitian are often traced to this sentence, how it is read bears on the whole external case for the late date.2

Background: the Greek and Latin of the sentence

Irenaeus wrote in Greek, and this section survives in Greek only where Eusebius quotes it: the verb is ἐωράθη (heōrathē), aorist passive third-person singular, "he/she/it was seen." The verb marks no gender, so its subject is supplied from context; the nearest noun, "the apocalyptic vision," and the person of the previous clause, "him who beheld," are both grammatically available, and the verb is used elsewhere of a person being seen. The old Latin version renders it visum est ("it was seen"), a neuter form, while apocalypsis is feminine in both Latin and Greek.3 A separate dispute concerns which John saw the vision: Dionysius of Alexandria assigned the Apocalypse to a John other than the apostle (see the two Johns); that question concerns authorship, and Dionysius did not redate the book.

The vision was seen at the end of Domitian's reign

Irenaeus dates the apocalyptic vision itself to the close of Domitian's reign, and so dates the book.

Held by: Eusebius (bishop of Caesarea, Church History 3.18.3, early 4th c.); G. K. Beale (The Book of Revelation, 1999); Mark Hitchcock (2005); J. A. T. Robinson (1976) grants the construal; rejects the Domitianic date.4

Irenaeus's argument is that had the Antichrist's name needed publishing, the seer would have published it, and the recency clause explains why the name need not be guessed now: the revelation came "almost in our day." On this reading the clause does its work only if what "was seen… almost in our day" is the vision, its content still fresh and reliable; that John was alive under Domitian would not bear on whether his book named the Antichrist. Beale holds that "the Apocalypse" is the proper subject of "was seen," as virtually all of Irenaeus's ancient readers understood. Eusebius quotes the sentence as his proof that John beheld the apocalyptic vision on Patmos under Domitian, and Jerome passes the same dating to the Latin West; on this reading the vision-as-subject construal is the one the sentence carried in antiquity. Robinson, who dates Revelation c. 68, reads Irenaeus the same way: he takes the testimony to date the vision to Domitian's reign and weighs it as evidence rather than re-construing it. Hitchcock defends this construal at length against Gentry.

John himself was seen, alive, late in Domitian's reign

The subject of "was seen" is John, who lived on into Domitian's day, so the sentence dates the seer's lifetime rather than his vision.

Held by: S. H. Chase (⚠ unverified, as cited by Gentry); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989); Dean Furlong (The Identity of John the Evangelist, 2020).5

The verb heōrathē can mean a person "was seen," and the person of the preceding clause, "him who beheld the apocalyptic vision," lies to hand as its subject. Read so, Irenaeus says that John was seen "no very long time since, but almost in our day, towards the end of Domitian's reign," and leaves the date of the vision itself open. Furlong presses that the Latin visum est is neuter and cannot agree with the feminine apocalypsis, so the Latin version does not establish the vision as the subject. Gentry argues that the sentence therefore need not date the writing, which on his internal evidence came decades earlier, under Nero; he adds that Irenaeus is elsewhere an unreliable chronicler, holding that Jesus lived to nearly fifty. Furlong develops the reading further, holding that John survived into the reign of Nerva or Trajan; on this reading the sentence belongs with the tradition of John's long life at Ephesus, a man still seen in Domitian's day.
