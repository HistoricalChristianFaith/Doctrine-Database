Does the Syriac subscription naming Nero evidence an early (Neronian) date for Revelation?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: the Syriac subscription; see also Moses Stuart and Kenneth Gentry

Related readings: Does Epiphanius's "Claudius Caesar" date witness an early (pre-Domitianic) date for Revelation? · Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign? · Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him?

The passage

The Revelation which was made by God to John the Evangelist on the island of Patmos, into which he was thrown by Nero Caesar.

— the Syriac title of the Apocalypse, in the fuller English form that circulates through the modern early-date literature (Kenneth Gentry). Moses Stuart, quoting the printed Syriac title page directly, gives it as "…written in Patmos, whither John was sent by Nero Caesar."1

The question

Whether the heading carried with the Apocalypse in the Syriac tradition, which names Nero as the emperor who banished John to Patmos, transmits an independent early memory of a Neronian exile or records what Syriac Christians believed when the heading was written. Greek and Latin writers name Domitian (or, in Epiphanius, Claudius), so the heading's weight bears on the external case for the early date.

Background: the heading and its edition

The substance of the heading (Patmos… by Nero Caesar) is stable across the reports, though the English forms vary from writer to writer ("thrown," "sent," "cast," "exiled"). Stuart quotes it from the title page of the printed Syriac Apocalypse and traces that edition: the text published by L. de Dieu in 1627 from the Scaliger/Leyden manuscript, and thence carried into the London and Paris Polyglots. John Gwynn's critical edition of a different Syriac copy, from the Crawford manuscript (1897), prints the Syriac text and a reconstructed Greek but no English rendering of the heading, its opening leaves being damaged.2 Revelation is absent from the Peshitta and was supplied in the later Syriac versions, the Philoxenian (507) and the Harklean (616). Ephrem the Syrian (d. c. 373) appeals to the Apocalypse.3 The Syriac History of John the son of Zebedee likewise places the exile under Nero.4

A Syriac tradition of a Neronian exile, corroborating the early date

The heading preserves a line of tradition, distinct from the Greek and Latin one, that remembered John's exile as Neronian.

Held by: Moses Stuart (Commentary on the Apocalypse, 1845); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989).5

The heading is a genuine title of the printed Syriac Apocalypse, quoted from its title page and traceable to a named manuscript and edition. It is explicit: it names the island, the apostle, and the emperor. It is the earliest notice outside the book to set the exile under Nero by name, and it does not stand alone in its own tradition, for the Syriac History of John also places the exile under Nero. The Syriac text of Revelation itself circulated well before the sixth-century versions, as Ephrem's use of the book indicates. For Stuart, who held a date near the end of Nero's reign, and for Gentry, the heading is the one ancient external witness that agrees with the internal evidence of the book: the temple still standing in chapter 11, the seven kings of chapter 17, and the number of the beast. A Christian community at the eastern edge of the Roman world, with its own transmission of the apostle's story, set down Nero as the emperor who banished John. On this reading the subscription gives the early date an ancient external attestation of its own, independent of the Greek and Latin notices that name Domitian.

A sixth–seventh-century heading, recording the belief of its time

The heading attests what Syriac Christians believed by the sixth century; its own descent from an earlier tradition cannot be traced.

Held by: Mark Hitchcock (The End Times Controversy, 2003; Dallas Theological Seminary dissertation, 2005).6

Hitchcock calls the heading "the first clear, accepted, unambiguous witness to the Neronic date" and places it in "two Syriac versions of the New Testament in the sixth and seventh centuries." Since Revelation entered the Syriac New Testament only with the Philoxenian and Harklean versions, a Syriac Apocalypse bearing this heading belongs to that period, some five centuries after John and four after Irenaeus. Ephrem's earlier use of the book dates the text's circulation, not the heading. Hitchcock argues that had the early date been the true one, some earlier and more local witness, in Asia Minor where the book was written, ought to survive. A heading is also the most fluid part of a manuscript: H. C. Hoskier catalogued dozens of divergent prologues to Revelation in the Greek tradition, and such titles were added and altered by copyists. A scribe could have reasoned back from the book's imagery (the number of the beast, the wounded and healed head) to a Neronian setting, and the heading itself does not show whether it rests on such an inference or on received memory. On this reading the subscription witnesses that some Syriac Christians, by the sixth century, held the exile to be Neronian, and the date of the book is to be settled from earlier testimony and from the text.
