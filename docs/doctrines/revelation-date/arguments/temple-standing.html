Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 11; see also Kenneth Gentry

Related readings: Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign? · Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign?

The passage

"And there was given me a reed like unto a rod: and the angel stood, saying, Rise, and measure the temple of God, and the altar, and them that worship therein. But the court which is without the temple leave out, and measure it not; for it is given unto the Gentiles: and the holy city shall they tread under foot forty and two months."

— Revelation 11:1–2 (KJV); and 11:8, which calls the city "the great city, which spiritually is called Sodom and Egypt, where also our Lord was crucified."1

The question

Whether the temple, altar and worshippers John is told to measure are the Herodian temple in Jerusalem, still standing as he wrote, and whether a passage with that outlook dates the composition of the whole book. The answer bears on the internal case for a date before the temple's destruction in August AD 70.

The Jerusalem temple, still standing as John writes

John is told to measure a functioning temple in a city under threat but not yet taken, so he writes before AD 70.

Held by: Moses Stuart (Commentary on the Apocalypse, 1845); J. A. T. Robinson (Anglican, Redating the New Testament, 1976); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989; "What Temple Appears in Revelation 11?", 2024).2

Read at its surface, the passage describes a sanctuary with an altar and worshippers, an outer court given over to the nations, and a "holy city" trodden down for forty-two months. Verse 8 pins that city to history as the place "where also our Lord was crucified." Gentry presses three supports. "The holy city" is a fixed biblical name for Jerusalem (Isaiah 48:2; Matthew 4:5). The treading down of 11:2 echoes Jesus' own prophecy that "Jerusalem shall be trodden down of the Gentiles" (Luke 21:24). And the forty-two months answer to the span of the Jewish War, from spring AD 67 to the temple's burning in August 70. If John wrote c. AD 95, a vision of measuring a temple that had lain in ruins for twenty-five years would be, in Gentry's phrase, "terribly anachronous." Robinson, who argued that the whole New Testament was written before 70, holds that the oracle "must bespeak a pre-70 situation." The same argument runs through Stuart and the preterist line, for whom a date before 70 lets the "great tribulation" of Revelation be the Roman war and the destruction of Jerusalem. On this reading Revelation 11 is one of the two internal anchors, with the seven kings of chapter 17, of a date before the temple fell.

A pre-70 oracle taken up into a later book

Verses 1–2 preserve an older Jewish prophecy from before 70, which John reworked into a book written later.

Held by: Julius Wellhausen, who proposed it; David Aune (Word Biblical Commentary, 1998).3

Wellhausen identified Revelation 11:1–2 as a fragment of a Jewish (Zealot) oracle uttered as Jerusalem's fall drew near, promising that the Romans would take the outer court and the city but not the inner sanctuary. Aune adopts the hypothesis and lists its marks in the text. The passage is introduced abruptly, without the book's usual "I saw" or "I heard" and with an unspecified speaker. The command to measure is never carried out. The Jerusalem temple is mentioned nowhere else in the book and is otherwise no concern of its author. And nothing is said of the temple's destruction, only that the outer court and the city are overrun, which is what a prophecy spoken before the event would expect. On this account the concrete, historical outlook of the verses belongs to inherited material, and the date of the oracle and the date of the book that quotes it are separate questions. Aune reads the oracle as genuinely pre-70 and dates the finished Apocalypse to c. AD 95, in the reign of Domitian.

A symbolic temple: the people of God measured for preservation

The temple, altar and worshippers stand for the people of God, whom John is told to measure as a sign of their protection.

Held by: G. K. Beale (NIGTC, 1999); Sam Storms ("Revelation 11:1–13").4

Beale observes that the Greek word for the sanctuary, naós, is used everywhere else in Revelation for the heavenly temple (7:15; 11:19; 14:15, 17; 15:5–8; 16:1, 17) or the eschatological one (3:12; 21:22), and that the only other occurrence of the exact phrase "temple of God" is 11:19, the heavenly sanctuary opened eighteen verses later. The New Testament repeatedly calls the people of God a temple (1 Corinthians 3:16–17; 2 Corinthians 6:16; Ephesians 2:19–22; 1 Peter 2:5; Revelation 3:12), and the act of measuring comes from Ezekiel 40–48, where an angel measures a temple in vision. Measuring "them that worship therein" with a rod fits people figuratively, as a sealing for spiritual preservation. The forty-two months is the "time, times, and half a time" of Daniel 7:25 and 12:7, a period the surrounding chapters use three more times: the two witnesses prophesy 1,260 days (11:3), the woman is nourished 1,260 days (12:6), and the beast has authority forty-two months (13:5). The book closes by describing a city with no temple at all, "for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it" (21:22). Storms reads the whole of 11:1–13 of the Church in the present age. On this reading the chapter portrays the protected people of God and their trial among the nations, and it fixes no date for the book.
