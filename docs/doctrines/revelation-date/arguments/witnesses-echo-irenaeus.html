Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Irenaeus; see also Clement of Alexandria, Victorinus of Pettau, Eusebius and Jerome

Related readings: Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign? · Does the Syriac subscription naming Nero evidence an early (Neronian) date for Revelation?

The passage

Irenaeus (c. 180), as Eusebius quotes him:

almost in our own generation, at the end of the reign of Domitian

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200), of the apostle John:

on the tyrant's death, he returned to Ephesus from the isle of Patmos

Victorinus of Pettau (c. 300), of John:

…he was in the island of Patmos, condemned to the labour of the mines by Cæsar Domitian.

— Eusebius, Church History 3.18.3; Clement, Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved? 42; Victorinus, Commentary on the Apocalypse, on Revelation 10:11 (trans. NPNF and ANF).1

The question

Whether the writers after Irenaeus who connect John's exile or vision with Domitian are independent witnesses to that date, or pass on Irenaeus's sentence of c. 180. The answer bears on how broad the external attestation for the Domitianic date is, and so on how much of the external case rests on Irenaeus alone.

Background: the later notices

Clement of Alexandria tells of John returning from Patmos "on the tyrant's death" but names no emperor; Eusebius, quoting the passage, identifies the tyrant as Domitian.2 Hegesippus (c. 150–180), as quoted by Eusebius, tells of Domitian summoning the grandsons of Jude and then staying the persecution; the fragment does not mention John, Patmos, or the Apocalypse.3 Victorinus, in the earliest extant commentary on Revelation, names Domitian and adds the labour in the mines, a detail absent from Irenaeus.4 Eusebius (c. 324) adduces Irenaeus's sentence as his proof that John saw the vision on Patmos under Domitian, and Jerome (c. 393) gives the same account in the Latin West.5

The later Domitianic notices derive from Irenaeus

Once the writers who pass on Irenaeus are set aside, the external evidence for a date in 95–96 is Irenaeus's single sentence.

Held by: Moses Stuart (Commentary on the Apocalypse, 1845); Thomas Randell (The Pulpit Commentary, 1880s); Milton Terry (Biblical Apocalyptics, 1898); Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989).6

The list of patristic witnesses to a Domitianic date looks long, but on this reading it is a chorus repeating one testimony. Eusebius does not corroborate Irenaeus so much as quote him: the sentence "at the end of the reign of Domitian" is his proof. Jerome then carries the Eusebian frame into Latin. Randell's summary is that "Eusebius and Jerome, in the fourth century, do not strengthen what they merely repeat." Clement names no emperor at all; the name Domitian is attached to his "tyrant" by Eusebius. Hegesippus speaks of a Domitianic persecution but says nothing of John or the Apocalypse. Once these are accounted for, the external evidence for 95–96 reduces to Irenaeus himself. Terry, weighing the witnesses, judged that "no great stress" can rest on the external testimony once its derivative character is seen, and that the whole of the evidence "excepting the statement of Irenaeus" favours the early date. Albert A. Bell Jr. states that Irenaeus's statement "is the only direct evidence for dating the Apocalypse to 95–6." On this reading the external case for the late date is a single sentence of c. 180, and the question of Revelation's date turns on what that sentence means, how reliable it is, and on the internal evidence of the book.

A tradition broader than one source

Clement and Victorinus attest the exile tradition independently of Irenaeus, and the later reception adds weight of its own.

Held by: G. K. Beale (NIGTC, 1999); Mark Hitchcock (Dallas Theological Seminary dissertation, 2005).7

Clement, writing at Alexandria c. 200, close in date to Irenaeus and in a different tradition, independently attests the story of John's exile to Patmos and his release on the tyrant's death, the story the Domitianic chronology presupposes. Clement is not repeating Irenaeus's sentence about when the vision was seen; he tells the story of John's return to Ephesus from Patmos. Victorinus, writing in the Latin West before Eusebius, names Domitian in his commentary on Revelation and adds a concrete detail, the labour of the mines, that Irenaeus never gave, which marks him as drawing on a strand of tradition beyond Irenaeus's words. Hegesippus, earlier than Irenaeus, attests a persecution under Domitian, the setting in which an exile to Patmos fits. On this reading the external case for the late date is an early witness, an independent Alexandrian account of the exile, an independent Latin commentator who names Domitian, and a reception through Eusebius and Jerome that carried the date forward.
