Does Epiphanius's "Claudius Caesar" date witness an early (pre-Domitianic) date for Revelation?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: Does the Syriac subscription naming Nero evidence an early (Neronian) date for Revelation? · Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign? · Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him?

The passage

the Holy Spirit compelled John to issue the Gospel in his old age when he was past ninety, after his return from Patmos under Claudius Caesar, and several years of his residence in Asia.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 51.12,2 (trans. Frank Williams); paired with 51.33,9, where John "prophesied… during the time of Claudius Caesar and earlier, when he was on the isle of Patmos."1

The question

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375), refuting the Alogi (who rejected John's Gospel and Apocalypse), twice places John's exile and prophecy on Patmos "under Claudius Caesar," where Irenaeus, Victorinus, Eusebius and Jerome name Domitian. What the notice records (a tradition of exile under the emperor Claudius, AD 41–54; a reference to Nero under his regnal name; or a confusion) is disputed, and bears on whether the ancient testimony to the book's date speaks with one voice.

A tradition of John's exile under the emperor Claudius

The notice preserves a received tradition that John was exiled to Patmos, and prophesied there, under Claudius.

Held by: Epiphanius (bishop of Salamis, Panarion, c. 375); Apringius of Beja (Tractatus in Apocalypsin, 531–548).2

Epiphanius gives the date twice, in two separate places of a work of controversy, and the second time he extends it: John prophesied "during the time of Claudius Caesar and earlier." His concern is to defend the apostolic origin of John's writings against the Alogi, and the date is stated as part of that defence. His notice stands apart from the Domitianic line that runs from Irenaeus. The same date reappears in the Latin West two centuries later: Apringius of Beja, commenting on Revelation 1:9, writes that "the ecclesiastical writers have taught that at the time of Claudius Caesar, when that famine which the prophet Agabus had announced in the Acts of the Apostles would come in ten years time was at its height, that during that difficulty this same Caesar, impelled by his usual vanity, had instituted a persecution of the churches," and that "it was during this time that he ordered John, the apostle of our Lord, Jesus Christ, to be transported into exile." Apringius presents the Claudian exile as the teaching of earlier church writers and sets it within a dated Claudian persecution, so on this reading a Claudius strand of tradition persisted beside the Domitianic one, in both Greek and Latin, and Epiphanius is one of its witnesses.

"Claudius Caesar" as Nero, a witness to the Neronian date

"Claudius Caesar" designates Nero, whose regnal name included it, so the notice witnesses a Neronian exile.

Held by: F. J. A. Hort (Anglican, The Apocalypse of St John I–III, published 1908)3; Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989), with other early-date writers.4

Nero's full imperial name was Nero Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus, and ancient writers sometimes name emperors by a subsidiary name; Hort notes that Nero "is often called on inscriptions Nero Claudius or Nero Claudius Caesar," and that "whatever Epiphanius may have meant, his authority meant and perhaps said Nero."3 Read so, Epiphanius places John's exile under Nero, the emperor in whose reign early-date advocates set the book on internal grounds: the "sixth king" who "is" (Revelation 17:10), the temple still standing (11:1–2), and the number of the beast. On any reading the notice is pre-Domitianic, so Epiphanius supplies a fourth-century patristic voice that does not say Domitian, and the Fathers are not unanimous for the late date. With the Syriac subscription, which names Nero outright, it gives the early date an external witness from antiquity. On this reading the regnal name brings Epiphanius's notice into line with the internal evidence for the mid-60s.

A chronological confusion

The notice is a slip or garbled tradition, not a considered rival reckoning of the book's date.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.5

On this reading "Claudius Caesar" is the emperor Claudius (41–54), which sets John's Patmos vision before most of the seven churches the book addresses had long been founded. The chronology of the passage is strained in itself: Epiphanius has John, "past ninety," write the Gospel only after returning from this Claudian exile, which would place the Fourth Gospel very early. On this reading Epiphanius is a writer often inaccurate in chronological detail (⚠ unverified), and the notice is one more such slip. Epiphanius writes "Claudius Caesar" without qualification, and elsewhere in the Panarion he names Nero as Nero (James "[He lived] until the time of Nero"),5 so the passage gives no sign of Nero's longer regnal name. The notice then records what one fourth-century writer believed, within a garbled chronology, rather than a separate memory of the date.
