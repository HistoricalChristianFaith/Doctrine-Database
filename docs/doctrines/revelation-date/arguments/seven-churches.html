Does the settled condition of the seven churches (Laodicea's wealth) require a late date?

Disputed reading · on the date of Revelation · witness: Revelation 2–3; see also Kenneth Gentry

Related readings: Does Revelation's emperor-worship setting fit Domitian's imperial cult, dating the book to the 90s? · Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date?

The passage

"And unto the angel of the church of the Laodiceans write… Because thou sayest, I am rich, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked."

— Revelation 3:14, 17 (KJV).1

The question

Whether the condition of the seven churches of Revelation 2–3 (Laodicea's boast of wealth after the earthquake of c. AD 60, Ephesus's lost "first love," and the age of the church at Smyrna) places the letters a generation after Paul's mission, in Domitian's reign, or fits churches of the mid-60s. The answer bears on the internal case for the late date.

Background: Laodicea's earthquake

Tacitus records: "One of the famous cities of Asia, Laodicea, was that same year overthrown by an earthquake, and, without any relief from us, recovered itself by its own resources." Paul's mission in Asia, from Ephesus, falls in the 50s (Acts 19).2

Mature, long-established churches, fitting the 90s

The letters presuppose churches decades old and in places declined, which fits the reign of Domitian better than the mid-60s.

Held by: James Moffatt (Introduction to the Literature of the New Testament, 1911); Colin Hemer (The Letters to the Seven Churches, 1986), on the setting as a whole; Leon Morris (Tyndale commentary, 1987); Kim Riddlebarger (Reformed, 2023).3

The churches of Revelation 2–3 have internal factions (the Nicolaitans, "Jezebel"), settled reputations, and in places a spiritual fatigue that reads as the waning of an initial fervour. Laodicea is the flagship instance. Morris presses it: the church was "rich, and increased with goods" (3:17), "but as the city was destroyed by an earthquake in AD 60/61 this must have been considerably later." Riddlebarger argues that "a recovery to the wealthy conditions described by John… would seemingly take decades, not the three or four years required by a pre-A.D. 70 date." Ephesus is charged with having "left its first love" (2:4), read as implying a generation since Paul planted it. Moffatt takes Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians 11:3, where the Philippians are those "in the midst of whom the blessed Paul laboured" and Polycarp writes "we [of Smyrna] had not yet known Him," to mean that Smyrna had no church in Paul's day. Hemer, who sets the letters against their archaeological and epigraphic background, judges the whole situation of the churches to fit a Domitianic setting, while warning that many allusions are "to factors operative in the cities over considerable periods of their history" and resting his date chiefly on the emperor cult. Read together, on this reading, several mature-church features converge on a date a generation after the apostle.

Churches already established and prosperous by the mid-60s

Laodicea's swift, unaided recovery and the churches' founding in the 50s fit a letter written under Nero.

Held by: Kenneth Gentry (Reformed preterist, Before Jerusalem Fell, 1989; "Is Laodicea a Problem for Revelation's Date?", 2015).4

Gentry reads Tacitus's notice as evidence of a swift recovery: a city wealthy enough to rebuild without imperial relief had its resources in hand at once, and the quake left some four years before a mid-60s date for Revelation, time enough to rebuild. The rebuke of 3:17 also concerns the church rather than the whole city. And the "riches" may be spiritual, not material at all: Gentry cites R. C. Trench and Robert Mounce, a late-date commentator, who writes that "the 'wealth' claimed by the Laodicean church… was not material but spiritual," a reading that sets the boast against the letter's reversal ("wretched, and miserable, and poor") and its counsel to buy "gold tried in the fire." The church at Corinth offers a parallel for such spiritual pride. On this reading the other letters fit the 60s as well: Paul planted the Ephesian church c. 52–55 and already foresaw defection among its elders (Acts 20:29–30), so a congregation ten to fifteen years old could have cooled; the Polycarp clause on Smyrna survives only in the Latin version, the Greek being lost at that point. The churches, on this reading, were established and troubled congregations by Nero's reign.
