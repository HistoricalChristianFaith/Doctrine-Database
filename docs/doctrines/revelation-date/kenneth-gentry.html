Kenneth L. Gentry Jr. on the Date of Revelation

Dates: 1989 (Before Jerusalem Fell) · Wikipedia
Summary: A book-length case for the early (Neronian) date. Gentry argues Revelation was written c. AD 65–66, under Nero and before Jerusalem's fall, resting the case on internal evidence — the temple still standing, the "sixth king" as Nero — and reading the subject of Irenaeus's "was seen" as John himself rather than the vision.

Claims & evidence

The internal case for a Neronian date

Gentry's central thesis is that the book's own contents date it before AD 70: Revelation 11:1–2 has John measure a temple that is still standing; Revelation 17:10's seven kings, of whom "one is," he identifies with the Julio-Claudian line down to Nero as the sixth; and the pervasive language of imminent judgment points at the coming destruction of Jerusalem. He gives internal (inspired) evidence priority over the external patristic testimony.
— Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., Before Jerusalem Fell: Dating the Book of Revelation (Tyler, TX: Institute for Christian Economics, 1989).

Gentry on the Irenaeus passage

Gentry treats Irenaeus, Against Heresies 5.30.3, as the principal external testimony for the late date, and argues three things about it: that the subject of "was seen" is ambiguous and may refer to John himself — who lived on into Domitian's day — rather than to the vision, so it need not date the writing; that Irenaeus is elsewhere an unreliable chronicler (e.g. his claim that Jesus lived to nearly fifty); and that his notices on Revelation are not wholly consistent. He enlists earlier scholars, such as S. H. Chase, for the "it was John who was seen" reading.
— Gentry, Before Jerusalem Fell (on the "was seen" construction and the reliability of Irenaeus).

Reception and debate

Each of Gentry's main arguments is set out, beside the readings of those who take the evidence differently, on its own page.

  • The temple of Revelation 11. Gentry calls a Domitianic vision of the standing temple (Rev 11:1–2) "terribly anachronous." Julius Wellhausen and David Aune read verses 1–2 as a pre-70 oracle taken up into a later book, Aune dating the finished Apocalypse c. 95; G. K. Beale reads the temple symbolically, observing that the book uses naós elsewhere of the heavenly or eschatological temple, and that 21:22 sees "no temple." See Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date?
  • The seven kings. Counting from Julius Caesar, Gentry makes Nero the reigning sixth king of Rev 17:10; he calls the "eighth… of the seven" (17:11) "a difficulty" and takes it to be Vespasian. Richard Bauckham notes that the ancient enumerations of the Caesars differ, giving Nero, Galba, or Vespasian as the sixth, and reads 17:11 as the Nero-redivivus legend, seen from after Nero's death; Aune, Beale and Craig Koester read the seven as the number of completeness. See Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign?
  • Irenaeus. Gentry, citing S. H. Chase, reads John as the subject of "was seen." Eusebius quotes the sentence as dating the vision, as G. K. Beale and the late-date commentaries read it; J. A. T. Robinson, who shares Gentry's early date, also reads Irenaeus as dating the vision to Domitian's reign and dates the book c. 68 on other grounds. See Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign?
  • Gentry's interpretive framework. Gentry writes as a postmillennial preterist, for whom an early date allows much of Revelation to be read as fulfilled in AD 70.
