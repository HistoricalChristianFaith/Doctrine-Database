Moses Stuart and the nineteenth-century early-date scholars on the Date of Revelation

Dates: 1845 (Stuart's Commentary on the Apocalypse) · Wikipedia
Summary: Moses Stuart argued the early (Neronian) date at length in America in 1845, drawing on German critics; in England the Cambridge scholars Westcott, Lightfoot, and Hort, and F. W. Farrar, dated the book to the years around Nero's death.

Claims & evidence

Stuart: Revelation was written c. AD 68, before Jerusalem fell

In his two-volume Commentary on the Apocalypse (Andover / New York, 1845), Moses Stuart argued for a date near the end of Nero's reign (c. AD 68) and before the fall of Jerusalem in 70, reading much of the book as a prophecy of that fall and of Rome's own convulsions. Drawing on the German critics Eichhorn, Ewald, and Lücke, he pressed that the visions had to be intelligible to their first-century readers — which, he held, pointed to the Neronian setting.
— Moses Stuart, A Commentary on the Apocalypse, 2 vols. (Andover: Allen, Morrill & Wardwell, 1845), Introduction.

The Cambridge line: Westcott, Lightfoot, Hort, and Farrar

The same early date was held by the leading English New Testament scholars of the era. B. F. Westcott, J. B. Lightfoot, and F. J. A. Hort each assigned the Apocalypse to Nero's reign or the years immediately following — an agreement among the three noted by Robert H. Mounce. F. W. Farrar gave the fullest popular case in The Early Days of Christianity (1882), reading the beast and its numbered name against Nero and the turmoil of AD 68–69.
— on the Cambridge trio, see Robert H. Mounce, The Book of Revelation (NICNT; Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1977), 32; F. W. Farrar, The Early Days of Christianity (London: Cassell, 1882), ch. xxviii.

Reception and debate

  • The early date in nineteenth-century scholarship. Internal-evidence dating made the Neronian date a leading scholarly position for much of the nineteenth century (⚠ unverified — no survey of the field cited). The commentaries of David Aune, G. K. Beale and Craig Koester (1997–2014) date the book under Domitian; see the late-date commentaries.
  • Stuart's other arguments. Stuart reads the measured temple of Revelation 11 as still standing when John wrote, adduces the Syriac heading naming Nero, and holds that the later Fathers' Domitianic notices derive from Irenaeus.
