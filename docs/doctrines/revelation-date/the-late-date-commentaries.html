The late-date commentaries on the Date of Revelation

Dates: 1997–2014 (Aune, Beale, Koester) · Wikipedia
Summary: The reference commentaries of David Aune, G. K. Beale, and Craig Koester each set out both cases and date the book c. AD 95–96, under Domitian; Aune allows that earlier material was folded into the final book.

Claims & evidence

Aune: a final edition c. 95, over earlier material

David Aune's three-volume Word Biblical Commentary holds the standard late date (c. 95, under Domitian) but by way of a two-edition theory: a first edition as early as c. AD 70, incorporating still older material, expanded into a final edition near the end of the century. Aune thus allows that Neronian-era material may underlie the book while dating its finished form to Domitian.
— David E. Aune, Revelation, Word Biblical Commentary 52A–C (Dallas: Word, 1997–98), Introduction, § "Date."

Beale: the imperial-cult setting and a Domitianic date

G. K. Beale's New International Greek Testament Commentary lays out both cases and settles on the Domitianic date, arguing among other things that the systematic pressure of the imperial cult that Revelation presupposes fits the end of the century better than Nero's reign, whose persecution was tied to the fire of AD 64 rather than to refusal of emperor-worship.
— G. K. Beale, The Book of Revelation, New International Greek Testament Commentary (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1999), Introduction.

Koester: c. 95–96, Domitianic

Craig Koester's Anchor Yale Bible commentary dates the book c. AD 95–96 under Domitian, setting it against the Greco-Roman and Jewish-Christian world of the late first century, with the imperial cult and the social and economic tensions of the Asian cities as its backdrop.
— Craig R. Koester, Revelation: A New Translation with Introduction and Commentary, Anchor Yale Bible 38A (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2014), Introduction.

Reception and debate

  • The grounds of the late date. Aune, Beale and Koester each date the book c. 95–96. These commentaries rest the late date on the external patristic testimony (Irenaeus and the later Fathers who name Domitian) together with the imperial-cult setting and the condition of the seven churches; each of these is set out with its rival readings on its own page (see Irenaeus's "was seen", the imperial cult, and the seven churches).
  • Different grounds within the late date. Aune allows early strata, Beale and Koester argue chiefly from the imperial-cult setting, and Yarbro Collins and Thompson argue against the older picture of a Domitianic persecution while keeping the late date. The agreement is on the date, not on its grounds.
