The Council of Trent on the veneration of relics

Dates: Session XXV, 3–4 December 1563 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Catholic charter for relic-veneration, issued in the same decree that reaffirmed the sacred images. Trent teaches that the bodies of the martyrs and saints are to be venerated, condemns those who deny it — and, conceding the Reformers' factual complaint, orders the abuses and superstitions of the relic-trade purged.

Claims & evidence

The bodies of the saints are to be venerated; those who deny it are condemned

Against the Reformers, Trent defines that the relics of the saints — once temples of the Holy Ghost, destined for resurrection — are to be honoured by the faithful, and anathematizes the contrary teaching.1

“The holy bodies of holy martyrs, and of others now living with Christ,—which bodies were the living members of Christ, and the temple of the Holy Ghost…—are to be venerated by the faithful; through which (bodies) many benefits are bestowed by God on men; so that they who affirm that veneration and honour are not due to the relics of saints… are wholly to be condemned, as the Church has already long since condemned, and now also condemns them.”

— Council of Trent, Session XXV (1563), “On the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images.” The same decree grounds the veneration of images in the “prototype” principle and Nicaea II (see the images half of the same decree).

Abuses and superstition are to be removed

Trent conceded the Reformers' factual point about the relic-trade — the frauds and profiteering Calvin had catalogued — charging the bishops to root out every superstition and “filthy lucre” from the cult while keeping the veneration itself.2

“All superstition shall be removed, all filthy lucre be abolished; finally, all lasciviousness be avoided…”

— Session XXV (1563). Trent also required that no new relics be admitted unless recognized and approved by the bishop — an attempt to answer the fraud problem by regulation rather than abolition.

Notes

  • The confessional divide fixed. Where Calvin read the honouring of relics as idolatry, Trent re-canonized the ancient practice for the Latin West and condemned the denial — while granting that the abuses attacked were real. The Catholic–Protestant split on relics dates from here.
  • Whether “veneration and honour” of relics is a lawful dulia or the idolatry the Reformers charged is weighed at “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?” (assessed contested).
  • This is the very decree whose title welds relics to images — “On the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images” — which is why the two doctrines are near neighbours on this wiki (see the veneration of images and icons). It also treats the invocation of the saints (see the invocation of the saints).
