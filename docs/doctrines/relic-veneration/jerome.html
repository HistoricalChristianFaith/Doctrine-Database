Jerome (Against Vigilantius) on the veneration of relics

Dates: Against Vigilantius written 406 (Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome's treatise defending the honouring of relics against the Gallic priest Vigilantius, whom it quotes at length. Vigilantius described the honouring of relics as all but a heathen ceremony brought into the churches; Jerome answered that Christians do not worship the martyrs but honour their remains, that emperors and bishops carry relics without sacrilege, and that the relics put demons to flight.

Claims & evidence

Vigilantius: kissing and adoring “a bit of powder” is a heathen ceremony

Vigilantius — a priest of Calagurris in Gaul, whose diocesan bishop, according to the editor’s introduction in the NPNF translation, was Exsuperius of Toulouse, “strongly in favour of the views of Vigilantius” — wrote a pamphlet charging that the cult of relics imported pagan practice into the church: the faithful kiss and worship a scrap of dust in a costly cloth, and light tapers by broad daylight for the martyrs. Jerome quotes him directly.1

“Why do you kiss and adore a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth?” … “Under the cloak of religion we see what is all but a heathen ceremony introduced into the churches: while the sun is still shining, heaps of tapers are lighted, and everywhere a paltry bit of powder, wrapped up in a costly cloth, is kissed and worshipped.”

— Vigilantius, as quoted in Jerome, Against Vigilantius 4. In Jerome's earlier Letter 109 (404), Vigilantius is reported as calling those who cherish the relics “ashmongers and idolaters who pay homage to dead men's bones” (Letter 109, §1).

Jerome: no one worships the martyrs — the honour redounds to Christ

Jerome replies that veneration is not adoration: no Christian takes the martyr for God, and the honour paid the relics passes to the Lord whose martyrs they are.2

“Madman, who in the world ever adored the martyrs? who ever thought man was God?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 5. Answering the charge that the honour terminates in the creature, Jerome holds that the martyrs are honoured for the sake of their Lord: when Israel honoured Samuel “they were forsooth, adoring Samuel and not Christ, whose Levite and prophet Samuel was” — the honour of the servant referred to the Master.

Jerome: emperors and bishops carry relics without sacrilege

Against the idolatry charge Jerome appeals to the practice of the church, up to its emperors and the bishop of Rome: if venerating relics were sacrilege, he argues, then the emperors and bishops who carried them would stand condemned.3

“Was the Emperor Constantius… guilty of sacrilege when he transferred the sacred relics of Andrew, Luke, and Timothy to Constantinople?… And at the present day is the Emperor Arcadius guilty of sacrilege, who… has conveyed the bones of the blessed Samuel from Judea to Thrace? Are all the bishops to be considered not only sacrilegious, but silly into the bargain, because they carried that most worthless thing, dust and ashes, wrapped in silk in a golden vessel?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 5. He adds that the bishop of Rome “offers sacrifices to the Lord over the venerable bones… of Peter and Paul… and judges their tombs worthy to be Christ's altars” (§8).

Jerome: the relics put demons to flight

Jerome also argues from miracles (§10): the “worthless dust” Vigilantius mocks is, he holds, attended by signs, and the demons confess its power.4

“Answer my question—how is it that poor worthless dust and ashes are associated with this wondrous power of signs and miracles?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 10. On the tapers, Jerome grants the pagans once lit lights for idols but distinguishes intent: “In the one case respect was paid to idols, and therefore the ceremony is to be abhorred; in the other the martyrs are venerated, and the same ceremony is therefore to be allowed” (§7).

Reception and debate

  • The sources for Vigilantius. Vigilantius's own book does not survive; his words are known through Jerome's quotation of them. Objections to the honouring of relics are recorded on this timeline also from Calvin (1543).
  • Letter 109 to Riparius (404). Two years before the treatise, answering a presbyter of Aquitaine who reported Vigilantius's preaching, Jerome wrote: “We, it is true, refuse to worship or adore, I say not the relics of the martyrs, but even the sun and moon, the angels and archangels, the Cherubim and Seraphim and ‘every name that is named, not only in this world but also in that which is to come.’ … Still we honour the relics of the martyrs, that we may adore Him whose martyrs they are.”5 Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) renders the same sentence of Ad Riparium (§1) as: “We do not worship, we do not adore [non colimus, non adoramus], for fear that we should bow down to the creature rather than to the Creator, but we venerate [honoramus] the relics of the martyrs in order the better to adore Him whose martyrs they are.”6
  • Paulinus of Nola had introduced Vigilantius to Jerome (395), according to the NPNF editor’s introduction to Against Vigilantius. Jerome's other controversies are traced on this wiki under the Origenist controversy and his correspondence with Augustine.
