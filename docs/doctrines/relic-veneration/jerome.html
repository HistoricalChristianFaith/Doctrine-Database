Jerome (Against Vigilantius) on the veneration of relics

Dates: Against Vigilantius written 406 (Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: The classic patristic defence of relic-veneration — and, because Jerome quotes his opponent at length, the fullest early record of the case against it. The Gallic priest Vigilantius attacked the honouring of relics as a heathen rite dressed up as religion; Jerome answered that Christians do not worship the martyrs but honour their remains, that emperors and bishops carry relics without sacrilege, and that the relics put demons to flight.

Claims & evidence

Vigilantius: kissing and adoring “a bit of powder” is a heathen ceremony

Vigilantius — a priest of Calagurris in Gaul, backed by his bishop Exsuperius of Toulouse — wrote a pamphlet charging that the cult of relics imported pagan practice into the church: the faithful kiss and worship a scrap of dust in a costly cloth, and light tapers by broad daylight for the martyrs. Jerome quotes him directly.1

“Why do you kiss and adore a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth?” … “Under the cloak of religion we see what is all but a heathen ceremony introduced into the churches: while the sun is still shining, heaps of tapers are lighted, and everywhere a paltry bit of powder, wrapped up in a costly cloth, is kissed and worshipped.”

— Vigilantius, as quoted in Jerome, Against Vigilantius 4. His objection is precisely the charge of idolatry: bodily honour paid to dead matter is the worship of the creature.

Jerome: no one worships the martyrs — the honour redounds to Christ

Jerome's core reply denies the premise: veneration is not adoration. No Christian takes the martyr for God; the honour paid the relics passes to the Lord whose martyrs they are.2

“Madman, who in the world ever adored the martyrs? who ever thought man was God?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 5. Answering the charge that the honour terminates in the creature, Jerome insists the martyrs are honoured for the sake of their Lord: when Israel honoured Samuel “they were forsooth, adoring Samuel and not Christ, whose Levite and prophet Samuel was” — i.e. the honour of the servant is referred to the Master. This is the latria/dulia distinction in substance, though Jerome does not use that crisp formula here.

Jerome: emperors and bishops carry relics without sacrilege

Against the idolatry charge Jerome appeals to the practice of the whole church, up to its emperors and the bishop of Rome: if venerating relics were sacrilege, then Christendom's rulers and prelates stand condemned — which is absurd.3

“Was the Emperor Constantius… guilty of sacrilege when he transferred the sacred relics of Andrew, Luke, and Timothy to Constantinople?… And at the present day is the Emperor Arcadius guilty of sacrilege, who… has conveyed the bones of the blessed Samuel from Judea to Thrace? Are all the bishops to be considered not only sacrilegious, but silly into the bargain, because they carried that most worthless thing, dust and ashes, wrapped in silk in a golden vessel?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 5. He adds that the bishop of Rome “offers sacrifices to the Lord over the venerable bones… of Peter and Paul… and judges their tombs worthy to be Christ's altars” (§8) — the practice Nicaea II would later make a rule.

Jerome: the relics put demons to flight

Jerome closes the case with the miracle argument: the “worthless dust” Vigilantius mocks is attended by signs, and the demons themselves confess its power.4

“Answer my question—how is it that poor worthless dust and ashes are associated with this wondrous power of signs and miracles?”

— Jerome, Against Vigilantius 10. On the tapers, Jerome grants the pagans once lit lights for idols but distinguishes intent: “In the one case respect was paid to idols, and therefore the ceremony is to be abhorred; in the other the martyrs are venerated, and the same ceremony is therefore to be allowed” (§7).

Notes

  • A ready-made two-sided debate. Because Vigilantius's own book is lost, we know his attack only through Jerome's hostile quotation — but that quotation preserves the Protestant-sounding case (idolatry, dead matter, pagan survivals) four centuries before the Reformation. Calvin would revive it almost verbatim.
  • Whether Jerome's reply succeeds — whether venerating relics is dulia or idolatry — is weighed at “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?” (assessed contested).
  • A crisper formula sometimes attributed to Jerome — “we do not worship, we do not adore… but we venerate the relics of the martyrs” — is a later paraphrase and does not appear verbatim in the NPNF text of this treatise; the substance, however, is his (§5, quoted above).
  • Jerome and Vigilantius' patron circle overlapped with figures Jerome knew: Vigilantius had been introduced to Jerome by Paulinus of Nola. Jerome's other great controversies are traced on this wiki under the Origenist controversy and his correspondence with Augustine.
