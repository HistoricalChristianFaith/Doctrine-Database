Augustine of Hippo on the veneration of relics

Dates: c. 354–430 (City of God Book 22 written c. 426) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gives the relic-cult its two decisive early notes at once: he catalogues the miracles God worked at the newly-arrived relics of St. Stephen in Roman Africa — and he insists, sharply and repeatedly, that Christians build no temples and offer no sacrifice to the martyrs, but to God alone at their tombs. The relic is honoured; the worship is God's.

Claims & evidence

Miracles are wrought at the relics of St. Stephen

When the relics of the protomartyr Stephen reached Africa (c. 424), Augustine recorded a wave of healings and even resurrections at the shrines that held them — a blind woman given sight, a dead man raised, a crushed child restored — and reckoned nearly seventy such miracles at Hippo alone within two years.1

“When the bishop Projectus was bringing the relics of the most glorious martyr Stephen to the waters of Tibilis, a great concourse of people came to meet him at the shrine. There a blind woman entreated that she might be led to the bishop who was carrying the relics. He gave her the flowers he was carrying. She took them, applied them to her eyes, and forthwith saw.”

— Augustine, City of God 22.8. He opens the chapter granting that “even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ, whether by His sacraments or by the prayers or relics of His saints,” and closes that at Calama, where the relics had been longer, the miracles were “incomparably more” numerous.

We build no temples and offer no sacrifice to the martyrs, but to their God

Against any suggestion that this is the worship of the dead, Augustine draws the line hard: the honour paid the martyrs is not the worship due God. Christians raise memorials, not temples; the altar over a martyr's body is an altar to God, and the priest sacrifices to God, never to the martyr.2

“To our martyrs we build, not temples as if they were gods, but monuments as to dead men whose spirits live with God. Neither do we erect altars at these monuments that we may sacrifice to the martyrs, but to the one God of the martyrs and of ourselves… For it is to God, not to them, he sacrifices, though he sacrifices at their monument; for he is God's priest, not theirs.”

— Augustine, City of God 22.10. He says the same at 8.27: “we do not build temples, and ordain priests, rites, and sacrifices for these same martyrs; for they are not our gods, but their God is our God. Certainly we honour their reliquaries, as the memorials of holy men of God…” — the relics are honoured, the sacrifice reserved to God.

Notes

  • Both halves of the doctrine, from one hand. Augustine is the fullest early witness to relic-miracles and the most explicit early guard against reading them as worship of the martyrs — the same two poles (genuine power in the relics; the honour distinguished from adoration) that Jerome holds against Vigilantius, and that the later latria/dulia theology systematizes.
  • Whether “we honour the reliquaries but sacrifice only to God” is a stable distinction or a distinction without a difference is weighed at “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?” (assessed contested).
  • Notably, the young Augustine had been more reserved about miracle-claims; City of God 22.8 marks his mature conviction that miracles “have not ceased” — and his firsthand testimony, naming places, bishops, and the healed, is among the best-attested relic-miracle reports of antiquity.
