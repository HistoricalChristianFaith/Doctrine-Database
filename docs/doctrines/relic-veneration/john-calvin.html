John Calvin (Treatise on Relics) on the veneration of relics

Dates: Traité des reliques published 1543 (Calvin 1509–1564) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin's treatise against the cult of relics argues from two directions: in principle, that keeping and honouring relics is superstition that breeds idolatry; and as to fact, that the relics displayed across Europe are largely false — duplicated bodies, multiplied heads, enough “true Cross” to load a ship.

Claims & evidence

Calvin: the desire for relics is never without superstition, and breeds idolatry

Calvin's governing thesis is that relics cannot be honoured without sliding into the worship due to Christ alone, and that the appetite for them is superstition and the parent of idolatry.1

“To have relics is a useless and frivolous thing, which will most probably gradually lead towards idolatry, because they cannot be handled and looked upon without being honoured, and in doing this men will very soon render them the honour which is due to Jesus Christ. In short, the desire for relics is never without superstition, and what is worse, it is usually the parent of idolatry.”

— Calvin, A Treatise on Relics (Krasinski trans.). An earlier objection of the same kind, from Vigilantius (as quoted by Jerome, 406), is recorded on this timeline.

Calvin: enough “true Cross” to load a ship

Calvin also argues from the number of relics on display: gather all the fragments of the “true Cross” venerated across Christendom, he writes, and they would fill a cargo ship, though the Gospel says one man carried the whole cross.2

“If we were to collect all these pieces of the true cross exhibited in various parts, they would form a whole ship's cargo. The Gospel testifies that the cross could be borne by one single individual; how glaring, then, is the audacity now to pretend to display more relics of wood than three hundred men could carry!”

— Calvin, A Treatise on Relics. He presses the same against the saints' bodies: “every apostle had more than four bodies, and each saint at least two or three” — and catalogues the rival heads of John the Baptist scattered from Amiens to Rome.

Reception and debate

  • Two arguments. The treatise makes an in-principle case (that honouring relics leads to idolatry) and an as-to-fact case (that the relics on display are largely false).
  • A Reformed historian’s account. Philip Schaff (Reformed church historian, 1819–1893) calls the tract “the most popular and effective of his anti-papal writings,” adding: “He indulged here very freely in his power of ridicule and sarcasm, which reminds one almost of Voltaire, but the spirit is altogether different.”3
  • Catholic statements on authenticity. Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) writes of “the multitude of unquestionably spurious relics with which the treasuries of great medieval churches were crowded,” and that the Church “approves of honour being paid to those relics which with reasonable probability are believed to be genuine and which are invested with due ecclesiastical sanctions.”4
  • The True-Cross figure. Thomas J. Craughwell (Catholic writer, National Catholic Register, 2011) traces the “cargo” line to Erasmus, who wrote of the cross that, “if all the fragments were collected together, they would appear to form a fair cargo for a merchant ship.” Craughwell reports that Charles Rohault de Fleury, in his Mémoire sur les instruments de la Passion (1870), estimated the volume of the original cross at 10,900 cubic inches, found that the fragments he measured came to 240 cubic inches, and, multiplying by ten to allow for lost and unrecorded pieces, arrived at 2,400 cubic inches, “not even a fifth of the estimated size of the cross upon which Christ was crucified.”5
  • The Council of Trent’s 1563 decree on relics has its own entry.
  • Calvin's rejection of the veneration of images is traced on the image-veneration timeline.
