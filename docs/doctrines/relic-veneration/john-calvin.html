John Calvin (Treatise on Relics) on the veneration of relics

Dates: Traité des reliques published 1543 (Calvin 1509–1564) · Wikipedia
Summary: The sharpest Reformation attack on the relic-cult. Calvin argues from two directions at once: in principle, that keeping and honouring relics is superstition that breeds idolatry; and in fact, that the relics displayed across Europe are a mass of frauds — duplicated bodies, multiplied heads, enough “true Cross” to load a ship — so that even on its own terms the cult rests on imposture.

Claims & evidence

The desire for relics is never without superstition, and breeds idolatry

Calvin's governing thesis is that relics cannot be honoured without sliding into the worship due to Christ alone; the appetite for them is superstition and the parent of idolatry.1

“To have relics is a useless and frivolous thing, which will most probably gradually lead towards idolatry, because they cannot be handled and looked upon without being honoured, and in doing this men will very soon render them the honour which is due to Jesus Christ. In short, the desire for relics is never without superstition, and what is worse, it is usually the parent of idolatry.”

— Calvin, A Treatise on Relics (Krasinski trans.). This is the charge Vigilantius had made eleven centuries earlier, now pressed by the Reformer with the full Reformed doctrine of worship behind it.

Enough “true Cross” to load a ship

Calvin's most famous stroke is empirical: gather all the fragments of the “true Cross” venerated across Christendom and they would fill a cargo ship — though the Gospel says one man carried the whole cross.2

“If we were to collect all these pieces of the true cross exhibited in various parts, they would form a whole ship's cargo. The Gospel testifies that the cross could be borne by one single individual; how glaring, then, is the audacity now to pretend to display more relics of wood than three hundred men could carry!”

— Calvin, A Treatise on Relics. He presses the same against the saints' bodies: “every apostle had more than four bodies, and each saint at least two or three” — and catalogues the rival heads of John the Baptist scattered from Amiens to Rome.

Notes

  • Two arguments, kept distinct. Calvin's in-principle case (honouring relics is idolatry) and his in-fact case (the relics are frauds) are logically separate: the second could be granted — and Catholic authorities themselves later conceded many relics spurious — without the first following, since a genuine relic would still have to be shown idolatrous to honour. The wiki weighs the in-principle question at “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?” (assessed contested); the fraud-catalogue is a separate, largely historical, indictment.
  • Calvin's polemic is mordant and sometimes overstated (his relic-counts are rhetorical, not audited), but the core factual point — rampant duplication and unprovenanced “relics” — was real, and Trent would answer it twenty years later by ordering that “all superstition shall be removed” while reaffirming the veneration itself.
  • Calvin appears on this wiki also as the fullest voice of the Reformed rejection of image-veneration; on relics he extends the same principle from pictures to bones.
