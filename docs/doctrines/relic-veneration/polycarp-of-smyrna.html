The Martyrdom of Polycarp on the veneration of relics

Dates: c. 155–160 (Polycarp martyred at Smyrna) · Wikipedia
Summary: The letter the church of Smyrna wrote to record the death of its bishop describes a martyr's remains gathered, prized, and honoured at an annual commemoration, and in the same passage distinguishes the love given to the martyrs from the adoration given to Christ.

Claims & evidence

The martyr's bones are gathered as treasures and laid in a fitting place

After Polycarp's body was burned, the Christians collected his charred bones, valuing them above gold and jewels, and deposited them where the community could gather.1

“Accordingly, we afterwards took up his bones, as being more precious than the most exquisite jewels, and more purified than gold, and deposited them in a fitting place, whither, being gathered together, as opportunity is allowed us, with joy and rejoicing, the Lord shall grant us to celebrate the anniversary of his martyrdom, both in memory of those who have already finished their course, and for the exercising and preparation of those yet to walk in their steps.”

— The Martyrdom of Polycarp 18 (ANF trans.). The Greek word rendered “the anniversary of his martyrdom” is genethlios — literally the martyr's birthday, that is, his birth into heaven, kept at the tomb.

The letter's distinction between adoring Christ and loving the martyrs

The same letter reports the pagans' and Jews' fear — voiced through Nicetes — that if the Christians got the body they would “begin to worship this one,” forsaking the crucified Christ; and it answers with a distinction between adoring Christ and loving the martyrs.2

“For Him indeed, as being the Son of God, we adore; but the martyrs, as disciples and followers of the Lord, we worthily love on account of their extraordinary affection towards their own King and Master, of whom may we also be made companions and fellow-disciples!”

— The Martyrdom of Polycarp 17 (ANF trans.). Objections to the honouring of relics as the worship of the creature are recorded on this timeline from Vigilantius (as quoted by Jerome, 406) and Calvin (1543).

Placement on the timeline

  • Earliest extant attestation. The letter is the earliest extant Christian text on this timeline to describe a martyr's physical remains deliberately preserved and honoured, with a yearly gathering at them.
  • Date. The document is a letter of the church of Smyrna, not a work of Polycarp himself. It is traditionally dated to shortly after his death (155; a date of 167 has also been proposed ⚠ unverified); Candida Moss (“On the Dating of Polycarp,” Early Christianity 1.4, 2010) has suggested a date of the document around AD 200 or later ⚠ unverified.3
  • Here Polycarp figures not as author but as the martyr whose remains the letter describes.
