The Martyrdom of Polycarp on the veneration of relics

Dates: c. 155–160 (Polycarp martyred at Smyrna) · Wikipedia
Summary: The letter the church of Smyrna wrote to record the death of its bishop is the earliest Christian text to show a martyr's remains gathered, prized, and honoured at an annual commemoration — and, in the same breath, the earliest to draw the line the whole later debate turns on: the martyrs are loved, Christ alone is worshipped.

Claims & evidence

The martyr's bones are gathered as treasures and laid in a fitting place

After Polycarp's body was burned, the Christians collected his charred bones, valuing them above gold and jewels, and deposited them where the community could gather. This is the earliest datable instance of a Christian relic-cult — the physical remains of a saint kept and honoured.1

“Accordingly, we afterwards took up his bones, as being more precious than the most exquisite jewels, and more purified than gold, and deposited them in a fitting place, whither, being gathered together, as opportunity is allowed us, with joy and rejoicing, the Lord shall grant us to celebrate the anniversary of his martyrdom, both in memory of those who have already finished their course, and for the exercising and preparation of those yet to walk in their steps.”

— The Martyrdom of Polycarp 18 (ANF trans.). The Greek word rendered “the anniversary of his martyrdom” is genethlios — literally the martyr's birthday, that is, his birth into heaven, kept at the tomb. The annual commemoration at the relics is the seed of the feast-day cult of the saints.

The martyrs are loved; Christ alone is worshipped

The same letter reports the pagans' and Jews' fear — voiced through Nicetes — that if the Christians got the body they would “begin to worship this one,” forsaking the crucified Christ; and it answers with the distinction the veneration tradition would rest on ever after.2

“For Him indeed, as being the Son of God, we adore; but the martyrs, as disciples and followers of the Lord, we worthily love on account of their extraordinary affection towards their own King and Master, of whom may we also be made companions and fellow-disciples!”

— The Martyrdom of Polycarp 17 (ANF trans.). The worry the narrative records — that honouring a martyr's remains slides into worshipping the martyr — is exactly the charge Vigilantius and, later, Calvin would press; the reply given here (adoration for Christ, love for the saints) is the germ of the later latria/dulia distinction.

Notes

  • The earliest relic-cult text. Nothing earlier shows Christians deliberately preserving and honouring a martyr's physical remains and returning to them yearly. Whether this warm devotion is the same thing as the later dogmatized cult — kissing, lights, incense, miracle-working bones, relics required in every altar — or a much simpler reverence later built up, is itself part of the historical question the timeline traces.
  • The document is a letter of the church of Smyrna, not a work of Polycarp himself; it is dated to shortly after his death (traditionally 155, some prefer 167).
  • Polycarp appears on this wiki also as a witness elsewhere; here he figures not as author but as the martyr whose remains occasion the first recorded relic-devotion.
