The Second Council of Nicaea (Canon VII) on relics

Dates: 787 (Seventh Ecumenical Council) · Wikipedia
Summary: The council that restored the images also legislated the relics: its seventh canon requires that relics of the martyrs be deposited in every church at its consecration, and threatens with deposition any bishop who consecrates a church without them. Relic-veneration passes here from custom to conciliar law.

Claims & evidence

No church is to be consecrated without relics

Nicaea II treats the removal of relics from churches as one of the iconoclast impieties to be reversed, and lays down a positive rule: churches already consecrated without relics are to have the defect supplied, and henceforth a bishop who consecrates a church without relics is to be deposed.1

“We decree therefore that relics shall be placed with the accustomed service in as many of the sacred temples as have been consecrated without the relics of the Martyrs. And if any bishop from this time forward is found consecrating a temple without holy relics, he shall be deposed, as a transgressor of the ecclesiastical traditions.”

— Second Council of Nicaea (787), Canon VII. The council frames this against the iconoclasts, “for as they took out of the churches the presence of the venerable images, so likewise they cast aside other customs which we must now revive.” The requirement survives in the Catholic altar stone or sepulchrum (a relic sealed in the altar) and its Orthodox counterpart, the antimension.

Notes

  • From devotion to law. Where Smyrna kept a martyr's bones out of love and Augustine reported miracles at them, Nicaea II requires relics — the practice is now built into the very consecration of a church. This is the same council that dogmatized the veneration of images (see the veneration of images and icons); the two decrees are issued together, which is why Trent later treats images and relics in one decree.
  • Canon 7 is a disciplinary canon (church order), distinct from the council's dogmatic definition on the images. Its rule about relics-in-altars was received in both East and West and remained in force for over a millennium (relaxed for the Latin church only in the 1970s revision of the rite of dedication).
