The Second Council of Nicaea (Canon VII) on relics

Dates: 787 (reckoned the Seventh Ecumenical Council by the Orthodox and Catholic Churches) · Wikipedia
Summary: The council that restored the images also legislated the relics: its seventh canon requires that relics of the martyrs be deposited in every church at its consecration, and threatens with deposition any bishop who consecrates a church without them.

Claims & evidence

Canon VII: no church to be consecrated without relics

Nicaea II treats the removal of relics from churches as one of the iconoclast impieties to be reversed, and lays down a positive rule: churches already consecrated without relics are to have the defect supplied, and henceforth a bishop who consecrates a church without relics is to be deposed.1

“We decree therefore that relics shall be placed with the accustomed service in as many of the sacred temples as have been consecrated without the relics of the Martyrs. And if any bishop from this time forward is found consecrating a temple without holy relics, he shall be deposed, as a transgressor of the ecclesiastical traditions.”

— Second Council of Nicaea (787), Canon VII. The council frames this against the iconoclasts, “for as they took out of the churches the presence of the venerable images, so likewise they cast aside other customs which we must now revive.”

Reception and later legislation

  • The same council as the images. Canon VII was issued by the council that defined the veneration of images (see the veneration of images and icons). The Council of Trent's decree of 1563 treats relics and images together (see Trent on images and Trent on relics).
  • Canon 7 is a disciplinary canon (church order), distinct from the council's dogmatic definition on the images.
  • In the West. Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) reports that after the council “had insisted with special urgency that relics were to be used in the consecration of churches,” the English Council of Celchyth “commanded that relics were to be used, and in default of them the Blessed Eucharist.”2 The Latin Rite of Dedication of a Church and an Altar (1977) provides: “The tradition in the Roman liturgy of placing relics of martyrs or other saints beneath the altar should be preserved, if possible,” adding that “It is better for an altar to be dedicated without relics than to have relics of doubtful authenticity placed beneath it.”3 The 1983 Code of Canon Law provides that “The ancient tradition of placing relics of martyrs or other saints under a fixed altar is to be preserved, according to the norms given in the liturgical books.”4
  • In the East. The Serbian Orthodox theologian Justin Popović quotes the council's decrees and writes that “from the depths of sacred antiquity, churches were built on the graves and relics of Saints, and the holy Liturgy is performed only on antimensia, in which are placed parts of the holy relics.”5
