Does Elisha's bones raising the dead (2 Kings 13:21) warrant the veneration of relics?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — a genuine biblical precedent for power in a holy man's remains, and so for the possibility of relic-efficacy; but it shows God acting through the bones, not anyone venerating them, so the step to the developed cult is where it is contested.

Related arguments: Scripture's relic-precedents — see also Paul's handkerchiefs (Acts 19:11–12)

The claim

“And it came to pass, as they were burying a man, that, behold, they spied a band of men; and they cast the man into the sepulchre of Elisha: and when the man was let down, and touched the bones of Elisha, he revived, and stood up on his feet.”

— 2 Kings 13:21 (KJV). The argument: Scripture itself records that the physical remains of a holy man carried life-giving power — a corpse touched Elisha's bones and rose. If God worked resurrection through a prophet's bones, then a saint's relics are not “worthless dust” but vessels God may honour and use; the honouring of relics has a biblical warrant.

Who advances it

The verse is the standard patristic proof-text for relic-devotion. The Apostolic Constitutions (6.30) cite it to argue that “of those that live with God, even their very relics are not without honour”; John Chrysostom deploys it in his Eulogy on St. Ignatius for the power of the martyrs' tombs; Ephrem the Syrian and later defenders read the miracle as a “seed of resurrection” shown in the saints' bodies. It stands beside Acts 19:11–12 as one of the two Scriptural precedents adduced for relics.

The evidence for

The strength here is that the datum is undeniable and canonical: an inspired historical text reports a miracle worked by contact with a dead prophet's remains. That establishes, against Vigilantius's and Calvin's premise that bones are mere dust, that God can and at least once did attach miraculous power to holy remains — the exact thing the relic-cult claims. The Fathers who cite it are not straining: the text really does present a saint's bones as a channel of divine power, and the Apostolic Constitutions draw the inference explicitly — if the body of Elisha raised the dead, it “would not have happened unless the body of Elisha were holy.”1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a precedent for relic-power — that God may work miracles through the physical remains of the holy — the verse is strong and squarely biblical. It defeats the flat claim that relics are “nothing but dust,” and the miracle-reports at relics from Augustine onward stand in a line the text opens.

What is overstated or imprecise. The verse records relic-power, not relic-veneration. The resurrection is accidental — a body hastily thrown into Elisha's tomb at the sight of raiders — not an act of honour, prayer, or cult; no one bows to the bones, kisses them, invokes Elisha, or keeps a feast. So it proves that God may glorify a saint's remains; it does not, by itself, establish the practice of venerating them (the kissing, lights, invocation, and altar-relics of the later cult). The move from “God worked a miracle through these bones” to “therefore we should venerate relics” is a reasonable inference, not a demonstration: the same power could ground simple gratitude to God without a cult of the bones. And it says nothing about whether the honour paid a relic is lawful dulia or idolatry — that turns on a separate question.2

Assessment

Plausible. As warrant for the reality of relic-power — that a holy person's remains may be a channel of God's miraculous action — the text is a sound and genuinely biblical precedent, and the Fathers who cite it read it fairly. As warrant for the developed veneration-cult it proves less: it shows God honouring a saint's bones, not the faithful venerating them, so the inference to the practice is coherent but not compelled. The honest verdict tracks that gap — a strong precedent for relic-efficacy, a partial one for the cult itself.
