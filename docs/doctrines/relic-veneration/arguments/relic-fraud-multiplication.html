Does the fraud and multiplication of relics discredit the cult?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the factual core is undeniable (duplicated bodies, multiplied heads, and quantities of “true Cross” that no single cross could yield are real and were admitted), so the naive form — that every venerated relic is authentic — fails outright; but the inference from “many relics are frauds” to “the practice is discredited” overreaches (abuse is not use, and a discredited specimen does not discredit the category) · advanced by John Calvin

The claim

“If we were to collect all these pieces of the true cross exhibited in various parts, they would form a whole ship's cargo. The Gospel testifies that the cross could be borne by one single individual; how glaring, then, is the audacity now to pretend to display more relics of wood than three hundred men could carry!”

— Calvin, A Treatise on Relics (1543). This is Calvin's in-fact argument, kept distinct from his in-principle one (that honouring relics is idolatry — weighed at “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?”). The thesis here: even granting that venerating a genuine relic could be lawful, the actual relic-cult of Christendom rests on demonstrable imposture — multiplied bodies, impossible duplication, an unregulated trade in unprovenanced “relics” — so the practice as it exists is discredited.

Who advances it

The charge is as old as the cult's critics: the Gallic priest Vigilantius (c. 400) already mocked the kissing of “a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth,” and the humanist Erasmus, in the Colloquies (“A Pilgrimage for Religion's Sake,” 1526), quipped that the fragments of the cross would make “a fair cargo for a merchant ship” — the line Calvin sharpened seventeen years later. In its modern form (medieval relic-forgery, the relic-trade, and the translatio frauds as a documented historical phenomenon) the case is carried by the historians of the cult — and, revealingly, was half-conceded by the Council of Trent itself.

The evidence for

The factual core is strong and, in its particulars, uncontested. Single saints were venerated in several places at once as whole bodies; the head of John the Baptist was shown at Amiens, at Rome (San Silvestro in Capite), at Constantinople, and elsewhere; Calvin's tally that “every apostle had more than four bodies, and each saint at least two or three” is rhetorical but points at a real scandal of duplication. The medieval relic-trade was extensive and lightly policed: relics were bought, sold, stolen (the celebrated furta sacra, pious relic-theft, was a recognised genre), and manufactured, and the profit motive (“filthy lucre,” in Trent's own phrase) drove a market in unverifiable objects. The decisive admission is the Catholic one: the Council of Trent, in the very decree (Session XXV, 1563) that defined relics “are to be venerated by the faithful,” ordered in the same breath that “all superstition shall be removed, all filthy lucre be abolished,” and that no new relics be admitted without the bishop's approval — a reform that presupposes exactly the abuses Calvin named.1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the relic-cult was riddled with fraud is not seriously disputable, and the strongest form of relic-devotion — confident veneration of this object as authentically the saint's or the Cross's — is undercut across the board by unverifiable provenance. Where a relic's authenticity is a load-bearing premise of the devotion offered it, and where that authenticity is in most cases unestablishable, the honest verdict is that a great many venerated relics have no claim on the devotion they receive. Calvin's factual indictment lands.

What is overstated or imprecise. Three moves keep the indictment from discrediting the practice as such. First, the counts are rhetorical, not audited — and where the most famous of them was later actually audited, it collapsed. In 1870 the architect-scholar Charles Rohault de Fleury, in his Mémoire sur les instruments de la Passion, inventoried the surviving fragments of the True Cross sanctuary by sanctuary with calipers and scales: he estimated the original cross at roughly 10,900 cubic inches of wood, and found the known fragments totalled about 240 cubic inches — and even after multiplying tenfold to allow for lost and private pieces, some 2,400 cubic inches, less than a fifth of a single cross. So far from a “ship's cargo,” the extant relics of the Cross would not build one cross — the empirical rebuttal turns Calvin's (and Erasmus's) most memorable figure on its head.2 Second, that some relics are spurious does not show that all are — the inference from a discredited specimen to a discredited category is a genetic fallacy; the existence of forged banknotes does not abolish currency, and the existence of a second Baptist-head does not touch the veneration of a genuine martyr's bone. Third, and decisively, abuse is not use: that a lawful practice was widely corrupted argues for reform, not abolition — which is precisely the conclusion Trent drew, keeping the veneration while purging the fraud. Calvin's own framing runs the two together (the frauds are marshalled to prove the practice idolatrous), but the fraud-argument, taken strictly, reaches only the frauds.3

Assessment

Contested. As history the argument is strong: the duplication and the trade were real, the provenance of most relics is unverifiable, and the strong-devotion-to-every-relic form of the cult cannot survive it — a point the Catholic tradition itself conceded and moved to reform. As a discrediting argument it overreaches: the leap from “many relics are fake” to “the practice is illegitimate” trades on the genetic fallacy and on collapsing abuse into use, and its most quotable datum — the “whole ship's cargo” of Cross-wood — was empirically overturned when someone finally measured. Strong by the lights of the historian of abuses, weak by the lights of the logician of the inference — genuinely contested.
