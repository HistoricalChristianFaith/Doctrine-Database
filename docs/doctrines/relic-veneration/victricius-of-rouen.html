Victricius of Rouen (De Laude Sanctorum) on the veneration of relics

Dates: De Laude Sanctorum (“In Praise of the Saints”) c. 396 (Victricius bishop of Rouen c. 330–c. 407) · Wikipedia
Summary: A sermon preached to welcome a gift of relics sent to Rouen by Ambrose of Milan. Victricius takes up the objection that a saint's power could hardly reside in a scrap of bone or a bloodstained cloth no bigger than the last joint of a finger, and answers that, because the martyr is joined to the indivisible, omnipresent God, the whole saint is present in every particle of the relic: division does not diminish, and the fragment is not a fraction. He also distinguishes what the martyr is “by gift” from what God is “by property.”

Claims & evidence

Victricius: the whole saint in every particle, “nothing in relics which is not complete”

Victricius's central claim addresses the objection to translating and dividing relics: a tiny fragment looks like a mere part, and a part seems to carry only part of the power. He answers that the relic is not divided into lesser and greater; the whole is present in the least piece.1

“There is nothing in relics which is not complete. … Where the healing power is intact, the limbs are intact. We say that flesh is held together by the glue of blood, and we affirm that the spirit also, wet with the moisture of blood, has taken on the fiery heat of the Word. … in relics, then, there is a reminder of perfection, not the injustice of division.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum (Clark trans.). On Victricius’s account, the mechanism is the martyr's union with God: because God is omnipresent and indivisible, and the saint is joined to Him, the saint too is wholly present in every part of the remains — in the fragment on the altar at Rouen no less than in the body in Italy or the self in heaven. The “particle” is therefore a “reminder of perfection, not the injustice of division”: on his account, to honour the fragment is to reach the whole saint, and through the saint, God.

Why they are called “relics”: words as signs

Addressing the objection that the very word reliquiae (“remains,” “leftovers”) implies something partial and diminished, Victricius answers that the name is a sign, not a measure of the thing's power.2

“Why, then, do we call them ‘relics’? Because words are images and signs of things.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum (Clark trans.). The label describes what is left behind after the martyr's death, not a fraction of the martyr's presence.

The martyr and God: “the same by gift, not by property”

Victricius raises the question that follows from his thesis: if the saint is so joined to God that the whole saint is in every relic, is the martyr then God, and is honouring the relic worshipping the martyr as God? He answers that the martyr shares God's life by gift, not by nature.3

“Is the martyr God? … the same by gift, not by property.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum 8 (as rendered in the Ehrman–Jacobs reader). On Victricius's account the martyrs are what God is by grace, not by their own substance, so the honour paid them is not the worship due God by nature. Compare the letter of Smyrna (c. 155: “Him… we adore; but the martyrs… we worthily love”) and Jerome (406).

Reception and debate

  • The sermon's subject. Where the letter of Smyrna records the keeping of a martyr's bones and Jerome defends honouring relics, Victricius gives an account of how a fragment can carry a whole saint — the “whole in the part,” grounded in the martyr's union with the indivisible God.
  • Its setting. Gillian Clark, whose translation is quoted above, places the sermon in a period when relic-translation was still contentious in the western churches — Roman law forbade disturbing the dead, and many Christians were repelled by the veneration of bone and blood — reads it as a striking, “overheated” argument deploying late-antique rhetoric and philosophy to make the new cult acceptable, and cautions against taking it as mainstream fourth-century doctrine ⚠ unverified.4
  • The relics welcomed were those of the martyrs Vitalis and Agricola and others, sent from Milan by Ambrose; Victricius had earlier been a soldier who left the army on conversion, and served as a missionary bishop in the far north-west of Gaul.
