Victricius of Rouen (De Laude Sanctorum) on the veneration of relics

Dates: De Laude Sanctorum (“In Praise of the Saints”) c. 396 (Victricius bishop of Rouen c. 330–c. 407) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest sustained theology of relics in the Latin West. Preaching to welcome a gift of relics sent to Rouen by Ambrose of Milan, Victricius answered the era's live objection — how can a saint's power reside in a scrap of bone or a bloodstained cloth, no bigger than the last joint of a finger? — with a bold thesis: because the martyr is joined to the indivisible, omnipresent God, the whole saint is present in every particle of the relic. Division does not diminish; the fragment is not a fraction. It is the theological bridge between the early relic-devotion of Polycarp and the later dogmatized cult, and it supplies the metaphysics that the dulia-or-idolatry question turns on.

Claims & evidence

The whole saint in every particle: “nothing in relics which is not complete”

Victricius's central and most original claim confronts the natural objection to translating and dividing relics: a tiny fragment looks like a mere part, and a part seems to carry only part of the power. He denies the premise outright. The relic is not divided into lesser and greater; the whole is present in the least piece.1

“There is nothing in relics which is not complete. … Where the healing power is intact, the limbs are intact. We say that flesh is held together by the glue of blood, and we affirm that the spirit also, wet with the moisture of blood, has taken on the fiery heat of the Word. … in relics, then, there is a reminder of perfection, not the injustice of division.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum (Clark trans.). The mechanism is the martyr's union with God: because God is omnipresent and indivisible, and the saint is joined to Him, the saint too is wholly present in every part of the remains — in the fragment on the altar at Rouen no less than in the body in Italy or the self in heaven. The “particle” is therefore a “reminder of perfection, not the injustice of division”: to honour the fragment is to reach the whole saint, and through the saint, God.

Why they are called “relics”: words are signs, not measures

Anticipating that the very word reliquiae (“remains,” “leftovers”) implies something partial and diminished, Victricius answers that the name is a sign, not a verdict on the thing's power.2

“Why, then, do we call them ‘relics’? Because words are images and signs of things.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum (Clark trans.). The label describes what is left behind after the martyr's death, not a fraction of the martyr's presence — a distinction that keeps the “whole in the part” thesis intact against the ordinary sense of the word.

The martyr is not God — “the same by gift, not by property”

Victricius guards the line the whole later debate rests on. If the saint is so joined to God that the whole saint is in every relic, is the martyr then God, and is honouring the relic worshipping the martyr as God? He answers with a careful formula: the martyr shares God's life by gift, not by nature.3

“Is the martyr God? … the same by gift, not by property.”

— Victricius, De Laude Sanctorum 8 (as rendered in the Ehrman–Jacobs reader). The martyrs are what God is by grace, not by their own substance; the honour paid them is therefore not the worship due God by nature. It is the latria/dulia distinction in substance — the same guard Smyrna set (“Him… we adore; but the martyrs… we worthily love”) and Jerome would restate a decade later.

Notes

  • Why it matters. Victricius is the first in the Latin West to give relic-veneration a worked-out metaphysic rather than a devotion or a defence. Polycarp's church kept the bones; Jerome would defend honouring them; Victricius explains how a fragment can carry a whole saint — the “whole in the part” that makes both the multiplication of relics and their veneration intelligible. His mechanism (union with the indivisible God) is exactly the hinge on which “Is venerating relics dulia or idolatry?” turns: if the honour genuinely passes through the whole saint to God, it is relative dulia; if it terminates in the matter, it is idolatry.
  • A controversial argument, not a settled doctrine. Relic-translation was still contentious in the western churches of the 390s — Roman law forbade disturbing the dead, and many Christians were repelled by the veneration of bone and blood. Victricius's sermon should be read as the striking, “overheated” argument it was, deploying late-antique rhetoric and philosophy to make the new cult acceptable — not as a statement of shared belief. (So the historian Gillian Clark cautions against taking it as mainstream fourth-century doctrine.)
  • The relics welcomed were those of the martyrs Vitalis and Agricola and others, sent from Milan by Ambrose; Victricius had earlier been a soldier who left the army on conversion, and served as a missionary bishop in the far north-west of Gaul.
