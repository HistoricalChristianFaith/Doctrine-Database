The Power of the Name

From its first pages the church believed that the Name has power — above all the name of Jesus. Demons flee at it, the sick are healed by it, prayer is offered in it, converts are baptized into it, and salvation is found in no other. “There is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” (Acts 4:12); God has “given him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow” (Philippians 2:9–11). This page traces how that conviction — that to invoke the Name is to lay hold of the One named, and that the Name itself is effectual in exorcism, prayer, baptism, and devotion — developed and was disciplined across the centuries, from the New Testament through the fathers to the Byzantine Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern controversies over invoking it.

The belief did not develop in a vacuum. The ancient world was full of names of power — the adjurations of the magical papyri, Jewish name-magic, the untranslatable divine names guarded by theurgists — and the church grew up claiming a real power for its own Name while drawing a hard line against sorcery. That line is the recurring tension this timeline follows: patristic writers insist the Name of Jesus truly casts out demons, yet condemn the magician who would wield a name as a formula. Where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins is a boundary the tradition polices again and again — and several of the arguments below turn on exactly that question. (This is a page of Christian theology and history — the efficacy of the Name in prayer, exorcism, baptism, and devotion, and the church’s boundary-drawing against magic. Pagan and Jewish name-lore appears only as historical backdrop, weighed critically, never as instruction.)

How this differs from the Divine Name: that timeline concerns the Tetragrammaton’s text and pronunciation — how יהוה was written, avoided, and voiced (Yahweh, Jehovah, the qere “Adonai”). This one concerns the efficacy and invocation of the Name — chiefly the name of Jesus — a distinct but adjacent question. The two meet in the fathers who held that the divine names carry a power lodged in their very sounds, untranslatable without loss; that name-realism is weighed under the first language of mankind (was the primordial language a divine gift?) and, where it shades toward magic, on the argument Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power? See also astrology and the stars, the neighbouring field the fathers policed for the same reason.

The biblical foundation

Before the fathers could theorize the power of the Name they had a Scripture that everywhere assumes it: a Name at which demons are subject, by which the lame are healed, in which prayer is answered and the believer is baptized, and outside which there is no salvation — together with two hard warnings that the Name is not a spell to be worked by outsiders. This is the layer every witness below builds on.

“In my name shall they cast out devils” — the Name in exorcism and healing

The earliest datum is the Name that subdues spirits. The seventy return from their mission “with joy, saying, Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name,” and the risen Lord promises that among the signs following those who believe, “In my name shall they cast out devils.”1 The apostles act on it at once. Peter heals the lame man at the temple gate not by his own power but by a word: “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk”; and Paul turns on the spirit in the slave-girl, “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.”2 The power is bound so tightly to the Name and not to the wielder’s standing that when the disciples try to stop a stranger “casting out devils in thy name,” the Lord overrules them: “Forbid him not: for there is no man which shall do a miracle in my name, that can lightly speak evil of me.”3 The Name is not a description of the act but its instrument; the fathers’ whole doctrine of exorcistic power in the Name begins here — as does the harder question the timeline must answer, why the Name avails an unknown outsider here yet turns on the sons of Sceva below.

“There is none other name” — the Name above every name

Hauled before the Sanhedrin and asked “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” Peter answers that the man was healed “by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” and presses it to its farthest reach: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.”4 The Philippian hymn sets the same Name at the summit of the cosmos: because of the obedience of the cross “God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth.”5 And the apostolic gospel makes calling on the Name the very hinge of being saved, taking up Joel’s oracle of the Day of the Lord: “whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” The word Joel spoke of the Name of the LORD (יהוה) Peter at Pentecost and Paul to the Romans apply, without apology, to the name of Jesus — so that to invoke the one Name is to invoke the other.6 The Name is thus not one power among many but the one before which all powers bend — the note the later Holy Name devotion would sound.

“Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name” — the Name in prayer, baptism, and life

The Name is also the medium of prayer and of belonging. “Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do,” the Lord promises; “ask, and ye shall receive, that your joy may be full”; “where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them”; and John writes his Gospel “that believing ye might have life through his name.”7 The convert is claimed by the Name in baptism — “baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost,” “be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins” — and lives out of it: he is “washed,” “sanctified,” “justified in the name of the Lord Jesus,” and when he is sick the elders pray over him, “anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord.”8 So that to be a Christian is to have the Name named over one. Invocation, gathering, baptism, and life all run through it.

“Jesus I know… but who are ye?” — the Name is not a spell

Yet the same Scriptures set the boundary the fathers would spend centuries defending: the Name has power, but it is not a formula severable from faith and from belonging to the One named. The vagabond Jewish exorcists at Ephesus try to wield it as a magic word — “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — and the evil spirit turns on them: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The sons of Sceva flee “naked and wounded,” and by the lesson “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified.”9 The Lord’s own warning is sharper still: “Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord… in thy name have [we not] cast out devils?… And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.”10 Even true works “in thy name” do not avail the one the Lord does not know. So the Name is effectual and yet not at anyone’s disposal — the exact tension the timeline below works out, and the line every father draws between the power of the Name and the presumption of the magician.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — the New Testament layer above, then the fathers who theorized and disciplined the Name’s power, the Byzantine tradition of the Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern controversy over invoking it. This spine is being built out; the figures named in the introduction are added here in chronological order as each is researched.

Justin Martyr — the name of Jesus crucified routs demons (c. 160)

Justin is the earliest father to make the exorcistic power of the Name a public, verifiable argument. Four times he appeals to a fact he says his hearers can check with their own eyes: demons are “overcome and subdued” when Christians exorcise them “in the name of Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate,” and “the power of whose name even the demons do fear” is a present, ongoing power — grounded not in the exorcist but in what the Father gave the Son through “the dispensation of His suffering.”11 Writing to the Roman authorities he makes it empirical: “numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city,” are healed by “Christian men exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ… though they could not be cured by all the other exorcists, and those who used incantations and drugs.”12 And he draws the doctrine’s central line himself: this one Name subdues the demon where the names of “kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs” do not, while the exorcists who trade on “craft… fumigations and incantations” are working sorcery, not wielding the Name — and even the true Name is no guarantee for the false worker the Lord does not know (Matthew 7:22–23, which Justin quotes in the same breath).13

Irenaeus of Lyons — the church works in the Name, not by incantation; the Gnostic misuse of secret names (c. 180)

Irenaeus states the doctrine’s whole tension inside one work. On the positive side, the church “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” drives out demons, heals the sick, and (he claims) has even raised the dead — “in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate,” done freely and for the benefit of others, “neither practising deception upon any, nor taking any reward.”14 And he draws the boundary himself: the church works these things “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art,” but by prayer and the plain invocation of Jesus — and it is this Name, “but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates, or of any other man whatever,” that heals; the magician Simon and his heirs, working “by means of magical deceptions,” can neither cure nor raise the dead as the church does “by means of prayer.”15 On the negative side, Irenaeus dissects the Marcosian and Valentinian “redemption” rite — Marcus “a perfect adept in magical impostures” who protracts “to great length the word of invocation,” and initiators who “repeat certain Hebrew words, in order the more thoroughly to bewilder those who are being initiated” (“Basema, Chamosse, Baoenaora…”) — as sorcery masquerading as sacrament, a rite he says was “instigated by Satan to a denial of that baptism which is regeneration to God.”16 The efficacy of the one Name and the condemnation of name-magic stand side by side in the earliest sustained form the timeline records — the boundary Origen would press harder against Celsus.

Tertullian of Carthage — the demons compelled to confess and flee at the naming of Christ; a public courtroom proof (c. 197)

Tertullian turns the exorcistic power of the Name into a standing dare to the Roman magistrates. Bring any demoniac before the tribunal, he writes: the possessing spirit, “bidden to speak by a follower of Christ,” will confess itself a demon, and — for “all the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ” — will leave the body it holds, “Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ… So at our touch and breathing… they leave at our command the bodies they have entered, unwilling, and distressed, and before your very eyes put to an open shame.” The power is not a formula the operator commands but the dread of “Christ as Judge” — and Tertullian heads off the obvious rejoinder before it is raised: “Do you say that it is done by magic…? You will not say anything of the sort, if you have been allowed the use of your ears and eyes.”17 To the proconsul Scapula he names cases reaching into the imperial establishment itself — clerks and kin freed from evil spirits, “the little boy of a third,” men of rank “delivered from devils,” and the emperor Severus cured by the Christian Proculus — the deliverances offered, like Irenaeus’s, “without reward or hire.”18 And he draws the far boundary himself: “magic… of which astrology is a species” is a “curious art” whose first teachers were the fallen “angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women” — so the very craft the Name overturns is the demons’ own gift, and the Christian who names Christ commands the spirits the magician serves.19

Origen of Alexandria — the fullest theory of why a name has power; names natural, not conventional (c. 248)

Origen gives the most developed patristic account of the doctrine — the theory the earlier witnesses assumed and the later ones inherit. Answering Celsus’s gibe that it makes no difference whether God be called Zeus, or Sabaoth, or Ammon, he argues that the nature of names is “a deep and mysterious subject”: names were given “by nature,” not “by arrangement,” so that a power is lodged in the very sounds — “it is not the things signified, but the qualities and peculiarities of words, which possess a certain power.” Hence the Hebrew divine names — Sabaoth, Adonai, “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, of Jacob” — are “possessed of great power” and make “even demons… vanquished,” yet render them into Greek and “the mention of the name is attended with no result”; the angelic names (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael) are fitted “appropriately to the duties which they discharge.”20 Above all, the name of Jesus “has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits”; Christians prevail “not by incantations… but by the name of Jesus,” a Name so bound to the One named rather than to the speaker that it is effectual “even… pronounced… by bad men” (Matthew 7:22).21 But Origen draws the boundary himself, and hardest: sorcery is forbidden by the law (“Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them”) and demonic in its power — the conjurers’ art is “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit,” and the Magi’s sorcery collapsed at the Nativity, “the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken.” The divine names vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts; the reality of names is no license for magic.22

Cyprian of Carthage — demons adjured by the Name confess and are cast out; the pagan gods unmasked as the spirits it expels (c. 250)

Cyprian is the Latin voice beside Origen at mid-century, pressing the practice the Greek had theorized. The demons, he writes, “when adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed” — smitten, howling, and confessing “whence they came,” the spirit driven out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects.” He turns the datum apologetically, first against the idolater and then against the persecutor Demetrianus, whom he dares to come and hear his own gods “betraying what they are”: the very spirits the pagan venerates “as lords” are the ones that, at a Christian’s voice and “the power of God,” “stand bound, and tremble as captives” — so the exorcism is at once a proof of the Name’s power and an unmasking of idolatry. He keeps the anti-magic boundary by inverting it: it was the unbelieving who, seeing Christ “dr[i]ve out… the demons by His word,” slandered the “authority of His power” as sorcery.23 And in the earliest Latin exposition of the Lord’s Prayer he reverences the Name in devotion: “Hallowed be Thy name” asks “not that we wish for God that He may be hallowed by our prayers, but that we beseech of Him that His name may be hallowed in us” — the believer “sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” in baptism — the devotional thread the Byzantine Jesus Prayer and the Western Holy Name devotion would carry forward.24

Athanasius of Alexandria — the Name’s power made an apologetic proof of the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ (c. 318)

Athanasius keeps the doctrine intact but turns it to a new use. Where the earlier witnesses wield the Name in exorcism, he marshals its power as public evidence: since “actions, and power over men, belong to the living only,” the plain fact that “where Christ is named… all idolatry is deposed” and every spirit “on barely hearing it flies and disappears” proves that the One named is not dead but risen — “and especially… God.”25 The demons themselves are pressed into the proof: they would not obey a dead man, yet “if they be manifestly expelled by the naming of His name… He is not dead,” for the spirits “see… that He is God, and hence they fly and fall at His feet.” The argument turns historical, too — the whole ancient apparatus of oracles, demon-cult, and magic that was “strong and active” before Christ has fallen silent “since Christ has begun to be preached everywhere” — and it ends in a standing dare: “let him come who would test by experience… let him use the Sign of that Cross… and he shall see how by its means demons fly, oracles cease, all magic and witchcraft is brought to nought.”26 Athanasius keeps the old boundary against magic and argues it freshly: Christ cannot be a magician, for “by a magician all magic is destroyed, instead of being confirmed,” and the very demons the magicians conjure “fly from Him as their Master.” The same doctrine he narrates as a life in his Life of Antony, where Antony routs demons “by prayer and speaking the name of Christ” and tells the pagan philosophers that “where the sign of the Cross is, magic is weak and witchcraft has no strength.”27

Cyril of Jerusalem — the liturgical face of the doctrine: the exorcisms of the catechumenate, the renunciation of Satan, and baptism “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost” (c. 350)

Cyril gives the doctrine its liturgical face. Where the earlier witnesses argue the Name’s power in public exorcism and apology, Cyril, catechizing the baptismal candidates at Jerusalem, shows it enacted in the church’s rite of initiation. Through Lent the convert is exorcised again and again: “receive with earnestness the exorcisms… without exorcisms the soul cannot be purified” — and when “the exorcists inspire terror by the Spirit of God… the hostile demon flees away.”28 On the night of baptism the candidate turns to face the West, “the region of sensible darkness,” and with arm outstretched “as though [Satan] were present” renounces him aloud — “I renounce thee, Satan” — the renunciation itself an act of the Name’s power, for “the blood of the Lamb without blemish Jesus Christ is made the charm to scare evil spirits,” and the “author of evil,” pursuing the convert “even to the very streams of salvation,” “disappears in the water of salvation.” Turning then from West to East, the candidate confesses “I believe in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost” — the Name put on as the enemy is put off.29 Cyril states the mechanism plainly: “the breathing of the saints, and the invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch and drive out evil spirits” — the Name is the fire before which the spirits cannot stand — and the naming climaxes at the font, where each candidate is “asked, whether he believed in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost… and descended three times into the water.” So the Name that routs the demon in the exorcism is the same Name named over the convert in baptism: the doctrine here is corporate, ritual invocation, over against the private brandishing it elsewhere polices — and Cyril keeps that boundary in the rite, listing among the renounced “service of the devil” the very “amulets, or charms written on leaves, sorceries, or other evil arts” the whole timeline sets the Name against.30

Diadochos of Photike — the Name turns inward: the continual invocation “Lord Jesus” in the heart (c. 486)

With Diadochos the doctrine pivots from expelling demons to holding the Name. Where the earlier witnesses argue the exorcistic power of the Name, the fifth-century bishop of Photike gives the praying intellect the Name as its whole occupation: “For the complete fulfillment of its purpose we should give it nothing but the prayer ‘Lord Jesus’… Let the intellect continually concentrate on these words within its inner shrine… Those who meditate unceasingly upon this glorious and holy name in the depths of their heart can sometimes see the light of their own intellect.” The invocation is grounded not in technique but in the Spirit — “no one can say ‘Lord Jesus’ except in the Holy Spirit” (1 Corinthians 12:3) — and grace itself, he says, comes “to repeat with” the soul the words “Lord Jesus,” “just as a mother teaches her child to repeat with her the word ‘father’… until she has formed in him the habit of calling for his father even in his sleep.”31 The Name is now kept, not brandished: the maturing soul “calls ceaselessly upon the Lord Jesus” until it has “the remembrance of the Lord Jesus unceasingly” in the heart — fulfilling Paul’s “pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17) and seeding the Byzantine Jesus Prayer the next witnesses carry forward.32

John Climacus and the Sinai tradition — the Name bound to the breath; the strongest weapon in heaven or earth (c. 630)

At Sinai the inward “Lord Jesus” is joined to the body’s own rhythm. The abbot John Climacus, in the Ladder of Divine Ascent, makes the invocation a thing of every breath: “Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with your every breath. Then indeed you will appreciate the value of stillness”33 — the line that gives the practice its other name, hesychasm (from hesychia, stillness). And he draws the old exorcistic power of the Name inward to the ascetic’s warfare: “Flog your enemies with the name of Jesus, for there is no stronger weapon in heaven or earth.” His Sinai successor Hesychios teaches the same — the heart that “breathes and invokes, endlessly and without ceasing, only Jesus Christ,” and “let the name of Jesus adhere to your breath” — so that the Name still routs spirits, but now on the battlefield of the praying heart.34

Gregory Palamas — the theological charter of the Jesus Prayer; the “single-phrased” invocation defended (c. 1340)

What Diadochos and Climacus transmitted, Palamas defended. When the Calabrian Barlaam ridiculed the Athonite monks who invoked the name of Jesus with bowed head and controlled breath, the archbishop of Thessalonica vindicated the whole method — naming, in his own voice, the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer,” in which “the intellect’s noetic activity is easily ordered and purified.”35 The body, he argued, is no obstacle to prayer but is drawn into it — it is “not out of place to teach people, especially beginners… through control of breathing” — and he quotes the inherited hesychast method he is defending: “Have no other occupation or meditation than the cry of: ‘Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me!’” This is the fixed formula of the Jesus Prayer as it had crystallized, vindicated at Constantinople (1341–1351) and grounded in Palamas’s distinction between God’s unknowable essence and the uncreated energies in which the one who invokes the Name truly shares.36

Bernardine of Siena — the Western Holy Name devotion; the IHS tablet charged as idolatry and vindicated (c. 1420)

In the Latin West the Name took a public, civic form. The Franciscan preacher Bernardine of Siena made devotion to the Holy Name of Jesus the centre of his missions, raising at the climax of each sermon a tablet painted with the monogram IHS in a blaze of golden rays and exposing it for the crowd’s veneration — and persuading the warring cities of Italy to strike the arms of their factions from wall and gateway and set the sacred initials there instead. He preached the Name as “the glory of preachers, because the shining splendor of that name causes his word to be proclaimed and heard.”37 The devotion drew the very charge this whole timeline turns on: he was accused of “having introduced a profane, new devotion which exposed the people to the danger of idolatry,” summoned to Rome (c. 1426), examined before Pope Martin V — John of Capistrano conducting his defence — and fully acquitted, the pope commending his teaching and pressing him to preach in the city. The attack was renewed under Eugene IV and finally quashed by that pope’s surviving Bull of 7 January 1432, which annulled the proceedings and silenced the accuser. So the Western church, like the Byzantine, ruled the veneration of the Name licit while policing the line against treating it as a talisman.38

The Way of a Pilgrim — the Jesus Prayer carried to a lay and Western readership (c. 1860)

The monastic practice reaches the laity in an anonymous Russian narrative. Its wanderer, arrested by St Paul’s “Pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17 — the very text that had launched Diadochos’s teaching), is taught by a starets the continual invocation of the Name: “The continuous interior Prayer of Jesus is a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart… at all times, in all places, even during sleep. The appeal is couched in these terms, ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’”39 He is to say it “three thousand times a day,” then six, then “twelve thousand times a day,” until the counted Prayer becomes the self-acting prayer of the heart the whole tradition aims at — the point Diadochos described and Climacus bound to the breath.40 More than any other book it carried the Jesus Prayer west; whether the continual invocation of the “divine Name of Jesus” is a distinct spirituality of the Name — and whether the Name itself is divine, the twentieth-century Athonite onomatodoxy (imiaslavie) dispute of 1913 — is weighed among this doctrine’s arguments.

Related arguments — the Name and magic

The boundary the whole doctrine polices: the Name has real power, yet is not a formula severable from faith and belonging to the One named. One argument states the mechanism (where the power sits); one tests it on the single most-cited cautionary proof-text; one reads the church’s one legislative boundary-marker on invoking the heavenly names (the Council of Laodicea’s canon on angel-invocation); and the last pushes the mechanism to its sharpest thesis — that in the Jesus Prayer the Name of God is God himself — the doctrine’s most extreme test of where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins.

Is the Name’s power intrinsic to the words, or the power of the Person invoked? (assessment: sound)

Is the Name’s power intrinsic to the words, or is it the power of the Person invoked? — sound: the efficacy is the Person’s, laid hold of by faith and relation to him (Acts 3:16, “through faith in his name”; the sons of Sceva and Matthew 7:22–23, the word without the Lord availing nothing; “it is Christ who worketh them”). Name-realism — a real, natural power in the very sounds of the divine names — is a coherent but overstated account of the mechanism, weighed on its own pages (the modest name-realism, plausible; the charge that Origen made names a magical art, contested). This is the crux that separates Christian devotion from magic.

The sons of Sceva (Acts 19:13–17): warrant or rebuke? (assessment: sound)

Does the episode warrant the power of the Name, or rebuke its magical misuse? — sound: it does both, and they are one finding. The evil spirit’s “Jesus I know, and Paul I know” concedes the Name’s real authority; the vagabond exorcists’ rout — they had the words exact yet fled “naked and wounded” — shows that authority is relational, not a spell in any mouth. Luke sets the scene just before the Ephesian magicians burn their books, the gospel drawing its own line between the Name and magic; the fathers are unanimous that the failure lies in the men, not the Name (Chrysostom: “not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith”).

Invoking angels by name: what did the Council of Laodicea (canon 35) forbid? (assessment: sound)

Where is the line between honouring the names heaven bears and name-invocation as magic? — sound: the mid-4th-century canon condemns a specific heterodox cult of angels — the Phrygian practice, already rebuked by Paul at Colossae, of making angels intermediary saviours who secure access to a too-distant God (bypassing Christ), invoked by name in the manner of the adjuration-magic — and not the honouring or naming of angels as such. The earliest interpreter, Theodoret, localizes the abuse to Phrygia and Pisidia and notes that lawful oratories of St Michael stood among the same people; Lightfoot reads the canon as the legislative echo of the Colossian “worshipping of angels”; the church’s reception permits the revealed names (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael) while forbidding invented ones. The blanket reading — that it bans all invocation of angels — lifts the words out of the abuse they named. The line the canon draws in law is the line the whole doctrine draws: no name in heaven or earth may stand where Christ’s mediation stands.

Is the Name of God God himself? The onomatodoxy (imiaslavie) thesis (assessment: contested)

Is the twentieth-century Athonite thesis that “the Name of God is God himself” a legitimate development, or a lapse into name-magic and pantheism? — contested: the Russian imiaslavie (“Name-glorifying”) dispute that peaked on Mount Athos in 1912–1913 pressed the Jesus Prayer to its maximal claim, and it splits along its own wording. In its crude form — that the very sounds and letters of the word are divine and work of themselves — it is the phonemic name-magic the whole doctrine polices, and is weak. In the refined form its best proponents actually advanced — the Name as a bearer of the uncreated energies (a Palamite grounding), so that God is truly present and operative in his Name while remaining beyond it (Losev: the Name is God through the energy, yet “God is not His name”) — it is a coherent, seriously-defended reading. Condemned as pantheistic by the Russian Holy Synod and the Ecumenical Patriarchate in 1913 (on a one-sided, state-enforced procedure), defended by Florensky, Bulgakov, and Losev, left open when the 1917–18 Moscow Council was aborted by the Revolution, and never ruled on by an Ecumenical Council — genuinely disputed, with no authoritative verdict. This is the extreme case of the intrinsic-power question above.
