The Power of the Name

From its first pages the church believed that the Name has power — above all the name of Jesus. Demons flee at it, the sick are healed by it, prayer is offered in it, converts are baptized into it, and salvation is found in no other. “There is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” (Acts 4:12); God has “given him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow” (Philippians 2:9–11). This page traces how that conviction — that to invoke the Name is to lay hold of the One named, and that the Name itself is effectual in exorcism, prayer, baptism, and devotion — developed and was disciplined across the centuries, from the New Testament through the fathers to the Byzantine Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern controversies over invoking it.

The belief did not develop in a vacuum. The ancient world was full of names of power — the adjurations of the magical papyri, Jewish name-magic, the untranslatable divine names guarded by theurgists — and the church grew up claiming a real power for its own Name while drawing a hard line against sorcery. That line is the recurring tension this timeline follows: patristic writers insist the Name of Jesus truly casts out demons, yet condemn the magician who would wield a name as a formula. Where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins is a boundary the tradition polices again and again — and several of the arguments below turn on exactly that question. (This is a page of Christian theology and history — the efficacy of the Name in prayer, exorcism, baptism, and devotion, and the church’s boundary-drawing against magic. Pagan and Jewish name-lore appears only as historical backdrop, weighed critically, never as instruction.)

How this differs from the Divine Name: that timeline concerns the Tetragrammaton’s text and pronunciation — how יהוה was written, avoided, and voiced (Yahweh, Jehovah, the qere “Adonai”). This one concerns the efficacy and invocation of the Name — chiefly the name of Jesus — a distinct but adjacent question. The two meet in the fathers who held that the divine names carry a power lodged in their very sounds, untranslatable without loss; that name-realism is weighed under the first language of mankind (was the primordial language a divine gift?) and, where it shades toward magic, on the argument Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power? See also astrology and the stars, the neighbouring field the fathers policed for the same reason.

The biblical foundation

Before the fathers could theorize the power of the Name they had a Scripture that everywhere assumes it: a Name at which demons are subject, by which the lame are healed, in which prayer is answered and the believer is baptized, and outside which there is no salvation — together with two hard warnings that the Name is not a spell to be worked by outsiders. This is the layer every witness below builds on.

“In my name shall they cast out devils” — the Name in exorcism and healing

The earliest datum is the Name that subdues spirits. The seventy return from their mission “with joy, saying, Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name,” and the risen Lord promises that among the signs following those who believe, “In my name shall they cast out devils.”1 The apostles act on it at once. Peter heals the lame man at the temple gate not by his own power but by a word: “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk”; and Paul turns on the spirit in the slave-girl, “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.”2 The power is bound so tightly to the Name and not to the wielder’s standing that when the disciples try to stop a stranger “casting out devils in thy name,” the Lord overrules them: “Forbid him not: for there is no man which shall do a miracle in my name, that can lightly speak evil of me.”3 The Name is not a description of the act but its instrument; the fathers’ whole doctrine of exorcistic power in the Name begins here — as does the harder question the timeline must answer, why the Name avails an unknown outsider here yet turns on the sons of Sceva below.

“There is none other name” — the Name above every name

Hauled before the Sanhedrin and asked “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” Peter answers that the man was healed “by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” and presses it to its farthest reach: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.”4 The Philippian hymn sets the same Name at the summit of the cosmos: because of the obedience of the cross “God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth.”5 And the apostolic gospel makes calling on the Name the very hinge of being saved, taking up Joel’s oracle of the Day of the Lord: “whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” The word Joel spoke of the Name of the LORD (יהוה) Peter at Pentecost and Paul to the Romans apply, without apology, to the name of Jesus — so that to invoke the one Name is to invoke the other.6 The Name is thus not one power among many but the one before which all powers bend — the note the later Holy Name devotion would sound.

“Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name” — the Name in prayer, baptism, and life

The Name is also the medium of prayer and of belonging. “Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do,” the Lord promises; “ask, and ye shall receive, that your joy may be full”; “where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them”; and John writes his Gospel “that believing ye might have life through his name.”7 The convert is claimed by the Name in baptism — “baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost,” “be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins” — and lives out of it: he is “washed,” “sanctified,” “justified in the name of the Lord Jesus,” and when he is sick the elders pray over him, “anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord.”8 So that to be a Christian is to have the Name named over one. Invocation, gathering, baptism, and life all run through it.

“Jesus I know… but who are ye?” — the Name is not a spell

Yet the same Scriptures set the boundary the fathers would spend centuries defending: the Name has power, but it is not a formula severable from faith and from belonging to the One named. The vagabond Jewish exorcists at Ephesus try to wield it as a magic word — “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — and the evil spirit turns on them: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The sons of Sceva flee “naked and wounded,” and by the lesson “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified.”9 The Lord’s own warning is sharper still: “Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord… in thy name have [we not] cast out devils?… And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.”10 Even true works “in thy name” do not avail the one the Lord does not know. So the Name is effectual and yet not at anyone’s disposal — the exact tension the timeline below works out, and the line every father draws between the power of the Name and the presumption of the magician.

Justin Martyr — the name of Jesus crucified routs demons (c. 160)

Justin is the earliest father to make the exorcistic power of the Name a public, verifiable argument. Four times he appeals to a fact he says his hearers can check with their own eyes: demons are “overcome and subdued” when Christians exorcise them “in the name of Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate,” and “the power of whose name even the demons do fear” is a present, ongoing power — grounded not in the exorcist but in what the Father gave the Son through “the dispensation of His suffering.”11 Writing to the Roman authorities he makes it empirical: “numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city,” are healed by “Christian men exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ… though they could not be cured by all the other exorcists, and those who used incantations and drugs.”12 And he draws the doctrine’s central line himself: this one Name subdues the demon where the names of “kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs” do not, while the exorcists who trade on “craft… fumigations and incantations” are working sorcery, not wielding the Name — and even the true Name is no guarantee for the false worker the Lord does not know (Matthew 7:22–23, which Justin quotes in the same breath).13

Irenaeus of Lyons — the church works in the Name, not by incantation; the Gnostic misuse of secret names (c. 180)

Irenaeus states the doctrine’s whole tension inside one work. On the positive side, the church “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” drives out demons, heals the sick, and (he claims) has even raised the dead — “in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate,” done freely and for the benefit of others, “neither practising deception upon any, nor taking any reward.”14 And he draws the boundary himself: the church works these things “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art,” but by prayer and the plain invocation of Jesus — and it is this Name, “but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates, or of any other man whatever,” that heals; the magician Simon and his heirs, working “by means of magical deceptions,” can neither cure nor raise the dead as the church does “by means of prayer.”15 On the negative side, Irenaeus dissects the Marcosian and Valentinian “redemption” rite — Marcus “a perfect adept in magical impostures” who protracts “to great length the word of invocation,” and initiators who “repeat certain Hebrew words, in order the more thoroughly to bewilder those who are being initiated” (“Basema, Chamosse, Baoenaora…”) — as sorcery masquerading as sacrament, a rite he says was “instigated by Satan to a denial of that baptism which is regeneration to God.”16 The efficacy of the one Name and the condemnation of name-magic stand side by side in the earliest sustained form the timeline records — the boundary Origen would press harder against Celsus.

Origen of Alexandria — the fullest theory of why a name has power; names natural, not conventional (c. 248)

Origen gives the most developed patristic account of the doctrine — the theory the earlier witnesses assumed and the later ones inherit. Answering Celsus’s gibe that it makes no difference whether God be called Zeus, or Sabaoth, or Ammon, he argues that the nature of names is “a deep and mysterious subject”: names were given “by nature,” not “by arrangement,” so that a power is lodged in the very sounds — “it is not the things signified, but the qualities and peculiarities of words, which possess a certain power.” Hence the Hebrew divine names — Sabaoth, Adonai, “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, of Jacob” — are “possessed of great power” and make “even demons… vanquished,” yet render them into Greek and “the mention of the name is attended with no result”; the angelic names (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael) are fitted “appropriately to the duties which they discharge.”17 Above all, the name of Jesus “has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits”; Christians prevail “not by incantations… but by the name of Jesus,” a Name so bound to the One named rather than to the speaker that it is effectual “even… pronounced… by bad men” (Matthew 7:22).18 But Origen draws the boundary himself, and hardest: sorcery is forbidden by the law (“Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them”) and demonic in its power — the conjurers’ art is “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit,” and the Magi’s sorcery collapsed at the Nativity, “the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken.” The divine names vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts; the reality of names is no license for magic.19

Athanasius of Alexandria — the Name’s power made an apologetic proof of the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ (c. 318)

Athanasius keeps the doctrine intact but turns it to a new use. Where the earlier witnesses wield the Name in exorcism, he marshals its power as public evidence: since “actions, and power over men, belong to the living only,” the plain fact that “where Christ is named… all idolatry is deposed” and every spirit “on barely hearing it flies and disappears” proves that the One named is not dead but risen — “and especially… God.”20 The demons themselves are pressed into the proof: they would not obey a dead man, yet “if they be manifestly expelled by the naming of His name… He is not dead,” for the spirits “see… that He is God, and hence they fly and fall at His feet.” The argument turns historical, too — the whole ancient apparatus of oracles, demon-cult, and magic that was “strong and active” before Christ has fallen silent “since Christ has begun to be preached everywhere” — and it ends in a standing dare: “let him come who would test by experience… let him use the Sign of that Cross… and he shall see how by its means demons fly, oracles cease, all magic and witchcraft is brought to nought.”21 Athanasius keeps the old boundary against magic and argues it freshly: Christ cannot be a magician, for “by a magician all magic is destroyed, instead of being confirmed,” and the very demons the magicians conjure “fly from Him as their Master.” The same doctrine he narrates as a life in his Life of Antony, where Antony routs demons “by prayer and speaking the name of Christ” and tells the pagan philosophers that “where the sign of the Cross is, magic is weak and witchcraft has no strength.”22
