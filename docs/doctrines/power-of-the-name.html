The Power of the Name

Christian writers from the New Testament onward attest the belief that the Name has power — above all the name of Jesus: that demons flee at it, the sick are healed by it, prayer is offered in it, converts are baptized into it, and salvation is found in no other. “There is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” (Acts 4:12); God has “given him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow” (Philippians 2:9–11). This page records, earliest extant attestation first, how that belief — that to invoke the Name is to lay hold of the One named, and that the Name itself is effectual in exorcism, prayer, baptism, and devotion — was stated, practised and bounded across the centuries, from the New Testament through the fathers to the Byzantine Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern controversies over invoking it.

The ancient world was full of names of power — the adjurations of the magical papyri, Jewish name-magic, the untranslatable divine names guarded by theurgists — and Christian writers claimed a real power for the name of Jesus while condemning sorcery. Patristic writers insist that the Name of Jesus casts out demons, yet condemn the magician who would wield a name as a formula, and where the power of the Name ends and magic begins recurs as a question across the timeline; several of the disputed readings below turn on it. Pagan and Jewish name-lore appears on this page as historical background.

How this differs from the Divine Name: that timeline concerns the Tetragrammaton’s text and pronunciation — how יהוה was written, avoided, and voiced (Yahweh, Jehovah, the qere “Adonai”). This one concerns the efficacy and invocation of the Name — chiefly the name of Jesus — a distinct but adjacent question. The two meet in the fathers who held that the divine names carry a power lodged in their very sounds, untranslatable without loss; that view of names is traced under the first language of mankind (was the primordial language a divine gift?) and, in its relation to magic, on the question whether Origen taught that certain names have a real, natural power. The biblical foundation

The New Testament texts the later witnesses cite speak of a Name at which demons are subject, by which the lame are healed, in which prayer is answered and the believer is baptized, and outside which there is no salvation, together with two passages that later writers read as warnings against using the Name as a spell.

“In my name shall they cast out devils” — the Name in exorcism and healing

The Gospels report the Name subduing spirits. The seventy return from their mission “with joy, saying, Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name,” and the risen Lord promises that among the signs following those who believe, “In my name shall they cast out devils.”1 In Acts the apostles act in the Name. Peter heals the lame man at the temple gate not by his own power but by a word: “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk”; and Paul turns on the spirit in the slave-girl, “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.”2 When the disciples try to stop a stranger “casting out devils in thy name,” the Lord overrules them: “Forbid him not: for there is no man which shall do a miracle in my name, that can lightly speak evil of me.”3

“There is none other name” — the Name above every name

Hauled before the Sanhedrin and asked “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” Peter answers that the man was healed “by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” and presses it to its farthest reach: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.”4 The Philippian hymn sets the same Name at the summit of the cosmos: because of the obedience of the cross “God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth.”5 Acts and Romans make calling on the Name the condition of being saved, taking up Joel’s oracle of the Day of the Lord: “whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” Peter at Pentecost and Paul to the Romans apply Joel’s words about the Name of the LORD (יהוה) to the name of Jesus.6

“Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name” — the Name in prayer, baptism, and life

The same texts speak of prayer, gathering and baptism in the Name. “Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do,” the Lord promises; “ask, and ye shall receive, that your joy may be full”; “where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them”; and John writes his Gospel “that believing ye might have life through his name.”7 The convert is claimed by the Name in baptism — “baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost,” “be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins” — and is “washed,” “sanctified,” “justified in the name of the Lord Jesus,” and when he is sick the elders pray over him, “anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord.”8

“Jesus I know… but who are ye?” — the sons of Sceva and “I never knew you”

Two passages are cited by later writers on the limits of the Name’s use. The vagabond Jewish exorcists at Ephesus adjure a spirit by it — “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — and the evil spirit turns on them: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The sons of Sceva flee “naked and wounded,” and “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified.”9 In Matthew the Lord warns: “Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord… in thy name have [we not] cast out devils?… And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.”10 How the later witnesses read the Ephesus episode is set out on What tradition did the Jewish exorcists of Acts 19:13–16 stand in?

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — after the New Testament texts above, the fathers who wrote on the Name’s power, the Byzantine tradition of the Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern literature of the Jesus Prayer.

Justin Martyr — the name of Jesus crucified routs demons (c. 160)

Justin’s writings are the earliest extant patristic appeal to exorcism in the Name as a public, checkable fact. Four times he appeals to a fact he says his hearers can check with their own eyes: demons are “overcome and subdued” when Christians exorcise them “in the name of Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate,” and “the power of whose name even the demons do fear” is a present, ongoing power, which he grounds not in the exorcist but in what the Father gave the Son through “the dispensation of His suffering.”11 Writing to the Roman authorities he points to cures: “numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city,” are healed by “Christian men exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ… though they could not be cured by all the other exorcists, and those who used incantations and drugs.”12 He contrasts this one Name, which he says subdues the demon, with the names of “kings, or righteous men, or prophets, or patriarchs,” which he says do not, while the exorcists who trade on “craft… fumigations and incantations” he counts as working sorcery; and he quotes the Lord’s warning to those who work in his name whom he does not know (Matthew 7:22–23, at Dialogue 76).13

Irenaeus of Lyons — exorcism in the Name, not by incantation; the Gnostic rites of secret names (c. 180)

Irenaeus writes in one work both of the church’s exorcisms in the Name and of Gnostic name-rites. The church “calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” drives out demons, heals the sick, and (he claims) has even raised the dead — “in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate,” done freely and for the benefit of others, “neither practising deception upon any, nor taking any reward.”14 He distinguishes the means: the church works these things “not… by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art,” but by prayer and the invocation of Jesus — and it is this Name, “but not that of Simon, or Menander, or Carpocrates, or of any other man whatever,” that heals; the magician Simon and his heirs, working “by means of magical deceptions,” can neither cure nor raise the dead as the church does “by means of prayer.”15 Irenaeus also describes the Marcosian and Valentinian “redemption” rite — Marcus “a perfect adept in magical impostures” who protracts “to great length the word of invocation,” and initiators who “repeat certain Hebrew words, in order the more thoroughly to bewilder those who are being initiated” (“Basema, Chamosse, Baoenaora…”) — a rite he treats as sorcery and says was “instigated by Satan to a denial of that baptism which is regeneration to God.”16

Tertullian of Carthage — the demons compelled to confess and flee at the naming of Christ; a challenge to the courtroom (c. 197)

Tertullian challenges the Roman magistrates to test the exorcistic power of the Name. Bring any demoniac before the tribunal, he writes: the possessing spirit, “bidden to speak by a follower of Christ,” will confess itself a demon, and — for “all the authority and power we have over them is from our naming the name of Christ” — will leave the body it holds, “Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ… So at our touch and breathing… they leave at our command the bodies they have entered, unwilling, and distressed, and before your very eyes put to an open shame.” He locates the power in the demons’ dread of “Christ as Judge,” and answers in advance the charge of magic: “Do you say that it is done by magic…? You will not say anything of the sort, if you have been allowed the use of your ears and eyes.”17 To the proconsul Scapula he names cases reaching into the imperial establishment itself — clerks and kin freed from evil spirits, “the little boy of a third,” men of rank “delivered from devils,” and the emperor Severus cured by the Christian Proculus — the deliverances offered, like Irenaeus’s, “without reward or hire.”18 He treats “magic… of which astrology is a species” as a “curious art” whose first teachers were the fallen “angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women” — so that, on his account, the craft of magic is the demons’ gift, and the Christian who names Christ commands the spirits the magician serves.19

Origen of Alexandria — a theory of why a name has power; names natural, not conventional (c. 248)

Origen gives an account of why names have power. Answering Celsus’s gibe that it makes no difference whether God be called Zeus, or Sabaoth, or Ammon, he argues that the nature of names is “a deep and mysterious subject”: names were given “by nature,” not “by arrangement,” so that a power is lodged in the very sounds — “it is not the things signified, but the qualities and peculiarities of words, which possess a certain power.” Hence the Hebrew divine names — Sabaoth, Adonai, “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, of Jacob” — are “possessed of great power” and make “even demons… vanquished,” yet render them into Greek and “the mention of the name is attended with no result”; the angelic names (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael) are fitted “appropriately to the duties which they discharge.”20 Above all, the name of Jesus “has already been seen… to have expelled myriads of evil spirits”; Christians prevail “not by incantations… but by the name of Jesus,” and the Name has been effectual “even… pronounced… by bad men” (Matthew 7:22).21 He also condemns sorcery: sorcery is forbidden by the law (“Neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them”) and demonic in its power — the conjurers’ art is “the proceedings of wicked men under the influence of an evil spirit,” and the Magi’s sorcery collapsed at the Nativity, “the falsity of their sorcery being manifested, and their power being broken.” On his account the divine names vanquish the demons the sorcerer courts.22

Cyprian of Carthage — demons adjured by the Name confess and are cast out; the pagan gods unmasked as the spirits it expels (c. 250)

Cyprian, writing in Latin at mid-century, describes the practice. The demons, he writes, “when adjured by us through the true God, at once yield and confess, and are constrained to go out from the bodies possessed” — smitten, howling, and confessing “whence they came,” the spirit driven out “even as the faith of the sufferer comes in aid, or the grace of the healer effects.” He turns the datum apologetically, first against the idolater and then against the persecutor Demetrianus, whom he dares to come and hear his own gods “betraying what they are”: the very spirits the pagan venerates “as lords” are the ones that, at a Christian’s voice and “the power of God,” “stand bound, and tremble as captives” — so that for him the exorcism both shows the Name’s power and unmasks idolatry. He turns the charge of magic around: it was the unbelieving who, seeing Christ “dr[i]ve out… the demons by His word,” slandered the “authority of His power” as sorcery.23 In his exposition of the Lord’s Prayer he treats the Name in devotion (Tertullian had expounded the same petition in On Prayer 3): “Hallowed be Thy name” asks “not that we wish for God that He may be hallowed by our prayers, but that we beseech of Him that His name may be hallowed in us” — the believer “sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” in baptism.24

Athanasius of Alexandria — the Name’s power as apologetic evidence for the Resurrection and the Godhead of Christ (c. 318)

Athanasius presents the Name’s power as public evidence: since “actions, and power over men, belong to the living only,” the fact, as he presents it, that “where Christ is named… all idolatry is deposed” and every spirit “on barely hearing it flies and disappears” is, he argues, evidence that the One named is not dead but risen — “and especially… God.”25 He draws the demons into the argument: they would not obey a dead man, yet “if they be manifestly expelled by the naming of His name… He is not dead,” for the spirits “see… that He is God, and hence they fly and fall at His feet.” He also argues from history — the whole ancient apparatus of oracles, demon-cult, and magic that was “strong and active” before Christ has fallen silent “since Christ has begun to be preached everywhere” — and it ends in a standing dare: “let him come who would test by experience… let him use the Sign of that Cross… and he shall see how by its means demons fly, oracles cease, all magic and witchcraft is brought to nought.”26 He argues that Christ cannot be a magician, for “by a magician all magic is destroyed, instead of being confirmed,” and the very demons the magicians conjure “fly from Him as their Master.” In the Life of Antony he narrates the same teaching, where Antony routs demons “by prayer and speaking the name of Christ” and tells the pagan philosophers that “where the sign of the Cross is, magic is weak and witchcraft has no strength.”27

Cyril of Jerusalem — the exorcisms of the catechumenate, the renunciation of Satan, and baptism “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost” (c. 350)

Catechizing the baptismal candidates at Jerusalem, Cyril describes the Name invoked in the church’s rite of initiation. Through Lent the convert is exorcised again and again: “receive with earnestness the exorcisms… without exorcisms the soul cannot be purified” — and when “the exorcists inspire terror by the Spirit of God… the hostile demon flees away.”28 On the night of baptism the candidate turns to face the West, “the region of sensible darkness,” and with arm outstretched “as though [Satan] were present” renounces him aloud — “I renounce thee, Satan” — and Cyril says that “the blood of the Lamb without blemish Jesus Christ is made the charm to scare evil spirits,” and the “author of evil,” pursuing the convert “even to the very streams of salvation,” “disappears in the water of salvation.” Turning then from West to East, the candidate confesses “I believe in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost”.29 Cyril describes the mechanism: “the breathing of the saints, and the invocation of the Name of God, like fiercest flame, scorch and drive out evil spirits”; and he describes the font, where each candidate is “asked, whether he believed in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost… and descended three times into the water.” Among the renounced “service of the devil” Cyril lists “amulets, or charms written on leaves, sorceries, or other evil arts.”30

John Chrysostom — “not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith”; the renunciation and the sign of the cross in daily life (c. 400)

Preaching on the sons of Sceva, Chrysostom explains the vagabond exorcists’ rout in one line — “then not the Name does anything, unless it be spoken with faith” — and insists that the demon is made to own Christ (“Jesus I know, and Paul I know”) before it is loosed on the men, “that there may not seem to be any slight put upon the Name of Jesus”: the collapse is “not any weakness of the Name, but all owing to the imposture of those men,” who worked “by way of trade” as Simon Magus had sought the Spirit “for the sake of merchandise.”31 In his catechetical preaching he answers converts who excused the village enchantress because she “utters nothing else than the name of God”: “even the demons uttered the name of God, but still they were demons” — they confessed Christ the “Holy One of God” and were driven off all the same — so that, on his account, a name on an enchantress’s lips gives her no share in its power.32 He also prescribes for daily life: the convert is to cross no threshold without the renunciation (“I leave thy ranks, Satan… and I join the ranks of Christ”) and the sign of the cross on his forehead — armour before which “even the devil himself, will be unable to hurt you at all” — which he sets against the amulets and “golden coins of Alexander of Macedon” the age trusted, for the cross “has undone the power of the devil.” The renunciation and the sign that Cyril describes in the baptismal rite Chrysostom prescribes for everyday life.33

Augustine of Hippo — the idols fall where Christ is named, the church exorcises rather than propitiates, and the licit and illicit sign (c. 415)

Augustine writes both of the Name’s power and of its misuse. He makes the apologetic argument Athanasius had made — that where Christ is named the idols fall: “the Christian religion is overthrowing all lying figments… in order that the grace of the true Saviour may deliver men from those gods which man has made” — and turns the exorcistic claim against the Platonists who would placate the spirits: men of God cast out the hostile power “by exorcising it, not by propitiating it… conquered in the name of Him who assumed humanity,” while in his own day the deliverances go on, for “even now miracles are wrought in the name of Christ, whether by His sacraments or by the prayers or relics of His saints” (the Hesperius household freed from evil spirits by prayer and “the sacrifice of the body of Christ,” a young woman of Hippo “immediately dispossessed of a devil”).34 He also distinguishes licit and illicit signs: amulets, incantations, and “marks which they call characters” are “a guilty superstition, springing out of a baleful fellowship between men and devils” — the herb drunk as medicine lawful, he holds, where the same herb “hung round the neck” as a charm is condemned, the line running not through the object but through whether the thing acts “by a natural virtue” or “by a sort of charm.”35 He warns against the seducers who “mingle the name of Christ with their incantations” — while allowing that a man may lay the Gospel at his aching head, “not because it is done for this purpose, but because the gospel is preferred to amulets”: on his account the line between devotion and charm runs through the disposition of the heart, not the outward object.36

Diadochos of Photike — the continual invocation “Lord Jesus” in the heart (c. 486)

Diadochos, the fifth-century bishop of Photike, gives the praying intellect the Name as its whole occupation: “For the complete fulfillment of its purpose we should give it nothing but the prayer ‘Lord Jesus’… Let the intellect continually concentrate on these words within its inner shrine… Those who meditate unceasingly upon this glorious and holy name in the depths of their heart can sometimes see the light of their own intellect.” He grounds the invocation in the Spirit — “no one can say ‘Lord Jesus’ except in the Holy Spirit” (1 Corinthians 12:3) — and grace itself, he says, comes “to repeat with” the soul the words “Lord Jesus,” “just as a mother teaches her child to repeat with her the word ‘father’… until she has formed in him the habit of calling for his father even in his sleep.”37 The maturing soul, he writes, “calls ceaselessly upon the Lord Jesus” until it has “the remembrance of the Lord Jesus unceasingly” in the heart.38

John Climacus and the Sinai tradition — the Name bound to the breath; “no stronger weapon in heaven or earth” (c. 630)

At Sinai the invocation is joined to breathing. The abbot John Climacus, in the Ladder of Divine Ascent, makes the invocation a thing of every breath: “Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with your every breath. Then indeed you will appreciate the value of stillness”39 — joining the remembrance of Jesus to hesychia (stillness), the term from which the practice takes its other name, hesychasm. He applies the Name to the ascetic’s warfare: “Flog your enemies with the name of Jesus, for there is no stronger weapon in heaven or earth.” Hesychios of Sinai teaches the same — the heart that “breathes and invokes, endlessly and without ceasing, only Jesus Christ,” and “let the name of Jesus adhere to your breath”.40

Bernard of Clairvaux — the sweetness of the Name: “light, food, medicine” (c. 1140)

Bernard writes of the sweetness of the Name. Preaching on the Song of Songs’ “thy name is as ointment poured forth” (1:3), the Cistercian abbot Bernard of Clairvaux reads the Name of the Bridegroom as oil with a threefold property — “it gives light, it nourishes, it anoints… When preached it gives light, when meditated it nourishes, when invoked it relieves and soothes”: light, food, and medicine.41 The same sermon contains the lines — “Write what you will, I shall not relish it unless it tells of Jesus… Jesus to me is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, a song in the heart” — and commends the Name as a remedy for every ill of the soul: the sad, the despairing, the tempted have only to invoke it, for “for all the ills and disorders to which flesh is heir, this name is medicine.”42 The hymn Jesu dulcis memoria was long ascribed to Bernard; its authorship is discussed in the sources cited.42

Gregory Palamas — the Jesus Prayer and the “single-phrased” invocation defended (c. 1340)

When the Calabrian Barlaam ridiculed the Athonite monks who invoked the name of Jesus with bowed head and controlled breath, Palamas, archbishop of Thessalonica, defended the method — naming, in his own voice, the “single-phrased Jesus Prayer,” in which “the intellect’s noetic activity is easily ordered and purified.”43 The body, he argued, is no obstacle to prayer but is drawn into it — it is “not out of place to teach people, especially beginners… through control of breathing” — and he quotes the inherited hesychast method he is defending: “Have no other occupation or meditation than the cry of: ‘Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me!’” This is the formula of the Jesus Prayer; councils at Constantinople (1341–1351) upheld Palamas’s teaching, which rests on his distinction between God’s unknowable essence and the uncreated energies in which, on his teaching, the one who invokes the Name shares.44

Bernardine of Siena — the Western Holy Name devotion; the IHS tablet, the charge of idolatry and the acquittal (c. 1420)

The Franciscan preacher Bernardine of Siena made devotion to the Holy Name of Jesus the centre of his missions, raising at the climax of each sermon a tablet painted with the monogram IHS in a blaze of golden rays and exposing it for the crowd’s veneration — and persuading the warring cities of Italy to strike the arms of their factions from wall and gateway and set the sacred initials there instead. He preached the Name as “the glory of preachers, because the shining splendor of that name causes his word to be proclaimed and heard.”45 He was accused of “having introduced a profane, new devotion which exposed the people to the danger of idolatry,” summoned to Rome (c. 1426), examined before Pope Martin V — John of Capistrano conducting his defence — and acquitted, the pope commending his teaching and pressing him to preach in the city. The attack was renewed under Eugene IV and ended by that pope’s surviving Bull of 7 January 1432, which annulled the proceedings and silenced the accuser.46

The Holy Name confraternity and feast — the Latin devotion institutionalized: the Society of the Holy Name, the universal feast, and the Jesuit monogram (c. 1564)

In the Latin church the devotion took institutional form. Reverence for the Name and the checking of its abuse were fixed as the charge of a papally approved confraternity — the Dominican Society of the Holy Name, whose object is “to beget due love and reverence for the Holy Name of God and Jesus Christ” and to suppress blasphemy, tracing its commission to the Second Council of Lyons (1274) and Gregory X’s letter to Blessed John of Vercelli, taking organized form in Didacus of Victoria’s Confraternity of the Holy Name of God against blasphemers, and approved by Pius IV (1564) and reserved to the Dominicans by Pius V’s Decet Romanum Pontificem (1571).47 The Feast of the Most Holy Name of Jesus, granted with its Office to the Franciscans by Clement VII in 1530, spread order by order and diocese by diocese until Innocent XIII set it on the calendar of the whole Latin church in 1721.48 The IHS monogram that Bernardine had displayed was taken by Ignatius of Loyola as the seal of the Society of Jesus (1541).49

The Way of a Pilgrim — the Jesus Prayer carried to a lay and Western readership (c. 1860)

An anonymous Russian narrative presents the practice to a lay reader. Its wanderer, arrested by St Paul’s “Pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17), is taught by a starets the continual invocation of the Name: “The continuous interior Prayer of Jesus is a constant uninterrupted calling upon the divine Name of Jesus with the lips, in the spirit, in the heart… at all times, in all places, even during sleep. The appeal is couched in these terms, ‘Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me.’”50 He is to say it “three thousand times a day,” then six, then “twelve thousand times a day,” until the counted Prayer becomes the self-acting prayer of the heart.51 It reached a Western readership in R. M. French’s English translation of 1930.50 Whether the Name of God is itself divine was disputed on Mount Athos in 1913, in the onomatodoxy (imiaslavie) controversy (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced on this page).

The Name and magic

How the evidence for name-magic in the world of the early church, and the church’s rulings on it, have been read.

  • What tradition did the Jewish exorcists of Acts 19:13–16 stand in? — a craft of exorcism worked for gain (Justin, Chrysostom), or the exorcism art attributed to Solomon (Bede, from Josephus).
  • What do the Greek Magical Papyri’s uses of the name of Jesus show about Christian invocation of the Name? — read with Celsus and Morton Smith, or with Irenaeus, Origen and Clinton E. Arnold.
  • What did canon 35 of the Council of Laodicea forbid? — a Phrygian angel-cult, the giving of unrevealed names to angels, or all invocation of angels.

The scribes and the sacred names

  • What do the nomina sacra of the earliest Christian manuscripts express? — the contracted, overlined forms of God, Lord, Jesus and Christ, read as reverence, as a marker of the sacred referent, or as a convention of unrecoverable origin.
