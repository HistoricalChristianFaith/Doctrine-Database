The Power of the Name

From its first pages the church believed that the Name has power — above all the name of Jesus. Demons flee at it, the sick are healed by it, prayer is offered in it, converts are baptized into it, and salvation is found in no other. “There is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” (Acts 4:12); God has “given him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow” (Philippians 2:9–11). This page traces how that conviction — that to invoke the Name is to lay hold of the One named, and that the Name itself is effectual in exorcism, prayer, baptism, and devotion — developed and was disciplined across the centuries, from the New Testament through the fathers to the Byzantine Jesus Prayer, the medieval Holy Name devotion, and the modern controversies over invoking it.

The belief did not develop in a vacuum. The ancient world was full of names of power — the adjurations of the magical papyri, Jewish name-magic, the untranslatable divine names guarded by theurgists — and the church grew up claiming a real power for its own Name while drawing a hard line against sorcery. That line is the recurring tension this timeline follows: patristic writers insist the Name of Jesus truly casts out demons, yet condemn the magician who would wield a name as a formula. Where the power of the Name ends and the presumption of magic begins is a boundary the tradition polices again and again — and several of the arguments below turn on exactly that question. Pagan and Jewish name-lore appears only as historical backdrop, weighed critically, never as instruction.)

How this differs from the Divine Name: that timeline concerns the Tetragrammaton’s text and pronunciation — how יהוה was written, avoided, and voiced (Yahweh, Jehovah, the qere “Adonai”). This one concerns the efficacy and invocation of the Name — chiefly the name of Jesus — a distinct but adjacent question. The two meet in the fathers who held that the divine names carry a power lodged in their very sounds, untranslatable without loss; that name-realism is weighed under the first language of mankind (was the primordial language a divine gift?) and, where it shades toward magic, on the argument Did Origen teach that certain names have a real, natural power? The biblical foundation

Before the fathers could theorize the power of the Name they had a Scripture that everywhere assumes it: a Name at which demons are subject, by which the lame are healed, in which prayer is answered and the believer is baptized, and outside which there is no salvation — together with two hard warnings that the Name is not a spell to be worked by outsiders. This is the layer every witness below builds on.

“In my name shall they cast out devils” — the Name in exorcism and healing

The earliest datum is the Name that subdues spirits. The seventy return from their mission “with joy, saying, Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name,” and the risen Lord promises that among the signs following those who believe, “In my name shall they cast out devils.”1 The apostles act on it at once. Peter heals the lame man at the temple gate not by his own power but by a word: “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk”; and Paul turns on the spirit in the slave-girl, “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.”2 The Name is not a description of the act but its instrument; the fathers’ whole doctrine of exorcistic power in the Name begins here.

“There is none other name” — the Name above every name

Hauled before the Sanhedrin and asked “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” Peter answers that the man was healed “by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” and presses it to its farthest reach: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.”3 The Philippian hymn sets the same Name at the summit of the cosmos: because of the obedience of the cross “God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth.”4 The Name is thus not one power among many but the one before which all powers bend — the note the later Holy Name devotion would sound.

“Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name” — the Name in prayer, baptism, and life

The Name is also the medium of prayer and of belonging. “Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do,” the Lord promises; “where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them”; and John writes his Gospel “that believing ye might have life through his name.”5 The convert is claimed by the Name in baptism — “baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost,” “be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins”6 — so that to be a Christian is to have the Name named over one. Invocation, gathering, baptism, and life all run through it.

“Jesus I know… but who are ye?” — the Name is not a spell

Yet the same Scriptures set the boundary the fathers would spend centuries defending: the Name has power, but it is not a formula severable from faith and from belonging to the One named. The vagabond Jewish exorcists at Ephesus try to wield it as a magic word — “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” — and the evil spirit turns on them: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The sons of Sceva flee “naked and wounded,” and by the lesson “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified.”7 The Lord’s own warning is sharper still: “Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord… in thy name have [we not] cast out devils?… And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.”8 Even true works “in thy name” do not avail the one the Lord does not know. So the Name is effectual and yet not at anyone’s disposal — the exact tension the timeline below works out, and the line every father draws between the power of the Name and the presumption of the magician.
