Is the Qur’anic prophet Shu‘ayb the same man as the biblical Jethro?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Jethro? · witness: Shu‘ayb and the Druze: the Islamic Jethro

The passage

And to [the people of] Madyan [We sent] their brother Shu‘ayb. He said, “O my people, worship Allāh; you have no deity other than Him… So fulfill the measure and weight and do not deprive people of their due…”

— Qur’an 7:85 (Saheeh International), source.

Now Moses kept the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian

— Exodus 3:1 (KJV), source.1

The question

The Qur’an sends the prophet Shu‘ayb to Midian; the Torah makes Jethro, “the priest of Midian,” the father-in-law of Moses. The dispute is whether the two names belong to one man, as the Muslim commentary tradition and the Druze hold, or to two figures of Midian, which bears on how the Islamic and Druze traditions about Jethro relate to the biblical narrative. No academic study of the question was opened for this page; the readings below rest on reference works and on writers within the traditions concerned.

Shu‘ayb and Jethro are one man, the prophet-priest of Midian

On this reading the prophet the Qur’an sends to Midian is the Midianite priest who sheltered Moses and gave him a wife.

Held by: the Muslim commentary tradition “starting with early commentators,” as A. J. Wensinck (Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd ed.) describes it ⚠ unverified; al-Tabari (Sunni exegete, d. 923) ⚠ unverified; the Druze community, for whom Shu‘ayb is the chief prophet.2

The reading starts from place and faith. The Qur’an’s Shu‘ayb is sent “to [the people of] Madyan” to preach the worship of the one God (Qur’an 7:85–93); the Torah’s Jethro is “the priest of Midian” who blesses the LORD and offers Him sacrifice (Exodus 18:12). Wensinck reports that the tradition “almost universally identifies the two because both are linked to the land of Midian and Moses.” Two God-fearing elders of the same land, both tied to Moses, are on this reading one figure under two names.3

The order of the stories fits the equation. The Qur’an narrates Shu‘ayb before the Moses cycle, and the Torah has Jethro as the elder into whose household Moses marries, so the prophet of Midian and the priest of Midian occupy the same place in the sequence. The tradition gave the equation its name: the English edition of Ibn Kathir’s Stories of the Prophets titles its chapter “Shu‘ayb (Jethro).”4

For the Druze the identification is a matter of faith. They hold Shu‘ayb “the same person as the biblical Jethro,” a “hidden” and “true” prophet who communicated directly with God and passed that knowledge to Moses, and the ancestor of the community. On this reading the Exodus scene in which Jethro teaches Moses how to govern (Exodus 18:17–24) records the same prophet’s instruction of Moses. The community’s holiest shrine, Nabi Shu‘ayb at Hittin, and its chief pilgrimage, the Ziyarat al-Nabi Shu‘ayb of 25–28 April, are kept as the tomb and the anniversary of the death of Shu‘ayb-Jethro.5

Shu‘ayb is an earlier prophet of Midian, not Moses’ father-in-law

On this reading Shu‘ayb is a messenger to Midian who lived before Moses, and the father-in-law who sheltered Moses is a different man.

Held by: Abdullah Yusuf Ali (The Holy Qur’an: Text, Translation and Commentary) ⚠ unverified; Jalees Ahmad (Ahmadiyya Muslim, “Prophets of Allah – Shu‘aib,” Al Hakam, 2023).6

The reading starts from the Qur’an’s wording. In Ahmad’s words, “the Holy Quran mentions both Prophet Shu‘aib and the father-in-law of Moses; however, it never suggests that they are the same person,” and the father-in-law “is not even referred to as a prophet in the Holy Quran or Islam.” The identification, on this reading, belongs to later commentary rather than to the text.7

The Qur’an’s sequence of messengers places Shu‘ayb early. Ahmad reads Surah Hud as setting him after the peoples of Noah, Salih and Lot and “not long after” Lot, and reads Surah al-A‘raf (7:104) as placing Moses’ advent after the destruction of Midian. Yusuf Ali, as reported, cites the genealogy Ibn Kathir gives (Shu‘ayb son of Mikil, son of Midian, son of Abraham, a great-grandson of the patriarch); on that lineage, he is reported to argue, the identification with Jethro, “who purportedly lived hundreds of years after Abraham,” is “impossible.” Some tellings make Shu‘ayb an ancestor of Jethro rather than the father-in-law himself ⚠ unverified.8

The two careers, on this reading, are also distinct. The Qur’anic Shu‘ayb preaches honest weights and measures, is rejected, and sees his people destroyed by earthquake, shriek and the “day of the black cloud” (Qur’an 26:176–191; 11:84–95). The Torah’s Jethro counsels Moses and “went his way into his own land” (Exodus 18:27). Shu‘ayb thus stands in the Qur’an’s line of messengers to peoples later destroyed, and the Midianite who received Moses is a separate, unnamed elder of the same land.9
