The three names as a redactional seam

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — that the father-in-law’s three names (with the Midianite/Kenite doubling) are the fingerprint of distinct sources joined in the Pentateuch — Reuel in the Yahwist (J), Jethro in the Elohist (E), Hobab from an old Kenite tradition — rather than one man carefully described. The distribution really does fall along recognized source lines, the “Jethro” passages cluster with the E/D place-name Horeb, and the same in-law’s being called both Midianite and Kenite is a second, matching seam — so the reading explains the data economically and without the verse-by-verse special pleading a harmony needs. But it rests on the contested Documentary Hypothesis, the source-assignments are not uniform (some critics put Hobab with Reuel in J), and serious scholars (Albright) read the traditions as “homogeneous,” explicable by an oral variant or a single tradent. Strong by source-critical lights, resisted by others, with no decider in the text · advanced by modern source criticism since the nineteenth century

Related arguments: the three names of Moses’ father-in-law — see also One father-in-law, not a seam — the three names harmonized

The claim

J names Moses’ father-in-law as Reuel or Hobab, whereas E knows him as Jethro, priest of Midian.

— the source-critical account, as stated in the Encyclopaedia Judaica treatment of Jethro.1 On this reading the puzzle that the harmonizers labor to dissolve is not a puzzle at all but a datum: the received Pentateuch is a composite, and the alternation of names is one of the ordinary marks by which its once-separate strands still show. The interpretation is weighed here, not any single proponent.

Who advances it

The reading is as old as the Documentary Hypothesis itself: since the nineteenth-century German critics distinguished J, E, and later strands, the three-fold name of Moses’ father-in-law has been a standard exhibit of the theory. The classic distribution assigns Reuel to J (Exodus 2:18; Numbers 10:29) and Jethro to E (Exodus 3:1; 18:1–27), with Hobab (Judges 4:11) a third, Kenite tradition — and “Reuel their father” in Exodus 2:18 read as either a secondary harmonizing gloss or a trace of the same J usage.1 The wiki’s modern-scholarship survey sets the reading among the four recurring critical results on the Jethro tradition.

The evidence for

The names track the source lines. The alternation is not random across the narrative but sorts by block: “Reuel” belongs to Exodus 2 and Numbers 10, “Jethro” to the burning-bush and counsel narratives of Exodus 3 and 18 — the very passages critics assign to different hands on independent grounds. That a name should distribute by stratum rather than by chronology is exactly what a composite text predicts and a single author does not.1

A converging marker: Horeb. The correlation does not stand alone. In Exodus 3:1, where the father-in-law is “Jethro,” the mountain of God is not called Sinai but Horeb — the place-name preferred by the E and D writers — so the “Jethro” datum and the “Horeb” datum line up in the same verse, each pointing to the same stratum. Two independent source-markers converging on one reading is stronger evidence than either alone.2

A second seam: Midianite or Kenite? The names are doubled by the ethnonyms. The Pentateuch makes Moses’ father-in-law a Midianite (Exodus 3:1; Numbers 10:29); Judges calls the same in-law a Kenite (Judges 1:16; 4:11). A single figure carried under two people-names, in just the books the names already divide, is a matching seam — and one the harmonizer must smooth by a further move (the Kenites a Midianite sub-clan), where the source reading simply reads it off the surface.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As an explanation the reading is economical and non-arbitrary. It accounts for the distribution of the names, for the Horeb correlation, and for the Midianite/Kenite doubling together, by one hypothesis, without choosing a different loose sense verse by verse; and the very awkwardness the harmonizers must labor to relieve is, on this reading, predicted rather than explained away — a redactor who inherited variant names simply left them standing. Where the harmony must supply an unattested marriage-relation or a shift of generation, the seam supplies only what source criticism already posits on other grounds.

What is overstated or contested. The reading’s confidence outruns its footing at three points. First, it presupposes the Documentary Hypothesis, which is itself contested — the neat partition of the Pentateuch into J, E, D, and P has been sharply criticized and revised across the last century, and a name-argument is only as secure as the source-model it rides on. Second, the assignments are not uniform: the “classic” Reuel-J / Jethro-E split is not the only critical division — some assign Hobab to J alongside Reuel, and treat “Reuel” in Exodus 2:18 as a misreading of Numbers 10:29 — so the exhibit does not point to one agreed reconstruction. Third, name-variation is not proof of documents: variant names for one figure can equally reflect oral tradition or a single tradent using a personal name and an honorific, and W. F. Albright, weighing the same data, judged the Jethro–Reuel–Hobab traditions “quite homogeneous.”4 The seam is a strong reading of the evidence, not a demonstration from it.

Assessment

Contested. By the lights of source criticism the redactional reading is the better explanation of the whole pattern: the names sort by stratum, the Horeb correlation and the Midianite/Kenite doubling converge on the same seams, and the account needs none of the selective looseness a harmony requires. But it is not a fact about the text so much as a verdict about a chosen model: it presupposes the Documentary Hypothesis, which remains genuinely disputed; its source-assignments differ among critics; and the same data admit an oral-variant or single-author explanation that respected scholars have preferred. The reading is strong where the documentary framework is granted and weak where it is questioned, with nothing in the passages themselves to break the tie — the classic shape of a contested interpretation, and the mirror of the harmonizing reading it opposes.
