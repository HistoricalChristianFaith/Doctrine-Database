The Fathers on Jethro

Dates: c. AD 240 (Origen’s Homilies on Exodus) – c. AD 591 (Gregory the Great’s Pastoral Rule) · a patristic reception stratum, not a single author: Origen of Alexandria (c. 240), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 390), John Chrysostom (c. 395), Augustine of Hippo (c. 420), and Gregory the Great (c. 591) · the Fathers read the Midianite priest allegorically, and along two joined lines — the right use of pagan learning and the delegation of the judges
Summary: Where the rabbis made Jethro the paradigm proselyte, the Fathers made him a figure — and a startling one, since Scripture has a Gentile priest instruct the man to whom God spoke “face to face.” Two readings grow from that scandal, and they are really one insight seen from two sides. First, the right use of pagan learning: Origen makes Moses’ acceptance of Jethro’s counsel the warrant for the Church to take up whatever is “said wisely by the Gentiles” — the same motif he elsewhere calls the “spoiling of the Egyptians” — and Gregory of Nyssa allegorizes the pagan priest’s daughter, Moses’ foreign wife, as the profane philosophy that may be “married” to serve virtue though it is “barren” of itself. Second, the delegation of the judges: Origen reads the “rulers of thousands” as the graded courts of the Church; Augustine, Chrysostom, and Gregory the Great dwell on Moses’ humility in taking correction from an outsider — a wise plan, says Augustine, is God’s whatever mind first thought it, and the pastor, says Gregory, should hand the people’s “earthly strifes” to deputies and keep himself free for “spiritual secrets.” In every case the Gentile at Sinai becomes a lesson for the Church: receive the truth wherever it is found, and govern by sharing the burden.

Where it sits

“The Fathers” here is not one book but a line of readers spanning three and a half centuries, from Origen’s Caesarean homilies (preached c. 239–242 and surviving in Rufinus’s Latin) to Gregory the Great’s Book of Pastoral Rule (c. 591). They do not treat Jethro as a chapter in a life of Moses but as a figure — a moment in the text that discloses a permanent truth about the Church — and their comments are gathered here as one patristic stratum, slotted on the timeline after the rabbinic stratum it runs alongside. Two allegorical lines recur, distinct but intertwined: the priest of Midian (and his household) read as the pagan learning the Church may rightly appropriate, and Jethro’s counsel of the judges read as the model of humility and delegated government in the ministry. Origen holds both together in a single homily; the later Fathers tend to develop one line or the other.1

Claims & evidence

Origen: the Gentile who instructs Moses, and the “spoiling of the Egyptians” (c. 240)

The load-bearing patristic text is Origen’s eleventh homily on Exodus, on the war with Amalek and the coming of Jethro. Origen is arrested by the sheer fact of the scene — the prophet to whom God spoke “face to face” taking direction from a pagan:

For when I perceive that Moses the prophet full of God, to whom God spoke “face to face,” accepted counsel from Jethro the priest of Madian, my mind goes numb with admiration. For the Scripture says, “And Moses heard the voice of his father-in-law, and did whatever he said to him.” He did not say, “God speaks to me and what I ought to do is delivered to me by a word from heaven,” and, “How shall I receive counsel from a man, and a Gentile at that, an alien from the people of God?” But he listens and does everything which he says. He hears not the one who speaks, but what he says. Whence also we, if perhaps now and then we discover something said wisely by the Gentiles, ought not immediately to despise what is said because we despise the author … but as the Apostle says, we should “prove all things and hold fast that which is good.”

— Origen, Homilies on Exodus XI.6. Jethro is thus the type of the Church’s freedom to receive truth from outside its own borders: Moses “hears not the one who speaks, but what he says.” Origen even makes the outsider’s good counsel a prophecy: Moses “accepted the counsel of a lower man … that he might … represent an image of the future mystery. For he knew that at some future time the Gentiles would offer good counsel with Moses, that they would bring a good and spiritual understanding to the Law of God.” This is the same principle Origen states more famously in his Letter to Gregory (Philocalia 13) under the figure of the spoiling of the Egyptians: the Christian should “take with you … those parts of the philosophy of the Greeks which are fit … to serve as general or preparatory studies for Christianity, and … so much of Geometry and Astronomy as may be helpful for the interpretation of the Holy Scriptures” — just as Israel “spoil[ed] the Egyptians” of their gold to furnish the sanctuary. (In that letter the motif is Exodus 12:35–36, not Jethro; but the reasoning is one, and Origen’s Jethro homily is where the same idea is keyed to the Midianite priest.)2

The second half of the same homily allegorizes Jethro’s counsel — the appointment of “rulers of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens” (Exodus 18:21) — as the graded courts of the Church. Moses, “occupied in the things of God,” keeps the teaching of the word; the “tribunes” and “centurions” and “rulers of fifty” settle “lesser judgments which pertain to each individual.” And the arrangement is not for this age only: “I think … this disposition has been given to the Church not only in the present age but is to be preserved also in the future age” — for “you also shall sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel.” So in Origen the one scene yields both readings the later Fathers divide between them: Jethro the Gentile teacher of the Church, and Jethro’s plan the charter of its delegated government.3

Gregory of Nyssa: the pagan priest’s daughter as “profane philosophy” (c. 390)

Gregory of Nyssa carries the “pagan learning” line further, though he attaches it not to Jethro’s counsel but to Jethro’s daughter — Zipporah, the foreign wife the priest of Midian gives Moses (Exodus 2:21). In the Life of Moses the marriage into the pagan household figures the Christian’s licit use of secular learning:

The foreign wife will follow him, for there are certain things derived from profane education which should not be rejected when we propose to give birth to virtue. Indeed moral and natural philosophy may become at certain times a comrade, friend, and companion of life to the higher way, provided that the offspring of this union introduce nothing of a foreign defilement.

— Gregory of Nyssa, Life of Moses II.37. But secular philosophy on its own Gregory judges sterile: it is figured by Pharaoh’s childless daughter, and “truly barren is profane philosophy, which is always in labor but never gives birth … [full of wind, its offspring] miscarry before they come to the light of the knowledge of God” (II.11). The same book states the “spoiling of Egypt” positively: the free soul should “equip themselves with the wealth of pagan learning … moral and natural philosophy, geometry, astronomy, dialectic, and whatever else is sought by those outside the Church, since these things will be useful when in time the divine sanctuary of mystery must be beautified with the riches of reason” (II.115) — and Gregory names his own brother as the exemplar: “Such a man was the great Basil, who acquired the Egyptian wealth … during his youth and dedicated this wealth to God for the adornment of the Church” (II.116). So on this figure Jethro’s house, not merely his advice, becomes the vehicle: the profane learning that must be married to the faith and made to serve it, or else remain barren.4

John Chrysostom: Moses humbler than his father-in-law (c. 395)

The Antiochene Chrysostom takes Jethro not as an allegory but as a lesson in humility, and holds Moses up against the pride of ordinary men who spurn good advice because they despise its giver:

For nothing was ever more humble than he; who, being leader of so great a people, and having overwhelmed in the sea the king and the host of all the Egyptians, as if they had been flies … yet felt exactly as if he had been an ordinary person, and as a son-in-law was humbler than his father-in-law, and took advice from him, and was not indignant, nor did he say, “What is this? After such and so great achievements, art thou come to us with thy counsel?” This is what most people feel; though a man bring the best advice, despising it, because of the lowliness of the person. But not so did he: rather through lowliness of mind he wrought all things well.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on 1 Corinthians I.4. It is the exact ethical counterpart to Origen’s exegetical point: the truth is not to be measured by “the lowliness of the person” who brings it — and Moses, hearing it from a Midianite, “wrought all things well.”5

Augustine: a wise plan is God’s, whoever devised it (c. 420)

Augustine puts the Jethro episode to programmatic use at the very opening of On Christian Doctrine. Answering those who claim that a divinely taught reader needs no human instruction, he lines up the cases in which God plainly does teach through a human means — Philip and the Ethiopian eunuch, and then Moses and Jethro:

Did not God talk with Moses, and yet he, with great wisdom and entire absence of jealous pride, accepted the plan of his father-in-law, a man of an alien race, for ruling and administering the affairs of the great nation entrusted to him? For Moses knew that a wise plan, in whatever mind it might originate, was to be ascribed not to the man who devised it, but to Him who is the Truth, the unchangeable God.

— Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, Preface 7. The principle — that a wise plan belongs to “the Truth, the unchangeable God,” in whatever mind it arose — is Augustine’s warrant both for learning from human teachers and, elsewhere, for the Christian use of pagan letters (his own “spoiling the Egyptians” passage on Moses “learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” stands later in the same work). Augustine also notes, in the City of God, that on Jethro’s plan Moses “took care to appoint teachers of reading and writing for God’s people before they had any written record of God’s law” — the “bringers-in of letters” of the Septuagint — folding the delegated officers of Exodus 18:21 into the story of how the people were made ready for the Law.6

Gregory the Great: delegate the “earthly strifes,” keep the “spiritual secrets” (c. 591)

The delegation line reaches its classic pastoral form in Gregory the Great’s Book of Pastoral Rule. For Gregory the reproof of Jethro is a warning to the overburdened pastor who lets administration crowd out contemplation:

Hence Moses, who speaks with God, is judged by the reproof of Jethro, who was of alien race, because with ill-advised labour he devotes himself to the people’s earthly affairs: and counsel too is presently given him, that he should appoint others in his stead for settling earthly strifes, and he himself should be more free to learn spiritual secrets for the instruction of the people.

— Gregory the Great, The Book of Pastoral Rule II.7. The one who “speaks with God” is corrected by “one of alien race” — the same scandal Origen and Augustine seized on — and the correction founds a rule of ministry: hand the temporal courts to deputies, and reserve the pastor for the word. Jethro, the Gentile priest, has become the church’s counsellor on the division of spiritual and temporal labour.7

Notes

  • Two lines, one insight. The “right use of pagan learning” reading (Origen’s Jethro homily and Letter to Gregory; Gregory of Nyssa’s foreign wife) and the “delegation of the judges” reading (Origen’s rulers-of-thousands; Augustine, Chrysostom, Gregory the Great) are not rivals but two applications of the single scandal the Fathers all feel — that a Gentile outsider instructs the mediator of the Law. Both answer it the same way: the truth, and the good order, that God gives through Jethro are God’s, and the people of God may receive them.
  • Where the allegory actually lands in Gregory of Nyssa. Gregory does not allegorize Jethro’s counsel; his “profane philosophy” figure runs through the pagan priest’s daughter (Moses’ foreign wife) and through Pharaoh’s barren daughter and the spoiling of Egypt. It belongs on this page because the foreign wife is Jethro’s — the marriage into the Midianite priest’s house is the vehicle — but the counsel-as-delegation move is Origen’s and the Latins’, not Gregory’s.
  • Jethro and the “spoiling of the Egyptians.” The two motifs are cousins in patristic exegesis: the Israelites’ plundering of Egyptian gold (Exodus 12:35–36) and Moses’ reception of a Midianite’s wisdom both figure the Church’s licit appropriation of what lies outside it. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa deploy both; Augustine has the Egyptian-gold version in On Christian Doctrine II. Jethro is the personal, and the “spoiling” the impersonal, form of one doctrine of Christian learning.
  • Text and editions. Origen’s Homilies on Exodus are quoted from Ronald E. Heine’s translation (Fathers of the Church 71); the Letter to Gregory from the Ante-Nicene Fathers (= Philocalia 13). Gregory of Nyssa’s Life of Moses is the Malherbe & Ferguson translation (Classics of Western Spirituality, 1978). Chrysostom, Augustine, and Gregory the Great are quoted from the standard Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations (Chrysostom on 1 Corinthians; Augustine, On Christian Doctrine and City of God; Gregory, Pastoral Rule).
