The Conquest of Canaan as Joshua Tells It

Presupposition: this wiki holds that Israel took Canaan as Joshua and Judges tell it, as the Church has read it from the New Testament on (see the conquest of Canaan in Christian tradition). Its date stays open. This page works inside one dating model, the Bible's own chronology (the Exodus c. 1446 BC, the crossing of the Jordan c. 1406 BC), and marks how the record fits that model; the rival model, a conquest c. 1250 BC, is weighed at the late-date argument.

The companion page, The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record, sets out the inscriptions and burn layers and asks what each one shows of the conquest and which dating of it each one fits. This page takes one of those datings, the Bible's own, and follows it through the record item by item: where the evidence fits it, what it costs, and what it leaves unexplained.1

The model fixes a single date. Read at face value, the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1 and Jephthah's 300 years in Judges 11:26 put the Exodus c. 1446 BC and the entry into Canaan c. 1406 BC.2 (Many who hold the conquest read those numbers schematically and date it c. 1250 BC instead; that is the rival model, and the late-date argument weighs it.)3 With the date fixed, sites that carry two candidate dates on the companion page collapse to one, and the evidence falls into place or does not.

The record can press on this model in three ways, and the marks keep them apart. A claimed trace may simply not be what it is claimed to be; that is weighed on its own argument page. A piece of evidence may cost this dating and not the other; that is marked here, scoped to this model. And a piece of evidence may press on every dating at once; that is an open puzzle for the presupposition, noted here and taken up on the companion page with the resolutions proposed for it. Each item below is marked: ✓ fits (the model expects it); ○ neutral (consistent, but it would fit the other dating too); △ costs (it fits only if a named dating result, site identification, or reading gives way); or ✗ unexplained (a residue this model cannot absorb). The events run in the model's own order, c. 1446 to c. 1200 BC.4

The timeline, on the early-date model

In the wilderness: the Shasu of Yhw (c. 1446–1406 BC)

○ neutral. On this model, Israel spends forty years in the Sinai, the Negev and the Edomite and Moabite fringe. A temple list of Amenhotep III at Soleb, carved c. 1400–1390 BC, names "the land of the Shasu of Yhw" among the semi-nomads of exactly that region.5 So the model finds a group tied to the name Yahweh in the right zone at the right moment. The mark stays neutral because the list names a land of Shasu, not Israel; the Soleb argument keeps that step open.6

Jericho falls (c. 1406 BC)

△ costs a date; also an open puzzle at every dating. On this model, burnt "City IV" at Tell es-Sultan must be Joshua's Jericho, so it fell c. 1400 BC, not c. 1550. If that date holds, the site lines up closely with Joshua 6: a fortified city, its mudbrick wall fallen down the slope, burned, with storage jars still full of grain.7 Full jars would fit a spring siege just after harvest (Joshua 3:15; 5:10) and a short one, and leaving them untouched would fit the ban on taking spoil (Joshua 6:17–18, 24).8 But that match is conditional on the date, and the date is the cost, a large one. Radiocarbon dating of short-lived charred grain from the burn layer itself returned c. 1562 ± 38 BC, and Kenyon's pottery sequence agrees. The model needs both to be about 150 years too early, and it rests instead on Bryant Wood's minority reading of the pottery and scarabs.9 The Jericho-dating argument rates that redating contested, leaning against.10 The same radiocarbon date presses on the rival model too: if City IV fell c. 1550, it is too early for a conquest c. 1250 as well. So Jericho is also an open puzzle for the presupposition itself, taken up with its proposed resolutions on the companion page. One of them would let this model keep its date without paying for it: the current excavators report a Late Bronze town over City IV's ruins, with a mudbrick wall on the old rampart, and some early-date writers now take that town, not City IV, for Joshua's Jericho. That trades the radiocarbon cost for a destruction not yet dated, and gives up the match of City IV's burn with Joshua 6; the Jericho-dating argument weighs it as live but provisional.11

Ai (c. 1406 BC)

△ costs a site identification; also an open puzzle at every dating. The mound long called Ai, et-Tell, had no city at all in the Late Bronze Age, so the model cannot put Joshua 7–8 there.12 It must place Ai at nearby Khirbet el-Maqatir, a small fortress of Late Bronze I that was destroyed by fire. That site matches the text's small city with a north gate, and on this model's date its burn falls in the right century.13 The cost is that the identification is a minority view that also needs a harmonization for the Bethel and Ai of Genesis; the Ai argument rates it weak.14 Et-Tell is as empty c. 1250 as c. 1406, so the rival model inherits the same problem, and Ai is also an open puzzle for the presupposition, taken up on the companion page.15

The altar on Mount Ebal and the assembly at Shechem (c. 1406–1400 BC)

Shechem: ✓ fits. Mount Ebal: △ costs a reading. Joshua never reports a battle for Shechem, yet Israel holds the central hills and gathers the nation there (Joshua 8:30–35; 24). On this model, the Amarna Letters fall a generation or two later and show Shechem's king, Labʾayu, accused of "giving the land" to the ʿApiru rather than resisting them. That fits a central hill country that came over by agreement and not by siege, in the modest form the Shechem argument defends.16 Mount Ebal works the other way. Adam Zertal's ramp-approached altar of unhewn stones matches the altar law closely, but it was built in early Iron I and buried c. 1140 BC, two centuries after this model's date.17 So the model cannot call it Joshua's altar. It must read the one altar found on the mountain as a later cult place, and leave the altar of Joshua 8:30 unlocated. That is a cost this model pays and the rival does not: on the late date the same structure can be Joshua's altar, so here the evidence tells the two datings apart.18 The lead "curse tablet" sifted from Zertal's dump in 2019 would restore an early date if its reading held, but it comes from unstratified spoil and its reading is disputed, so nothing here rests on it.19

The southern and northern campaigns: only Hazor burned (c. 1400 BC)

✓ fits, twice. Joshua names exactly three cities that Israel burned: Jericho, Ai and Hazor. Of the northern cities it says "Israel burned none of them, save Hazor only."20 Hazor has a Late Bronze I destruction from about the fifteenth century as well as its great thirteenth-century fire. The model assigns the earlier burn to Joshua, as Douglas Petrovich argues, which leaves the later fire for the next era.21 The second fit is an absence. Because the text says the other cities were taken but not burned, the model expects no wave of burn layers across Canaan around 1400 BC. That is what the ground shows: the destructions W. F. Albright once read as one campaign (Bethel, Debir, Lachish) scatter across more than a century, and the model can assign them to later events.22 The destruction-layers argument rates the one-campaign cluster weak; on this model that failure is what the text predicts.23

A land not fully taken: Jerusalem and the Amarna Letters (c. 1360–1332 BC)

✓ fits on Jerusalem; ○ on the ʿApiru; ✗ on Egypt. On this model, the Amarna archive falls early in the period of the Judges. The Bible says Judah could not drive the Jebusites out of Jerusalem (Joshua 15:63; Judges 1:21), and the letters show Jerusalem still ruled by a Canaanite king, ʿAbdi-Ḥeba, writing to Pharaoh.24 The letters also describe city-kings losing towns to roving ʿApiru bands. The model can read Israel as part of that unrest, but ʿApiru was a social label for uprooted people across the Near East, not a name for Israel. So the mark stays neutral: the Habiru argument rates the strong equation weak.25 The residue is Egypt. The fifteenth and fourteenth centuries are when Egypt held Canaan most firmly, with Thutmose III and Amenhotep II campaigning and garrisoning there and the Amarna letters addressed to Pharaoh as overlord. Yet Joshua and Judges never mention Egyptian rule or troops in the land, and no Egyptian record mentions Israel's arrival.26 The best answers to that double silence are weighed below; they narrow it but do not remove it, so it stays this model's ✗. It belongs to this dating alone: the late date puts the entry after Egypt's grip had loosened and does not face it.27

Deborah and Barak against Jabin of Hazor (c. 1230 BC)

○ neutral. With Joshua's fire assigned to the earlier burn, the model reads Hazor's great thirteenth-century destruction as the end of the later war with Jabin "king of Canaan, that reigned in Hazor" (Judges 4–5). That fire was fierce, deliberate and human-caused, and the city's statues were beheaded.28 Judges says Israel "destroyed Jabin" but does not say the city was burned, so the assignment is open but not required by the text. The Hazor argument also leaves open a rival reading of this layer, an internal revolt, which fits it as well.29

"Israel" on the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC)

○ neutral. The model has Israel in Canaan for two centuries by Merneptah's fifth year, still a loose confederation of tribes and not a kingdom. The stele names Israel with the sign for a people, not a city or a land, which suits a tribal Israel.30 It is neutral because both dating models put Israel in Canaan by then; the stele is later than either date, and does not tell the early one from the late one.31

The highland villages (c. 1200 BC)

△ costs a reading. Around 1200 BC the central hill country goes from about 25 villages to over 300, with a recurring set of traits: almost no pig bones, the four-room house, collared-rim jars, and no temples.32 On the late date this boom is the settlement itself. On this model it comes two centuries after the entry, so the model must read the first generations as living mostly in tents and moving with their flocks, settling into villages only later. That leaves little mark in the ground, and it suits Judges' picture of a land held loosely and contested for generations.33 It is a real cost: the model has to explain a two-century period with little archaeological trace. The pig-free diet suits an Israelite food law. The pottery is harder, on any dating: the material-culture argument notes that it grows out of Canaanite forms rather than arriving with newcomers, and that is one of the open puzzles for the presupposition taken up on the companion page.34

Egypt's silence, steelmanned

The model faces two silences, and they need different answers. The Bible says nothing of Egyptian rule in Canaan, and Egypt's records say nothing of Israel's arrival. Early-date scholars have offered five answers that work inside this model.35

  1. Egypt held the lowlands; Israel took the hills. This is the main answer, pressed by Bryant Wood. Egypt ruled through treaties with local kings and kept few troops in the land, based in the lowlands at Gaza, Joppa, Megiddo and Beth-shean. The towns Judges says Israel could not take (Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Megiddo, Gezer) sit in or beside that zone, while Joshua's campaigns run through the hill country, where Egypt's hold was light: in the Amarna letters Labʾayu of Shechem acts largely on his own account.36 On this reading Israel and Egypt need not have met, and the Bible's silence is what the geography predicts.
  2. Egypt was weakened after the Exodus. Douglas Petrovich makes Amenhotep II the pharaoh of the Exodus. That king's last Asian campaign, in his ninth year, was a brief raid into southern Canaan that took 3,600 ʿApiru captives, which Petrovich reads as recaptured Hebrews; afterwards Egypt's great Asian campaigns largely stop and a peace with Mitanni follows. On this reading Egypt could not or would not intervene when Israel entered the land, and a pharaoh would not record the defeat that caused it.37 The pause in campaigning is real, but it has an ordinary explanation in the Mitanni peace, and the ʿApiru-as-Hebrews step is the equation the Habiru argument rates weak. So this answer is consistent with the evidence, not shown by it.
  3. Egypt is in the text under other names. John Garstang read the "hornet" that drives out the Canaanites (Exodus 23:28; Joshua 24:12) as a symbol of Pharaoh, so that Egypt's campaigns had softened the land before Israel came. Reference works report that this reading "is not generally accepted," and a literal reading is available.38 Robert D. Miller II proposes translating the opening of Judges 5:2 as a dating formula, "when Pharaohs ruled," which would make the Song of Deborah remember Egyptian rule. Some also read the name of Sisera, the general Jabin sends against Israel, as linked to Egypt. These are possible readings, not secure ones: the phrase in Judges 5:2 is rendered in several other ways, and Sisera's Egyptian link is offered only as a suggestion.39
  4. Egypt did name Israel early. Manfred Görg, Peter van der Veen and Christoffer Theis read a name on a granite pedestal in Berlin as "Israel," possibly from the eighteenth dynasty, two centuries before Merneptah. If sound, this would answer the second silence directly. But the piece has no known findspot, the spelling would be unique, and the reading is disputed, so the Berlin-pedestal argument rates it weak.40
  5. It is an argument from silence. Egyptian lists rarely name highland peoples, and the Bible leaves out much that other records show; Wood points to events in Assyrian records that Kings never mentions. James Hoffmeier, who presses the objection hardest, grants that the silence "by itself is not decisive."41 The limit on this answer is the Amarna archive itself, which describes highland politics in detail, naming the kings of Shechem and Jerusalem and the troublemakers around them, yet never names Israel or a leader like Joshua.42

The rival model. Kenneth Kitchen and Hoffmeier date the conquest c. 1230 BC instead, after Egypt's grip had loosened, so that Merneptah's campaign answers Israel's rise; Hoffmeier notes that on the early date Judges names every oppressor except Egypt across the centuries of Egypt's rule, which he calls "strange indeed." The late date is the presupposition's other dating model, and it answers this silence directly, at costs of its own that the late-date argument weighs: it must read the 480 years schematically, and it shares the Jericho and Ai puzzles. David Rohl instead shortens Egypt's own chronology, a scheme Wood himself calls "quite unworkable" from the Palestinian evidence.43 This page follows the Bible's own dates; the other options are open inside the presupposition.

Where this leaves the mark. The first silence has a coherent answer: if Israel held the hills and Egypt the plains, the Bible need not mention Egypt, and that half of the residue becomes ○ neutral. The second silence is narrowed but not removed. Answers 2, 4 and 5 soften it, yet the Amarna letters cover the highlands in the very decades this model has Israel there and do not name it. Reading Israel among the letters' ʿApiru is the only way to place it in them, and that equation is weak. So the ✗ stands for this model, now confined to Egypt's silence about Israel.44

What the model settles

Holding the conquest on the Bible's own dates turns several open disputes into single answers.

  • Jericho: City IV fell c. 1400 BC; Kenyon's c. 1550 and the radiocarbon date must both give way.45
  • Ai: Khirbet el-Maqatir, not et-Tell.46
  • Hazor: two fires, two events: the Late Bronze I burn is Joshua's, the thirteenth-century burn belongs to the Judges period.47
  • The burn layers: only three cities were burned, so no conquest-wide destruction horizon is expected, and none is found.48
  • The Amarna Letters: a snapshot of the early Judges period, with Jerusalem still in Canaanite hands.49
  • Mount Ebal: Zertal's structure is a later cult place, not Joshua's altar, which is unlocated.50

The bill

On this model, this is what the record costs.

  • △ Jericho's radiocarbon date (the largest cost): a high-precision date on short-lived grain from the destruction layer, backed by Kenyon's pottery sequence, must be about 150 years too early (or, if Joshua's Jericho is the Late Bronze town above City IV, a destruction of that town not yet dated).51
  • △ Ai's location: a minority site identification plus a harmonization of the Genesis toponyms.52
  • △ The highland gap: about two centuries of Israelite presence with little settlement trace.53
  • △ Mount Ebal: the one altar found on the mountain must be a later cult place and Joshua's altar unlocated, where the late date can take the same structure as his.54
  • ✗ Egypt's silence about Israel: the model places the conquest at the height of Egyptian rule over Canaan. The Bible's silence about Egypt has a coherent answer (Israel in the hills, Egypt in the plains), but the Amarna letters, which cover the highlands in detail, never name Israel. The steelman above narrows this residue without removing it.55

The four △ costs rest on three independent mechanisms: a radiocarbon result (Jericho), a site identification (Ai), and the two-century gap between this model's entry and the Iron I horizon where Israel's villages and the Ebal altar appear, which carries both the highland gap and Ebal. Granting one does not clear the others. Against them, the model's fits are specific: Hazor as the one burned northern city, the absence of a conquest-wide burn horizon that the text itself predicts, a battle-free Shechem, and a still-Canaanite Jerusalem in the Amarna Letters. The profile of Jericho's destruction is not counted among them: it matches Joshua 6 only once the △ above is paid.56

Two of these costs are not this model's alone. Jericho's date and Ai's empty mound press on the late date as well, so they are open puzzles for the presupposition that every dating has to carry. The late date sheds the Egypt ✗, the Ebal △ and the highland gap, at the costs weighed on its own page.57
