Astrology and the Stars

From the second century to the seventh, most of the Christian writers on this timeline argue against astrology — in their usage chiefly genethlialogy, the casting of a birth-horoscope to read a person's character and destiny from the position of the heavens. The grounds they give are largely theological and moral. Astral determinism, they hold, destroys free will, empties reward and punishment of meaning, makes the Creator the author of every sin, and usurps the providence of God; several of them (Tatian, Tertullian, Lactantius) add that the art was not discovered but taught, by the fallen angels. Recurring topics run through the entries: the argument from twins; the Magi's star; and the line several writers draw between the casting of nativities and the study of the courses of the heavens, which Clement praises as lifting the mind to its Maker and Isidore names astronomy. Other Christian-era witnesses took a different line toward the art — the astrologer Firmicus Maternus, Zeno of Verona's baptismal reuse of the zodiac, the Valentinian teaching that the baptized pass out from under Fate, and the astral determinism that Leo the Great and the First Council of Braga condemned and attributed to the Priscillianists. How far these amount to a Christian accommodation of astrology is a disputed reading. The Fathers treat astrology as a species of divination, and the same triage of a portent's source — from God, from a demon, or from nature — runs through the sister question of the discernment of dreams (Aquinas ranks divining by the stars and divining by dreams side by side in his one treatise on divination, licensing each only where it traces to God or a natural cause, not the demons). What follows are the witnesses, earliest first.

Related doctrines: Astrology and the heavens — see also The Pillars of Seth (the rival, pious aetiology of astronomy — the same celestial science credited to the righteous line of Seth rather than the fallen angels) · The Living Heavens (are the sun, moon, and stars themselves alive — ensouled rational beings, or inanimate bodies moved by angels?).

Timeline

Listed earliest first. Most of the witnesses reject astral fatalism, on different grounds — demonological, logical, empirical, moral, and natural-philosophical; Clement, Origen and Isidore set apart a study of the stars that they approve; Firmicus Maternus defends the art itself.

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

Ignatius describes the Nativity star as the sign at whose appearing magic was destroyed. On his way to martyrdom he tells the Ephesians that when Christ was manifested "a star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars," so novel that "every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared; ignorance was removed, and the old kingdom abolished." The letter does not mention the Magi; taking this star as theirs is a reading of it alongside Matthew 2.1

Tatian (c. 165)

Tatian traces Fate itself to the fallen angels. The demons, he says, "having shown [men] a plan of the position of the stars, like dice-players… introduced Fate, a flagrant injustice"; the zodiac is their fabrication, "the delineation of the zodiacal circle is the work of gods." The Christian, for Tatian, is free of that slavery: "we are superior to Fate, and instead of wandering demons, we have learned to know one Lord who wanders not; and, as we do not follow the guidance of Fate, we reject its lawgivers." The tradition that the same rebel angels taught the forbidden arts links this doctrine to the sons of God and the Nephilim.2

Bardaisan of Edessa (c. 200)

The earliest Syriac witness on this timeline argues from the diversity of human law. In The Book of the Laws of Countries, Bardaisan (through his pupil Philip) argues that whole nations living under the same stars keep utterly incompatible customs, and that Christians everywhere keep one law regardless of the heavens: "wherever we are, we are all called after the one name of Christ… nor does the Fate of the celestial Governors compel them." He states the rule generally: "the laws of men prevail over the decree of the stars." (Bardaisan still allowed the stars a hand in outward fortune; his firm denial is that they compel the will.)3

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement distinguishes the study of the heavens from the casting of nativities. Astronomy, for Clement, is a genuine good that raises the mind toward God: by it a man "is elevated along with heaven… studying ever divine things… from which Abraham… ascended to the knowledge of Him who created them." He reports the Chaldean doctrine of planetary influence as theirs and does not adopt the casting of nativities.4

Tertullian (c. 203)

Tertullian classes astrology as a species of condemned magic, and its origin with the rebel angels: "those angels, the deserters from God, the lovers of women, were likewise the discoverers of this curious art." He grants that the Magi's art was real, but time-bound: "that science has been allowed until the Gospel, in order that after Christ's birth no one should thenceforward interpret any one's nativity by the heaven" — and the Magi, warned to return "another way," figure the astrologer forsaking his craft. He concludes: "You know nothing, astrologer, if you know not that you should be a Christian." (On the deserter-angels as the teachers of the art, cf. the Nephilim.)5

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 225)

Hippolytus devotes the opening of his Refutation of All Heresies to laying bare the Chaldeans' technique — the casting of the horoscope, the arithmetic of the zodiac — in order to discredit it, and to show that the teachers he opposes had borrowed the astrologers' machinery. The art, he says, is "calculated both to deceive and blind the soul indulging in vain expectations"; and he names a single point of failure: "if the horoscope be removed… the entire system of the Chaldeans vanishes along with (them)."6

Origen (c. 231)

Origen builds his position on the proof-text cited for astrology — "let them be for signs" (Genesis 1:14). The stars, he argues, signify but do not cause: they are a book whose signs the angelic "Powers" (and, exceptionally, inspired men like the Magi) can read, while ordinary men, "deceived by certain observations… supposed that they from whom they thought they received the signs were the original causes of the events which the Word says the signs indicate." On this account foreknowledge is kept without astral necessity, and free will is untouched.7

Methodius of Olympus (c. 290)

In the eighth discourse of the Symposium, Thecla presses a historical argument: the constellations are recent human inventions, so no one was ever really "born under a sign," and "our ancestors lived without destiny." She draws the moral conclusion, holding "that it is now possible even for a child to… perceive their error: that to do good or evil is in our own power, and not decided by the stars."8

Lactantius (c. 310)

Lactantius gives the demonological aetiology at length in Latin. In Book II of the Divine Institutes Lactantius sets out the Genesis 6 story in his own two-tier form — God sends angels to guard mankind, they are "polluted… by intercourse with women" and fall to the earth, and from them spring a second, earth-bound kind of demon — and then names those spirits, under the chapter heading "That Astrology, Soothsaying, and Similar Arts are the Invention of Demons," as its source: "These were the inventors of astrology, and soothsaying, and divination… and the art of magic." For Lactantius the art is no human discovery but a demonic fraud floated "to turn away the minds of men from the worship of the true God." He carries the liberation half of the motif too: quoting Hermes, the man who has known God "is not even bound by fate," "for over a pious man neither evil demon nor fate has any power." (On the deserter-angels as the teachers of the art, cf. the Nephilim.)9

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 315)

Eusebius gives the whole sixth book of his Preparation for the Gospel to arguing against astral fate. Arguing in his own voice, he presses the moral reductio: a determining Fate destroys free will and with it philosophy, religion, and law — "there will be an end of your philosophy, an end also of religion… if universal causation has been usurped by necessity and fate" — and to make men "lifeless puppets pulled by strings" contradicts the freedom each man feels in his own choosing. He then cites the pagans' own oracles, quoted from Porphyry, as saying they foretell "not from any divine power… but from observation of the stars… just as men do," and enlists the philosophers and Bardaisan — whose diversity-of-customs argument survives in Greek only through this book, making Eusebius both a witness in his own right and the channel that carries the earlier Bardaisan witness above.10

Firmicus Maternus (c. 336)

The one witness on this timeline who defends the art itself. Firmicus, a Sicilian senator, wrote an eight-book Latin handbook of astrology, the Mathesis, defending genethlialogy as a sacred science; then, a decade or so later and now a Christian, he wrote De errore profanarum religionum urging the emperors to abolish paganism. In the Mathesis astral fate is kept, but folded under "the Highest God," the emperor alone is exempted from the stars' decree ("the Emperor alone is not subject to the course of the stars… his destiny is governed by the judgment of the Highest God"), and the astrologer is charged to be pure, honest, and above bribery. Whether he was already a Christian when he wrote it is debated (see his detail page), and whether he belongs to a Christian accommodation of astrology is a disputed reading.11

Basil of Caesarea (c. 378)

The sixth homily of Basil's Hexaemeron is his treatment of astrology. He presses three lines at once: the practical — the birth-instant can never actually be fixed, so both the astrologers and those who gape at them "are supremely ridiculous"; the ridiculous — deriving a man's character from the beast a constellation is merely named after ("He who is born under the Balance will be just… Is not this the height of folly?"); and the moral — that astral fate makes law, judgment, "reward for justice… punishment for sin" all pointless, and, since God made even the "malignant" stars, "the Creator [becomes] the author of evil." Ambrose follows Basil's lines in his own Hexameron (below).12

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380)

Gregory's dialogue Against Fate argues against a pagan astrologer on the same free-will ground, adding a fresh empirical argument: whole cities of people, born at every different hour, perish together "in a single instant" when a Bithynian city falls to earthquake and fire — a common fate that no shared horoscope can explain. He finds the animal-qualities of the signs incoherent, and concludes that human action "seems determined by each person's free will."13

Ambrose of Milan (c. 387)

Ambrose carries Basil's dossier into the Latin West, in the sixth sermon of his own Hexameron (on the fourth day). He first argues from the order of creation: the luminaries were made only "on the fourth day," after the earth had already greened, so "the beetle is older than the sun, the grass is older than the moon" — the life of the world cannot hang on lights kindled after it. Then he presses Basil's three lines. The practical — the birth-instant can never be caught, for while the midwife works "the Chaldean listens, sets the horoscope," "points pass, time flies irreparably," and so "the whole task is full of vanity." The ridiculous — to call a man born under Aries generous "because the ram willingly sheds its fleece" is to read a human character off "the qualities of cheap food." And the reductio — were birth star-fixed, "kings would be born daily" and no throne could pass to a son. The Chaldeans' art is a spider's web in which "the weak get entangled… while those with stronger senses" break through.14

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Preaching on the Magi, Chrysostom takes up the objection that the Bethlehem star vindicates astrology: it cannot, he argues, because it was no ordinary star at all. It travelled "from north to south," shone while the sun was up, and "when they were to move, it moved; when to stand, it stood" — conduct "not… of a star, but of some power highly endued with reason." Christ came, he says, to "put down astrology, and take away fate, and… overthrow all such sorcery."15

Augustine of Hippo (c. 400–417)

Augustine, who had consulted the mathematici in his youth, later wrote against them at length. Across the Confessions, On Christian Doctrine, and City of God he assembles his case: the horoscope-casters "sell inexperienced men into a miserable bondage" and traffic in "a baleful fellowship between men and devils"; twins, a free man's son and a slave born "at the same moment," and Esau and Jacob are his evidence that identical charts yield opposite lives; the astrologers' own potter's-wheel escape he turns against them; and astral fatalism, he insists, shifts the blame for every sin onto "the Creator and Ordainer of heaven and stars."16

Gregory the Great (c. 591)

Against the Priscillianists, who, he reports, cited the Bethlehem star to prove "that every man is born under the influence of a star," Gregory answers from the Gospel's own word order: the star "went before them till it came and stood over where the Child was" — so it was "not the Child who followed the star, but the star that followed the Child." Man, he adds, was made before and above the luminaries: "the stars [were made] for man," not man for the stars. And Jacob, born gripping Esau's heel yet utterly unlike him in life, is his counter-example to any birth-fixed fate.17

Isidore of Seville (c. 620)

In the Etymologies the encyclopedist separates astronomy (the courses and motions of the heavens) and even the "natural" part of astrology (the seasons, the reckoning of sun and moon) from its "superstitious" part — "that which the mathematici follow, who prophesy by the stars… and endeavor to predict the nativities and characters of men from the course of the stars." In his scheme astronomy and the natural part are kept, and the nativity-casting is condemned.18

Disputed readings

How far the early Christian response to astrology extended among Christians.

  • Did some early Christians accommodate astrology? — turns on Firmicus Maternus, Zeno of Verona, the Excerpta ex Theodoto, and the condemnations of the Priscillianists.19
