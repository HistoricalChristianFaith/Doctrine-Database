Original sin & the Fall

Original sin is the doctrine of what Adam’s first transgression did to his descendants: whether, and how, the fault of the garden reaches a newborn who has done nothing. This timeline records the extant witnesses, earliest first: Irenaeus on the race that “offended in the first Adam” (c. 180); Tertullian’s account of the soul propagated from Adam (c. 210); Origen and Cyprian on the “filth” and the “contagion” of birth; Ambrosiaster’s Latin “in whom all sinned” (c. 375) and Ambrose; John Chrysostom’s reading of the same verse (c. 391); Pelagius and Caelestius, who denied any transmission (Caelestius’s theses were condemned at Carthage in 411); Augustine’s anti-Pelagian writings (c. 412–430); the councils of Carthage (418) and Orange (529); John of Damascus (c. 730); the Council of Trent (1546); and John Calvin’s 1559 Institutes. The related questions of grace, justification, and the baptism of infants are traced on the predestination, justification, and infant-baptism timelines.

The communions and their theologians describe what is inherited from Adam. The Lutheran Augsburg Confession (1530) teaches that all “are born with sin, that is, without the fear of God, without trust in God, and with concupiscence; and that this disease, or vice of origin, is truly sin, even now condemning and bringing eternal death upon those not born again through Baptism and the Holy Ghost.”1 The Reformed Westminster Confession (1646) holds that, Adam and Eve “being the root of all mankind, the guilt of this sin was imputed; and the same death in sin, and corrupted nature, conveyed to all their posterity,”2 and of the corruption of nature that remains in the regenerate that “both itself, and all the motions thereof, are truly and properly sin.”3 The Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (1974) writes that in the Greek patristic understanding of man “there would be no place … for the concept of inherited guilt, or for a ‘sin of nature,’” although “human nature incurs the consequences of Adam’s sin.”4 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) teaches that original sin is “transmitted by propagation to all mankind,” that it is called “sin” “only in an analogical sense: it is a sin ‘contracted’ and not ‘committed’,” that it “does not have the character of a personal fault in any of Adam’s descendants,” and that human nature “has not been totally corrupted: it is wounded.”5

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons — “offended in the first Adam” (c. 180)

Irenaeus writes of the race as bound up in its head. In his scheme of recapitulation, what Adam did, humanity did, and what the “second Adam” undoes, He undoes for the race. God, he writes, is the one “whom indeed we had offended in the first Adam, when he did not perform His commandment. In the second Adam, however, we are reconciled, being made obedient even unto death.” The offense is ours in Adam; the reconciliation is ours in Christ. The passage does not describe how Adam’s sin reaches his descendants.6

Tertullian — the vitium originis passed with the soul (c. 210)

Tertullian holds that each soul is propagated from the parents’ souls as the body from their bodies (traducianism, from tradux, the vine-shoot), so that the whole race descends from Adam as “the nascent sprout of a tree… derived from Adam as its root” — and the soul carries a fault with it. There is, he says, “an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin”: “the corruption of our nature is another nature.” Hence “every soul, then, by reason of its birth, has its nature in Adam until it is born again in Christ… and because unclean, it is actively sinful.” This is the vitium originis, a fault of origin, which Tertullian distinguishes from “the evil which supervenes on the soul from the intervention of the evil spirit.” In On Baptism he counselled delaying the baptism of little children.7 See the detail page: Tertullian on original sin.

Origen — the “filth” of birth and infant baptism from the apostles (c. 248)

Origen, in the Greek East, reasons from the practice of the church to the newborn’s condition. Pairing Psalm 51:5 with Job 14:4 in its Septuagint form (“no one is clean from filth, not even if his life be of one day”), he infers that “whatever soul is born in the flesh is polluted with the filth of iniquity and sin”; and he takes the church’s baptizing of infants “for the remission of sins” as an apostolic tradition that would be “superfluous” unless there were something in the child to remit. On Romans 5, he reasons from solidarity as Hebrews reasons of Levi in Abraham’s loins: “all men who are born and have been born in this world” were “in the loins of Adam while he was still in paradise,” and so death “passed also into those who were held in his loins.” His Latin translator Rufinus renders Paul’s clause “in whom all sinned.”8 See the detail page: Origen on original sin.

Cyprian and the Council of Carthage — the “contagion of the ancient death” (253)

Cyprian of Carthage, answering bishop Fidus for a council of sixty-six African bishops, writes that the infant, though it “has not sinned” by any act of its own, has “being born after the flesh according to Adam… contracted the contagion of the ancient death at its earliest birth”; in baptism “to him are remitted, not his own sins, but the sins of another.” The question before the council was practical — whether a newborn must wait for the eighth day to be baptized — and the letter answers that it need not, giving the contracted contagion among its reasons.9 See the detail page: Cyprian of Carthage (on the infant-baptism timeline).

Ambrosiaster — “in Adam all sinned as though in the lump”: the Latin in quo (c. 375)

The anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster reads Romans 5:12 in a Latin text that renders Paul’s eph’ hō (ἐφ’ ὅ) as in quo — “in whom all sinned” — takes the “whom” to be Adam, and concludes: “in Adam all sinned as though in the lump; for he himself, having been corrupted by sin, all whom he begot were born under sin. From him, then, all are sinners, because from him himself we all derive.” (The Latin for “as though in the lump” is quasi in massa.)10 How the Greek phrase should be construed is disputed. Chrysostom’s reading is given in his entry below. Charles Hodge (Reformed, Princeton, 1864) takes eph’ hō as “because” (“All men die, because all sinned”) and reads “all sinned” through verses 18–19: “They sinned in Adam. His sin was regarded as theirs”; “the only possible way in which all men can be said to have sinned in Adam, is putatively.”11 Stéphane Harent (Catholic, 1911) writes that several Latin Fathers understood “in whom all have sinned” to mean all have sinned in Adam, an interpretation that “would be an extra proof of the thesis of original sin, but it is not necessary”; that “Modern exegesis, as well as the Greek Fathers, prefer to translate ‘and so death passed upon all men because all have sinned’”; and, accepting that translation, he reads the clause through verse 19 (“by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners”) as speaking of “one first sin which was enough to transmit equally to all men a state of sin and the title of sinners.”12 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) writes that the Latin in quo omnes peccaverunt “was used in the West to justify the doctrine of guilt inherited from Adam” but that “such a meaning cannot be drawn from the original Greek,” since eph’ hō “can be translated as ‘because,’ a meaning accepted by most modern scholars of all confessional backgrounds.”13 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the verse as “because all men sinned,” beside verse 19, and grounds the transmission on the “unity of the human race,” the whole race being in Adam “as one body of one man.”14 See the detail page: Ambrosiaster on original sin.

Ambrose of Milan — “In Adam I fell… guilty as I was in him” (c. 387)

Ambrose states the solidarity in the first person: “In Adam I fell, in Adam I was cast out of Paradise, in Adam I died; how shall the Lord call me back, except He find me in Adam; guilty as I was in him, so now justified in Christ.” On Luke he glosses Romans 5:12 with the same in quo: “All indeed died in Adam… and so it passed through to all men, in whom all sinned.” He speaks of guilt contracted in Adam as well as of death.15

John Chrysostom — all “become mortal” from Adam (c. 391)

Preaching at Antioch, John Chrysostom reads the same verse differently. Paul’s clause, he says, means that Adam “having once fallen, even they that had not eaten of the tree did from him, all of them, become mortal.” The consequence Chrysostom names is that all “become mortal.” Theodoret of Cyrus writes that each “receives the sentence of death not because of the sin of his first ancestor but because of his own sin.”16 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) describes “a consensus in Greek patristic and Byzantine traditions in identifying the inheritance of the Fall as an inheritance essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness,” and quotes Theodoret on the baptism of infants: “If the only meaning of baptism is the remission of sins, why would we baptize the newborn children who have not yet tasted of sin?”17

Pelagius — sin by imitation, no inherited sin (c. 411)

Pelagius and his follower Caelestius deny that Adam’s sin is transmitted. On their account Adam harmed his posterity by setting a bad example: “as long as people sin as Adam sinned they die,” and a newborn, having done nothing, bears no sin. Caelestius’s theses (that Adam’s sin “injured only himself, and not the human race,” and that infants “at their birth are in the same state that Adam was before the transgression”) were condemned at Carthage in 411.18 See the detail page: Pelagius (on the predestination timeline).

Augustine of Hippo — inherited guilt, concupiscence, and the “mass of perdition” (c. 412–430)

Augustine of Hippo wrote against the Pelagian positions from 412 until his death in 430. He holds that all were “bound by the chain of death in that one man in whom all men sinned, even though they added no sins of their own”; that the transmission is by propagation, not imitation, carried through the disordered concupiscence of generation (“What, then, is generated must be regenerated… what has been contracted may be remitted”); and that the whole race is one massa perditionis, a “mass of perdition” justly condemned, from which the elect are drawn as “vessels of mercy.” He argues from the church’s practice: since the church everywhere baptizes infants “for the remission of sins” and they have no actual sins, “it is the guilt of original sin which is healed in them.” He held that an infant dying unbaptized is condemned, though to a “mildest” punishment.19 In Against Two Letters of the Pelagians he reads Romans 5:12 as “wherein all have sinned,” cites for that reading the commentary now called Ambrosiaster under the name of “the sainted Hilary,” and brings forward Cyprian and Ambrose as witnesses on original sin.20 Against Julian of Eclanum he names Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Innocent, John, Basil and Jerome as bishops and a presbyter whose judgement on original sin he sets against Julian; Harent (1911) describes this as invoking “the testimony of eleven Fathers, Greek as well as Latin.”21 The International Theological Commission (2007) reports that later medieval authors, “from Peter Abelard on,” interpreted Augustine’s “mildest punishment” as the privation of the vision of God, a teaching it describes as having “modified the strict opinion of St. Augustine.”22 See the detail page: Augustine of Hippo on original sin.

The Council of Carthage condemns Pelagianism (418)

The plenary Council of Carthage of 1 May 418 (its canons confirmed by Pope Zosimus) anathematized any who says that Adam was created mortal, so that “whether he had sinned or not, he would have died in body”; and any who denies that infants should be baptized, or says that they “derive from Adam no original sin, which needs to be removed by the laver of regeneration,” from which it would follow that in them “the form of baptism for the remission of sins, is to be understood as false and not true.”23

The Second Council of Orange — the whole man “changed for the worse” (529)

The Second Council of Orange, presided over by Caesarius of Arles and confirmed by Pope Boniface II, teaches that it is “the whole man, that is, both body and soul, that was ‘changed for the worse’ through the offense of Adam’s sin,” and anathematizes any who declares “that it is only the death of the body which is the punishment for sin, and not also that sin, which is the death of the soul, passed through one man to the whole human race.” Its canons on grace and the will are traced on the predestination timeline.24

John of Damascus — “clothed about with death”; man “endowed with free-will” (c. 730)

John of Damascus, gathering the Greek fathers in his Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, writes that Adam’s transgression left the race “clothed about with death, that is, mortality and the grossness of flesh… condemned to death, and made subject to corruption”. In Book 2 he describes man as “a creature endowed with free-will” and, being rational, “master of his acts”, who “has the power to curb his appetite or to indulge it as he pleases”; in Book 4 he writes that “the law of sin, settling in the members of our flesh, makes its assault upon us through it,” and “takes me captive (even though I make the law of God my will and set my love on it, and make not sin my will).”25 See the detail page: John of Damascus on original sin.

The Council of Trent — the Decree Concerning Original Sin (Session V, 1546)

The Council of Trent, in its fifth session, decreed that Adam “immediately lost the holiness and justice wherein he had been constituted,” and anathematized any who says that he “has only transfused death, and pains of the body, into the whole human race, but not sin also, which is the death of the soul” — citing Romans 5:12 as “in whom all have sinned.” This sin, “transfused into all by propagation, not by imitation, is in each one as his own.” The guilt of original sin is remitted by the grace of baptism, not “only rased, or not imputed.” In the baptized “there remains concupiscence, or an incentive (to sin); which, whereas it is left for our exercise, cannot injure those who consent not”; the council declares that the Catholic Church “has never understood it to be called sin, as being truly and properly sin in those born again, but because it is of sin, and inclines to sin.”26

John Calvin — “a hereditary corruption and depravity of our nature” (1559)

John Calvin, in the 1559 edition of his Institutes, defines the thing directly: “Original sin, then, may be defined a hereditary corruption and depravity of our nature, extending to all the parts of the soul, which first makes us obnoxious to the wrath of God, and then produces in us works which in Scripture are termed works of the flesh.” Being “perverted and corrupted in all the parts of our nature, we are, merely on account of such corruption, deservedly condemned by God”; and he adds, “This is not liability for another’s fault.” On his account the corruption is itself culpable before God, not merely the soil of later sins; the Reformation doctrine of justification is traced on its own timeline.27
