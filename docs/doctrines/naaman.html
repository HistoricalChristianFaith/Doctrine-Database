Who Was Naaman the Syrian?

Naaman is the great Gentile convert of the Hebrew Bible — a foreign general healed by Israel’s God and sent home a worshipper of the LORD. Scripture introduces him in a single chapter, but a full one: “Naaman, captain of the host of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper” (2 Kings 5:1). On the word of a captive Israelite servant-girl he goes to Elisha, is told to wash seven times in the Jordan, rages at so mean a cure, is talked round by his own servants, and comes up from the river with “his flesh… like unto the flesh of a little child” (2 Kings 5:14). He returns to confess “there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel,” begs “two mules’ burden of earth” to worship the LORD on Israelite soil, and asks pardon in advance for the one compromise his office forces on him — that he must still bow, at his king’s side, “in the house of Rimmon.” The chapter closes on the dark foil to his faith: Elisha’s servant Gehazi, who chases the Syrian for the reward the prophet refused and inherits Naaman’s leprosy for his greed.

Few Old Testament episodes were put to more Christian and Jewish use. Jesus makes Naaman the very type of the outsider who receives what Israel would not — “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” (Luke 4:27) — a word that nearly got him killed at Nazareth. The Fathers read the sevenfold washing in the Jordan as the standing type of Christian baptism; the moral theologians worried the “house of Rimmon” into the classic test-case of outward conformity against inward faith; and the rabbis debated whether Naaman became a full convert or only a ger toshav, a resident who feared the God of Israel without taking on the whole Law. Was his confession the faith of a true convert or the henotheism of a man who simply added a greater God to his world? Does the Jordan cure prefigure baptism, or is that a reading laid on it later? And what is a believer to do, cornered like Naaman between an inward loyalty and an outward act?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Naaman but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the narrative of 2 Kings and its echo on the lips of Jesus, the Fathers who made the Jordan-washing a figure of baptism (Irenaeus, Ambrose, Cyril of Jerusalem, Chrysostom, Augustine), the moral casuistry of the “house of Rimmon,” and the Jewish traditions that weighed his conversion. The genuinely contested questions — was he a full convert or a henotheist? does the Jordan cure prefigure baptism? may a believer bow outwardly while worshipping inwardly? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. Naaman is a prime instance of a recurring biblical type, the Gentile who meets Israel’s God, and belongs with the other outsiders on this shelf who cross that threshold.

The biblical portrait (2 Kings 5, with Luke 4:27)

The whole later tradition grows from a single chapter and one saying of Jesus that turns on it. 2 Kings 5 presents Naaman at the height of worldly honour and the depth of affliction at once — “captain of the host of the king of Syria… a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper.” The cure comes through the humblest link in the chain: “a little maid” carried captive out of Israel, waiting on Naaman’s wife, who wishes her master were “with the prophet that is in Samaria.” Sent at last to Elisha, Naaman is met not by the prophet but by a messenger with a bare instruction — “Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean” — and goes off “in a rage,” affronted that no spectacle attended it and scorning the Jordan beside “Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus.” His own servants reason him back (“if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it?”), and the obedience he grudged carries the miracle: “then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God: and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”1

What follows is why the episode mattered so much to the later tradition. The healed general returns to Elisha and makes the great confession of the Gentile convert — “Behold, now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel” — and, refused any reward, begs instead “two mules’ burden of earth,” that on Israelite soil he may “offer neither burnt offering nor sacrifice unto other gods, but unto the LORD.” Then he lays before the prophet the one reservation his office presses on him: that when his master “goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon… the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing” — and Elisha answers only, “Go in peace.” The chapter ends on its dark counter-figure: Gehazi, Elisha’s servant, who runs after Naaman for the silver and raiment the prophet had declined, and hears the sentence “the leprosy therefore of Naaman shall cleave unto thee, and unto thy seed for ever” — going out “a leper as white as snow.” Centuries later Jesus, preaching at Nazareth, makes Naaman the standing type of the outsider who receives what Israel refuses: “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” — a word so provoking that his hearers “rose up, and thrust him out of the city.” The Gentile leper, the sevenfold washing, and the confession of the one true God are the seeds of everything the tradition would make of him.2
