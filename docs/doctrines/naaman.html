Who Was Naaman the Syrian?

Naaman is the Syrian general of the Hebrew Bible healed by Israel’s God and sent home a worshipper of the LORD. Scripture introduces him in a single chapter: “Naaman, captain of the host of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper” (2 Kings 5:1). On the word of a captive Israelite servant-girl he goes to Elisha, is told to wash seven times in the Jordan, goes off in a rage at the instruction, is persuaded by his own servants, and comes up from the river with “his flesh… like unto the flesh of a little child” (2 Kings 5:14). He returns to confess “there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel,” asks for “two mules’ burden of earth” to worship the LORD on Israelite soil, and asks pardon in advance for bowing, at his king’s side, “in the house of Rimmon.” The chapter closes with Elisha’s servant Gehazi, who runs after the Syrian for the reward the prophet refused and is struck with Naaman’s leprosy.

Christian and Jewish readers returned to the episode often. Jesus, preaching at Nazareth, cites Naaman — “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” (Luke 4:27) — after which his hearers “thrust him out of the city” (4:29). Irenaeus, Ambrose, Caesarius of Arles and Bede read the sevenfold washing in the Jordan as a figure of Christian baptism, and Origen reads the river as “our Jordan,” the cleanser of leprosy; the Babylonian Talmud classes Naaman a ger toshav, a resident gentile who kept the Noahide commandments without taking on the whole Law, rather than a full proselyte; and his request for pardon in the “house of Rimmon” (2 Kings 5:18) was discussed as a case of outward conformity: the Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 74b) cites it for a coerced, private bow, holding that “Elisha did not criticize him for acting in this manner”; Theodoret of Cyrus (Questions on Kings, Q. 19) glosses the plea as a compelled outward presence joined to inward worship of the true God; and John Calvin (On Shunning the Unlawful Rites of the Ungodly, 1537), answering conformists who pleaded the verse, reads the bow as “duty and service to his Sovereign,” not worship. The questions the reception turns on include what kind of conversion his confession records, how the Jordan washing relates to baptism, and how Elisha’s “Go in peace” answers the request about Rimmon.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Naaman but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out by earliest extant source. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the narrative of 2 Kings and its citation by Jesus in Luke, the Jewish traditions on his standing as a convert and on his part in Ahab’s death, and the Fathers who read the Jordan-washing in connection with baptism (Irenaeus, Origen, Ambrose, Caesarius of Arles, Bede). Other non-Israelites who meet Israel’s God in the Hebrew Bible have their own timelines here: the Midianite priest Jethro (“now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods,” Exodus 18:11), the Queen of Sheba (who comes “from the uttermost parts of the earth” and blesses the LORD), the seer Balaam (hired to curse Israel, who blesses it instead), and the magicians Jannes and Jambres (who withstand Moses).

Timeline

The biblical portrait (2 Kings 5, with Luke 4:27)

The later tradition draws on a single chapter and on one saying of Jesus that refers to it. 2 Kings 5 presents Naaman at the height of worldly honour and the depth of affliction at once — “captain of the host of the king of Syria… a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper.” The cure comes through “a little maid” carried captive out of Israel, waiting on Naaman’s wife, who wishes her master were “with the prophet that is in Samaria.” Sent at last to Elisha, Naaman is met not by the prophet but by a messenger with a bare instruction — “Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean” — and goes off “in a rage,” affronted that no spectacle attended it and scorning the Jordan beside “Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus.” His own servants reason with him (“if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it?”), and he goes: “then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God: and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”1

The rest of the chapter supplies the details later readers took up. The healed general returns to Elisha and makes his confession — “Behold, now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel” — and, refused any reward, begs instead “two mules’ burden of earth,” that on Israelite soil he may “offer neither burnt offering nor sacrifice unto other gods, but unto the LORD.” Then he asks pardon in advance: that when his master “goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon… the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing” — and Elisha answers only, “Go in peace.” The chapter ends with Gehazi, Elisha’s servant, who runs after Naaman for the silver and raiment the prophet had declined, and hears the sentence “the leprosy therefore of Naaman shall cleave unto thee, and unto thy seed for ever” — going out “a leper as white as snow.” Jesus, preaching at Nazareth in Luke’s Gospel, sets Naaman beside the widow of Sarepta (Luke 4:25–27): “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” — after which his hearers “rose up, and thrust him out of the city.”2

The Jewish traditions (Josephus, the Talmud, and the medieval commentators; from c. AD 93) — the resident-convert and the archer who felled Ahab

Jewish sources develop Naaman in two directions, one legal and one legendary. In law, the Babylonian Talmud classes him with a single word: he was not a full proselyte (ger tzedek) but a ger toshav, a “resident” gentile who forswore idolatry and kept the Noahide commandments without taking on the whole Torah — set beside Nebuzaradan, whom the same passage calls a righteous convert.3

In legend, a separate strand answers a riddle in Naaman’s first verse — “by him the LORD had given deliverance unto Syria” (2 Kings 5:1): deliverance by what feat? By identifying Naaman with the anonymous bowman who “drew a bow at a venture, and smote the king of Israel” (1 Kings 22:34), the tradition makes the victory Aram received “by him” the death of King Ahab at Ramoth-gilead. Its earliest extant attestation is in Josephus, where the archer who wounds the king “through his breastplate” is named Naaman; Rashi welds the two verses (“he drew back his bow innocently and killed [Ahab]”), the Targum to Chronicles names him at the death-scene, and Ginzberg gathers the midrash into its miraculous form. On this identification, the archer who killed Ahab and the leper healed in the Jordan are the same man.4 Full detail »

Irenaeus of Lyons (Fragments from the Lost Writings 34, c. 180) — “purified upon his being baptized”

Irenaeus’s fragment is the earliest extant Christian text surveyed here to read the sevenfold dipping as a figure of baptism: “It was not for nothing that Naaman of old, when suffering from leprosy, was purified upon his being baptized, but [it served] as an indication to us.” The leprosy is sin, the Jordan is the font, and the man who comes up is “spiritually regenerated as new-born babes,” the figure sealed with the Lord’s own word that one must be “born again through water and the Spirit” (John 3:5). The fragment joins the leper as sinner, the sacred water with the invocation of the Lord, and the new birth.5 Full detail »

Origen of Alexandria (the Commentary on John, Book 6, c. 233) — the mystery of “our Jordan”

Origen reads the episode through the river. Naaman rages because “he does not see that our Jordan is the cleanser of those who are impure from leprosy” — it is the Jordan that heals, not the prophet, whose office is only “to direct to the healing agency.” And “as none is good but one, God the Father, so among rivers none is good but the Jordan, nor able to cleanse from his leprosy him who with faith washes his soul in Jesus.” In this passage Origen’s accent falls on the river and on Christ as the cleanser of the leper.6 Full detail »

Ambrose of Milan (De mysteriis and De sacramentis, c. 390) — Naaman in the instruction of the newly baptized

Ambrose uses the figure in catechesis. Instructing the newly baptized at Milan on the rite they had just received, he sends them back to “the lessons lately gone through from the Kings”: Naaman “being forthwith cleansed… understood that it is not of the waters but of grace that a man is cleansed.” The captive maid who pointed him to the prophet is “the Church of God” gathered from the Gentiles; and of the font he says, “not all water heals; but the water that has the grace of Christ heals… Water does not heal unless the Spirit descends and consecrates that water.” In Ambrose’s exposition, Naaman’s cure illustrates this distinction between the element and its consecration.7 Full detail »

Caesarius of Arles (Sermon 129, c. 520) — Elisha as Christ, Naaman as the Gentiles

Caesarius reads the episode as a sustained figure of baptism. Elisha figures Christ and Naaman the Gentiles; the prophet who does not himself touch him figures Christ sending the nations to baptism “through his apostles”; and Naaman “recovered his health in the same river that later Christ consecrated by his baptism.” The count is the sevenfold grace of the Spirit — “‘Wash seven times,’ he said, because of the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit” — and the restored flesh is regeneration: “When Naaman descended into the river as a figure of baptism, ‘his flesh became like the flesh of a little child,’” for “all who are baptized are still called infants, whether they are old or young.”8 Full detail »

The Venerable Bede (the Commentary on Luke, on Luke 4:27, c. 710) — Naaman as the people of the nations

Commenting on the saying of Jesus at Nazareth — “none… was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” — Bede reads the episode as a figure of baptism. Naaman, whose name he glosses “beauty,” is “the people of the nations… purified from all foulness of mind and body through the sacrament of baptism”; the sevenfold washing saves because it is “the only type of baptism that regenerates by the Holy Spirit,” and his flesh “appeared like the flesh of a small child after the washing.” Bede also reads Naaman’s after-acts as the baptismal liturgy itself: his renunciation of foreign gods is the renouncing of Satan, his confession the confession of faith, his portion of holy earth the communion of the Lord’s body.9 Full detail »
