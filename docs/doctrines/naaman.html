Who Was Naaman the Syrian?

Naaman is the great Gentile convert of the Hebrew Bible — a foreign general healed by Israel’s God and sent home a worshipper of the LORD. Scripture introduces him in a single chapter, but a full one: “Naaman, captain of the host of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper” (2 Kings 5:1). On the word of a captive Israelite servant-girl he goes to Elisha, is told to wash seven times in the Jordan, rages at so mean a cure, is talked round by his own servants, and comes up from the river with “his flesh… like unto the flesh of a little child” (2 Kings 5:14). He returns to confess “there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel,” begs “two mules’ burden of earth” to worship the LORD on Israelite soil, and asks pardon in advance for the one compromise his office forces on him — that he must still bow, at his king’s side, “in the house of Rimmon.” The chapter closes on the dark foil to his faith: Elisha’s servant Gehazi, who chases the Syrian for the reward the prophet refused and inherits Naaman’s leprosy for his greed.

Few Old Testament episodes were put to more Christian and Jewish use. Jesus makes Naaman the very type of the outsider who receives what Israel would not — “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” (Luke 4:27) — a word that nearly got him killed at Nazareth. The Fathers read the sevenfold washing in the Jordan as the standing type of Christian baptism; the moral theologians worried the “house of Rimmon” into the classic test-case of outward conformity against inward faith; and the rabbis debated whether Naaman became a full convert or only a ger toshav, a resident who feared the God of Israel without taking on the whole Law. Was his confession the faith of a true convert or the henotheism of a man who simply added a greater God to his world? Does the Jordan cure prefigure baptism, or is that a reading laid on it later? And what is a believer to do, cornered like Naaman between an inward loyalty and an outward act?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Naaman but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the narrative of 2 Kings and its echo on the lips of Jesus, the Fathers who made the Jordan-washing a figure of baptism (Irenaeus, Origen, Ambrose, Caesarius of Arles, Bede), the moral casuistry of the “house of Rimmon,” and the Jewish traditions that weighed his conversion. The genuinely contested questions — was he a full convert or a henotheist? does the Jordan cure prefigure baptism? may a believer bow outwardly while worshipping inwardly? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. Naaman is a prime instance of a recurring biblical type — the Gentile who meets Israel’s God and confesses him — whose nearest kin are the Midianite priest Jethro (“now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods,” Exodus 18:11) and the Queen of Sheba (who comes “from the uttermost parts of the earth” and blesses the LORD), and whose dark foils are the pagan seer Balaam (overruled to bless against his will) and the magicians Jannes and Jambres (who oppose God’s messenger).

Timeline

The biblical portrait (2 Kings 5, with Luke 4:27)

The whole later tradition grows from a single chapter and one saying of Jesus that turns on it. 2 Kings 5 presents Naaman at the height of worldly honour and the depth of affliction at once — “captain of the host of the king of Syria… a great man with his master, and honourable… a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper.” The cure comes through the humblest link in the chain: “a little maid” carried captive out of Israel, waiting on Naaman’s wife, who wishes her master were “with the prophet that is in Samaria.” Sent at last to Elisha, Naaman is met not by the prophet but by a messenger with a bare instruction — “Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean” — and goes off “in a rage,” affronted that no spectacle attended it and scorning the Jordan beside “Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus.” His own servants reason him back (“if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it?”), and the obedience he grudged carries the miracle: “then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God: and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”1

What follows is why the episode mattered so much to the later tradition. The healed general returns to Elisha and makes the great confession of the Gentile convert — “Behold, now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel” — and, refused any reward, begs instead “two mules’ burden of earth,” that on Israelite soil he may “offer neither burnt offering nor sacrifice unto other gods, but unto the LORD.” Then he lays before the prophet the one reservation his office presses on him: that when his master “goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon… the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing” — and Elisha answers only, “Go in peace.” The chapter ends on its dark counter-figure: Gehazi, Elisha’s servant, who runs after Naaman for the silver and raiment the prophet had declined, and hears the sentence “the leprosy therefore of Naaman shall cleave unto thee, and unto thy seed for ever” — going out “a leper as white as snow.” Centuries later Jesus, preaching at Nazareth, makes Naaman the standing type of the outsider who receives what Israel refuses: “many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” — a word so provoking that his hearers “rose up, and thrust him out of the city.” The Gentile leper, the sevenfold washing, and the confession of the one true God are the seeds of everything the tradition would make of him.2

The Jewish traditions (Josephus, the Talmud, and the medieval commentators; from c. AD 93) — the resident-convert and the archer who felled Ahab

Alongside the Christian reading, Jewish readers developed Naaman in two directions at once, one legal and one legendary. In law, the Babylonian Talmud fixes his standing with a single word: he was not a full proselyte (ger tzedek) but a ger toshav, a “resident” gentile who forswore idolatry and kept the Noahide commandments without taking on the whole Torah — set, as the lesser category, beside Nebuzaradan the righteous convert. The label withholds only covenant standing, not sincerity: the status is granted only after a formal renunciation of idols, so it presupposes the very turn Naaman’s confession records (weighed on the conversion-or-henotheism page).3

In legend, a separate strand answers a riddle in Naaman’s first verse — “by him the LORD had given deliverance unto Syria” (2 Kings 5:1): deliverance by what feat? By identifying Naaman with the anonymous bowman who “drew a bow at a venture, and smote the king of Israel” (1 Kings 22:34), the tradition makes the victory Aram received “by him” the death of King Ahab at Ramoth-gilead. The identification is old — already in Josephus the archer who wounds the king “through his breastplate” is named Naaman; Rashi welds the two verses (“he drew back his bow innocently and killed [Ahab]”), the Targum to Chronicles names him at the death-scene, and Ginzberg gathers the midrash into its miraculous form. So the very Gentile the LORD used to strike down the idolatrous Ahab is the Gentile the LORD would later heal and win.4 Full detail »

Irenaeus of Lyons (Fragments from the Lost Writings 34, c. 180) — the earliest reading: “purified upon his being baptized”

The Christian reading begins with Irenaeus, and it begins already whole. In a surviving fragment he takes the sevenfold dipping and calls it flatly a figure of baptism: “It was not for nothing that Naaman of old, when suffering from leprosy, was purified upon his being baptized, but [it served] as an indication to us.” The leprosy is sin, the Jordan is the font, and the man who comes up is “spiritually regenerated as new-born babes,” the figure sealed with the Lord’s own word that one must be “born again through water and the Spirit” (John 3:5). Every element the later tradition would elaborate — the leper-sinner, the sacred water joined to the invocation, the new birth — is here in a single stroke.5 Full detail »

Origen of Alexandria (the Commentary on John, Book 6, c. 233) — the mystery of “our Jordan”

Origen reads the episode through the river. Naaman rages because “he does not see that our Jordan is the cleanser of those who are impure from leprosy” — it is the Jordan that heals, not the prophet, whose office is only “to direct to the healing agency.” And “as none is good but one, God the Father, so among rivers none is good but the Jordan, nor able to cleanse from his leprosy him who with faith washes his soul in Jesus.” Origen’s accent falls on the river of grace and on Christ as the sole cleanser of the leper, so his reading is baptism-adjacent — the mystery of the water of Christian cleansing — more than a spelled-out doctrine of the rite, which the Latin catechists would supply.6 Full detail »

Ambrose of Milan (De mysteriis and De sacramentis, c. 390) — the catechist’s standing type of the font

With Ambrose the figure becomes formal catechesis. Instructing the newly baptized at Milan on the rite they had just received, he sends them back to “the lessons lately gone through from the Kings”: Naaman “being forthwith cleansed… understood that it is not of the waters but of grace that a man is cleansed.” The captive maid who pointed him to the prophet is “the Church of God” gathered from the Gentiles; and the point for the font is exact — “not all water heals; but the water that has the grace of Christ heals… Water does not heal unless the Spirit descends and consecrates that water.” The element is nothing without the consecration; Naaman proves it.7 Full detail »

Caesarius of Arles (Sermon 129, c. 520) — the fullest figure of baptism

Caesarius draws every thread together. Elisha figures Christ and Naaman the Gentiles; the prophet who does not himself touch him figures Christ sending the nations to baptism “through his apostles”; and Naaman “recovered his health in the same river that later Christ consecrated by his baptism.” The count is the sevenfold grace of the Spirit — “‘Wash seven times,’ he said, because of the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit” — and the restored flesh is regeneration: “When Naaman descended into the river as a figure of baptism, ‘his flesh became like the flesh of a little child,’” for “all who are baptized are still called infants, whether they are old or young.”8 Full detail »

The Venerable Bede (the Commentary on Luke, on Luke 4:27, c. 710) — the typology handed to the medieval West

Reading the very saying of Jesus that set the episode before the church — “none… was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” — Bede gathers the Fathers’ reading and passes it to the Latin Middle Ages. Naaman, whose name he glosses “beauty,” is “the people of the nations… purified from all foulness of mind and body through the sacrament of baptism”; the sevenfold washing saves because it is “the only type of baptism that regenerates by the Holy Spirit,” and his flesh “appeared like the flesh of a small child after the washing.” Bede presses further than his predecessors, reading Naaman’s after-acts as the baptismal liturgy itself: his renunciation of foreign gods is the renouncing of Satan, his confession the confession of faith, his portion of holy earth the communion of the Lord’s body.9 Full detail »

Arguments

What did Naaman become — a true convert, or a henotheist? — sound (his turn is genuine and exclusive) / the henotheism reading is weak

The central interpretive crux of the figure. Naaman’s one chapter packs three things into a breath: a sweeping confession (“there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel”), an exclusivity vow (sacrifice “unto other gods” renounced, “but unto the LORD”), and two acts that sit uneasily beside them — the request for “two mules’ burden” of Israelite soil to worship on (2 Kings 5:15–18) and the reserved bow in the house of Rimmon. Three readings compete: a full convert, a henotheist who keeps a territorial theology, and the rabbinic ger toshav, a resident semi-convert. As the claim that the healed Syrian genuinely and exclusively turned to the God of Israel, the conversion reading is sound: the confession is universal, the vow formally renounces every other cult, and the whole reception — Jesus making him the type of saving Gentile faith (Luke 4:27), Ephrem the Gentile who shamed Israel’s unbelief, the rabbis a man who had truly forsworn idols, Calvin one who had “first openly sworn” to sacrifice to no other god — affirms a real turn. The sharpest counter, folded in, is the henotheism reading: the earth request betrays a still-territorial God (Cambridge Bible: “no notion of Jehovah but as a territorial deity”). That residue is real — the theology is, in Guzik’s word, “untaught” — but as a claim that Naaman was merely a henotheist and no true convert it is weak: it under-reads the exclusivity vow in the very same verse, over-reads a load of soil into a doctrine of divine real-estate, and cuts against a narrative that treats his turn as a triumph. The ger-toshav label, rightly understood, confirms the genuine turn (the category requires a formal renunciation of idolatry) while denying only full proselyte status — a matter of covenant standing, not sincerity. The honest verdict is the one the story invites: a real convert, imperfectly instructed.

Does the Jordan cure prefigure baptism? — sound (as a typological figure) / overstated (in its maximal form)

The single most widely-used Christian reading of Naaman is the one traced above: the sevenfold washing of the leprous Gentile in the Jordan (2 Kings 5:10–14) as a figure of Christian baptism. As a typological figure the reading is sound: the correspondence is dense and multiply-anchored — water as the medium, the same Jordan of Christ’s own baptism, the cleansing joined to the prophet’s word, the Gentile recipient, and the flesh restored “like unto the flesh of a little child” as rebirth — and it instances a grammar the New Testament itself authorizes, which reads the Flood (1 Peter 3:20–21) and the Red Sea (1 Corinthians 10:1–2) as baptismal types; above all, Jesus himself makes Naaman the type of the outsider who receives what Israel refused (Luke 4:27). That its plain sense is a healing miracle is no defeater — the plain sense of the Flood is a judgment, yet it is still an antitype of the font. What is overstated is only the maximal form: Scripture never itself names Naaman a baptismal type (as it does the Flood and the Red Sea), so the reading rests on patristic inference, not apostolic citation, and should not be pressed as a prediction the narrative was authored to make or as a cipher (the number seven = the sevenfold Spirit) the writer encoded. The type is genuine; the claim that the text institutes or foretells the rite is not. Cross-linked to the infant-baptism timeline.

The two mules of earth: an altar of holy soil and the sanctity of the Land — sound (as sacred-land piety) / overstated (as the origin of the later holy-earth customs)

Naaman’s parting request — “two mules’ burden of earth,” that on Israelite soil he may sacrifice “unto the LORD” alone (2 Kings 5:17) — read not as a tell of henotheism (that question is settled on the conversion page) but as an act of sacred-land piety: soil on which to raise an altar and worship the LORD on His own ground. As the plain reading it is sound: the commentators converge on the altar intent, on the model of the earthen altar of “in all places where I record my name” (Exodus 20:24); Ephrem takes the dust as a faith that shamed Israel; Bede allegorizes the holy earth as the Christian’s communion in the Lord’s body; and the sensibility recurs widely — the diaspora clod of Israel’s soil in the coffin, Helena’s imported dust, the “holy field” of Pisa. What is overstated is the maximal, genealogical form — that the request originated those customs. The Jewish burial practice rests on its own Torah exegesis (the earthen altar of Exodus 20 read with “his land shall atone for his people,” Deuteronomy 32:43, in Ketubot 111a), and the Campo Santo on a late crusader Golgotha-relic legend; neither needs or names Naaman. He is the biblical locus classicus of the impulse to carry holy ground home — the first to do it — not the historical cause of those who followed.

The house of Rimmon: does Elisha license outward conformity? — weak (as a general licence for accommodation)

Naaman’s one reservation — that his office will still make him bow at his king’s side “in the house of Rimmon,” for which he asks pardon in advance — and Elisha’s bare reply, “Go in peace” (2 Kings 5:18–19), became the classic proof-text of the ethic of accommodation: may a believer perform, or outwardly conform to, an act of false worship under duress while worshipping the true God inwardly? In the Reformation it was the founding proof-text of “Nicodemism,” the outward conformity of crypto-Protestants at the Catholic Mass. As a general licence the reading is weak: the strongest exegesis makes “Go in peace” a bare farewell that rules on nothing (Keil–Delitzsch: “without either approving or disapproving”), the Hebrew is the ordinary send-off to a living man (lēkh lə-shālôm), and the bow may not even be an act of worship — Calvin, refuting the Nicodemites, reads it as the aide merely bending under the weight of the king he supports, “not to feign worship to an idol.” Both traditions that seriously weighed it either confined it narrowly (the rabbis license only a coerced, private bow by a resident semi-convert, as the exception inside a rule that commands martyrdom for public idolatry) or refused it outright (the Reformers sent the conformist to Josiah, not Naaman). What survives is only the narrow, pastoral point the text does make: a prophet does not break a bruised reed, and declines to demand of a raw convert under duress the martyrdom he would later require of the instructed — a toleration of weakness, not a charter for dissimulation.
