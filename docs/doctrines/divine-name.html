The Divine Name (the Tetragrammaton) — Yahweh or Jehovah?

Israel's God has a personal name. In the Hebrew Bible it is written with four consonants — יהוה, Yod–He–Waw–He, the Tetragrammaton (Greek for "four letters") — and it stands there some 6,800 times, from its self-disclosure at the burning bush ("I AM THAT I AM… the LORD God… this is my name for ever," Exodus 3:14–15) to the psalmist's "thou, whose name alone is JEHOVAH, art the most high over all the earth" (Psalm 83:18). How that name is to be read, vocalized, and handled has been discussed for well over two thousand years, and the two English forms most people know, Yahweh and Jehovah, each come out of that discussion.

This page traces the story of the Name across time, earliest witness first: the Second-Temple reverence that stopped pronouncing it, reading Adonai ("Lord") aloud in its place (the practice reflected in the Kyrios of the surviving Septuagint copies and the "the LORD" of later Bibles); the Greek Fathers who nonetheless transcribed what they had heard — Clement's Jave, Origen's Iao, the Iabe of Epiphanius and Theodoret; Jerome, who calls the Name "unspeakable" and records how Greek readers, finding it in Hebrew letters, read it as "ΠΙΠΙ"; the Masoretes, who fixed the consonantal text and pointed the Name with vowels (whose relation to Adonai is a disputed reading); the medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists who read the pointed Name as "Jehovah"; Gesenius and the nineteenth-century Hebraists who argued the older sound was "Yahweh"; and the modern Sacred-Name and Jehovah's-Witness movements, which print a form of the Name in their Bibles and use it in worship. The subject here is the practice and understanding of the Name among Jews and Christians — how to say it, whether to say it, and what has been held to turn on it — not a single dogma.

Timeline

The chronological witnesses are being added, earliest first; the five below run from the earliest Christian transcriptions to the fifth-century reports of how the Name was actually spoken. Still to come are the Second-Temple avoidance and the Kyrios of the surviving Septuagint copies, which precede them, the Masoretic pointing, the medieval "Jehovah," Gesenius's "Yahweh," the KJV/ASV, and the modern Sacred-Name and Jehovah's-Witness movements.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 215)

Clement of Alexandria gives one of the earliest extant Christian transcriptions: the "mystic name of four letters" affixed to the high priest and the innermost sanctuary "is called Jave, which is interpreted, 'Who is and shall be'" — a Greek rendering (Iaou in the manuscript) glossed by the self-naming of God at the burning bush.1

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen, refuting the Gnostic sorcerers who traded on potent-sounding names, reports the Hebrew forms of the Name — Iao, Jah, Sabaoth, Adonai — and insists that these, drawn "from the Hebrew Scriptures," are "names of one and the same God," not the several deities the magicians imagined; the passage doubles as evidence of how the Name was written and spoken in the Greek-speaking church and its magical fringe (that fringe — the survival of Iao, Sabaoth, and Adonai as voces magicae in the Greek Magical Papyri — is discussed on the Name in the Greek Magical Papyri).2 In his notes on Psalm 2 he adds a textual observation: in "the more accurate exemplars" the Name is written not in Greek but in Hebrew characters, "not, however, in the current script, but in the most ancient" — the archaic paleo-Hebrew letters. (On Greek readers taking such letters for Π-Ι-Π-Ι, see Jerome below.)3

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377)

Epiphanius of Salamis, running through the Hebrew names of God in his heresy-catalogue against the Gnostic Archontics, renders the Tetragrammaton in Greek as Ἰαβέ (Iabe) — which his modern translator gives as "Jahveh" — and glosses it "He who was and is, He who forever is," tying it to the burning-bush self-naming; the two-syllable ya-be he reports is what he takes the Name's sound to be.4

Jerome (c. 384)

Jerome, listing the ten Hebrew names of God for Marcella, calls the Tetragrammaton "unspeakable" (ἀνεκφώνητον) and records that some Greek readers, taking the Hebrew letters left in the text for the visually similar Greek ones, read the Name aloud as ΠΙΠΙ ("Pipi") — a report both that the Name stood in Hebrew letters in some Greek books and that it was not read aloud as written.5

Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 453)

Theodoret of Cyrus reports how the Name was pronounced in his day: answering a question on the Name revealed to Moses, he states that the Samaritans say it Ἰαβέ (Iabe) while the Jews say Ἀϊά (Aïa — the "I AM" of Exodus 3:14, not the Tetragrammaton itself).6 Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) took the Samaritan form as the one that "probably approaches the real sound of the Divine name closest" and derived "Jahveh (Yahweh)" from it.7
