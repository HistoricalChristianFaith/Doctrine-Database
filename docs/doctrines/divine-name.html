The Divine Name (the Tetragrammaton) — Yahweh or Jehovah?

Israel's God has a personal name. In the Hebrew Bible it is written with four consonants — יהוה, Yod–He–Waw–He, the Tetragrammaton (Greek for "four letters") — and it stands there some 6,800 times, from its self-disclosure at the burning bush ("I AM THAT I AM… the LORD God… this is my name for ever," Exodus 3:14–15) to the psalmist's "thou, whose name alone is JEHOVAH, art the most high over all the earth" (Psalm 83:18). Yet how that name is to be read, vocalized, and handled has been contested for well over two thousand years — and the two English forms most people know, Yahweh and Jehovah, are the endpoints of that long argument, not neutral spellings.

This page traces the story of the Name across time, earliest witness first: the Second-Temple reverence that stopped pronouncing it, reading Adonai ("Lord") aloud in its place (so the Septuagint's Kyrios and every "the LORD" of later Bibles); the Greek Fathers who nonetheless transcribed what they had heard — Clement's Jave, Origen's Iao, the Iabe of Epiphanius and Theodoret; Jerome, who calls the Name "unspeakable" and records how Greek scribes, leaving it in archaic Hebrew letters, produced the notorious misreading "ΠΙΠΙ"; the Masoretes, who fixed the consonantal text and pointed it with the vowels of the spoken substitute; the medieval readers who fused those borrowed vowels with the consonants to coin the hybrid "Jehovah"; Gesenius and the nineteenth-century Hebraists who argued the older sound was "Yahweh"; and the modern Sacred-Name and Jehovah's-Witness movements that make the form of the Name a matter of obedience. The "belief that developed" here is the church's whole practice-and-understanding of the Name — how to say it, whether to say it, and what turns on getting it right — not a single dogma.

Timeline

The chronological witnesses are being added, earliest first; the five below run from the earliest Christian transcriptions to the fifth-century reports of how the Name was actually spoken. Still to come are the Second-Temple avoidance and Septuagint Kyrios-substitution that precede them, the Masoretic pointing, the medieval "Jehovah," Gesenius's "Yahweh," the KJV/ASV, and the modern Sacred-Name and Jehovah's-Witness movements. The Name's earliest extrabiblical attestation (the Egyptian "Shasu of yhw" at Soleb) and the disputed reading of a second Yahweh figure are weighed under Arguments below.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 215)

Clement of Alexandria gives one of the earliest Christian transcriptions: the "mystic name of four letters" affixed to the high priest and the innermost sanctuary "is called Jave, which is interpreted, 'Who is and shall be'" — a two-syllable Greek rendering close to the modern "Yahweh," glossed by the self-naming of God at the burning bush.1

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen, refuting the Gnostic sorcerers who traded on potent-sounding names, reports the Hebrew forms of the Name — Iao, Jah, Sabaoth, Adonai — and insists that these, drawn "from the Hebrew Scriptures," are "names of one and the same God," not the several deities the magicians imagined; the passage doubles as evidence of how the Name was written and spoken in the Greek-speaking church and its magical fringe.2 In his notes on Psalm 2 he adds the textual observation at the root of the whole later confusion: in "the more accurate exemplars" the Name is written not in Greek but in Hebrew characters, "not, however, in the current script, but in the most ancient" — the archaic paleo-Hebrew letters whose resemblance to the Greek capitals Π-Ι-Π-Ι bred the "Pipi" misreading Jerome would record.3

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377)

Epiphanius of Salamis, running through the Hebrew names of God in his heresy-catalogue against the Gnostic Archontics, renders the Tetragrammaton in Greek as Ἰαβέ (Iabe) — which his modern translator gives as "Jahveh" — and glosses it "He who was and is, He who forever is," tying it to the burning-bush self-naming; the two-syllable ya-be he reports, not the later three-syllable "Jehovah," is what he takes the Name's sound to be.4

Jerome (c. 384)

Jerome, listing the ten Hebrew names of God for Marcella, calls the Tetragrammaton "unspeakable" (ἀνεκφώνητον) and records the celebrated blunder by which Greek readers, mistaking the Hebrew letters left in the text for the visually similar Greek ones, read the Name aloud as ΠΙΠΙ ("Pipi") — the classic proof that its true sound was already being lost and that leaving it in archaic Hebrew script inside a Greek book actively bred misreadings.5

Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 453)

Theodoret of Cyrus gives the single most-cited patristic report of the living pronunciation: answering a question on the Name revealed to Moses, he states that the Samaritans say it Ἰαβέ (Iabe) while the Jews say Ἀϊά (Aïa — the "I AM" of Exodus 3:14, not the Tetragrammaton itself). The Samaritan two-syllable ya-be, from the one community that never adopted the taboo on speaking the Name, is the chief external support for the modern "Yahweh" — though Theodoret wrote centuries after the Name had left common Jewish speech, worked from the Greek and Syriac rather than the Hebrew, and may here depend on Epiphanius.6

Arguments

The off-timeline sub-claims about the Name — how it was vocalized, its earliest attestation, its pronunciation, and the "second Yahweh" reading — are being worked as argument pages. The first is weighed here; several others are already weighed on adjacent timelines and are linked below:

  • Is "Jehovah" a late hybrid vocalization (the qere perpetuum), not the historic pronunciation? — sound. The pointed יְהֹוָה carries the vowels of the spoken substitute Adonai, not the Name's own; splice those borrowed vowels to the consonants YHWH and you get the impossible hybrid "Jehovah" — a form absent from antiquity and first current among medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists (Martini c. 1270, popularized by Galatinus, 1518). Two trims: the vowels are the surrogate's adapted, not copied exactly, and Galatinus popularized rather than coined the form; and the verdict rates the form's origin, not the propriety of continuing to use it.
  • The earliest extrabiblical attestation of the Name — the Egyptian topographical lists' "land of the Shasu of yhw" (Soleb, 14th c. BC) — is weighed on the conquest timeline: the Shasu-of-Yahweh inscription.
  • The Name and the "second Yahweh" / Angel-of-Yahweh figure is weighed on the divine-council timeline: the two-Yahwehs / Angel-of-Yahweh reading.
  • The Name's circulation in the magical and onomastic traditions of late antiquity is weighed with Origen: Origen and the divine names in magic.
