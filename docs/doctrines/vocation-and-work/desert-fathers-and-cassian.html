The Desert Fathers and John Cassian on vocation and the dignity of work

Dates: the Egyptian ascetics, 4th c.; John Cassian’s Institutes c. 420 (Cassian, c. 360–435) · Wikipedia · back to the vocation timeline
Summary: The Egyptian desert made manual labor a fixed part of the monk’s day — not for the sake of what the work produced but as a discipline of the soul, the chief remedy against accidie (sloth, the “noonday demon”). John Cassian, who spent years among the Egyptian monks and then carried their teaching to the Latin West, systematized the tradition in Book X of his Institutes and handed on its watchword: “a monk who works is attacked by but one devil; but an idler is tormented by countless spirits.” This is the fountainhead of the long strand that prized labor first as ascesis and a guard against idleness — the esteem Benedict would later inherit and codify.

Claims & evidence

Accidie: the “noonday demon” that idleness feeds

Cassian counts accidie — a weary, restless distaste for one’s place and work — the sixth of the monk’s eight combats, and the one most dangerous to the solitary. It settles on him at midday, drives him from his cell, and above all “makes the man lazy and sluggish about all manner of work”:

Our sixth combat is with what the Greeks call αὶκηδία, which we may term weariness or distress of heart. This is akin to dejection, and is especially trying to solitaires, and a dangerous and frequent foe to dwellers in the desert; and especially disturbing to a monk about the sixth hour, like some fever which seizes him at stated times… Lastly, there are some of the elders who declare that this is the “midday demon” spoken of in the ninetieth Psalm.

— John Cassian, Institutes X.i (“Of the Spirit of Accidie”).1 Because idleness is what accidie feeds on, work is not incidental to the monastic life but the front line of its defence — a diagnosis that gives manual labor a spiritual, not merely economic, necessity.

The saying of the Egyptian fathers: one devil, or countless spirits

The desert’s verdict on idleness Cassian records as a maxim handed down from the old Egyptian elders — the saying the whole later tradition would quote:

Whence this saying has been handed down from the old fathers in Egypt: that a monk who works is attacked by but one devil; but an idler is tormented by countless spirits.

— Institutes X.xxiii.2 Cassian ties the saying to a hard sociological observation: the monasteries of the East are full because the monks “are supported by the resources of their own labour,” while in the West, where monks live “through the liberality of another,” “love of ease and restlessness of heart does not suffer them to continue long in the place.”

Work as the single remedy at the root of idleness

Expounding Paul’s command to the Thessalonians to “work with your own hands” (and the rule “if any will not work, neither should he eat”), Cassian treats the apostle as a physician who cures a swarm of faults with one prescription — labor:

The cause of all these ulcers, which spring from the root of idleness, he heals like some well-skilled physician by a single salutary charge to work; as he knows that all the other bad symptoms, which spring as it were from the same clump, will at once disappear when the cause of the chief malady has been removed.

— Institutes X.xiv.3 The Egyptian fathers, he adds, therefore “never allow monks… to be idle, estimating the purpose of their hearts and their growth in patience and humility by their diligence in work” — and the surplus of that work becomes charity, provisions for “the parts of Libya which suffer from famine” and for “those who are pining away in the squalor of prison,” so that “by such an offering of the fruit of their hands they offer a reasonable and true sacrifice to the Lord.”4

Abbot Paul, who burned a year’s work every year

The purest statement of labor-as-ascesis is Cassian’s tale of Abbot Paul, a hermit so remote that no market for his craft existed. He wove palm-leaves all year anyway, and each year burned the whole store — work done not for its product but for the soul:

When his cave had been filled with a whole year’s work, each year he would burn with fire that at which he had so diligently laboured: thus proving that without manual labour a monk cannot stop in a place nor rise to the heights of perfection: so that, though the need for food did not require this to be done, yet he performed it simply for the sake of purifying his heart, and strengthening his thoughts, and persisting in his cell, and gaining a victory over accidie and driving it away.

— Institutes X.xxiv.5 Here the dignity of work is set wholly loose from its economic use: the labor is worthless as production and priceless as discipline — the sharpest form of the desert’s conviction that a working monk is a saved monk.

Behind Cassian: Antony and the angel plaiting rope

Cassian systematized what the earlier desert sayings had already made proverbial. The very first saying of Abba Antony in the Apophthegmata Patrum gives the pattern — the alternation of work and prayer, revealed to the beset hermit by an angel:

When the holy Abba Anthony lived in the desert he was beset by accidie, and attacked by many sinful thoughts. He said to God, “Lord, I want to be saved, but these thoughts will not leave me alone. What shall I do in my affliction? How can I be saved?” A short while afterwards, when he got up to go out, Anthony saw a man like himself sitting at his work, getting up from his work to pray, then sitting down and plaiting a rope, then getting up again to pray. It was an angel of the Lord sent to correct and reassure him. He heard the angel saying to him, “Do this and you will be saved.” At these words, Anthony was filled with joy and courage. He did this, and he was saved.

— The Sayings of the Desert Fathers, Antony 1.6 The braided rope and the rising to pray are the seed of the whole tradition: manual labor woven into the rhythm of the holy life as the remedy against sloth — the shape Benedict would later fix into a monastic horarium.

Notes

  • On this timeline the desert tradition sits between Paul (whose “work with your own hands” Cassian is expounding) and Benedict, whose Rule ch. 48 — “Idleness is the enemy of the soul” — inherits and codifies exactly this esteem for handwork. Benedict knew Cassian: an early note to the Institutes pairs the two texts, and the desert saying stands behind the later motto ora et labora.
  • An honest qualification about the kind of esteem. The desert prizes labor first as ascesis — a discipline against a vice and a guard for prayer — more than as a calling with its own dignity or a service to a wider society. This is the very strand the summary flags as the long medieval reservation: manual labor valued chiefly “as a discipline against idleness and a remedy for sin.” The Reformation’s doctrine of Beruf (Luther) would later universalize dignity to every honest trade in the world; the desert’s achievement is narrower and prior — it made work indispensable to the holy life, which is what Benedict, and through him the Latin West, received.
  • Cassian appears on this wiki also as a witness on several other timelines — on the Old Testament canon, Christian friendship, predestination, the discernment of dreams, and elsewhere; the present page treats only the desert teaching on labor. This block is a collective witness (the anonymous Egyptian fathers and Antony, as gathered in the Apophthegmata, together with Cassian who reports and systematizes them), which is why it carries a descriptive slug rather than a single person’s name.
