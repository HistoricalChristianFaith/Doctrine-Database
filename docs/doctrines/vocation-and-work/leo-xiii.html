Leo XIII on vocation and the dignity of work (“Rerum Novarum”)

Dates: 1810–1903 (the encyclical Rerum Novarum, “On the Condition of Labour,” issued 15 May 1891; Gioacchino Pecci, pope 1878–1903) · Wikipedia
Summary: Rerum Novarum is the founding charter of modern Catholic social teaching, and it stands on this timeline as the point at which the Church’s magisterium takes up the dignity of labor as a public question of the industrial age. Against both liberal capitalism and socialism, Leo XIII insists that manual work is honourable rather than servile — “working for gain is creditable, not shameful, to a man, since it enables him to earn an honorable livelihood” — while “to misuse men as though they were things… is truly shameful and inhuman.” He grounds the worker’s dignity and rights in man’s standing as a rational creature made in God’s image, defends the right to private property, the right to a just wage sufficient to support a frugal worker, and the right to rest hallowed by religion. Ninety years later John Paul II issued Laborem Exercens on this encyclical’s anniversary, explicitly extending its account of work — making Leo XIII the head of the modern Catholic line this timeline’s twentieth-century voices carry forward.

Claims & evidence

Honest labor is creditable, not shameful; to misuse men as things is inhuman

The following duties bind the wealthy owner and the employer: not to look upon their work people as their bondsmen, but to respect in every man his dignity as a person ennobled by Christian character. They are reminded that, according to natural reason and Christian philosophy, working for gain is creditable, not shameful, to a man, since it enables him to earn an honorable livelihood; but to misuse men as though they were things in the pursuit of gain, or to value them solely for their physical powers — that is truly shameful and inhuman.

— Rerum Novarum §20 (1891).1 This is the direct answer to the ancient contempt for the workshop with which this timeline opens. Where antiquity had ranked the labourer beneath the free man precisely because he worked with his hands for pay, Leo makes wage-work an honourable thing and the degradation lie instead in the master who treats a worker as an instrument. The employer’s duty is to “respect in every man his dignity as a person” — the same personalist principle John Paul II would later make the axis of Laborem Exercens.

The dignity and rights of the worker rest on man’s rational nature and the image of God

It is the mind, or reason, which is the predominant element in us who are human creatures; it is this which renders a human being human, and distinguishes him essentially from the brute. And on this very account — that man alone among the animal creation is endowed with reason — it must be within his right to possess things not merely for temporary and momentary use, as other living things do, but to have and to hold them in stable and permanent possession.

— Rerum Novarum §6 (1891).2 For Leo the whole social doctrine of the encyclical — the right to property, to a just wage, to rest — follows from what man is: a rational creature, not a beast of burden. Because reason “renders a human being human,” the worker cannot be reduced to his labour-power. The right to private property, in particular, he grounds directly in that rational nature:

For, every man has by nature the right to possess property as his own. This is one of the chief points of distinction between man and the animal creation.

— Rerum Novarum §6 (1891).3 The right to hold property is not, for Leo, a concession of the state but a corollary of man’s reason; and the working man’s savings and small estate are simply “his wages under another form” — which is why he sets the encyclical against socialism’s proposal to transfer private holdings to the community.

Labor is both personal and necessary — which is why a bare wage-bargain is not enough

To labor is to exert oneself for the sake of procuring what is necessary for the various purposes of life, and chief of all for self preservation… Hence, a man’s labor necessarily bears two notes or characters. First of all, it is personal, inasmuch as the force which acts is bound up with the personality and is the exclusive property of him who acts, and, further, was given to him for his advantage. Secondly, man’s labor is necessary; for without the result of labor a man cannot live, and self-preservation is a law of nature, which it is wrong to disobey.

— Rerum Novarum §44 (1891).4 The distinction is the hinge of Leo’s argument on wages. Considered merely as personal, labour could be sold for whatever the worker chooses to accept, even nothing; but because it is also necessary — a man must work to live — the wage-bargain is never a purely private contract between equals, and natural justice sets a floor beneath it. This anticipates the very move John Paul II would make in giving the subjective dimension of work (the worker as person) priority over its objective side.

The just wage: a dictate of natural justice beneath any free contract

Let the working man and the employer make free agreements, and in particular let them agree freely as to the wages; nevertheless, there underlies a dictate of natural justice more imperious and ancient than any bargain between man and man, namely, that wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner. If through necessity or fear of a worse evil the workman accept harder conditions because an employer or contractor will afford him no better, he is made the victim of force and injustice.

— Rerum Novarum §45 (1891).5 Here the dignity of the worker becomes a concrete economic demand. A contract freely made can still be unjust if it leaves the labourer unable to live; the “living wage” principle of later Catholic social teaching is stated here in seed. Freedom of contract is affirmed, but bounded by a justice “more imperious and ancient” than the bargain itself — the worker’s dignity is not his to sign away for want of a better offer.

The right to rest, hallowed by religion

From this follows the obligation of the cessation from work and labor on Sundays and certain holy days. The rest from labor is not to be understood as mere giving way to idleness; much less must it be an occasion for spending money and for vicious indulgence, as many would have it to be; but it should be rest from labor, hallowed by religion. Rest (combined with religious observances) disposes man to forget for a while the business of his everyday life, to turn his thoughts to things heavenly.

— Rerum Novarum §41 (1891).6 The worker’s dignity entails a right the market may not override: a share of his time is owed not to production but to God and to his own soul. The right to rest is not, for Leo, a mere humane concession to fatigue but the protection of the “rights of God… the most sacred and inviolable of rights” in the person of the worker — so that the same principle that dignifies labor also sets its limit.

Work belongs to man’s nature; the fall made its toil, not the work itself, a burden

As regards bodily labor, even had man never fallen from the state of innocence, he would not have remained wholly idle; but that which would then have been his free choice and his delight became afterwards compulsory, and the painful expiation for his disobedience.

— Rerum Novarum §17 (1891).7 Leo restates the biblical distinction the timeline names at its head (Gen 2:15 against Gen 3:17–19): work is not itself the curse. Even in innocence man “would not have remained wholly idle”; labor was to have been his “free choice and his delight,” and it is only its compulsion and painful toil that the fall imposed. The dignity of work therefore reaches back behind the fall to the good creation — the same premise on which the whole tradition on this page rests.

Notes

  • Where it stands on the timeline. Rerum Novarum (1891) is the founding charter of modern Catholic social teaching and the first magisterial voice on this timeline to address the dignity of labor as a public question of the industrial age. It fills the ninety-year gap between the Reformers’ doctrine of calling and the twentieth-century voices, and it is the document John Paul II took up in Laborem Exercens (1981) — issued deliberately on its ninetieth anniversary — so that Leo XIII heads the line of Catholic magisterial teaching this timeline’s modern witnesses carry forward.
  • Between capitalism and socialism. Leo weighs against both. He rejects socialism’s abolition of private property as an injustice to the worker himself (whose savings are “his wages under another form”), and he rejects the “hard-heartedness of employers and the greed of unchecked competition” that treats labour as a mere commodity. The dignity of work is the axis on which he turns against each — the worker is a rational person, neither a unit of the collective nor an instrument of profit.
  • “On the Condition of Labour.” The encyclical is known by its Latin incipit, Rerum Novarum (“of new things” — i.e. of revolutionary change); its descriptive English title is On the Condition of Labour (or On Capital and Labour). The just-wage and right-to-rest teaching here became the seed-bed of the whole subsequent tradition of Catholic social encyclicals (Quadragesimo Anno, 1931; Laborem Exercens, 1981; Centesimus Annus, 1991).
