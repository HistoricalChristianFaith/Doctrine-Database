The Hebrew Scriptures on vocation and the dignity of work

Dates: c. 15th–3rd century BC (the Pentateuch and the Wisdom books, on the traditional reckoning) · back to the vocation timeline
Summary: Long before the philosophers’ contempt for the workshop, the Hebrew Scriptures dignify ordinary labor and craft at the root. Three strands anchor the timeline here: Adam is set in the garden “to dress it and to keep it” before the fall, so work is not itself the curse; Bezalel, the first person Scripture says is “filled with the spirit of God,” is filled precisely for craftsmanship, making artisan skill a divine charism; and the Wisdom books honour the diligent worker, promising that a man skilled at his trade “shall stand before kings.” These texts are the positive foundation the later tradition — monastic, medieval, and Reformation — builds upon.

Claims & evidence

Labor before the fall: the garden is worked in innocence

The first thing Scripture reports God doing with the man after forming him is to give him work. Before any sin, before the sentence of Genesis 3, Adam is placed in Eden to tend and guard it:

And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.

— Genesis 2:15 (KJV).1 That the labor comes before the fall is the load-bearing point: the curse of Genesis 3 falls on the toil of work — the thorns, the sweat of the face, the ground’s resistance (Gen 3:17–19) — not on work as such, which is given as part of the good creation. The Latin tradition read the verse this way explicitly. Augustine held that the garden-work “was not toilsome,” quite unlike the labor to which Adam was condemned after sinning; Bede, expounding Genesis, quotes him at length, that pre-fall labor was

… not the affliction of labor, but the exhilaration of the will, when those things which God created would thrive more joyfully and fruitfully with the help of human work, from which the Creator Himself would be more abundantly praised, who had given the reasoning and skill of working to the soul set in an animal body…

— Augustine, quoted in Bede, Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 2:15).2 Chrysostom likewise took the garden-work to be “painless and without difficulty,” appointed so that the man would not slip into idleness amid the garden’s delights — work, on his reading, was a good ordered into paradise itself, not a consequence of the fall.3

Craftsmanship as a divine charism: Bezalel, “filled with the spirit of God”

When the tabernacle is to be built, God names its chief craftsman and describes his skill as a gift of the Spirit. Bezalel is, notably, the first individual in Scripture said to be “filled with the spirit of God” — and the endowment is for workmanship, in metal, stone, and wood:

See, I have called by name Bezaleel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah: And I have filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship, To devise cunning works, to work in gold, and in silver, and in brass, And in cutting of stones, to set them, and in carving of timber, to work in all manner of workmanship.

— Exodus 31:2–5 (KJV).4 The charge is repeated when the work begins, and there the artisan’s gift is said to include the power to teach his craft to others, both Bezalel and Aholiab being “filled… with wisdom of heart, to work all manner of work” — engraving, weaving, embroidery, and design:

And he hath filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship… And he hath put in his heart that he may teach, both he, and Aholiab… Them hath he filled with wisdom of heart, to work all manner of work, of the engraver, and of the cunning workman, and of the embroiderer… and of the weaver, even of them that do any work, and of those that devise cunning work.

— Exodus 35:31–35 (KJV).5 The fathers noticed the point: Gregory of Nyssa takes it for granted that Bezalel “would not have taken the work in hand had he not previously acquired his knowledge by Divine inspiration” — the craftsman’s competence is itself a thing given from above.6 Skilled manual work is thus not beneath the sacred; it is the very medium through which the dwelling-place of God is made, and the skill to do it is named a work of the Spirit.

The Wisdom books honour the diligent worker

The Wisdom literature turns from craft to conduct, honouring the diligent and industrious worker and setting him in the company of kings:

Seest thou a man diligent in his business? he shall stand before kings; he shall not stand before mean men.

— Proverbs 22:29 (KJV).7 Bede, commenting, reads the diligent worker eschatologically — whoever is “quick in his work, that is, in his good work… diligent and attentive” will “stand in the presence of the apostles in the day of the last examination” — folding the honour of faithful labor into the hope of the last judgment.8 The same esteem runs through the corpus: the worthy woman of Proverbs 31 “seeketh wool, and flax, and worketh willingly with her hands,” and Ecclesiastes presses the worker to wholeheartedness — “Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might.”9

Notes

  • This block gathers the positive dignifying texts — work as gift, charism, and honour. The distinct question of whether the toil of labor is a penalty of the fall (Gen 2:15 read against the sentence of Gen 3:17–19) is a matter of interpretation weighed on its own; the point here is only that Scripture places work in the good creation before it places the curse on its hardship.
  • Bezalel is a standing counter-example to the ancient ranking that despised the artisan: the Hebrew Scriptures make the craftsman’s skill a charism of the same Spirit later poured out on prophets and kings, and the tabernacle itself the product of consecrated handwork. The observation that he is the first person Scripture describes as “filled with the spirit of God” is widely noted.
  • On this timeline these texts sit at the root, earliest of all, before the monastic tradition (Benedict) wrote handwork into the shape of a holy life and the Reformers universalized the doctrine of calling. The tentmaking apostle Paul — who “laboured, working with our own hands” (1 Cor 4:12) — carries the same Hebrew-Scripture esteem for labor into the apostolic age.
