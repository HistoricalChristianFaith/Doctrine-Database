John Paul II on vocation and the dignity of work (“Laborem Exercens”)

Dates: 1920–2005 (the encyclical Laborem Exercens, “On Human Work,” issued 14 September 1981, on the ninetieth anniversary of Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum; Karol Wojtyła, pope 1978–2005) · Wikipedia
Summary: Laborem Exercens is the modern capstone of Catholic teaching on labor, and it gathers the whole tradition on this timeline into one thesis: the dignity of work is grounded not in what is produced but in who produces it. Against every account that measures work by its output, its technology, or its capital — its objective dimension — John Paul II sets the subjective dimension first: the worker is a person made in the image of God, and so “work is ‘for man’ and not man ‘for work’.” From this he draws the social principle of “the priority of labour over capital,” grounds work in the Genesis mandate to subdue the earth (so that the labourer “reflects the very action of the Creator”), and reads Christ the carpenter of Nazareth — the God who spent most of his earthly life at the workbench — as the “Gospel of work,” the decisive proof that ordinary labor is a share in the Creator’s own activity and not a servile burden.

Claims & evidence

Work belongs to the image of God and the call given “from the beginning”

Man is made to be in the visible universe an image and likeness of God himself, and he is placed in it in order to subdue the earth. From the beginning therefore he is called to work. Work is one of the characteristics that distinguish man from the rest of creatures, whose activity for sustaining their lives cannot be called work. Only man is capable of work, and only man works, at the same time by work occupying his existence on earth. Thus work bears a particular mark of man and of humanity, the mark of a person operating within a community of persons.

— Laborem Exercens, Introduction (1981).1 Work is not a consequence of the fall in this account but a mandate given with creation itself: because man is the imago Dei, and because that image carries the charge to “subdue the earth” (Gen 1:28), labor is written into human nature from the first. John Paul II develops the point directly from the Genesis mandate:

Man is the image of God partly through the mandate received from his Creator to subdue, to dominate, the earth. In carrying out this mandate, man, every human being, reflects the very action of the Creator of the universe.

— Laborem Exercens §4 (1981).2 This is the biblical root the timeline names at its head — the garden worked before the fall and the craftsman filled with the Spirit — now made the premise of a whole social doctrine.

The subjective dimension: man is the subject of work, so work is “for man”

The encyclical’s central and most characteristic move is to distinguish the objective sense of work (what is made — the product, the technology, the capital) from its subjective sense (the worker himself, the person who acts), and to give the subjective the priority:

As a person, man is therefore the subject of work. As a person he works, he performs various actions belonging to the work process; independently of their objective content, these actions must all serve to realize his humanity, to fulfil the calling to be a person that is his by reason of his very humanity.

— Laborem Exercens §6 (1981).3 The consequence is a direct reversal of the ancient contempt with which this timeline opens — the ranking of men into higher and lower according to the kind of work they did:

The sources of the dignity of work are to be sought primarily in the subjective dimension, not in the objective one. Such a concept practically does away with the very basis of the ancient differentiation of people into classes according to the kind of work done.

— Laborem Exercens §6 (1981).4 From the same principle he draws the ethical rule that governs the rest of the encyclical:

It only means that the primary basis of the value of work is man himself, who is its subject. This leads immediately to a very important conclusion of an ethical nature: however true it may be that man is destined for work and called to it, in the first place work is “for man” and not man “for work”.

— Laborem Exercens §6 (1981).5 The decisive warrant for locating dignity in the worker rather than the work is the carpenter of Nazareth — the God who chose to spend most of his life doing manual labor:

…the one who, while being God, became like us in all things devoted most of the years of his life on earth to manual work at the carpenter’s bench. This circumstance constitutes in itself the most eloquent “Gospel of work”, showing that the basis for determining the value of human work is not primarily the kind of work being done but the fact that the one who is doing it is a person.

— Laborem Exercens §6 (1981).6

The priority of labour over capital

…we must first of all recall a principle that has always been taught by the Church: the principle of the priority of labour over capital. This principle directly concerns the process of production: in this process labour is always a primary efficient cause, while capital, the whole collection of means of production, remains a mere instrument or instrumental cause.

— Laborem Exercens §12 (1981).7 The social principle is the subjective priority applied to economics: because the worker is a person and the means of production are things, labor can never be treated as one commodity among the factors it employs. This is the axis on which the encyclical turns its critique against both “rigid” capitalism and collectivism — each, in its own way, inverting the order by subordinating the human subject to the material instruments of production.

Work as a sharing in the activity of the Creator — Christ the “man of work”

The closing part of the encyclical, “Elements for a Spirituality of Work,” gathers the argument into a theology of labor as participation in God’s own creating:

The word of God’s revelation is profoundly marked by the fundamental truth that man, created in the image of God, shares by his work in the activity of the Creator and that, within the limits of his own human capabilities, man in a sense continues to develop that activity, and perfects it as he advances further and further in the discovery of the resources and values contained in the whole of creation.

— Laborem Exercens §25 (1981).8 And the incarnate God who worked wood makes the “Gospel of work” not a doctrine only but a life:

Therefore this was also “the gospel of work”, because he who proclaimed it was himself a man of work, a craftsman like Joseph of Nazareth… he belongs to the “working world”, he has appreciation and respect for human work. It can indeed be said that he looks with love upon human work and the different forms that it takes, seeing in each one of these forms a particular facet of man’s likeness with God, the Creator and Father.

— Laborem Exercens §26 (“Christ, the Man of Work”) (1981).9 Dorothy Sayers had written, four decades earlier, that “no crooked table legs or ill-fitting drawers ever… came out of the carpenter’s shop at Nazareth”; John Paul II makes the same image the hinge of the church’s official teaching — the workbench of Nazareth as the standing proof that God dignifies ordinary labor by sharing in it.

Notes

  • Where it stands on the timeline. Laborem Exercens is the first of John Paul II’s three social encyclicals and was issued for the ninetieth anniversary of Rerum Novarum (1891), Leo XIII’s founding charter of modern Catholic social teaching. It is the modern-magisterial capstone of the doctrine this page traces: it takes up the Reformation’s universalizing of vocation (Luther’s Beruf) and the biblical dignifying of labor at the timeline’s root, restates them as official Catholic teaching, and presses them against twentieth-century industrial and economic conditions — the same adversary Sayers named a generation before.
  • “Priority of labour over capital” is not a socialist slogan. The encyclical uses the phrase to reject collectivism as firmly as unrestrained capitalism: the priority is of the human person over material things, not of one economic class over another. Read as a bare economic ranking it would misstate him; read as he frames it (the subjective dimension governs the objective), it is the personalist principle applied to the workplace.
  • John Paul II elsewhere on this wiki. He appears on the timeline of divorce and remarriage for the 1981 apostolic exhortation Familiaris Consortio, issued the same year as this encyclical — the same pontificate setting out the church’s modern teaching on the family and on human work within months of each other.
