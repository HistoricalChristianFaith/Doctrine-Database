John Calvin on vocation and the dignity of work

Dates: c. 1559 (the doctrine of the vocatio as set out in the final Latin Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.x.6; Calvin 1509–1564, the reformer of Geneva) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin is the second Reformer of the doctrine of calling. He teaches that every man’s mode of life is a “station assigned him by the Lord”: a person is to labour where God has posted him, so that even the meanest task “will not be so mean and sordid as not to have a splendour and value in the eye of God.” But he holds the doctrine without rigidity — the calling is a settled vocation, not a life sentence to one trade.

Claims & evidence

Every man’s calling is a station assigned by God — and so no honest work is too mean to shine before him

The last thing to be observed is, that the Lord enjoins every one of us, in all the actions of life, to have respect to our own calling. He knows the boiling restlessness of the human mind, the fickleness with which it is borne hither and thither, its eagerness to hold opposites at one time in its grasp, its ambition. Therefore, lest all things should be thrown into confusion by our folly and rashness, he has assigned distinct duties to each in the different modes of life. And that no one may presume to overstep his proper limits, he has distinguished the different modes of life by the name of callings. Every man’s mode of life, therefore, is a kind of station assigned him by the Lord, that he may not be always driven about at random.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion III.x.6 (“How to Use the Present Life, and the Comforts of It”), trans. Henry Beveridge.1 For Calvin the calling (Lat. vocatio) is not a private ambition but a post God has stationed a man at, so that his whole life is ordered by it — “in every thing the call of the Lord is the foundation and beginning of right action.” The obscure man is not to despise his lot but to keep his ground, “that he may not desert the post at which God has placed him,” and every station’s cares are borne the more patiently for knowing “that God has laid on the burden.” From that the dignity of ordinary work follows directly:

This, too, will afford admirable consolation, that in following your proper calling, no work will be so mean and sordid as not to have a splendour and value in the eye of God.

— Institutes III.x.6, trans. Beveridge.2 This is the same universalizing move that Luther’s doctrine of Beruf made — the sacredness of the ordinary station — now grounded in Calvin’s doctrine of providence: because God appoints the station, the labour done in it is service rendered to him, and its outward meanness is nothing to its worth in his sight. (The Latin Calvin uses for the appointed station is statio, a soldier’s sentry-post; Ford Lewis Battles’ later English renders the phrase “a sort of sentry post … so that he may not heedlessly wander about throughout life,” which captures the military image Beveridge softens to “station.”)

The calling is a settled vocation, not a life sentence to one trade

A calling in Scripture means a lawful mode of life, for it has a relation to God as calling us… But here it is asked, whether Paul means to establish any obligation, for it might seem as though the words conveyed this idea, that every one is bound to his calling, so that he must not abandon it. Now it were a very hard thing if a tailor were not at liberty to learn another trade, or if a merchant were not at liberty to betake himself to farming. I answer, that this is not what the Apostle intends, for he has it simply in view to correct that inconsiderate eagerness, which prompts some to change their condition without any proper reason…

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians 7:20 (“Let every man abide in the same calling wherein he was called”), trans. John Pringle.3 Expounding Paul’s rule that a man should “abide in the calling” he was called in, Calvin refuses to read it as chaining a person to one occupation for life. What Paul condemns is not a change of trade for good reason but “that restlessness, which prevents an individual from remaining in his condition with a peaceable mind.” The calling, then, is a settled and God-given vocation to be pursued with contentment — but it is a discipline of the restless heart, not a prohibition on ever moving. The point guards the doctrine of calling against a fatalism it is easily mistaken for.

Notes

  • Calvin as the second Reformer of vocation. Alongside Luther, Calvin universalized the medieval idea of vocatio — long reserved for the monastery and the priesthood — to every honest station. Where Luther grounds the dignity of work chiefly in faith and the service of the neighbour, Calvin grounds it in providence: God posts each person, so that labour done in one’s station is obedience to a divine assignment. The two together are the “Reformation’s doctrine of calling” that the modern voices on this timeline (Sayers, John Paul II) inherit — and that Weber’s critique made the Calvinist calling in particular its subject (see the Weber argument).
  • Calvin elsewhere on this wiki. He appears as a witness on many other timelines. Most relevant here is his page on the purpose of the liberal arts, where the same conviction — that true learning and honest work are God’s gifts, to be received with gratitude, not despised — is built into the Geneva Academy (1559).
  • The text’s history. The treatment of the calling stands in Book III, chapter 10, of the Institutes; the material was present from the 1539 Latin edition onward and reached its final form in the 1559 edition cited here. The 1 Corinthians commentary was first published in 1546.
