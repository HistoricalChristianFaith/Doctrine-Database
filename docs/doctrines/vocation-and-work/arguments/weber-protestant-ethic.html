Did the Protestant doctrine of calling produce the “spirit of capitalism”?

Disputed reading · on Vocation and the Dignity of Work · witness: Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–05; Talcott Parsons trans., 1930)

The passage

The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are forced to do so.

— Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (Parsons trans.), concluding chapter, source. Weber continues that “victorious capitalism, since it rests on mechanical foundations, needs its support no longer,” and that “the cloak” of the calling had become “an iron cage.”1

The question

Max Weber argued in two essays of 1904–05 that the Reformed, and above all the English Puritan, ethic of the calling helped form the disciplined, accumulative temper of modern rational capitalism. What is disputed is whether that religious ethic was a formative cause of the capitalist spirit, an affinity running alongside economic change, or a correlate of something else; the question concerns historical causation, not the theological doctrine of calling traced on the vocation timeline.

The Reformed ethic of the calling helped form the capitalist spirit

On this reading the Puritan ethic of methodical labour in a worldly calling supplied a decisive cultural ingredient of modern rational capitalism.

Held by: Max Weber (sociologist, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, 1904–05).2

Weber begins from an observation already discussed in the German Catholic press of his day: that in regions of mixed confession “business leaders and owners of capital… are overwhelmingly Protestant.” He offers a mechanism for it. The Puritan divines he reads, Richard Baxter’s Christian Directory above all, exalt methodical labour in a calling, treat idleness and time-wasting as sins against God, and make worldly diligence a spiritual duty. The Calvinist, unable to earn or read his election, found in disciplined work in his calling the sign of it; the same asceticism forbade him to spend its fruits on luxury. Systematic work together with a ban on enjoyment leaves reinvestment, and so an inner-worldly asceticism pursued for the sake of the soul bred, without intending it, the calculating and duty-bound “spirit” Weber found in Benjamin Franklin’s maxims.3

Weber distinguishes the capitalism he means from the acquisitiveness found in every age. Acquisition “as an adventure has been at home in all types of economic society”; what is new is an economy “rationalized on the basis of rigorous calculation,” set against “the adventurers’ capitalism, oriented to the exploitation of political opportunities and irrational speculation.” Earlier merchant and banking wealth is on his account a different thing from the methodical temper he sets out to explain.4 He frames the inquiry as a search for “certain correlations between forms of religious belief and practical ethics,” an elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) between a religious ethic and an economic form, and he disclaims monocausation: “it is, of course, not my aim to substitute for a one-sided materialistic an equally one-sided spiritualistic causal interpretation of culture and of history.” Read at that strength, the thesis names one formative strand among the many that made the modern economy.5

Capitalism’s sources lie in medieval commerce and material change

On this reading accumulative, credit-and-profit capitalism existed before the Reformation and in Catholic lands, and its rise is explained by commerce, trade routes, bullion, technology and law rather than by the Reformed calling.

Held by: Henri Pirenne (historian, 1914); H. M. Robertson (economic historian, Aspects of the Rise of Economic Individualism, 1933); Kurt Samuelsson (economic historian, Religion and Economic Action, 1957); Fernand Braudel (historian, Afterthoughts on Material Civilization and Capitalism, 1977).6

Pirenne set out that “all the essential features of capitalism — individual enterprise, advances in credit, commercial profits, speculation” are present from the twelfth century in Venice, Genoa and Florence, and, as Tawney quotes him, took what Weber and Troeltsch called the Calvinist spirit to be the spirit of the new men whom the economic revolution of the age brought into business. The great banking-and-mining houses of the sixteenth century, the Fuggers and Thürzos of Augsburg and the Medici of Florence, were Catholic, and the commercial capitalism of Antwerp and the Mediterranean flourished before Calvin was born. Braudel judged the Protestant-origins theory “clearly false”: the northern Protestant centres “took over the place that earlier had so long and brilliantly been occupied by the old capitalist centres of the Mediterranean.”7

Robertson, writing against Weber’s school, documented a supple Catholic ethic of commerce, credit and profit among the casuists and confessors of the Counter-Reformation, the Jesuits above all, as serviceable to capitalism as anything in Geneva; with a business-friendly ethic on both sides of the confessional line, he located the drivers in material factors.8 Samuelsson’s book-length study argued that Franklin was an Enlightenment deist rather than a Calvinist, that the Puritan divines repeatedly condemned the pursuit of riches and the love of money, and that the correlation between Calvinism and economic success does not appear when the cases are counted; he concluded that no demonstrable functional relationship links the two.9 On this account the rise of capitalism is told through the history of trade, credit and the commercial cities, in which Catholic and Protestant merchants alike took part.

Religion and economic change shaped each other

On this reading the Puritan ethic and the rising commercial order acted on one another, so that later Puritanism was as much moulded by economic change as it moulded it.

Held by: R. H. Tawney (economic historian, Holland Memorial Lectures 1922, published as Religion and the Rise of Capitalism, 1926; foreword to Parsons’s translation of Weber, 1930).10

Tawney wrote the foreword that introduced Weber to English readers in 1930 and treats Weber’s essay as illuminating on its chosen theme, the doctrines of Puritan divines. His case is that causation runs in both directions. “Why insist that causation can work in only one direction?” he asks; “Is it not a little artificial to suggest that capitalist enterprise had to wait, as Weber appears to imply, till religious changes had produced a capitalist spirit? Would it not be equally plausible, and equally one-sided, to argue that the religious changes were themselves merely the result of economic movements?” Commercial expansion, the new men and the pressures of a money economy reshaped religion; three generations of economic development and political agitation lay between Calvin and the English Puritan writers, and “the Calvinism which fought the English Civil War… was not that of its founder.” Puritanism helped to mould the social order and was in its turn moulded by it, so that the historian traces with Weber the influence of religious ideas on economic development and grasps as well “the effect of the economic arrangements accepted by an age on the opinion which it holds of the province of religion.”11 On this account the Puritan ethic of the calling and the commercial order grew up together, each reinforcing the other across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

A Protestant–prosperity link carried by literacy

On this reading Protestant regions did grow richer, but through the literacy the Reformation fostered rather than through a distinctive ascetic work ethic.

Held by: Sascha O. Becker and Ludger Woessmann (economists, “Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History,” 2009).12

Becker and Woessmann test the correlation Weber started from with quantitative data from the counties of nineteenth-century Prussia. They find that Protestant regions were indeed more prosperous, and they trace the gap to human capital: the Reformation’s insistence that every believer read the Bible produced higher literacy in Protestant populations, and on their estimates that literacy accounts for the whole of the Protestant–prosperity difference, with no residual role left for a distinctive ascetic work ethic. On this account the regularity Weber observed in the occupational statistics of his own day is real, and the Reformation had a hand in it, but the mechanism runs through schooling and reading. The reading thus keeps the Reformation as an economic cause while relocating that cause from the doctrine of the calling to the spread of literacy.
