Is human work a participation in God’s own creative activity?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that human labor, because man is made in the image of the Creator, genuinely participates in and (in a delegated, analogical sense) continues God’s own creative-ordering activity — rightly named sub-creation — is well grounded in the imago Dei and the creation mandate, and read this way across the tradition; the one thing to hold with care is the wording, since “co-creation” pressed to blur the Creator/creature distinction (creatio ex nihilo is God’s alone) overstates — a caution the best proponents already build in · advanced by Dorothy L. Sayers and John Paul II

Related arguments: the theology of calling — see also extending “vocation” to every honest trade

The claim

…man, created in the image of God, shares by his work in the activity of the Creator and… within the limits of his own human capabilities, man in a sense continues to develop that activity, and perfects it as he advances further and further in the discovery of the resources and values contained in the whole of creation.

— John Paul II, Laborem Exercens §25 (1981), Vatican edition.1 The interpretation under weighing is the theological thesis two witnesses on this timeline share: that ordinary work is not merely a permitted necessity but a participation in the Creator’s own making — that because the human worker images the divine Maker, to make and to cultivate is to reflect and extend the activity of the God who made the world. It is the affirmative core beneath the doctrine of calling: not only that every honest trade is a vocation (weighed separately), but why — because the maker images the Maker.

Who advances it

The thesis runs through two witnesses here. Dorothy Sayers hangs the whole of Why Work? (1942) on it: work is “the natural exercise and function of man — the creature who is made in the image of his Creator,” so that “man, made in God’s image, should make things, as God makes them, for the sake of doing well a thing that is well worth doing.” Making is thus not a penalty of the fall but the very exercise of the imago Dei.2 John Paul II makes the same move the theological climax of Laborem Exercens (§§4, 25): man is the image of God “partly through the mandate received from his Creator to subdue… the earth,” so that “in carrying out this mandate, man, every human being, reflects the very action of the Creator of the universe.”3 Behind both stands a long lineage. The scriptural root is the imago Dei read together with the creation mandate; a strong modern scholarly form is the “royal-representative” reading of the image, on which the human being is God’s vice-regent, set to continue the Creator’s ordering of the world. And the term that has done most to keep the thesis precise is J. R. R. Tolkien’s sub-creation: man as a “sub-creator” who, imaging the Maker, “make[s] still by the law in which we’re made” — a maker under, and reflecting, the one Creator.4

The evidence for

The strength of the reading is that it does not hang on a single proof-text but on the plain shape of the opening chapters of Genesis. The God whom man is made to image is, in the immediate context, the Creator. Genesis 1 says nothing of God before it shows him making — “in the beginning God created” — and it is this God, the Maker of heaven and earth, whose image man is said to be in the same chapter: “Let us make man in our image… and let them have dominion”; “so God created man in his own image… and God said unto them… subdue it: and have dominion” (Gen 1:26–28). The image is stated and the mandate to rule and cultivate is given in one breath, so that to bear the image is, in the text’s own logic, to be set over the creation as one who orders and develops it.5 The garden confirms it. Man is placed in Eden “to dress it and to keep it” (Gen 2:15) — to cultivate and extend what God has planted; God makes the raw creation, and man is given the work of bringing it toward its fruitfulness. And God himself is said to work and then to rest (Gen 2:2–3), so that the rhythm of labor is patterned on the Creator’s own, one more mark that the worker images the working God.

This is not a modern embellishment read back into the text. The “royal-representative” reading — that the image language draws on the ancient practice by which a king’s image marked his sovereignty over a territory, here democratized to the whole human race as God’s vice-regents — is the mainstream construal of Genesis 1 in modern Old Testament scholarship, and it makes human cultural and productive labor the direct discharge of the image-bearer’s commission.6 The Fathers already read man’s making and ruling as belonging to the image: Gregory of Nyssa took the very endowments of reason and the crafting hand as the marks of a creature made to be “a living image” of the divine sovereignty over creation. And the New Testament will call the Christian worker, at least in the work of the gospel, a fellow-labourer with God — “we are labourers together with God” (1 Cor 3:9) — the same participatory grammar, one rung higher.7

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core is firm. The imago Dei and the creation mandate are as plainly in the text as anything in Genesis, the God imaged there is the Maker, and the tradition — patristic, Reformation, Catholic, and the modern writers on either side — has read human making and cultivating as a reflection and extension of that Maker’s work. So the thesis that honest work participates in God’s creative-ordering activity is a legitimate drawing-out of the text, not a pious flourish, and it supplies the affirmative ground the whole doctrine of vocation stands on.

What is overstated or imprecise. The weakness, where there is one, is in the wording, not the reading.

(1) “Co-creation” can efface the Creator/creature distinction. God creates ex nihilo — out of nothing, by word alone, originating being itself; man only ever re-forms material God has already made, working by laws and with powers he did not give himself. To create, in the strict sense, “is proper to God alone” (Aquinas): the human worker never originates being, only shapes it. So “co-creator,” or “man continues God’s creation” taken flatly, can blur an absolute gap and flatter the creature. The careful proponents already guard against exactly this: John Paul II writes that man “in a sense continues to develop” the Creator’s activity and only “within the limits of his own human capabilities”; Sayers’s “as God makes them” is analogical, not identical; and Tolkien’s term is deliberately sub-creation — a maker under the Creator, reflecting a “refracted light,” never a rival source of it. Read with those fences the thesis is a participation by analogy and delegation, and it is exactly right; unfenced, the language overreaches. This is a caution about phrasing to keep on the proponents’ side, not a rival thesis, and it does not touch the reading it qualifies.8

(2) The content of the image is broader than “the capacity to make.” The tradition has variously located the image in reason, in righteousness, in relationship, and in royal dominion, and it would overstate to reduce the imago to human creativity as such. But the argument does not need that narrow equation: it runs on the image together with the explicit mandate to subdue and cultivate (Gen 1:28; 2:15), which is agreed ground whatever else the image includes. And because the image is marred — though not erased — by the fall, fallen work reflects the Creator truly but imperfectly; the thesis describes work’s vocation, not a claim that all actual labor achieves it.

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation the argument serves — that human work genuinely participates in, and in a delegated and analogical sense continues, God’s own creative-ordering activity, rightly called sub-creation — is well grounded. It rests on the imago Dei and the creation mandate, both firmly in the text of Genesis, where the God the worker images is the Maker himself, and it is read this way from the Fathers through the Reformers to modern Catholic teaching. The one thing to hold with care is not the thesis but its wording: “co-creation,” or “man continues God’s creation,” pressed so as to blur the Creator/creature distinction — creatio ex nihilo is God’s alone — overstates, and where a proponent lets the phrase run unfenced it should be flagged as an overreach of language. But the best statements of the reading already build the fence in (John Paul II’s “in a sense” and “within the limits,” Sayers’s analogical “as,” Tolkien’s sub-creation), so the caution trims the phrasing without weakening the interpretation. The verdict is sound on the reading, with “co-creator” language kept to its analogical and delegated sense.
