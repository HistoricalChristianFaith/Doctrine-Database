Paul the Apostle on vocation and the dignity of work

Dates: c. AD 50 (the stay at Corinth and the Thessalonian letters) · Wikipedia · the traditions about Paul · back to the vocation timeline
Summary: Paul carries the Hebrew Scriptures’ esteem for labor into the apostolic age and turns it into a rule of life. A tentmaker by trade, he kept working with his own hands even while preaching — refusing the support he had every right to claim so as not to burden the churches — and pressed the same discipline on his converts: “work with your own hands,” and, more sharply, “if any would not work, neither should he eat.” He is the apostolic warrant to which Benedict and the whole later tradition of the dignity of work appeal.

Claims & evidence

The tentmaker who kept his trade while he preached

Arriving at Corinth, Paul lodges and works with a Jewish couple of the same trade, and supports himself by his craft while reasoning in the synagogue on the sabbath:

And found a certain Jew named Aquila, born in Pontus, lately come from Italy, with his wife Priscilla; (because that Claudius had commanded all Jews to depart from Rome:) and came unto them. And because he was of the same craft, he abode with them, and wrought: for by their occupation they were tentmakers. And he reasoned in the synagogue every sabbath, and persuaded the Jews and the Greeks.

— Acts 18:2–4 (KJV).1 Chrysostom dwells on the point against the ancient contempt for the workshop: that an apostle whom “angels honored… and demons trembled at” nonetheless “stayed with a tentmaker and sewed skins” is precisely why “no one who is a craftsman” should “be ashamed.” The labor was no disgrace but a fitness: “to him it was much more suitable than any king’s palace… And wrought, though he preached. Let us be ashamed, who though we have no preaching to occupy us, live in idleness.”2

Working night and day, refusing the support he had a right to

Paul insists that a preacher of the gospel has every right to be maintained by those he serves — “the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel” — and then, having established the right, renounces it, working to keep the gospel “without charge” so that no one could accuse him of preaching for gain:

Nevertheless we have not used this power; but suffer all things, lest we should hinder the gospel of Christ… But I have used none of these things… What is my reward then? Verily that, when I preach the gospel, I may make the gospel of Christ without charge, that I abuse not my power in the gospel.

— 1 Corinthians 9:12, 15, 18 (KJV).3 He makes the same self-supporting labor the badge of his apostleship — setting it, in a catalogue of his hardships, against the ease of the “false apostles”: “And labour, working with our own hands: being reviled, we bless; being persecuted, we suffer it.”4 Chrysostom reads the line exactly so — “now against the false apostles who endure neither toil nor peril, while they themselves receive the fruits” — contrasting Paul, who “together with our perils from without… also strain ourselves to the utmost with perpetual labor.”5

The precept: “work with your own hands”

What Paul practised he commanded. To the Thessalonians he makes quiet, self-supporting labor a matter of Christian conduct, so that the believer depends on no one and gives the outsider no cause for reproach:

And that ye study to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you; That ye may walk honestly toward them that are without, and that ye may have lack of nothing.

— 1 Thessalonians 4:11–12 (KJV).6 Chrysostom presses the plain sense — “with your hands” — against those who would spiritualize the command away: “Where are those, who look out for work that is spiritual? Seest thou how he takes from them every excuse, saying, ‘with your hands’?” The point of the labor is love of neighbour, “that one does not receive of others, nor live idly, but by working imparts to others.”7

The rule: “if any would not work, neither should he eat”

In the second letter the exhortation hardens into a command. Paul recalls his own example — that he “wrought with labour and travail night and day” so as not to be chargeable to any of them, though he had the right to be — and lays down the rule that has echoed through the tradition ever since:

Neither did we eat any man’s bread for nought; but wrought with labour and travail night and day, that we might not be chargeable to any of you: Not because we have not power, but to make ourselves an ensample unto you to follow us. For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.

— 2 Thessalonians 3:8–10 (KJV).8 Chrysostom marks the sharpened tone — the first letter “discourses somewhat more mildly… the words of one exhorting to virtue,” but here “is nothing of this kind, but ‘if any one will not work, neither let him eat’” — and grounds the command in Paul’s own toil: “For if Paul, not being under a necessity… did nevertheless work… night and day… much more ought others to do this.”9

Notes

  • On this timeline Paul sits at the root of the apostolic age, after the Hebrew Scriptures’ dignifying of craft and labor and before the monastic tradition (Benedict) wrote handwork into the shape of a holy life. Benedict appeals to Paul directly — the monks are “monks in truth, if they live by the work of their hands, as did also… the Apostles” — and the whole Reformation doctrine of calling reaches back past the monastery to this apostolic precedent of the tradesman who preached.
  • The scope of “if any would not work, neither should he eat” (2 Thess 3:10) — whether it lays down a general Christian duty to labor, or addresses a specific problem of idle disorderliness at Thessalonica (some reading it against an over-eager expectation of Christ’s imminent return) — is a question of interpretation weighed on its own (assessment: sound — a general rule, rightly bounded by its occasion); this witness block records the text and its early reception, not that adjudication. Chrysostom himself notes both edges: the command is real, yet the same Paul who gave it added, “And you, brothers, do not lose heart in doing good,” so that it is not to be turned into a pretext for withholding mercy from the genuinely unemployed.
  • Paul appears on this wiki also on the remarriage timeline and as the subject of a biographical tradition-timeline; the present page treats only his teaching and practice on labor.
