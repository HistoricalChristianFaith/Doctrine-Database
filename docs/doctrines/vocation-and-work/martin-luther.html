Martin Luther on vocation and the dignity of work (the doctrine of Beruf)

Dates: c. 1520 (the works cited span 1520–1532: To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, 1520; The Estate of Marriage, 1522; the Exposition of Psalm 147, 1532; Luther 1483–1546, the Wittenberg reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther taught that every honest station — the cobbler’s bench, the farmer’s field, the parent’s household — is a Beruf, a calling from God in which a Christian serves God by serving his neighbour. On his account the “priesthood of all believers” leaves no ranking of a “spiritual estate” above a “temporal” one; the humblest domestic work, done in faith, is pleasing to God; and God works through the ploughman and the labourer as through “masks” behind which he provides.

Claims & evidence

Luther on the “spiritual” and “temporal” estates (1520)

It is pure invention that pope, bishops, priests and monks are to be called the “spiritual estate”; princes, lords, artisans, and farmers the “temporal estate.” That is indeed a fine bit of lying and hypocrisy. Yet no one should be frightened by it; and for this reason — viz., that all Christians are truly of the “spiritual estate,” and there is among them no difference at all but that of office, as Paul says in I Corinthians 12:12, We are all one body, yet every member has its own work, where by it serves every other, all because we have one baptism, one Gospel, one faith, and are all alike Christians.

— To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation (1520), trans. C. M. Jacobs.1 The first of the “walls” Luther attacks in the treatise is the distinction of a clerical “spiritual estate” from a lay “temporal estate.” On his account baptism, gospel, and faith — not ordination — make a person “spiritual,” so the only difference between a priest and a farmer is the office each has been given to do. From this he draws a conclusion about ordinary labor:

A cobbler, a smith, a farmer, each has the work and office of his trade, and yet they are all alike consecrated priests and bishops, and every one by means of his own work or office must benefit and serve every other, that in this way many kinds of work may be done for the bodily and spiritual welfare of the community, even as all the members of the body serve one another.

— To the Christian Nobility (1520), trans. Jacobs.2 On Luther’s account the trades are not a lower realm the Christian must leave to reach a “religious” life; each is a consecrated office through which the worker serves the whole body — the priesthood of all believers applied to daily work.

Household work done in faith (1522)

Now you tell me, when a father goes ahead and washes diapers or performs some other mean task for his child, and someone ridicules him as an effeminate fool, though that father is acting in the spirit just described and in Christian faith, my dear fellow you tell me, which of the two is most keenly ridiculing the other? God, with all his angels and creatures, is smiling, not because that father is washing diapers, but because he is doing so in Christian faith.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), trans. Walther I. Brandt.3 In the same treatise Luther, against the ranking of celibacy above marriage, holds that the domestic estate and its drudgery are a place of certainty about God’s pleasure, because God ordained the estate itself:

For this they have God’s word, Genesis 1 [:28], and they can be certain that he does not lie. They can therefore also be certain that the estate of marriage and everything that goes with it in the way of conduct, works, and suffering is pleasing to God.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), trans. Brandt.4 Luther locates domestic labor at the centre of the Christian’s certainty of pleasing God. On his account it is not the diaper but the faith in which it is washed that God smiles on, and the station in which that faith is exercised is a divine ordinance.

God works through the labourer as through a “mask” (1532)

What else is all our work to God — whether in the fields, in the garden, in the city, in the house, in war, or in government — but just such a child’s performance, by which he wants to give his gifts in the fields, at home, and everywhere else? These are the masks of God, behind which he wants to remain concealed and do all things.

— Exposition of Psalm 147 (1532), Luther’s Works vol. 14, pp. 114–115.5 Expounding the psalm’s praise of the God who “filleth thee with the finest of the wheat” (Ps 147:14), Luther argues that God could give grain without the ploughman, but chooses instead to give it through him. The farmer, the magistrate, the soldier, and the parent are the “masks” (larvae Dei) behind which God hides to do his providential work — so that ordinary labor is, on his account, the instrument of God’s action. Alongside the priesthood of all believers, this gives Luther a second ground for vocation: the worker serves his neighbour, and God serves the neighbour through the worker.

Notes

  • The word Beruf. Luther grounded the calling of every honest station in the priesthood of all believers and in God’s working through the labourer. Max Weber (1904–05) traced the modern sense of Beruf to the Bible translations: “in its modern meaning the word comes from the Bible translations, through the spirit of the translator, not that of the original,” and “In Luther’s translation of the Bible it appears to have first been used at a point in Jesus Sirach (xi. 20 and 21) precisely in our modern sense.”6 Whether Luther’s doctrine bore on the “Protestant work ethic” that Weber tied to capitalism is the subject of a disputed reading on this timeline.
  • The “milkmaid” image. Luther’s doctrine is often summed up in the picture of God milking the cows through the vocation of the milkmaid; the line is quoted as Luther’s in Gustaf Wingren’s study Luther on Vocation (1957) ⚠ unverified (its place in Luther’s works is not yet sourced). The “masks of God” passage of the Exposition of Psalm 147 quoted above has the ploughman as its example.
  • Luther elsewhere on this wiki. He appears on the timeline of the liberal arts for his campaign to keep children in school against the profit-minded parent — the same conviction that learned service is a calling, not merely a living — and on predestination for The Bondage of the Will (1525).
