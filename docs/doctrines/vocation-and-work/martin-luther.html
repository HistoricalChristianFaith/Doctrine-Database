Martin Luther on vocation and the dignity of work (the doctrine of Beruf)

Dates: c. 1520 (the hinge works span 1520–1532: To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, 1520; The Estate of Marriage, 1522; the Exposition of Psalm 147, 1532; Luther 1483–1546, the Wittenberg reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther is the hinge of this doctrine. Against the medieval reservation of a divine calling (vocatio) to the monastery and the priesthood, he taught that every honest station — the cobbler’s bench, the farmer’s field, the parent’s household — is a Beruf, a calling from God in which a Christian serves God by serving his neighbour. The “priesthood of all believers” abolished the ranking of a “spiritual estate” above a “temporal” one; the humblest domestic work, done in faith, is a worship pleasing to God; and God himself works through the ploughman and the labourer as through “masks” behind which he provides.

Claims & evidence

The “spiritual” and “temporal” estates are a false ranking — every trade is a consecrated office (1520)

It is pure invention that pope, bishops, priests and monks are to be called the “spiritual estate”; princes, lords, artisans, and farmers the “temporal estate.” That is indeed a fine bit of lying and hypocrisy. Yet no one should be frightened by it; and for this reason — viz., that all Christians are truly of the “spiritual estate,” and there is among them no difference at all but that of office, as Paul says in I Corinthians 12:12, We are all one body, yet every member has its own work, where by it serves every other, all because we have one baptism, one Gospel, one faith, and are all alike Christians.

— To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation (1520), trans. C. M. Jacobs.1 Luther’s first “wall” to breach is the medieval partition of Christendom into a higher clerical caste and a lower lay one. Baptism, gospel, and faith — not ordination — make a person “spiritual,” so the only real difference between a priest and a farmer is the office each has been given to do. From this he draws the conclusion that dignifies ordinary labor directly:

A cobbler, a smith, a farmer, each has the work and office of his trade, and yet they are all alike consecrated priests and bishops, and every one by means of his own work or office must benefit and serve every other, that in this way many kinds of work may be done for the bodily and spiritual welfare of the community, even as all the members of the body serve one another.

— To the Christian Nobility (1520), trans. Jacobs.2 The trades are not a lower realm the Christian must escape to reach a “religious” life; each is already a consecrated office through which the worker serves the whole body. This is the priesthood of all believers turned onto daily work — the theological root of the Reformation’s universalizing of vocation.

The humblest household work, done in faith, is pleasing to God (1522)

Now you tell me, when a father goes ahead and washes diapers or performs some other mean task for his child, and someone ridicules him as an effeminate fool, though that father is acting in the spirit just described and in Christian faith, my dear fellow you tell me, which of the two is most keenly ridiculing the other? God, with all his angels and creatures, is smiling, not because that father is washing diapers, but because he is doing so in Christian faith.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), trans. Walther I. Brandt.3 In the same treatise Luther overturns the medieval exaltation of celibacy over marriage by insisting that the domestic estate and its drudgery are, of all things, a place of certainty about God’s pleasure — because God ordained the estate itself:

For this they have God’s word, Genesis 1 [:28], and they can be certain that he does not lie. They can therefore also be certain that the estate of marriage and everything that goes with it in the way of conduct, works, and suffering is pleasing to God.

— The Estate of Marriage (1522), trans. Brandt.4 What the ancients despised as banausic — and what the medieval hierarchy of the counsels placed beneath the vowed life — Luther locates at the very centre of the Christian’s certainty of pleasing God. It is not the diaper but the faith in which it is washed that God smiles on; and the station in which that faith is exercised is itself a divine ordinance, not a mere concession to the fallen body.

God works through the labourer as through a “mask” (1532)

What else is all our work to God — whether in the fields, in the garden, in the city, in the house, in war, or in government — but just such a child’s performance, by which he wants to give his gifts in the fields, at home, and everywhere else? These are the masks of God, behind which he wants to remain concealed and do all things.

— Exposition of Psalm 147 (1532), Luther’s Works vol. 14, pp. 114–115.5 Expounding the psalm’s praise of the God who “filleth thee with the finest of the wheat” (Ps 147:14), Luther argues that God could give grain without the ploughman, but chooses instead to give it through him. The farmer, the magistrate, the soldier, and the parent are the “masks” (larvae Dei) behind which God hides to do his providential work — so that ordinary labor is not a distraction from God’s action but the very instrument of it. This is the positive complement to the priesthood of all believers: vocation is dignified not only because the worker serves his neighbour, but because God serves the neighbour through the worker.

Notes

  • The hinge from “vocation” as the monk’s word to vocation as everyone’s. Before Luther, vocatio had narrowed to the “call” into the religious life; the ploughman and the milkmaid had a living, but not a calling. Luther’s move — grounding every honest station in the priesthood of all believers and in God’s own working through it — is what universalized the dignity of work. He does not thereby demean the trades in the ancient manner; he raises them, making the labourer’s bench a consecrated office. Whether this recovery of a biblical dignity of labor also seeded the “Protestant work ethic” that Max Weber later tied to capitalism is a separate question, weighed in this timeline’s Arguments rather than here.
  • The word-study is the weak part — the doctrine is not. Luther grounded the extension partly on his German rendering of klēsis (“calling”) with Beruf, a word that already carried the everyday sense of a trade — but the New Testament klēsis denotes the call to salvation, not one’s occupation (Eph 4:1; 1 Cor 1:26; Rom 11:29), and even “abide in the calling wherein he was called” (1 Cor 7:20) concerns social conditions and contentment, not the divinity of a trade. So the universalizing of vocation is best defended as a theological development from the priesthood of all believers, the dignity of work, and providence — not as a plain reading of the Greek. The proof-text is over-read; the doctrine it serves stands on firmer, broader ground, and is rated sound on the argument page: Is “vocation” rightly extended from the monastery to every honest trade?
  • The “milkmaid” image. Luther’s doctrine is often summed up in the picture of God milking the cows through the vocation of the milkmaid — a vivid formulation popularized by Gustaf Wingren’s classic study Luther on Vocation (1957) as a distillation of the Exposition of Psalm 147, rather than a sentence Luther himself wrote in those words. The genuine passage is the “masks of God” text above, where the ploughman — not the milkmaid — is Luther’s own example.
  • Luther elsewhere on this wiki. He appears on the timeline of the liberal arts for his campaign to keep children in school against the profit-minded parent — the same conviction that learned service is a calling, not merely a living — and on predestination for The Bondage of the Will (1525). The doctrine of vocation also underwrites the argument, on the liberal-arts timeline, that the mechanical and trade arts are a dignified Christian vocation.
