Dorothy L. Sayers on vocation and the dignity of work (“Why Work?”)

Dates: c. 1942 (the address “Why Work?” delivered at Eastbourne, 23 April 1942; collected in Creed or Chaos? and Other Essays, 1949; Sayers 1893–1957, the novelist, playwright, medievalist, and Dante translator) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing during the Second World War, Sayers addresses vocation in an industrial and consumer economy. She reframes work not as a means to earn a living, nor even as a service to the community first of all, but as “the natural exercise and function of man — the creature who is made in the image of his Creator.” From that one doctrinal premise she draws three propositions: that work is the thing one lives to do, not merely the thing one does to live; that the secular vocation is sacred, so that the first demand religion makes on a carpenter is that he make good tables; and — the one she expected to sound “the most revolutionary of all” — that the worker’s first duty is to serve the work itself, not the profit it earns nor even the community it serves. Against a society that, she argued, had reduced labor to earning and consuming, she sets an account of daily work as the medium in which the worker offers himself to God.

Claims & evidence

Sayers’s premise: work as the exercise of the creature made in the Creator’s image

What I urged then was a thoroughgoing revolution in our whole attitude to work. I asked that it should be looked upon, not as a necessary drudgery to be undergone for the purpose of making money, but as a way of life in which the nature of man should find its proper exercise and delight and so fulfill itself to the glory of God. That it should, in fact, be thought of as a creative activity undertaken for the love of the work itself; and that man, made in God’s image, should make things, as God makes them, for the sake of doing well a thing that is well worth doing.

— “Why Work?” (1942).1 The whole address hangs on a single doctrinal proposition, which she states at its centre:

…namely, that work is the natural exercise and function of man – the creature who is made in the image of his Creator. You will find that any of them if given in effect everyday practice, is so revolutionary (as compared with the habits of thinking into which we have fallen), as to make all political revolutions look like conformity.

— “Why Work?” (1942).2 On her account, because the human maker images the divine Maker, making is the exercise of the imago Dei rather than a penalty of the fall, and the dignity of work is a nature it expresses rather than a reward it earns.

Sayers’s first proposition: work as “the thing one lives to do”

The first, stated quite briefly, is that work is not, primarily, a thing one does to live, but the thing one lives to do. It is, or it should be, the full expression of the worker’s faculties, the thing in which he finds spiritual, mental and bodily satisfaction, and the medium in which he offers himself to God.

— “Why Work?” (1942).3 Her example is the unpaid hobby: a man will pour loving labor into work that can never pay, because “his satisfaction comes, in the godlike manner, from looking upon what he has made and finding it very good. He is no longer bargaining with his work, but serving it.”4 Work becomes hateful, she argues, only when it is treated as a means to gain — “for then, instead of a friend, it becomes an enemy from whom tolls and contributions have to be extracted.” The same address criticizes a society that, she argued, had reduced labor to earning and consuming: “a society in which consumption has to be artificially stimulated in order to keep production going is a society founded on trash and waste, and such a society is a house built upon sand.”5

Sayers’s second proposition: the secular vocation as sacred

It is the business of the Church to recognize that the secular vocation, as such, is sacred. Christian people, and particularly perhaps the Christian clergy, must get it firmly into their heads that when a man or woman is called to a particular job of secular work, that is as true a vocation as though he or she were called to specifically religious work.

— “Why Work?” (1942).6 The Church’s great failure, she charges, has been to let work and religion become “separate departments,” approaching the intelligent carpenter only to keep him sober and bring him to church on Sundays. On her account the Church’s word should go to the work itself:

What the Church should be telling him is this: that the very first demand that his religion makes upon him is that he should make good tables…. No crooked table legs or ill-fitting drawers ever, I dare swear, came out of the carpenter’s shop at Nazareth. Nor, if they did, could anyone believe that they were made by the same hand that made Heaven and earth.

— “Why Work?” (1942).7 From this she draws the maxim that condenses her whole account of sacred labor — “The only Christian work is good work well done” — and its converse, that “work must be good work before it can call itself God’s work.”8 The carpenter of Nazareth is her central image: the incarnate God worked wood, and on her account worked it truly.

Sayers’s third proposition: serving the work

This brings me to my third proposition; and this may sound to you the most revolutionary of all. It is this: the worker’s first duty is to serve the work. The popular catchphrase of today is that it is everybody’s duty to serve the community, but there is a catch in it.

— “Why Work?” (1942).9 The catch, she says, is the order of the two great commandments: to put the community (the neighbour) before the work (done as unto God) is to put the second commandment before the first, and so to make man the measure of all things. Aiming directly at serving the community, she argues, actually falsifies the work — the playwright who writes to please the audience produces a bad play — so that paradoxically “the only way to serve the community is to forget the community and serve the work.”10 She closes by folding the three propositions into one charge to the Church, borrowing the Schoolmen’s distinction (via Jacques Maritain) between the end pursued by the workman and the end served by the work: “If work is to find its right place in the world, it is the duty of the Church to see to it that the work serves God, and that the worker serves the work.”11

Notes

  • Sayers and the doctrine of calling. Sayers was a lay Anglican writing four centuries after Luther. Like his doctrine of Beruf, her address treats every secular calling as a true vocation in which God is served in the work, not alongside it. Her target is a modern economy that, she argued, had reduced work to a cash return and a stimulus to consumption; her “house built upon sand” is aimed at that economy.
  • “Serve the work.” Sayers grounds her third proposition — that the worker’s first duty is to the work rather than to the community — in the priority of the first commandment over the second, arguing that the neighbour is best served by work done with integrity before God.
  • Sayers elsewhere on this wiki. She appears on the timeline of the liberal arts for The Lost Tools of Learning (1947), an essay on schooling.
