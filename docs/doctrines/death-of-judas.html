The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · The Bronze Sea: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

It is the one apostle whose death the New Testament records twice, and the two notices do not read alike. Matthew 27:3–10 has Judas repent, fling the thirty pieces of silver into the temple, and “went and hanged himself”; the priests, unwilling to put blood-money in the treasury, buy the potter’s field with it, “wherefore that field was called, The field of blood.” Acts 1:16–19, in Peter’s speech, has Judas himself “purchase a field with the reward of iniquity; and falling headlong, he burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out,” so that the field was called Akeldama, “the field of blood.” Three points of tension follow: the manner of death (a hanging, or a fall and bursting), who bought the field (the priests, or Judas), and why it bears its name (the priests’ blood-money, or Judas’s own spilled blood); a fourth and lighter one is sometimes added — Matthew’s remorseful Judas against Acts’s silence about any remorse. It is a textbook “Bible contradiction,” of the same family as the two slayers of Goliath — two canonical texts that seem to collide over one event.1

This page gathers the witnesses to the crux, earliest first — not the sources for Judas the man (those are set out on his biographical timeline) but the chain of readers who felt the difficulty and tried to reconcile it. The story runs one way: the divergence widens before it is smoothed. Papias, within a generation or two, supplies not a bridge but a third death; the fourth-century Latin church fuses the notices in the very wording of its text; the Greek commentators state the harmony as a mechanism — the rope broke, or the hanging failed — until the medieval Byzantines splice all three accounts into one continuous end; and modern criticism reopens the question, reading Matthew and Acts as independent traditions of a death whose manner was never remembered in common. Whether the notices truly describe one death, one purchase, and one name — and why Matthew credits the oracle to Jeremiah — is weighed adversarially under Arguments.

Timeline

Papias of Hierapolis (c. 110)

Papias, a hearer of the elders and the earliest post-apostolic voice, is the first to deepen the crux rather than settle it. He reports neither a hanging nor a simple fall but a monstrous swelling: Judas’s “body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.” A fuller form, transmitted by Theophylact, adds eyes swollen past a physician’s probing and a lingering death on a deserted estate. Papias keeps the “bowels gushed out” of Acts but supplies a cause the New Testament never gives — so that within living memory of the apostles the traitor’s end is already told three ways.2

The fourth-century harmonizers (c. 380–420)

By the end of the fourth century the notices were being told as one sequence, without a sense that anything needed smoothing. The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) run Matthew and Acts together in a single sentence — Judas “repented, and hanged himself, and burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out” — and Apollinaris of Laodicea, quoting Papias, offers a worked reconciliation: Judas “did not die by hanging, but lived on, having been cut down before he was suffocated.” The clearest witness is Augustine, whose Latin copy of Acts has already absorbed the hanging into the verse itself: “he bound his own neck, and cast down on his face, burst asunder in the midst” (collum sibi alligavit, et deiectus in faciem, disruptus est medius). Jerome’s Vulgate fixed the same fusion more briefly, et suspensus crepuit medius, “and being hanged he burst in the middle” — the harmony achieved not by argument but by transmission.3

Oecumenius (c. 550)

The Greek commentary on Acts carried under the name of Oecumenius states the reconciliation in its explicit, exegetical form — the first surviving statement of the harmony as a mechanism rather than a fused text. On the two accounts he writes: “if he now fell forward… there is no contradiction. For both happened: the cord by which the hanging occurred broke, and therefore he was carried forward onto the ground and had fallen.” Matthew reports the hanging, Acts the aftermath, and a snapped rope joins them. The Latin West kept pace: Bede’s Commentary on Acts (c. 716) takes the fused death for granted — “the noose’s knot strangled the throat from which the voice of betrayal had emerged” — and reads Judas perishing in mid-air “according to the example of Ahithophel and Absalom,” the very Old Testament template modern critics will fasten on.4

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1107)

The medieval Byzantine coda carries the reconciling instinct to completion. Theophylact joins the notices not by a snapped rope but by a failed hanging: “Judas did not die in the noose, but lived on even after, since he was taken down before he strangled to death.” To fill out the manner of the death itself he then reaches back past both Gospels, quoting “Papias, a disciple of John… in the fourth book” at length — the swelling, the sightless eyes, the deserted estate. Matthew’s hanging is retained but rendered non-fatal, Acts’ bursting is its sequel, and Papias’s swelling supplies the drawn-out death in between: three divergent notices spliced into one continuous biography of the traitor’s end.5

Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1118)

A near-contemporary of Theophylact gives the same reconciliation from the other end of the crux. Commenting on Matthew 27, where there is no bursting to explain, Euthymius Zigabenus reaches across to Acts unprompted: the noose was of “despair,” but it did not kill — “being recognized by some he was taken down, that he should not be choked,” and only afterward, having pitched forward and swelled, “he burst asunder and was split in the middle, and all his bowels gushed out, as the book of the Acts says.” It is the failed hanging of Theophylact exactly, but more restrained: Euthymius keeps only the swelling and bursting, credits them to Acts itself, and names no source but Chrysostom — the harmony without Papias’s marvel. He even carries over Acts’ own participle (prēnēs, “prostrate, headlong”) and, on the field, corrects “some” who put its purchase after the death, making it one transaction on Chrysostom’s authority.6

Modern scholarship

Modern criticism reopens what the harmonizers closed. Kirsopp Lake’s 1933 study reads the three accounts as independent developments shaped by scriptural precedent — Matthew’s hanging from Ahithophel, Acts’ bursting from Wisdom of Solomon 4:19 (whose two operative words recur in Acts 1:18), Papias’s swelling from the death of Antiochus in 2 Maccabees 9 — concluding that “there was a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence.” C. K. Barrett holds flatly that “no harmonization of these accounts is possible,” and Ernst Haenchen, Hans Conzelmann, Richard Pervo, Jesse Robertson (a monograph on the question), and Arie Zwiep decline to combine them — Zwiep warning that conflating the two “into a third, harmonized version” yields a narrative “neither story was ever meant” to give. Against them Darrell Bock, Craig Keener, and I. Howard Marshall judge the notices reconcilable, Bock answering Barrett that “things are not so clear” and that the differing details are “reconcilable.” The one point scarcely disputed is the historical core both texts and even Bart Ehrman grant: Judas betrayed Jesus, the betrayal-money bought a field known in Jerusalem as the field of blood, and he died violently and soon.7

Arguments

The contested sub-claims of the crux — each cutting across the timeline rather than sitting at one date — are weighed adversarially on their own pages. Four are hosted with Judas’s biographical timeline, where three are read together as “the accounts of Judas’s end” — the manner of death, the field, and Matthew’s remorse against Acts’s silence — the fourth being the Jeremiah citation; two more — on the source-value of Papias’s swelling account, and on whether the field of blood is a real, locatable site whose archaeology fits — are weighed here. The settled floor beneath them all is the historical core just named; what is genuinely disputed is whether the divergent details can be read as one event — and what the earliest post-canonical notice is actually worth.

Do Matthew’s hanging and Acts’ bursting describe one death? (assessment: plausible)

Matthew’s hanging and Acts’ bursting: one death, told twice — the reading that the two notices describe a single death, Matthew giving the means and Acts the state the body was found in. Its strong form does not rest on the popular apologetic props (a decomposing body, a cliff above the Hinnom): Acts’ prēnēs genomenos means “having become prostrate,” not “falling headlong,” and a hanged body in Jerusalem at Passover would be taken down within the day. The reading is plausible — ancient rather than a modern patch, and defended by a major commentator on Acts — but short of settled: Acts’ two operative words are the two words of Wisdom 4:19, the ruin of a traitor is a well-worn literary pattern, Papias already supplies a third version, and most who work closely on Acts read the notices as independent.8

Is Papias’s swollen Judas an independent early tradition? (assessment: weak)

Papias’s swollen Judas: an independent early tradition? — whether the earliest post-canonical notice, the grotesque swelling-and-crushing preserved by Papias, is a third memory of real source-value or a later elaboration of Acts. It is genuinely early (c. 110) and genuinely distinct from both Gospels — which is worth something: it shows the manner of the traitor’s death was not fixed in common memory a generation after Acts. But as an independent tradition of what actually became of Judas it is weak: its one verbatim contact with the New Testament is the “bowels gushed out” of Acts 1:18 (pointing to elaboration, not a separate memory), its whole shape is the stock grotesque death of the impious traitor (Antiochus, Herod), and it reaches us only through late, still-growing catena-quotation from a writer Eusebius thought credulous about marvels. Not a verdict reached by ruling the marvellous out in advance, but one on attestation, wording, and genre.9

Did the priests and Judas buy one field, bearing one name? (assessment: plausible)

Matthew’s priests and Acts’ Judas: one purchase, one name — the reading that one plot, one transaction, and one name lie behind both notices. Acts does not say Judas bought the field (ektēsato is “acquired, got”), the priests buying it with his money; and where Matthew gives a cause for the name, Acts gives only a result, leaving the reason unstated. Plausible, and read exactly so by the earliest commentators on Acts, but short of settled: Acts on its own links blood to that ground only through Judas’s death, and most critical scholarship takes the two verses as independent popular etymologies of a genuine Jerusalem place-name.10

Does Acts’s silence about Judas’s remorse conflict with Matthew? (assessment: sound)

Matthew’s repentant Judas and Acts’s silence — the fourth and lightest divergence: Matthew has Judas “repent” and confess “I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood,” while Acts’s notice says nothing of remorse and frames him only as one who “by transgression fell.” Taken as a charge of contradiction it fails outright, because Acts does not deny the remorse — it does not raise the subject. Peter’s speech compresses Judas’s whole end into one clause to explain why the twelfth apostolic seat must be refilled; its silence about his state of mind establishes nothing, and its bleak wording is no rival portrait, since in Matthew it is Judas himself who confesses the sin and hangs himself. No ancient harmonizer even felt the difficulty. The reconciling reading is sound — though “sound” means only that the texts do not collide, not that Matthew’s remorse scene is thereby proved history against the reading that takes it for his own composition.11

Is the field of blood a real, locatable site, and does the archaeology fit? (assessment: plausible)

Aceldama on the ground — setting aside whether the two texts agree, is there a real place answering to them, and does the ground match? The identification is unusually strong: one strip on the southern slope of the Hinnom below Mount Zion has been called Aceldama from Eusebius and Jerome through the Piacenza pilgrim (c. 570, who finds it a burial ground for foreigners) and the medieval charnel house to the present, and the 1989 excavation of three Second Temple rock-cut tombs there confirms it was a genuine cemetery — matching Matthew’s “to bury strangers in.” What the ground does not confirm are the two signature descriptors: no potter’s kiln or clay-works has been dug (the “potter’s field” rests on the name and the Hinnom’s clay, not on excavation), and a cemetery is neutral between “field of blood” and the minority reading “field of sleep” (Klostermann’s Aramaic dmk). So the site is real and its burial use well-grounded, but the “potter’s” and “blood” specifics stay open: plausible.12

Why does Matthew credit the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet”? (assessment: sound)

Why Matthew names Jeremiah — the third difficulty in Matthew’s scene: the quoted words come mainly from Zechariah 11, yet Matthew credits “Jeremy.” Best read as a deliberate composite — Zechariah’s words wrapped in Jeremiah’s imagery (potter, purchased field, a place renamed for innocent blood) and named for the senior prophet. This is sound: coherent, consonant with Matthew’s demonstrable habit with fulfilment citations, and the mainstream explanation, so the bare charge of a blunder is not the reasonable verdict; what remains underdetermined is only why Jeremiah in particular, where several sub-explanations compete.13
