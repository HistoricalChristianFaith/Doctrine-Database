The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1)

Judas is the one apostle whose death the New Testament records twice, and the two notices do not read alike. Matthew 27:3–10 has Judas repent, fling the thirty pieces of silver into the temple, and “went and hanged himself”; the priests, unwilling to put blood-money in the treasury, buy the potter’s field with it, “wherefore that field was called, The field of blood.” Acts 1:16–19, in Peter’s speech, has Judas himself “purchase a field with the reward of iniquity; and falling headlong, he burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out,” so that the field was called Akeldama, “the field of blood.” Three differences between the notices are discussed: the manner of death (a hanging, or a fall and bursting), who bought the field (the priests, or Judas), and why it bears its name (the priests’ blood-money, or Judas’s own spilled blood). Matthew’s Judas also repents; Acts records no remorse. The passage belongs with other cases in which two canonical texts seem to report one event differently, such as the two slayers of Goliath.1

This page gathers the readers of the two notices, earliest first — not the sources for Judas the man (those are set out on his biographical timeline) but those who wrote about how the notices relate. Papias (c. 110) gives a third account of the death, a swelling and a crushing; Latin texts of Acts known to Augustine and Jerome carry the hanging inside the Acts verse; Greek commentators explain the two notices by a sequence of events — the rope broke (Oecumenius), or the hanging failed (Theophylact, Euthymius), Theophylact adding Papias’s account to the sequence; and modern commentators divide between those who read Matthew and Acts as separate traditions that cannot be combined and those who read them as reconcilable. How Papias’s account has been read is set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

Papias of Hierapolis (c. 110)

Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, who reported gathering the sayings of those who had heard the apostles’ disciples, gives the earliest extant post-canonical account of Judas’s death. He reports neither a hanging nor a simple fall but a monstrous swelling: Judas’s “body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.” A fuller form, transmitted by Theophylact, adds eyes swollen past a physician’s probing and a lingering death on a deserted estate. Papias shares the “bowels gushed out” of Acts but gives a cause the New Testament does not, so that by c. 110 the end of Judas is extant in three forms.2

The fourth-century witnesses (c. 380–420)

By the end of the fourth century the notices were being told as one sequence. The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) run Matthew and Acts together in a single sentence — Judas “repented, and hanged himself, and burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out” — and Apollinaris of Laodicea, quoting Papias, offers a worked reconciliation: Judas “did not die by hanging, but lived on, having been cut down before he was suffocated.” Augustine quotes a Latin copy of Acts that carries the hanging inside the verse itself: “he bound his own neck, and cast down on his face, burst asunder in the midst” (collum sibi alligavit, et deiectus in faciem, disruptus est medius). Jerome’s Vulgate reads, more briefly, et suspensus crepuit medius, “and being hanged he burst in the middle”; Kirsopp Lake (1933) described this Old Latin reading as a harmonizing gloss that the Vulgate then absorbed.3

Oecumenius (c. 550; late tenth century per Moss)

The Greek commentary on Acts carried under the name of Oecumenius states the reconciliation as an exegetical argument, with a broken rope as its mechanism. On the two accounts he writes: “if he now fell forward… there is no contradiction. For both happened: the cord by which the hanging occurred broke, and therefore he was carried forward onto the ground and had fallen.” On this account Matthew reports the hanging, Acts the aftermath, and a broken rope joins them. In the Latin West, Bede’s Commentary on Acts (c. 716) reads the death as one event — “the noose’s knot strangled the throat from which the voice of betrayal had emerged” — and reads Judas perishing in mid-air “according to the example of Ahithophel and Absalom.”4

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1107)

Theophylact joins the notices not by a broken rope but by a hanging that did not kill: “Judas did not die in the noose, but lived on even after, since he was taken down before he strangled to death.” For the manner of the death itself he quotes “Papias, a disciple of John… in the fourth book” at length — the swelling, the sightless eyes, the deserted estate. In his account Matthew’s hanging is not fatal, Acts’s bursting follows it, and Papias’s swelling supplies the drawn-out death in between, so that the three notices are told as one sequence.5

Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1118)

A near-contemporary of Theophylact gives the same reconciliation from the Matthew side. Commenting on Matthew 27, where there is no bursting to explain, Euthymius Zigabenus brings in Acts: the noose was of “despair,” but it did not kill — “being recognized by some he was taken down, that he should not be choked,” and only afterward, having pitched forward and swelled, “he burst asunder and was split in the middle, and all his bowels gushed out, as the book of the Acts says.” It is Theophylact’s failed hanging, but Euthymius keeps only the swelling and bursting, credits them to Acts itself, and names no source but Chrysostom; he does not cite Papias. He uses Acts’s own participle (prēnēs, “prostrate, headlong”) and, on the field, rejects the view of “some” who put its purchase after the death, making it one transaction on Chrysostom’s authority.6

Modern scholarship

Modern commentators divide over whether the notices can be combined; their views are set out on the modern-scholarship page. Kirsopp Lake’s 1933 study reads the three accounts as developments of one loose traitor-death tradition, without direct literary dependence, each shaped by scriptural precedent — Matthew’s hanging from Ahithophel, Acts’ bursting from Wisdom of Solomon 4:19 (whose two operative words recur in Acts 1:18), Papias’s swelling from the death of Antiochus in 2 Maccabees 9 — concluding that “there was a loose tradition of the way in which the death of a traitor ought to correspond to his offence.” C. K. Barrett holds that “no harmonization of these accounts is possible,” and Ernst Haenchen, Hans Conzelmann, Richard Pervo, Jesse Robertson (a monograph on the question), and Arie Zwiep decline to combine them — Zwiep warning that conflating the two “into a third, harmonized version” yields a narrative “neither story was ever meant” to give. Darrell Bock, Craig Keener, and I. Howard Marshall read the notices as reconcilable, Bock answering Barrett that “things are not so clear” and that the differing details are “reconcilable.” Bart Ehrman, who reads the two death-notices as at odds, takes Akeldama to be a pre-Christian Jerusalem place-name that both traditions explain.7
