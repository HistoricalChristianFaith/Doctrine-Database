Did 1 Samuel 17:4 first give Goliath's height as "four cubits and a span" or "six cubits and a span"?

Disputed reading · on How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · witnesses: 4QSama and the Masoretic codices

The passage

"And there went out a champion out of the camp of the Philistines, named Goliath, of Gath, whose height was six cubits and a span."

— 1 Samuel 17:4, Masoretic Text (Hebrew shesh ammot wa-zaret), Jewish Publication Society translation, source.1

"And there went forth a mighty man out of the army of the Philistines, Goliath, by name, out of Geth, his height was four cubits and a span."

— The Septuagint, 1 Kings (1 Kingdoms) 17:4, trans. Sir Lancelot C. L. Brenton, source.2 The Qumran Samuel scroll 4QSama (first century BC) and Josephus (Antiquities 6.171, c. 93) also give "four cubits and a span."3 At about 18 inches to the cubit and 9 to the span, the two figures come to about 9′9″ and 6′9″.

The question

"Four cubits and a span" stands in 4QSama, the Greek codices Vaticanus and Alexandrinus, the Lucianic Greek and Josephus; "six cubits and a span" stands in the Masoretic codices, Symmachus, Origen's fifth column, the Vulgate, the Peshitta and the Targum.4 What is disputed is which figure stood in the earliest recoverable text of Samuel, how the other arose, and whether the two figures measure different heights at all; English Bibles have printed both (the ESV "six" with a note giving "four"; the Catholic New American Bible of 1970 "six and a half feet," its revised edition "six cubits and a span" with a note giving "four").5

"Four" is the earlier reading; "six" arose by a copying slip

In brief: the oldest Hebrew copy and the Old Greek preserve the original "four," and the Masoretic "six" came in when a copyist's eye caught the "six hundred" of verse 7.

Held by: P. Kyle McCarter, I Samuel (Anchor Bible, 1980) ⚠ unverified; J. Daniel Hays (Ouachita Baptist University), JETS (2005, 2007); the New American Bible (Catholic, 1970), on the "four" reading only.6

Hays treats the height variant by text-critical method, apart from the long and short forms of 1 Samuel 17–18, since the verse is in both. 4QSama, from the middle of the first century BC, is the oldest extant Hebrew manuscript of the verse. Alexandrinus carries the longer Masoretic-type form of chapters 17–18 yet keeps "four," as does the Lucianic recension, and Eugene Ulrich concluded that Josephus's bible for Samuel "was in Greek, it was a slightly revised form of OG [Old Greek], and it was intimately affiliated with the 4QSama tradition." Hays concludes that "four" was "very likely the original reading in the Old Greek." No Hebrew copy earlier than the Aleppo Codex, which he dates to AD 935, reads "six"; Hays grants that Symmachus (c. AD 200) places it in the proto-Masoretic text by that date, followed by Origen's fifth column and the Vulgate.7

For the origin of "six," Hays points to parablepsis: "The Hebrew word in verse 4 for cubit… is very similar to the word in verse 7 for hundred," so "the scribe's eye dropped to verse 7 and picked up the six from 'six hundred.'" He finds little evidence that a Jewish scribe before 200 BC would have shrunk Goliath to make the account believable, and cites Ulrich and Ralph Klein for the many accidental omissions and doublings in the Masoretic text of Samuel.8 In Samuel–Kings, he adds, the Greek gives the same cubit figure as the Hebrew in all but a few of the 49 occurrences of "cubit," and its few differences run both ways.9 Hays states that his case is not made to render the story believable: "I do not have any trouble accepting a 9′9″ Goliath, if that is what the original text stated." He asks that translations print "four cubits and a span," "relegating the variant reading of 'six cubits and a span' to the notes and/or the margins."9,10

Hays reads the narrative as a contrast between David and Saul. Saul stood "from his shoulders and upward… higher than any of the people" (1 Samuel 9:2) and had armor to match Goliath's (17:38–39), so a Goliath of about 6′9″ was Saul's to fight; Saul's own warning to David speaks of Goliath's training, not his height (17:33). In an Israelite army of men averaging some five feet to five feet three, such a warrior was "still formidable indeed."11

"Six" is the original reading; "four" is a later reduction

In brief: the Hebrew "six" is the original, and a later copyist shortened Goliath, deliberately, to bring him nearer to Saul's size or to make the story easier to believe.

Held by: William D. Barrick (The Master's Seminary), "Goliath's Height" (2007); Peter J. Gurry and John D. Meade, "How Tall Was Goliath? A Textual Dilemma" (Crossway, 2022), granting that "four" has "the earlier and perhaps better external evidence"; Peter Goeman (Shepherd's Theological Seminary), "How Tall Was Goliath?" (2026).12

Gurry and Meade note that the versions carrying "six" — "the Three, the Vulgate, the Peshitta, and the Targum" — "usually agree with the MT." The eye-skip that would turn "four" into "six" "requires the scribe's eye to skip farther down the column than normal for this kind of mistake." A deliberate change explains the variant better, and runs more easily from six to four: "A six-nine Goliath (still tall by ancient standards) probably matched Saul's own physical stature," so a scribe might have shortened Goliath to underscore "Saul's lack of courage." They conclude that "the nine-nine Goliath is probably more likely original because a later scribe probably found a contextual reason (1 Sam. 9:2) to shorten Goliath to six nine."13

Barrick holds that the shorter height in the Qumran scroll "appears to be a deliberate alteration, not the reflection of an original reading nor even a scribal slip of the pen," and that the Qumran copyists "were not always accurate or careful in the way they copied the biblical texts," pointing to the Great Isaiah Scroll's omitted words reinserted by another hand.14 Goeman reports that scholars regard 4QSama as "a unique scroll that provides unique challenges," agreeing now with the Greek and now with the Masoretic Text and sometimes with neither, and he follows Alexander Rofé of the Hebrew University, who proposed retitling it Midrash Sefer Shemuel "since the scroll functions more like a commentary than a strict preservation of the biblical text." He judges it "much more likely that a scribe changed the reading from six cubits to four cubits so that the story would be more believable, or that Saul would be viewed as more of a coward."15

Both read the height from the armor. Barrick reckons the coat at 5,000 shekels of bronze (about 126 pounds) and the spearhead at 600 shekels of iron (about 15 pounds), and Goeman finds that "Given the significant weight of Goliath's equipment, it seems more consistent to see Goliath as a 9-foot giant." Saul, head and shoulders above other men, "was probably close to 6.5 feet in height himself," Barrick writes, and he asks why Saul, a man who did not shrink from battle, would not take the field against a Goliath of his own size: "A height of 9.5 feet makes a lot more sense in the overall context than 6.5 feet."14,15

Both figures measure one man, in different cubits

In brief: the Hebrew "six" is original and counts in common cubits; the Greek "four" converts the same height into the longer Egyptian royal cubit, so the two readings do not conflict.

Held by: Clyde E. Billington (historian, Northwestern College, St Paul), "Goliath and the Exodus Giants: How Tall Were They?" JETS (2007).16

Billington argues that "while the mt and the lxx/dss appear to be in conflict with one another, they actually are not," that both readings put Goliath "about 8 feet tall," and that "The 6 cubits and a span given for the height of Goliath in the Hebrew mt is the original reading; it is not a textual error"; he asks translators to "retain the 6-cubits reading." The cubit, the length from elbow to fingertip, varied with the person measuring, and an 18-inch cubit fits a man of about 5′8″. Measured by an Israelite of five feet to five feet three, such as David, with a cubit of some 16½ inches or spans of 7½–8 inches, six cubits and a span comes to roughly 8′2″–8′11″. Scripture knows more than one cubit: Deuteronomy 3:11 measures Og's bed "by ordinary cubit," and Ezekiel 40:5 a rod of cubits "a cubit and a handbreadth." Egypt's standardized royal cubit was 20.65 inches. A Jewish translator at Alexandria, rendering the six common cubits into royal cubits, had to choose between four and five; at four royal cubits and a span Goliath is "slightly over 7 feet 8 inches." Codex Venetus's "five cubits and a span" may record another translator's choice, and 4QSama either was corrected from the Greek or was a copy made "for or by a Jew from Alexandria." The surrounding Scripture fits a large man: the Egyptian of 1 Chronicles 11:23 was five cubits tall, and Goliath's armor and weapons, "well over 160 lbs.," weighed about three times a classical hoplite's.17
