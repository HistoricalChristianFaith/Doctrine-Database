Josephus on How Tall Was Goliath?

Dates: c. 93 (the Antiquities of the Jews) · Wikipedia
Summary: A first-century witness to the shorter number. Retelling the duel of 1 Samuel 17, Josephus gives the champion's height as four cubits and a span (about 6 feet 9 inches), the reading of the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Samuel scroll, against the Masoretic "six cubits and a span" (about 9 feet 9 inches). Eugene Ulrich concluded that the bible Josephus used for Samuel was a Greek text closely affiliated with the 4QSama tradition.

Claims & evidence

Goliath is "four cubits and a span" in tallness

"a man of vast bulk, for he was of four cubits and a span in tallness, and had about him weapons suitable to the largeness of his body"

— Antiquities of the Jews 6.171, trans. William Whiston, source. The passage sits in Josephus's retelling of the challenge from "a man out of the camp of the Philistines, whose name was Goliath, of the city of Gath," who steps out between the two armies. His figure — four, not six, cubits and a span — agrees with the Old Greek and the Qumran scroll of Samuel.

Reception and debate

  • His place among the witnesses. Josephus wrote at the end of the first century, before the oldest surviving Septuagint codices. J. Daniel Hays counts him as a witness to "four" alongside the Dead Sea Samuel scroll (4QSama) and the Septuagint, noting that "Josephus obviously used a Greek OT" resembling the Lucianic recension, which in 1 Samuel "regularly agrees with the tradition represented by 4QSama" ("Reconsidering the Height of Goliath," JETS 48/4 (2005), 704).
  • His biblical text for Samuel. Eugene Ulrich's study The Qumran Text of Samuel and Josephus (Harvard Semitic Monographs, 1978) concluded: "His bible was in Greek, it was a slightly revised form of OG [Old Greek], and it was intimately affiliated with the 4QSama tradition" (259, as quoted by Hays, 704 n. 6).
  • The "six" reading. In Hebrew, the "six cubits and a span" is extant from the Masoretic codices of the tenth century; Hays holds that Symmachus's Greek (c. AD 200) shows it in the proto-Masoretic text by that date (705–706). Whether "four" or "six" is the earlier reading is set out on the disputed-reading page.
