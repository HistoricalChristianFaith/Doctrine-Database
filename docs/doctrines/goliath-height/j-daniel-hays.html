J. Daniel Hays on How Tall Was Goliath?

Dates: 2005 (“Reconsidering the Height of Goliath,” Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society 48/4) · JETS 48/4 (2005): 701–714
Summary: Hays, professor of Old Testament at Ouachita Baptist University, argues that the original reading of 1 Samuel 17:4 is “four cubits and a span” (about 6′9″), and that the Masoretic “six cubits and a span” (about 9′9″) is a later scribal error. He argues the older witnesses — 4QSama (the oldest Hebrew), the Septuagint (Vaticanus, Alexandrinus, and the Lucianic recension), and Josephus — preserve “four,” while “six” is secondary. The shorter Goliath, he holds, still towered over Israelite men of about 5′0″–5′3″.

Claims & evidence

“Four cubits and a span” is the original reading

Hays marshals the manuscript evidence for the shorter figure: 4QSama, the oldest surviving Hebrew text of Samuel (middle of the first century BC), reads “four cubits and a span”; so do the major Greek witnesses — Codex Vaticanus (the oldest complete Greek Bible), Codex Alexandrinus, and the Lucianic recension — and so does the first-century historian Josephus. The Masoretic “six cubits and a span,” by contrast, is attested only in medieval Hebrew codices; the earliest, the Aleppo Codex, he dates to AD 935. Hays grants that Symmachus (c. AD 200) places “six” in the proto-Masoretic text by that date, followed by Origen’s fifth column and the Vulgate, and explains it as parablepsis: a copyist’s eye dropping from “cubit” in verse 4 to the similar word “hundred” in verse 7 and picking up the “six” of “six hundred.” He concludes that “four cubits and a span” is “the most likely original reading.” “Reconsidering the Height of Goliath,” JETS 48/4 (2005): 701–714 (also at Galaxie).

The narrative read with a Goliath of ~6′9″

Hays holds that a shorter Goliath still fits the narrative. Drawing on the work of Victor Matthews and on skeletal evidence such as the crucified man from Giv’at ha-Mivtar, he puts the average Israelite male of David’s time at “somewhere between 5′0″ and 5′3″, but certainly no taller than 5′6″” — so a man of nearly 6′9″ would still have loomed a full head or more above the ranks he defied. Hays adds a narrative point: 1 Samuel 9:2 singles out Saul as the exceptionally tall Israelite, head and shoulders above his people — precisely the man who, by his own stature, should have gone out to meet the Philistine champion, and did not. Saul’s warning to David, he notes, speaks of Goliath’s training, not his height (17:33). “Reconsidering the Height of Goliath,” JETS 48/4 (2005): ~710–713.

Scholars cited by Hays, and the response

Hays cites P. Kyle McCarter’s Anchor Bible I Samuel (1980) among scholars who treat “six cubits” as probably a copyist’s mistake, Emanuel Tov’s handbook for parablepsis, and Eugene Ulrich on the text of Samuel used by Josephus and on the copying errors of the Masoretic text of Samuel (704–707). Other scholars have answered him. Clyde E. Billington argued that the Greek “four” reflects a conversion of the height into Egyptian royal cubits (longer than the common cubit), which would put Goliath back near eight feet, and defended the Masoretic “six.” Clyde E. Billington, “Goliath and the Exodus Giants,” JETS 50/3 (2007): 489–508. William D. Barrick (2007) defended the received Hebrew text, holding the Qumran reading a deliberate alteration. William D. Barrick, “Goliath’s Height”. Hays answered Billington in the same issue, granting that a difference of cubits is “at least plausible” as an explanation of the variants and rejecting the conversion to the Egyptian royal cubit. J. Daniel Hays, “The Height of Goliath: A Response to Clyde Billington,” JETS 50/3 (2007): 509–516 (the 2005 article is indexed at Galaxie). Peter J. Gurry and John D. Meade (2022) judge “six” “probably more likely original,” granting that “four” has “the earlier and perhaps better external evidence.” “How Tall Was Goliath? A Textual Dilemma,” Crossway.

Reception and debate

  • The disputed reading. Whether “four” or “six” is the original reading is set out on the disputed-reading page. Hays’s conclusion follows the witnesses — 4QSama, the Septuagint, and Josephus — against the Masoretic codices.
  • The positions. Hays argues from the manuscript witnesses and the narrative; Billington from the size of the cubit and the armor; Barrick from the armor and the narrative, and from his view of the Qumran copyists; Gurry and Meade from the likelier direction of a deliberate scribal change.
  • Provenance. Quotations of measurements here follow Hays’s own figures in the JETS article; the cubit is reckoned at about 18 inches and the span at about 9 inches, yielding the 6′9″ / 9′9″ contrast he draws.
