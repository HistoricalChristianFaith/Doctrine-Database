The Christian Theology of Friendship

Christianity did not invent the philosophy of friendship — it inherited one. Long before the gospel, the classical world had thought friendship (philia, amicitia) through to a fine point: for Aristotle it was a species of virtue, or could not exist without it, and its highest form belonged only to the good, who love the friend for his own sake and become, in the old phrase, “a single soul in two bodies”; for Cicero, writing his Laelius de Amicitia as Rome's republic died, true friendship could exist only among the good and was the one thing whose usefulness no one doubted and whose loss no wealth could repair. This is the material the church received. The belief that develops on this page is not friendship as such but the Christianization of that inheritance — what happens to the pagan ideal of the two virtuous souls when a third term is introduced: Christ, and through him God, as a party to the friendship rather than its mere occasion.

The transformation runs along several seams. The gospel makes friendship a name for the highest love — “Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends” (John 15:13) — and, astonishingly, a name for the relation God offers the creature: “I have called you friends” (John 15:15); Abraham is “the friend of God” (James 2:23). Where the classical ideal reserved perfect friendship for the few good men and made it fragile before betrayal, the Christian reworking has to reckon with the command to love the enemy, the universality of charity, and the danger that a fierce particular attachment competes with the love owed to all and to God. So the fathers both prize friendship and watch it warily; the monastic writers ask whether a monk who has renounced the world may keep a friend; and the question is handed to the modern age — most memorably by C. S. Lewis — as one of the four loves, the one the modern world, unlike the ancient, has half forgotten how to esteem.

The timeline below traces that arc across time, earliest first: from the classical bookends the church was reading (Aristotle, Cicero), through the patristic Christianizers of amicitia (Ambrose, who rewrote Cicero's De Officiis; the Cappadocian friendship of Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus; Augustine, who buried a friend and grieved that he had loved a mortal as if he would not die; John Cassian, who asked what makes a friendship last), to the great medieval synthesis of Aelred of Rievaulx — “God is friendship” — and on to the modern re-examination. It is a development, not a timeless essay: watch the classical ideal enter the church and come out changed.
