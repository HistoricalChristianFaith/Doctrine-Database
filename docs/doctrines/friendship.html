The Christian Theology of Friendship

Christianity did not invent the philosophy of friendship — it inherited one. Long before the gospel, the classical world had thought friendship (philia, amicitia) through to a fine point: for Aristotle it was a species of virtue, or could not exist without it, and its highest form belonged only to the good, who love the friend for his own sake and become, in the old phrase, “a single soul in two bodies”; for Cicero, writing his Laelius de Amicitia as Rome's republic died, true friendship could exist only among the good and was the one thing whose usefulness no one doubted and whose loss no wealth could repair. This is the material the church received. The belief that develops on this page is not friendship as such but the Christianization of that inheritance — what happens to the pagan ideal of the two virtuous souls when a third term is introduced: Christ, and through him God, as a party to the friendship rather than its mere occasion.

The transformation runs along several seams. The gospel makes friendship a name for the highest love — “Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends” (John 15:13) — and, astonishingly, a name for the relation God offers the creature: “I have called you friends” (John 15:15); Abraham is “the friend of God” (James 2:23). Where the classical ideal reserved perfect friendship for the few good men and made it fragile before betrayal, the Christian reworking has to reckon with the command to love the enemy, the universality of charity, and the danger that a fierce particular attachment competes with the love owed to all and to God. So the fathers both prize friendship and watch it warily; the monastic writers ask whether a monk who has renounced the world may keep a friend; and the question is handed to the modern age — most memorably by C. S. Lewis — as one of the four loves, the one the modern world, unlike the ancient, has half forgotten how to esteem.

The timeline below traces that arc across time, earliest first: from the classical bookends the church was reading (Aristotle, Cicero), through the patristic Christianizers of amicitia (Ambrose, who rewrote Cicero's De Officiis; the Cappadocian friendship of Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus; Augustine, who buried a friend and grieved that he had loved a mortal as if he would not die; John Cassian, who asked what makes a friendship last), to the great medieval synthesis of Aelred of Rievaulx — “God is friendship” — and on to the modern re-examination. It is a development, not a timeless essay: watch the classical ideal enter the church and come out changed.

The biblical foundation

Before the fathers could Christianize amicitia they had a Scripture to read, and it gave them more than a stray proverb: a paradigm friendship told at length, a Lord who renames his followers “friends,” a patriarch styled “the friend of God,” a body of wisdom on choosing and keeping friends, and one hard warning that not every friendship is safe to have. This is the layer every witness below builds on.

David and Jonathan — the covenant of two souls

The Old Testament’s set-piece of friendship is the bond of David and Jonathan. At their first meeting “the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul,” and the two “made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul.”1 That covenant is renewed as Saul turns murderous — Jonathan risks his father’s spear to warn David and later seeks him out to “strengthen his hand in God” — and it closes in David’s lament over the fallen Jonathan: “very pleasant hast thou been unto me: thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women.”2 The fathers took this as Scripture’s own lesson in friendship rather than a mere tale. John Chrysostom opens a discourse, “I will now cite you from the Scriptures a wonderful instance of friendship,” and reads the whole arc — Jonathan feeling no envy though the kingdom was passing to David, sparing not his father for his friend’s sake, and David repaying the kindness to Jonathan’s children — as the pattern “I would wish all to entertain both towards the living and the dead.”3 Ambrose, reworking Cicero for the church, likewise holds David up as one who “truly loved his friends, so he thought that he was loved as much in return,” and prizes the friendship “which maintains virtue” above wealth, honour, or power.4

“I have called you friends” — Christ and the disciples

The decisive New Testament turn is Jesus’ word in the upper room. He first makes friendship the name of the highest love — “Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends”5 — and then, astonishingly, offers that name to the creature: “Henceforth I call you not servants… but I have called you friends; for all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto you.”6 The fathers seized on both halves. Ambrose reads the verse as the charter of Christian friendship itself: “God himself made us friends instead of servants… He gave us a pattern of friendship to follow,” the mark of which is that “a friend, if he is a true one, hides nothing,” as the Lord “poured forth the mysteries of his Father.”7 Augustine dwells on the condescension — “Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you… What great condescension!”8 — and Chrysostom on the promotion: the disciples are raised from service to friendship.9

“The friend of God” — Abraham

Friendship is even predicated of the relation between God and a man. Abraham is called “the seed of Abraham my friend” and, in the New Testament, “he was called the Friend of God.”10 This title, already old, is one of the earliest things Christian writing does with the theme: Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth in the first century, notes that “Abraham, styled ‘the friend,’ was found faithful, inasmuch as he rendered obedience to the words of God,” grounding the friendship in obedient faith.11 Cyril of Jerusalem presses the same point — Abraham “was not called a friend of God until he believed”12 — so that friendship with God, not merely between men, enters the tradition from the start.

The wisdom of friendship — and its warning

Around these exemplars the wisdom books supply the working maxims the fathers quote constantly: “A friend loveth at all times”; there is “a friend that sticketh closer than a brother”; “faithful are the wounds of a friend”; “iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend.”13 Ecclesiastes gives the case against solitude — “Two are better than one… for if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth” — which Jerome and later the monastic writers turn into an argument that a person needs a companion.14 Sirach adds the warmest ancient praise of the thing: “A faithful friend is a strong defence… A faithful friend is the medicine of life,” while warning at length to test a friend before trusting him.15 The “faithful wounds” line in particular becomes a fathers’ commonplace for honest rebuke: Ambrose — “rebukes are good, and often better than a silent friendship” — and Augustine, who prefers “love mingled with severity” to “deceit with indulgence.”16

Yet the same Scriptures set a hard limit. James warns that “the friendship of the world is enmity with God: whosoever therefore will be a friend of the world is the enemy of God” — a text Bede reads as branding “adulterers those who… turn from the love of heavenly wisdom to the embrace of worldly friendship.”17 So the biblical layer hands the fathers not only a warrant to prize friendship but the very tension the timeline below works out: friendship is among the highest goods, and a friendship wrongly placed is among the gravest dangers.
