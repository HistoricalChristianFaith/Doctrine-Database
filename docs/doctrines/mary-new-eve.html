Mary as the New Eve

From the middle of the second century the fathers read the Annunciation against the fall: as Christ is the second Adam who undoes the first Adam’s fall (Romans 5; 1 Corinthians 15), so Mary is the second Eve whose obedient “yes” — “Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word” (Luke 1:38) — answers and reverses the disobedient “no” of the first Eve. The two women are matched term for term: each a virgin, each addressed by a spirit’s word, each acting by belief or unbelief. Eve believed the serpent and bore disobedience and death; Mary believed the angel and bore the author of life. Whether the parallel, set within Christ’s recapitulation of the fall, also assigns Mary an active part of her own in its reversal is disputed between interpreters; see Disputed readings. The typology is also cited in later teaching on the Theotokos, the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption, traced on those timelines.

This timeline records the reading from Justin Martyr (c. 160) to the Second Vatican Council’s Lumen Gentium (1964), earliest extant attestation first. Mary’s perpetual virginity is traced on its own timeline; several witnesses here (Epiphanius, Jerome) set the Eve–Mary antithesis within writing on virginity. The three earliest witnesses state the parallel within argument against opponents — Justin against Trypho, Irenaeus against the Gnostics, Tertullian against Marcion — each within an argument about how Christ became man.

Related doctrines: The Virgin Mary — see also the perpetual virginity of Mary · Theotokos (Mother of God) · the Immaculate Conception · the Assumption / Dormition · the Tomb of the Virgin

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 160)

Justin Martyr gives the earliest extant form of the parallel: Christ became man through the Virgin “in order that the disobedience which proceeded from the serpent might receive its destruction in the same manner in which it derived its origin” — Eve, a virgin, conceived the serpent’s word and brought forth disobedience and death, while the Virgin Mary received faith and joy at Gabriel’s word and replied, “Be it unto me according to thy word.”1

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus sets the antithesis inside his doctrine of recapitulation: Mary is “found obedient” where Eve was disobedient, so that “the knot of Eve’s disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary—for what the virgin Eve had bound fast through unbelief, this did the virgin Mary set free through faith.”2 Elsewhere he calls the Virgin Mary the advocata of the virgin Eve (rendered “patroness” in the Ante-Nicene Fathers; the word’s sense is disputed), “virginal disobedience having been balanced in the opposite scale by virginal obedience.”3

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian, arguing against Marcion that Christ took real flesh from the Virgin, restates the parallel as a “contrary operation”: the ensnaring word crept into the ear of the virgin Eve “to build the edifice of death,” so the Word of God was introduced into a virgin’s soul “to raise the fabric of life… that what had been reduced to ruin by this sex might by the selfsame sex be recovered to salvation. As Eve had believed the serpent, so Mary believed the angel.”4

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril of Jerusalem gives the antithesis in catechesis. Teaching baptismal candidates why the Word was made man, he gives as a second reason the Eve–Mary reversal: “Through Eve yet virgin came death; through a virgin, or rather from a virgin, must the Life appear: that as the serpent beguiled the one, so to the other Gabriel might bring good tidings.”5 The two-membered structure — virgin Eve / serpent’s word against virgin Mary / angel’s word — matches Justin’s form; here it appears unargued, as baptismal instruction rather than polemic.

Ephraem the Syrian (c. 370)

Ephraem the Syrian sets the parallel in Syriac hymnody. In the Hymns on the Nativity he sets “Eve our mother” against “Eve’s daughter” the Virgin, folding the antithesis into the Adam–Christ and virgin-earth typology: “To Eve our mother a man gave birth… The virgin earth, she bare that Adam that was head over the earth! The Virgin bare to-day the Adam that was Head over the Heavens.”6 Elsewhere he pictures Eve herself lifting her eyes from Sheol to rejoice that “the Son of her daughter… came down to raise up the mother of His mother” — the “Blessed Babe, that bruised the head of the Serpent.”7

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377)

Epiphanius of Salamis, writing against the group he calls Antidicomarianites, who held that Mary bore later children, reads Eve’s title “mother of the living” as pointing past Eve to Mary, who alone “brings forth the Living One.”8 He states the antithesis: “Eve has become the occasion of human deaths… But Mary, through whom Life was born for us, is the occasion of life… since Eve in Paradise fell into the sin of disobedience while still a virgin, the obedience of grace in its turn has come through the Virgin.”9

Jerome (c. 384)

Jerome, exhorting Eustochium to consecrated virginity, gives the antithesis in Latin: now that the Virgin has borne “the mighty God,” “now the chain of the curse is broken. Death came through Eve, but life has come through Mary. And thus the gift of virginity has been bestowed most richly upon women, seeing that it has had its beginning from a woman.”10 The Latin reads mors per Evam, vita per Mariam; he ties it to the virgin-Eve motif, that “in paradise Eve was a virgin, and it was only after the coats of skins that she began her married life.”11

Ambrose of Milan (c. 389)

Ambrose of Milan, reading Christ’s temptation against Adam’s fall, sets the “virgin earth” of Adam beside the Virgin of Christ: “Through a woman came folly, through a virgin came wisdom. Death came through a tree, life through a cross.”12 Elsewhere he adds that “our flesh was cast out of paradise by a man and woman and was joined to God through a Virgin,”13 and summons Eve to be renewed in the second Eve: “Come, therefore, Eve, now Mary.”14

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

A Nativity homily transmitted among John Chrysostom’s works (its authorship questioned) gives the full antithesis, naming Mary at every step: “the Devil deceived Eve while a virgin; for this reason Gabriel shared the good news with Mary while a virgin. But Eve, when she was deceived… gave birth to a word [that was the] cause of death… after Mary was told the good news, she bore the Word in flesh, the agent of our eternal life. Eve’s word pointed out a tree, through which she thrust Adam from Paradise. But the Word from the virgin pointed out the Cross, through which He led the bandit (representing Adam) into Paradise.”15 In the securely attributed works surveyed on his page (the homilies On Matthew, On Romans and On John), Chrysostom most often pairs Eve with fallen humanity.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 396)

Augustine of Hippo states the antithesis and turns it against the Manichaeans, who scorned birth from a woman as defiling. In The Christian Combat he sets the two members side by side: “Through a woman came death; through a woman, life: through Eve, ruin; through Mary, salvation. The former, corrupted, followed the deceiver; the latter, uncorrupted, gave birth to the Saviour.”16 Preaching later on why Christ chose to be born of a woman at all, he develops the reversal: “By deceiving, poison was offered to man through a woman: by repairing, salvation is offered to man through a woman… A woman announced death to her husband in paradise: and women announced salvation to men in the Church.”17

Proclus of Constantinople (c. 430)

Proclus of Constantinople, preaching in Nestorius’s own cathedral in defense of the title Theotokos, folds the antithesis into the case for a real Incarnation in its Greek “conception through hearing” form: “Through ears that disobeyed, the serpent poured in his poison; through ears that obeyed, the Word entered in order to build a living temple.”18 The reversal — the serpent’s word to the virgin Eve answered by the Word’s entry to the virgin Mary — appears here within the Ephesus-era debate over Mary’s title.

Peter Chrysologus (c. 440)

Peter Chrysologus, preaching in Latin at Ravenna in the years of the Council of Ephesus, gives the antithesis as a Latin couplet: Christ willed to be born “that just as death came to all through Eve, so life might return to all through Mary” (ut sicut per Evam venit ad omnes mors, ita per Mariam rediret omnibus vita). Reading the woman of the leaven parable (Matthew 13:33) as Mary, he turns Eve’s own title inside out: Mary is “the mother of all the living… she who in Adam was the mother of all the dead.”19

Hesychius of Jerusalem (c. 440)

Hesychius of Jerusalem, in a festal homily praising the Virgin, packs the reversal into a single clause of a rolling encomium: she is “the splendid ornament of our nature… who freed Eve from her shame and Adam from the curse, who cut off the bold insolence of the dragon.”20 The clause states the New-Eve motif in compressed form — the Virgin undoing Eve’s shame and cutting off the dragon’s insolence — a Greek witness of the Ephesus generation.

The Akathist Hymn (c. 500)

With the Akathist Hymn to the Theotokos — an anonymous Greek kontakion still sung through the Byzantine Lent — the New-Eve typology appears in liturgical hymnody. Greeting the Virgin at the Annunciation, the archangel hails her in the first stanza as the one through whom “the curse will disappear,” the “Restoration of the fallen Adam” and the “Redemption of the tears of Eve” (Greek tôn dakryôn tês Euas hê lytrôsis).21 The hymn does not argue the parallel; it sings it as praise. Its date is disputed (fifth to sixth century); Egon Wellesz ascribed it to Romanos the Melodist, and Leena Mari Peltomaa dates it to the fifth century; it is placed here at a midpoint.

Jacob of Serugh (c. 500)

Jacob of Serugh gives the Syriac antithesis in the sixth century, in the sung, typological key of Ephraem and with the ear at its centre: “See how Eve’s ear inclines and hearkens to the voice of the deceiver when he hisses deceit to her. But come and see the Watcher instilling salvation into Mary’s ear and removing the insinuation of the serpent from her.”22 The organ of Eve’s undoing becomes the door of Mary’s rescue; the Spirit, Jacob adds, “made her like Eve of old” and set her in the state of Eden “before they sinned.”23

John of Damascus (c. 730)

John of Damascus states the reading in a fixed formula: Mary, giving her flesh to the Son of God, is “satisfying the debt of the first mother” and bringing forth “the new Adam.”24 In his homilies on the Dormition he lets Adam and Eve themselves greet Mary as “our blessed daughter, who hast removed the penalty of our disobedience… Through us sorrow came out of good; through thee good from sorrow.”25

John Henry Newman (1866)

John Henry Newman, replying to E. B. Pusey’s Eirenicon, states the reading: the Eve–Mary parallel is “the great rudimental teaching of Antiquity from its earliest date” about Mary — “She is the Second Eve” — the primitive patristic root rather than a medieval accretion, citing Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian as his witnesses.26 From that root he argues the later Marian doctrine as a development, and on the Immaculate Conception he calls the inference “immediate” and the argument “conclusive.”27

The Second Vatican Council (1964)

With Lumen Gentium 56 the Second Vatican Council takes up the reading. Quoting the fathers by name, the council declares that “the knot of Eve’s disobedience was untied by Mary’s obedience,” and that they “still more often… say: ‘death through Eve, life through Mary.’” It describes Mary as “freely cooperating… through faith and obedience.”28

Disputed readings

How the earliest Eve–Mary texts, and the verse of Genesis most often read with them, have been interpreted.

The reach of the typology

Two readings concern what the second-century parallel says about Mary: one the parallel as a whole, one a single word in Irenaeus.

  • Does the second-century Eve–Mary parallel assign Mary an active part of her own in the reversal of the fall? — on what Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian assign to Mary in setting her against Eve.29
  • Does Irenaeus’s advocata (Against Heresies V.19.1) present Mary as Eve’s intercessor? — on the sense of a Latin word whose Greek original is lost.30

Genesis 3:15

  • Who crushes the serpent’s head in Genesis 3:15? (the Vulgate ipsa and the Hebrew hû) — on the Latin “she,” the Hebrew “he,” and how far the verse bears on Mary.31
