F. W. Farrar on the Mark of the Beast and the Number 666

Dates: 1882 (The Early Days of Christianity) · Wikipedia
Summary: The English cleric and popular historian whose Early Days of Christianity (1882) presented to English readers the 1830s German proposal that 666 ciphers Neron Qesar in Hebrew letters. In The Early Days of Christianity (1882) Farrar lays out the Nero gematria in plain terms and adds the "double solution": the same name, spelled the Latin way without the final Hebrew nun, yields the 616 of the variant tradition.

Claims & evidence

666 as Neron Kesar, the name a Jew would give Nero

"No Jew ever thought of Nero except as 'Neron Kesar,' and this gives at once — נרון קסר = 50 + 200 + 6 + 50 + 100 + 60 + 200 = 666."

— F. W. Farrar, The Early Days of Christianity (London: Cassell, 1882), 471. Farrar spells the imperial name in Hebrew consonants — NRWN QSR — and sums the letters (nun 50, resh 200, waw 6, nun 50; qoph 100, samekh 60, resh 200) to exactly 666. The point he presses is that a first-century Jew would have written and reckoned the name in just this form.

The 616 variant as the same name in its Latin (Nero, not Neron) spelling

On the following page Farrar accounts for the ancient variant reading 616 as the very same cipher. A Jewish-Christian scribe, he suggests, knew that "to Romans, and Gentiles generally, the name was always Nero Cæsar, not Neron"; dropping the final Hebrew nun (ן = 50) from Neron lowers the total from 666 to 616, and such a copyist "may have altered the reading because he imagined that… it made the solution more suitable and easy."
— Farrar, The Early Days of Christianity, 473 (running head "Nero Redivivus"). This is the "double solution": one name, two spellings, matching both numbers the manuscripts preserve.

Notes

  • Earlier proposers. R. H. Charles (The Revelation of St. John, ICC, 1920, 367) credits the Hebrew-gematria solution to four scholars, Holtzmann, Benary, Hitzig and Reuss, who, P. J. Williams reports, proposed it independently ("P115 and the Number of the Beast," Tyndale Bulletin 58.1 (2007), 151, n. 2); Ferdinand Benary's derivation dates from 1836, and Ferdinand Hitzig's and Eduard Reuss's from 1837; C. F. A. Fritzsche (1831) is also named as a proposer (⚠ unverified). Farrar presented it to an English audience.
