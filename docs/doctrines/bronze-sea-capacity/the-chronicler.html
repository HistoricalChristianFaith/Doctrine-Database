The Chronicler on the bronze sea's capacity

Dates: c. 400 BC (the composition of 1–2 Chronicles) · Wikipedia
Summary: Retelling Solomon's temple from Kings, the Chronicler gives the sea's capacity as "three thousand baths" — and does so with a doubled verb, "received and held," that is the hinge of the whole dispute.

Claims & evidence

"It received and held three thousand baths"

"Also he made a molten sea of ten cubits from brim to brim, round in compass, and five cubits the height thereof; and a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about… And the thickness of it was an handbreadth, and the brim of it like the work of the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies; and it received and held three thousand baths."

— 2 Chronicles 4:2, 5 (KJV). The description matches Kings detail for detail — the same ten cubits brim to brim, five cubits high, handbreadth-thick lily-worked rim — save in the capacity, where the Chronicler reads three thousand baths against the two thousand of 1 Kings 7:26, and in the verb, where he has two words where Kings has one.

The doubled verb

Where Kings says the sea plainly "contained" (yāḵîl) two thousand baths, the Chronicler writes that it "received and held" (maḥăzîq… yāḵîl) three thousand. Matthew Poole rendered the fuller Hebrew as the sea "strengthening itself, (to wit, to receive and hold as much as it could, or being filled to its utmost capacity,) it contained, or could contain, three thousand baths." [Poole, on 1 Kings 7:26] Everything in the crux turns on how that doubled verb is read.

Notes

  • Two readings of the double verb. On the textual reading it is Hebrew pleonasm — two near-synonymous verbs for a single act of holding, which Keil & Delitzsch call "pleonastic" — and the "three thousand" is then a copyist's inflation of an original "two." On the harmonistic reading the two verbs name two measures: what the sea could receive brim-full (three thousand) against what it ordinarily held (two thousand). The first is weighed on the scribal-error page, the second on the working-fill page.
  • The Chronicler's habit is to preserve, not to inflate. As his source-critics note, the Chronicler copies Kings closely; where his number diverges the natural suspicion falls on transmission, not on a rival tradition about the basin — especially since the vessel's own dimensions cap its capacity near two thousand. Compare the Chronicler on Ahaziah's age, where his Masoretic text likewise carries the larger, harder number.
