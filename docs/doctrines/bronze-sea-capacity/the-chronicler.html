The Chronicler on the bronze sea's capacity

Dates: c. 400 BC (the composition of 1–2 Chronicles) · Wikipedia
Summary: Retelling Solomon's temple from Kings, the Chronicler gives the sea's capacity as "three thousand baths" — and does so with a doubled verb, "received and held," where Kings has one.

Claims & evidence

"It received and held three thousand baths"

"Also he made a molten sea of ten cubits from brim to brim, round in compass, and five cubits the height thereof; and a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about… And the thickness of it was an handbreadth, and the brim of it like the work of the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies; and it received and held three thousand baths."

— 2 Chronicles 4:2, 5 (KJV). The description matches Kings detail for detail — the same ten cubits brim to brim, five cubits high, handbreadth-thick lily-worked rim — save in the capacity, where the Chronicler reads three thousand baths against the two thousand of 1 Kings 7:26, and in the verb, where he has two words where Kings has one.

The doubled verb

Where Kings says the sea "contained" (yāḵîl) two thousand baths, the Chronicler writes that it "received and held" (maḥăzîq… yāḵîl) three thousand. Matthew Poole rendered the fuller Hebrew as the sea "strengthening itself, (to wit, to receive and hold as much as it could, or being filled to its utmost capacity,) it contained, or could contain, three thousand baths." [Poole, on 1 Kings 7:26] Interpreters read that doubled verb in different ways (below).

Reception and debate

  • Readings of the double verb. Keil & Delitzsch call it "pleonastic" and take the "three thousand" for a copying error in Chronicles; Ellicott's commentary judges the slip "more likely" in Kings and takes the second verb for "a gloss borrowed from Kings," and the Cambridge Bible leaves the book open (on 2 Chronicles 4:5). Matthew Poole and John Gill read the two verbs as two measures: what the sea could receive brim-full (three thousand) against what it ordinarily held (two thousand). The Babylonian Talmud (Eruvin 14b) instead takes the three thousand as a heaped dry measure against two thousand of liquid. These and other readings are set out on the disputed-reading page.
  • The Chronicler's numbers. Montgomery and Gehman hold that "The Chronicler, as often, expands the figure to 3000 baths, followed by Josephus" (ICC Kings, 1951, p. 173); Keil & Delitzsch place the difference in transmission, among "orthographical errors in the Chronicle" (on 2 Chronicles 4:5). Compare the Chronicler on Ahaziah's age, where his Masoretic text likewise carries the larger number.
