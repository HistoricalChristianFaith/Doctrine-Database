Matthew Poole on the bronze sea's capacity

Dates: 1685 (the Annotations upon the Holy Bible, published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: The early-modern annotator who keeps both numbers by making them measure different fills — two thousand baths in ordinary use, three thousand brim-full — grounding the reading in the Chronicler's fuller Hebrew.

Claims & evidence

"What it did actually and usually contain" vs. "what it could contain if it were filled to the brim"

He "here speaks of what it did actually and usually contain, to wit, two thousand baths, which was sufficient for use; and in 2 Chronicles 4:5, he speaks of what it could contain if it were filled to the brim."

— Matthew Poole, Annotations upon the Holy Bible, on 1 Kings 7:26. Poole rests the distinction on the Chronicler's fuller phrasing: the Hebrew words there "differ from these, and properly sound thus, strengthening itself, (to wit, to receive and hold as much as it could, or being filled to its utmost capacity,) it contained, or could contain, three thousand baths." On his reading Kings gives the working fill and Chronicles the maximum, and both texts stand as written.

The same solution from John Gill

"in common, for the use of the priests, whether for washing their hands and feet, or dipping their bodies, it had no more than 2,000 baths in it, but, if filled up to the brim, it would hold 3,000."

— John Gill, Exposition of the Old and New Testament, on 1 Kings 7:26. Gill gives Poole's harmonization in the same terms, and adds a Jewish alternative — that the Chronicler's baths were a heaped, dry measure that would hold more — which reaches the same end of saving both figures.

Notes

  • A genuine textual hook, a hard physical problem. The harmonization is not arbitrary: the Chronicler really does write "received and held," a doubled verb Kings lacks. But it runs into the geometry that the text-critics press — if the vessel's stated dimensions cap it near two thousand baths (Thenius, in Keil & Delitzsch), then three thousand cannot be its brim-full capacity, and the doubled verb reads more naturally as Hebrew pleonasm than as two calibrated measures. Poole's reading is weighed on the working-fill argument page.
  • The harmonizer's stock move. As in Solomon's stalls (stables vs. stalls) and Ahaziah's age (the years of the house of Omri), Poole's first instinct is a "different thing being measured" harmonization that keeps the letter of both texts. Here the thing measured is the fill of the same basin.
