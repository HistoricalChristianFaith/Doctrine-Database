Do the two verses measure different fills — 2,000 baths in ordinary use, 3,000 filled to the brim?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — it has a real textual hook in the Chronicler's doubled verb, but the geometry that caps the vessel near 2,000 baths denies 3,000 as a brim-full maximum, and the doubled verb reads more naturally as pleonasm · advanced by Matthew Poole and John Gill, after the older annotators.

Related arguments: reconciling the capacity — see also Is the "three thousand" a scribal inflation of "two thousand"?

The claim

He "here speaks of what it did actually and usually contain, to wit, two thousand baths, which was sufficient for use; and in 2 Chronicles 4:5, he speaks of what it could contain if it were filled to the brim."

— Matthew Poole, on 1 Kings 7:26, link. The interpretation: the two numbers are not rivals but two different measurements of one basin — Kings the water ordinarily kept in it, Chronicles the maximum it could receive brim-full — so both texts are literally true and there is no error to explain. The reading leans on the Chronicler's fuller Hebrew, where the sea "received and held" (a doubled verb) three thousand where Kings simply "contained" two.1

Who advances it

It is the classic harmonizer's route. Matthew Poole (1685) presses it, grounding it in the Chronicler's "strengthening itself… to receive and hold as much as it could"; John Gill gives it in the same terms — "in common… it had no more than 2,000 baths in it, but, if filled up to the brim, it would hold 3,000" — and the Pulpit Commentary and Cambridge Bible allow it as a possibility ("perhaps the smaller quantity was about what was usually kept in supply, the larger what it could contain if it were quite full").2 It stands against the text-critical reading — associated with Keil & Delitzsch — that calls the "three thousand" a copyist's slip.

The evidence for

The Chronicler's doubled verb is real. This is the argument's genuine foothold, and it is not nothing. Kings has the single plain verb; the Chronicler writes two, "received and held" (maḥăzîq… yāḵîl). A distinction between what a vessel could receive and what it ordinarily held is a natural sense to read from two verbs where one would do.3

It saves the letter of both texts. Where the scribal reading concedes an error somewhere in transmission, this reading lets every word of both verses stand as written — the traditional harmonizer's prize, and the reason it has attracted so many annotators.4

Adversarial assessment

The geometry defeats it. The same computation that helps the textual reading sinks this one. If the vessel's stated dimensions cap its capacity near two thousand baths — "could not exceed 2000 baths" (Thenius, in Keil & Delitzsch) — then three thousand cannot be its brim-full maximum, because the basin does not hold three thousand at any fill. A "working fill vs. maximum" reading needs the maximum to be physically real; here it is not, unless one enlarges the cubit or the bath enough to break the objection, at which point the harmonization is resting on the same disputed measures it was meant to sidestep.5

The doubled verb reads better as pleonasm. Hebrew narrative pairs near-synonymous verbs for emphasis routinely; Keil & Delitzsch call the Chronicler's phrase exactly that — "pleonastic," an emphatic way of saying "held," not a signal of two calibrated measures. Ellicott goes further and treats the second verb as "a gloss borrowed from Kings." On either reading the doubled verb carries no news about a maximum.6

The two-thirds fill is unsignposted. The reading has to posit that the sea was habitually kept at exactly two thousand of a three-thousand capacity — a precise two-thirds fill that no text mentions and no priestly rubric requires. It is arithmetic assembled after the fact to reconcile the numbers, not a datum the passage supplies. (The kindred "the bath measure changed size" proposal fares no better: it too rescues both figures only by positing an unattested shift precisely large enough to close the gap.)7

Assessment

Weak. The reading is more than a bare possibility — the Chronicler really does write a doubled verb Kings lacks, and that is a fair place to start. But it does not hold on its best case: the geometry that caps the vessel near two thousand baths denies three thousand as any real fill, the doubled verb is better explained as Hebrew pleonasm (or a gloss) than as two measures, and the tidy two-thirds working fill is read into the text rather than out of it. Like the "stables-vs.-stalls" reading on Solomon's stalls and the "dynasty-reckoning" reading on Ahaziah's age, it saves the letter of both texts only by keeping the figure everything else marks as inflated. Rate it weak.
