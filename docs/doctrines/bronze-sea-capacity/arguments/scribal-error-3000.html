Why do 1 Kings 7:26 (two thousand baths) and 2 Chronicles 4:5 (three thousand baths) give different capacities for the bronze sea?

Disputed reading · on The Bronze Sea's Capacity: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? · witnesses: the book of Kings and the Chronicler

The passage

And it was an hand breadth thick, and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies: it contained two thousand baths.

— 1 Kings 7:26 (KJV), source.

And the thickness of it was an handbreadth, and the brim of it like the work of the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies; and it received and held three thousand baths.

— 2 Chronicles 4:5 (KJV), source. Both accounts give the same dimensions: ten cubits from brim to brim, five cubits high, and a line of thirty cubits around it.1

The question

In two otherwise matching descriptions of Solomon's bronze sea, Kings gives its capacity as two thousand baths and Chronicles as three thousand, where Kings has one verb ("contained") and Chronicles two ("received and held"). Interpreters differ over whether the two figures measure different things (different units, or different fills), or whether one of them arose in copying or in the Chronicler's retelling. The answer bears on how the relation of Chronicles to Kings, and the transmission of numbers in both, has been understood.

The two figures count in different measures

In brief: Kings states the sea's capacity in one measure and Chronicles in another, so both figures describe the same vessel.

Held by: the Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 14b (c. 450–550); Rashi (Jewish, c. 1075–1105); Radak, David Kimhi (Jewish, on 2 Chronicles 4:5, c. 1185–1235); and Cornelius a Lapide (Catholic, Jesuit, Commentary on 3 Kings, d. 1637).2

The Talmud reaches the bronze sea while reckoning the volume of a ritual bath. It computes that the sea's two thousand baths are six thousand se’a, "the volume of exactly one hundred and fifty ritual baths," and fits that figure to the dimensions with Rami bar Yeḥezkel's teaching that "the three lower cubits were square and the upper two were round." It then raises Chronicles: "Isn’t it written elsewhere with regard to Solomon's Sea: 'It received and held three thousand bat' (ii Chronicles 4:5)?" Its answer: "That is referring to the heaped measure of dry goods that the sea could hold, as dry goods can be heaped above the brim." Kings gives the liquid measure, Chronicles the dry.3

Rashi repeats the answer: "Our Rabbis explained it refers to a dry measure, since the overflow was one third of the capacity of the receptacle." Radak gives it as a general rule of vessels: "any vessel holds one-third more heaping solids than it does liquids, which cannot be heaped; for what extends above the vessel amounts to one-third of the vessel." He supports the ratio from the Mishnah (Kelim 15:1), where a chest that "holds forty se’ahs of liquid" holds "two kors of solid," and "two kors of solid is sixty se’ahs": two to three, the proportion of the two verses.4

Cornelius a Lapide finds the same proportion between two baths rather than two kinds of contents. With Villalpando and Alcazar he holds that "the bath was of two kinds, a larger and a smaller; the larger was one and a half times the other, so that two larger equaled three smaller." Kings counts in the larger bath and Chronicles in the smaller: "These are the larger baths numbering two thousand, which are equivalent to three thousand smaller baths." On either form of the reading the text of both books stands as written, and the two numbers state one capacity.5

Kings gives the working fill, Chronicles the brim-full capacity

In brief: the sea usually held two thousand baths and could hold three thousand filled to the brim, and the Chronicler's two verbs mark the larger figure as its full capacity.

Held by: Matthew Poole (English Nonconformist, Annotations upon the Holy Bible, 1685); John Gill (Particular Baptist, Exposition); Richard Challoner (Catholic, notes to the Douay–Rheims Old Testament, 1750); and the Explanatory Bible edited by A. P. Lopukhin (Russian Orthodox, from 1904).6

The reading starts from the wording of Chronicles. Where Kings says only that the sea "contained" two thousand baths, the Chronicler writes that it "received and held" three thousand. Poole takes the fuller phrase as the key: Kings "speaks of what it did actually and usually contain, to wit, two thousand baths, which was sufficient for use," and Chronicles "of what it could contain if it were filled to the brim," the Chronicler's Hebrew meaning "strengthening itself, (to wit, to receive and hold as much as it could, or being filled to its utmost capacity,) it contained, or could contain, three thousand baths." Gill gives the same account of the sea's use: "in common, for the use of the priests … it had no more than 2,000 baths in it, but, if filled up to the brim, it would hold 3,000." Cajetan and Ribera, as Cornelius a Lapide reports them, read the Hebrew machaziq the same way, as filling the vessel "strongly" or fully.7

Challoner's note on the Douay text puts it as a matter of practice: two thousand baths "was the quantity of water which was usually put into it: but it was capable, if brim-full, of holding three thousand." The Lopukhin Explanatory Bible, giving the explanation of Lightfoot and Metropolitan Philaret, ties the reading to the vessel's shape. The basin was not a cylinder but had the form of an opened lily-cup with a bulging middle, "like a goblet," and the diameter and circumference may have been taken independently, "because in different places of the vessel its circumference changed." On that ground it explains the two capacities: the larger "could denote the full capacity of the bronze sea up to the very brim, and the former, how much was usually poured in." On this reading both books report the vessel accurately, each by its own measure of fullness.8

A numeral was corrupted in copying

In brief: the two books once agreed, and one figure was altered in transmission, in Chronicles or in Kings.

Held by: Keil & Delitzsch (Lutheran, Biblical Commentary on the Old Testament, 1872); Ellicott's Commentary for English Readers (Anglican); Barnes' Notes (compiled from the Speaker's Commentary, Anglican); and the Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges.9

Keil & Delitzsch give both the mechanism and the reason: "The latter statement has arisen from the confusion of ג (3) with ב (2); since, according to the calculation of Thenius, the capacity of the vessel, from the dimensions given, could not exceed 2000 baths." On the parallel verse they file the "3000 baths instead of 2000" among "orthographical errors in the Chronicle," and read the Chronicler's second verb as "only pleonastic," an emphatic "receiving baths, 3000 it held," with "no sufficient reason to strike out the words." On their account Kings preserves the figure the vessel could hold.10

Ellicott's commentary locates the slip in the spelled-out Hebrew numbers. It reports that for Kings' "two thousand" "Most critics assume this to be correct," and sketches how a scribe might have read ’alāphîm, "thousands," for ‘alpayim, "two thousand," and added "three." But it judges it "more likely that the numeral 'three' having been inadvertently omitted from the text of Kings, the indefinite word 'thousands' was made definite by turning it into the dual 'two thousand'"; "Either mistake would be possible, because in the unpointed text ‘alāphîm and ’alpayim are written alike." It also suggests that the second verb in Chronicles is "a gloss borrowed from Kings," citing the Vulgate and noting that the Syriac and Arabic omit the clause. Barnes allows that "either here or in Kings the text may have been accidentally corrupted," and the Cambridge Bible that "Whether the textual corruption is to be attributed to 1 Kin. or to Chron. cannot be determined." On this reading the two books described one vessel with one number, and the difference belongs to the history of the copied text.11

The figures are approximate, and the Chronicler enlarged Kings' number

In brief: neither figure is an exact measurement, and the three thousand of Chronicles is the Chronicler's own enlargement of the two thousand he found in Kings.

Held by: Edward L. Curtis and Albert A. Madsen (International Critical Commentary on Chronicles, 1910), on the approximate figures only; and James A. Montgomery and Henry S. Gehman (International Critical Commentary on Kings, 1951).12

Curtis and Madsen treat the sea's numbers as round figures. Of the dimensions they write that "The numbers of this verse are only approximate, since 10 cubits … in diameter would give a circumference of 31.4159 cubits instead of 30 cubits." They compute the capacity themselves and find that "Both estimates appear too large": at a bath of about 65 pints, the smaller figure of two thousand baths would come to some sixteen thousand gallons, while the stated dimensions "in a cylinder give only 10,798 gallons … and if a hemisphere 6,376 gallons." "The true capacity," they conclude, "was probably somewhere between these figures." They also note that the Greek of 1 Kings at this point, though it carries the verse, is "lacking, however, the statement of the capacity of the tank."13

Montgomery and Gehman set out how far the size of the bath itself is uncertain. They report estimates of 36.44 litres (Nowack, Benzinger), about 45–46 litres from jars found at Lachish (C. H. Inge, 1941), and 22 litres (W. F. Albright, 1943), and the view of Nowack and Šanda "that too little is known of the form of the vessel to estimate its capacity." On the difference between the books they write: "The Chronicler, as often, expands the figure to 3000 baths, followed by Josephus." On this reading the three thousand is the Chronicler's own figure, in keeping with his practice elsewhere, and it is carried forward by Josephus in the first century.14
