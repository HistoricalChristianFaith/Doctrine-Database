The book of Kings on the bronze sea's capacity

Dates: c. 560 BC (final form after the release of Jehoiachin in Babylon, the last event it records, 2 Kings 25:27) · Wikipedia
Summary: The older of the two accounts of Solomon's temple furnishings, and a written source of the Chronicler. It gives the sea's capacity as "two thousand baths."

Claims & evidence

"It contained two thousand baths"

"And he made a molten sea, ten cubits from the one brim to the other: it was round all about, and his height was five cubits: and a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about… And it was an hand breadth thick, and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies: it contained two thousand baths."

— 1 Kings 7:23, 26 (KJV). The notice comes at the close of the catalogue of Hiram of Tyre's bronze work for the temple: the two pillars, the ten lavers on their wheeled stands, and this one great "sea," a great cast basin resting on twelve cast oxen (a hemisphere in Josephus's description; its form is disputed, see the disputed-reading page), in which the priests washed. The capacity is the last detail given, and Kings gives it as two thousand baths — the bath being, in Ellicott's phrase, "the largest of Hebrew liquid measures."

Reception and debate

  • The figure and the geometry. From the sea's stated dimensions — ten cubits across, five deep, round in compass — Thenius, cited by Keil & Delitzsch, computed that the vessel "could not exceed 2000 baths"; Ellicott's commentary reports that for the Kings figure "Most critics assume this to be correct," though it judges it more likely that "three" dropped out of Kings (on 2 Chronicles 4:5). Curtis and Madsen, with a different bath, judge that "Both estimates appear too large" (ICC Chronicles, 1910, pp. 230–231). The rival readings are set out on the disputed-reading page.
  • One verb, not two. Where the Chronicler will write that the sea "received and held" three thousand baths, Kings has the single plain verb — "it contained two thousand." Keil & Delitzsch read the Chronicler's doubled verb as emphatic pleonasm (and his "three thousand" as a copyist's slip); Poole reads it as naming a second, brim-full measure. See the Chronicler.
  • Other Kings–Chronicles numbers. As with Solomon's stalls and Ahaziah's age, Kings and Chronicles retell one event and a single number diverges.
