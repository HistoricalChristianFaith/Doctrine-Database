The book of Kings on the bronze sea's capacity

Dates: c. 560 BC (the Deuteronomistic history reaching its final form in the exile) · Wikipedia
Summary: The older of the two accounts of Solomon's temple furnishings, and the Chronicler's written source. It gives the sea's capacity as "two thousand baths" — the figure the vessel's own dimensions will bear.

Claims & evidence

"It contained two thousand baths"

"And he made a molten sea, ten cubits from the one brim to the other: it was round all about, and his height was five cubits: and a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about… And it was an hand breadth thick, and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies: it contained two thousand baths."

— 1 Kings 7:23, 26 (KJV). The notice comes at the close of the catalogue of Hiram of Tyre's bronze work for the temple: the two pillars, the ten lavers on their wheeled stands, and this one great "sea," a hemispherical basin resting on twelve cast oxen, in which the priests washed. The capacity is the last detail given, and Kings gives it as two thousand baths — the bath being, in Ellicott's phrase, "the largest of Hebrew liquid measures."

Notes

  • The figure the geometry supports. From the sea's stated dimensions — ten cubits across, five deep, round in compass — the vessel's volume can be computed, and the computation caps it near two thousand baths (Thenius, cited by Keil & Delitzsch). So the Kings number is the one the basin could actually hold; it is the reading most critics take as prior. This is weighed on the scribal-error argument page.
  • One verb, not two. Where the Chronicler will write that the sea "received and held" three thousand baths, Kings has the single plain verb — "it contained two thousand." The difference in phrasing is the whole hinge of the dispute: whether the Chronicler's doubled verb is emphatic pleonasm (so his "three thousand" is a copyist's slip) or names a second, brim-full measure. See the Chronicler.
  • The same textual family as the sibling cruxes. As with Solomon's stalls and Ahaziah's age, Kings and Chronicles retell one event and a single number diverges. Here, unlike Ahaziah, neither figure is impossible — only one is improbable on the geometry.
