Simon Peter

Simon Peter stands first in every Gospel list of the Twelve. This page sets out what the sources report about him — his origin and family, his name, his mission, and his death — earliest extant source first: the New Testament, Clement of Rome, Ignatius, the apocryphal Acts of Peter, Tertullian, Gaius of Rome, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome. This is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on Peter. Two questions have their own pages under Disputed readings: whether Peter led the church at Rome and died there, and whether he was crucified head-downward. For the sources common to all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament reports Peter’s origin, family, name, mission and the prophecy of his death. He is Simon, son of Jonah, a fisherman of Bethsaida and Capernaum and the brother of Andrew; at their first meeting Jesus renames him — “thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone” (Greek Petros) — and later builds a saying on the name: “thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church.”1 He is married — the Gospels heal his wife's mother — and Paul names “Cephas” in asking, “Have we not power to lead about a sister, a wife”; Tertullian and John Chrysostom read the verse of the apostles' wives, and Jerome, Ambrosiaster, Augustine and Theophylact of women who supplied the apostles from their means.2 He is named first in the lists of the Twelve and is the chief speaker in Acts 1–12, from Pentecost onward; at Antioch Paul “withstood him to the face” over table-fellowship with Gentiles. Of his end the New Testament says only that Jesus foretold it — “when thou shalt be old … another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not” — while his own first letter is sent from “Babylon,” which Eusebius reports “they say” meant Rome, called “by a figure, Babylon,” and which the classicist Otto Zwierlein (2009) reads as a metaphor for exile; see Did Peter lead and die at Rome?3

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth, gives the earliest extant notice of Peter's martyrdom outside the New Testament (the letter is traditionally dated c. 96; Zwierlein dates it about 120–125). Peter and Paul are “the greatest and most righteous pillars” of the church who were “persecuted and put to death”: Peter “endured not one or two, but numerous labours, and when he had at length suffered martyrdom, departed to the place of glory due to him.” Clement names neither the manner nor the place; he writes from Rome, and places both deaths in “our own generation.”4

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

On his own way to martyrdom, Ignatius writes ahead to the Roman church, “I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you”; A. C. McGiffert (1890) reads it as implying “that Peter had been in Rome,” and Zwierlein holds the letter pseudonymous and not datable before 160; see Did Peter lead and die at Rome?5

The Acts of Peter (c. 190)

The apocryphal Acts of Peter — a second-century narrative — contains two scenes of Peter's death that later sources repeat: the Quo vadis, in which the fleeing apostle meets Christ on the road (“Lord, whither art Thou going?” — “I am going to Rome to be crucified”) and turns back; and his crucifixion head-downward, at his own request, “for I am not worthy to be crucified like my Lord” (both scenes are quoted here from the later Greek Acts of Peter and Paul). It is the earliest extant source for the inverted cross; how far that detail is history is discussed on Was Peter crucified head-downward?6

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian of Carthage points converts to Rome, “how happy is its church … where Peter endures a passion like his Lord's!” — the earliest extant statement that Peter died by crucifixion. He does not specify the posture of the cross.7

Gaius of Rome (c. 200)

Gaius, a Roman churchman under Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, answers a rival by pointing to the apostles' graves: “I can show the trophies (tropaia) of the apostles. For if you will go to the Vatican or to the Ostian way, you will find the trophies of those who laid the foundations of this church.” Preserved by Eusebius, who took the “trophies” to be the apostles' graves, this is the earliest extant reference to a monument of Peter on the Vatican hill. A small shrine of about 160 found in the excavations of 1939–49 beneath St Peter's Basilica is known as the “Trophy of Gaius.”8

Origen (c. 230)

Origen, quoted by Eusebius, is the earliest named writer to record the posture: Peter preached to the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Bithynia, Cappadocia, and Asia, and “at last, having come to Rome, he was crucified head-downwards; for he had requested that he might suffer in this way.” The posture had already appeared in the anonymous Acts of Peter.9

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius gives the earliest extant connected account. In it Peter came to Rome under Claudius to confront Simon Magus; Mark, “a follower of Peter,” set down his preaching as a Gospel; and under Nero “Paul was beheaded in Rome itself, and … Peter likewise was crucified” — a report Eusebius says is substantiated by the apostles' names in the Roman cemeteries, by Gaius's trophies, and by Dionysius of Corinth, who wrote that Peter and Paul “taught together in like manner in Italy, and suffered martyrdom at the same time.”10

Jerome (393)

Jerome gives a compact Latin life. In it Peter, son of John, of Bethsaida, brother of Andrew and chief of the apostles, was bishop of Antioch, preached to the dispersion, then “pushed on to Rome in the second year of Claudius to overthrow Simon Magus, and held the sacerdotal chair there for twenty-five years until … the fourteenth year of Nero.” He was crucified “with his head towards the ground and his feet raised on high, asserting that he was unworthy to be crucified in the same manner as his Lord,” and lies “buried at Rome in the Vatican near the triumphal way.”11

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on Peter at Rome and on the manner of his death.

  • Did Peter lead and die at Rome? — whether Peter was ever at Rome, and whether he founded or governed its church.
  • Was Peter crucified head-downward? — the posture first found in the Acts of Peter and Origen, and the request Jerome reports.
