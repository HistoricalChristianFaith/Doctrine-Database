Does the Filioque introduce “two causes” and destroy the Father’s monarchy?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the crude form of the charge (double procession posits two independent causes) is met by the Latin “one principle and a single spiration,” in which the Son spirates only by receiving that power from the Father, so the Father stays sole ultimate cause — a distinction even the modern Roman clarification and the joint Orthodox–Catholic dialogue grant; but the deeper Eastern worry (that grounding the Spirit’s origin in both persons shifts the unity of the Godhead from the Father’s person to the shared essence, and that “one principle” relabels rather than dissolves the problem) is genuinely unresolved and turns on prior commitments · advanced by Photios of Constantinople, Vladimir Lossky, and the Eastern tradition.

Related arguments: is double procession sound? — see also Does John 15:26’s “who proceedeth from the Father” exclude procession from the Son?

The claim

“if two causes are discerned in the divine, sovereign, and transcendent Trinity… then where is the much-hymned, divine majesty of the Monarchy?”

— Photios I, On the Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit §11, Myriobiblos text.1 This is the systematic objection to the Filioque, distinct from the exegetical one about John 15:26 and independent of any single proof-text. The Eastern grammar of the Trinity rests on the monarchy of the Father: the Father alone is the one cause (aitia), source, and origin (archē) of the Godhead, from whom the Son is begotten and the Spirit proceeds. That the Father is the single personal source is what secures the Trinity’s unity and keeps it from being three Gods. To say the Spirit proceeds also “from the Son” is therefore to posit a second cause in the Godhead — and a Trinity with two causes has lost its monarchy, drifting (Photios charges) toward both polytheism (two principles) and Sabellianism (the persons confused).

Who advances it

The objection was given its classic form by Photios in the ninth century and is the core of the Eastern dogmatic case, restated by Mark of Ephesus at Florence and sharpened in the twentieth century by Vladimir Lossky, who located the whole quarrel in whether the unity of God is grounded in a person or in a nature. Its patristic backbone is the Cappadocian taxis — Basil’s ordered Godhead with the Father as “the original cause of all things” and Gregory of Nyssa’s “one is the Cause, and another is of the Cause” — crystallised by John of Damascus: “we do not speak of the Spirit as from the Son… For the Father alone is cause.”2 The Latin answer runs through Augustine’s principaliter to the definition of the Council of Florence.

The evidence for

The objection is stronger than a mere debating point, for three reasons. First, the monarchy of the Father is not one school’s theory but the near-universal grammar of the Greek fathers: the Father alone is aitia, and both the Son’s begetting and the Spirit’s procession are defined precisely as relations of origin to the Father. On that grammar, being the unoriginate source is the Father’s unique hypostatic property, and to let the Son share in it is to blur the very mark that distinguishes the Father’s person. Second, the objection is systematic, not exegetical: even granting (as the companion page does) that John 15:26 does not by itself exclude the Son, the monarchy argument bites at a different level — on the structure of the Godhead — and so is not answered merely by finding the Son somewhere in the Spirit’s economy. Third, Lossky presses a real dilemma that the Latin order does not obviously escape: “The Greek Fathers always maintained that the principle of unity in the Trinity is the person of the Father… This is why the East has always opposed the formula of filioque which seems to impair the monarchy of the Father: either one is forced to destroy the unity by acknowledging two principles of Godhead, or one must ground the unity primarily on the common nature.”3 Latin trinitarianism does tend to begin from the one divine essence and treat the persons as relations within it; if the Spirit is spirated by Father and Son precisely as both possessors of the one essence, then the source of the Spirit has quietly migrated from the Father’s person to the shared nature — which is the depersonalising Lossky says the monarchy exists to prevent.

Adversarial assessment

What is answered. The crude form of the charge — that double procession asserts two causes — lands against a caricature the Latin tradition never held. Its whole point is the reverse. Augustine already qualified the doctrine with principaliter: the Spirit proceeds “principally” from the Father, who by begetting the Son gave the Son too that the Spirit should proceed from him — so the Son is a source only as one who has received being-a-source from the Father, never an unoriginate second origin.4 Florence made this explicit: the Spirit “proceeds from both eternally as from one principle and a single spiration,” not two.5 As Aquinas puts it, even in breathing forth the Spirit together the Father retains priority, giving the Son all that he has. And the 1995 Roman clarification concedes the decisive Eastern point outright: the Father is the sole cause (aitia, the object of the Greek verb ekporeusis) of the Spirit, while the broader Latin processio names only the Son’s mediating role, not a rival origin.6 This is no modern rescue: Maximus the Confessor had drawn exactly this distinction in the seventh century, defending the Roman usage to the Greeks on the ground that the Latins “have not made the Son the cause of the Spirit… the Father is the only cause,” using “and the Son” only to name the Spirit’s coming-forth through him. So Photios’s syllogism — double procession ⇒ two causes ⇒ no monarchy — fails at the first step on the Filioque’s best statement: correctly understood, it affirms the Father’s monarchy rather than denying it.

What survives. The reconciliation is not clean, and three things keep the dispute genuinely open. (1) Florence still calls the Son “principle” (principium) of the Spirit’s subsistence “according to the Latins” — a share in being source that the strict Eastern grammar reserves to the Father’s hypostasis alone; whether “one principle” is a real synthesis or a relabelling of two causes as one is exactly the contested hinge. (2) Lossky’s deeper charge is not touched by “single spiration”: his worry is that any shared principle grounds unity in the common essence rather than the Father’s person — and “Father and Son as one principle” can be read as confirming precisely that. (3) The most careful modern joint study, the 2003 North American Orthodox–Catholic Consultation, found that both traditions do affirm the Father as “the primordial source (archē) and ultimate cause (aitia) of the divine being,” yet judged the remaining differences, “though subtle,… substantial” and still “in tension” — concluding only that they “need no longer divide us,” not that either formula simply answers the other.7

Assessment

Contested. As a demand that the Father’s monarchy be preserved, the objection voices the settled patristic grammar and exerts real philosophical pressure on an essence-first trinitarian order — strong by Eastern dogmatic lights. But its load-bearing inference, that double procession as such posits two independent causes and so destroys the monarchy, is met by the Latin “one principle and a single spiration”: the Son spirates only by receiving spirative power from the Father, who remains the sole ultimate cause — a distinction the 1995 Roman clarification and the joint Orthodox–Catholic dialogue both grant. What remains live is subtler and unresolved: whether “one principle” reconciles or merely renames two sources, and whether locating the Spirit’s origin in both persons shifts the ground of unity from the Father’s person to the shared essence. That question turns on prior commitments the formulas do not settle, with strong advocates and no clear winner. Flag as overreach any claim that the Filioque, on its best statement, asserts two causes; credit the enduring force of the monarchy concern in its Losskian, personal-unity form.
