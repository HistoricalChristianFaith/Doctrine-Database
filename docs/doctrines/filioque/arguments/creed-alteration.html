Did the councils’ ban on “a different faith” (Ephesus, canon 7) forbid adding the Filioque to the creed?

Disputed reading · on The Filioque · witnesses: Pope Leo III and the Council of Aachen · Photios of Constantinople · the Council of Florence

The passage

When these things had been read, the holy Synod decreed that it is unlawful for any man to bring forward, or to write, or to compose a different (ἑτέραν) Faith as a rival to that established by the holy Fathers assembled with the Holy Ghost in Nicæa. But those who shall dare to compose a different faith, or to introduce or offer it to persons desiring to turn to the acknowledgment of the truth, whether from Heathenism or from Judaism, or from any heresy whatsoever, shall be deposed, if they be bishops or clergymen; bishops from the episcopate and clergymen from the clergy; and if they be laymen, they shall be anathematized.

— Council of Ephesus (431), Canon VII, trans. Henry R. Percival, NPNF² vol. 14, source.1 The Council of Chalcedon (451), after receiving the creeds of Nicaea and of Constantinople, renewed the ban: “no one shall be suffered to bring forward a different faith (ἑτέραν πίστιν), nor to write, nor to put together, nor to excogitate, nor to teach it to others.”2

The question

The words “and the Son” entered the Latin recited creed in Spain in the late sixth or the seventh century (589 in the received acts of the Third Council of Toledo; 653 on the dating of A. E. Burn, Knut Schäferdiek and Shawn S. Smith), spread through the Frankish churches, were declined by Pope Leo III for the creed at Rome, and were first sung at a papal Mass in 1014.3 Whether the conciliar ban on “a different faith” forbids any addition to the creed’s words, or only a creed contrary to it, and who may add to or explain the common creed, is disputed. The North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation (2003) records that “Orthodox theology has regarded the ultimate approval by the Popes, in the eleventh century, of the use of Filioque in the Latin Creed as a usurpation of the dogmatic authority proper to ecumenical Councils alone,” while “Catholic theology has seen it as a legitimate exercise of his primatial authority to proclaim and clarify the Church’s faith.”4 Whether the doctrine of double procession is true is a separate dispute.

A ban on adding to the common creed

In brief: the councils fixed the creed’s wording as the confession of the whole Church, so no single part of the Church may add to it, even words it holds to be true.

Held by: Pope Leo III, to Charlemagne’s envoys (809–810);5 Photios, patriarch of Constantinople, Encyclical to the Eastern Patriarchs (867);6 Mark of Ephesus (Orthodox), Greek spokesman at Ferrara–Florence (1438–39);7 and Theodore Stylianopoulos (Greek Orthodox), “The Filioque: Dogma, Theologoumenon or Error?” (1986).8

Canon 7 makes it “unlawful for any man” to bring forward, write or compose a different faith, and it names no body exempt from the rule. On this reading the creed is the common possession of every church that confesses it, fixed by councils whose authority is that of the whole Church, and its words may be changed only by that same authority. Leo III held the doctrine of double procession himself and still kept it out of the creed: Percival reports that he “opposed the insertion of the Filioque on the express ground that the General Councils had forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary,” and the 2003 Consultation that he disapproved the clause in the creed of 381 because its Fathers, “no less inspired by the Holy Spirit than the bishops who had gathered at Aachen,” “had chosen not to include it.” He set up the creed in Latin and Greek, without the addition, on two silver shields in St. Peter’s.3,5 Photios called the creed the “Nicene Symbol of Faith — which by both synodal and universal decisions possesses invincible power,” and held that the addition alone “would be sufficient to subject them to a thousand anathemas.”6 At Florence the Greeks, with Mark of Ephesus as their spokesman, held that the ban on another faith forbade “anyone, even a subsequent General Council, to add anything to the creed,” and Mark called the addition the cause of the schism and illegal because Ephesus forbade it.7 For Stylianopoulos the canonical and the doctrinal questions go together: “Because the filioque was added to the Nicene Creed we must ask whether or not it sits well within it,” and he concludes that the Augustinian “and” “should be removed from the Nicene Creed on theological as well as canonical grounds.” He locates the canonical question in the authority the creed confers: “The real problem, then, is the uncanonical inclusion of the filioque in the Creed which automatically attributes to it dogmatic authority.”8 On this reading the creed sung at the Eucharist is the confession of the undivided Church, and keeping its text unaltered is how every local church keeps communion with the councils that gave it.

A ban on a contrary creed, leaving room for explanation

In brief: the councils forbade a rival creed that contradicts Nicaea, not words that make its meaning explicit, and the Filioque is such an explanation, added by competent authority.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic), Summa Theologiae (composed 1265–1274);9 the Council of Florence (Catholic), Laetentur Caeli (1439);10 Henry R. Percival (Episcopal priest), The Seven Ecumenical Councils (1899);11 and Avery Dulles, S.J. (Catholic), “The Filioque: What Is at Stake?” (1995).12

On this reading “different” (heteran) names a rival faith. Percival, following Pusey, notes that the canon was passed just after the council had approved the creed of the presbyter Charisius and condemned a Nestorian one, so that “ἑτέραν must mean ‘different,’ ‘contradictory,’ and not ‘another’ in the sense of mere explanatory additions.” Cyril of Alexandria, who presided at Ephesus and, in Percival’s judgement, “probably himself framed” the canon, wrote that one who explains his faith “innovates in nothing, nor doth he frame any new exposition of faith.” Chalcedon renewed the ban after setting out a much fuller statement of faith, and so, Percival argues, “did not hold the enlargement of the things proposed as de fide to be prohibited, but only the producing of things contradictory to the faith.” At Florence Cardinal Julian Cesarini argued that a ban on every addition would condemn anyone who held a truth not in the Nicene text, “such as that God is eternal or incomprehensible.”11 Aquinas reads the councils the same way: “subsequent councils are not to be described as making a new symbol of faith; but what was implicitly contained in the first symbol was explained by some addition directed against rising heresies,” and when the denial of procession from the Son arose, “the matter was explicitly defined by the authority of the Roman Pontiff, by whose authority also the ancient councils were summoned and confirmed.”9 Maas adds that the councils “had not forbidden to explain the same faith or to propose the same Creed in a clearer way,” and that their sanctions “affected individual transgressors… they did not bind the Church as a body.”13 Dulles reports the Latin reply at Florence, that the words of Ephesus “were intended to prevent any change of the faith, but not any change in the words of the creed,” and holds it correct “because the Nicene Creed, to which the fathers at Ephesus were referring, did not yet have the words about the procession of the Holy Spirit that were added at Constantinople and were still to be approved by Chalcedon.” He sets the Western view beside Nicaea’s addition of the homoousion and Constantinople’s clause on the Spirit, and writes: “Nothing can deprive the church of its power to retouch the creed provided that its meaning is not deformed.”12 Florence defined that “the explanation of those words ‘and from the Son’ was licitly and reasonably added to the creed for the sake of declaring the truth and from imminent need.”10 Of the eleventh-century papal approval of the clause, the 2003 Orthodox–Catholic Consultation records that “Catholic theology has seen it as a legitimate exercise of his primatial authority to proclaim and clarify the Church’s faith.”4 On this reading the clause belongs to the same faith as the creed of 381, made explicit against a later error.

The creed of 381 as the normative common text, the clause as a Western explication

In brief: the text of 381 is normative and common to East and West; the Filioque is a Western explication of it, not the council’s words. Some holders allow its continued Western use on that understanding; others ask for the 381 text to be restored in the liturgy.

Held by: John Pearson (Church of England), An Exposition of the Creed (1659);14 the Lambeth Conference (Anglican, 1978 and 1988);15 the Methodist Conference of Great Britain (1990);16 and the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity (Catholic, 1995).17,18

Pearson holds that “the addition of words to the formal Creed without the consent, and against the protestations of the Oriental Church be not justifiable; yet that which was added is nevertheless a certain truth, and may be so used in that Creed by them who believe the same to be a truth; so long as they pretend it not to be a definition of that Council, but an addition or explication inserted, and condemn not those who, out of a greater respect to such synodical determinations, will admit of no such insertions.”14 The Pontifical Council acknowledges “the conciliar, ecumenical, normative, and irrevocable value” of “the Symbol professed in Greek at Constantinople in 381,” adds that “No profession of faith peculiar to a particular liturgical tradition can contradict this expression,” and describes the Latin Filioque as an implicit teaching “made explicit in their liturgical version of the Symbol.” The Catholic Church, it notes, has refused to add “and the Son” to the Greek text “even of Latin rite,” and “The liturgical use of this original text remains always legitimate in the Catholic Church.”17 The Methodist report frames the question apart from the doctrine: “even if its doctrinal value is firmly endorsed, a question still remains: was the West right to insert it, unilaterally, into the ecumenical creed which received widest affirmation in the early church?” It describes the clause as clarifying “(in a Western direction) a point on which the Creed is silent,” and the Conference resolved its willingness “to restore the Nicene Creed to the form agreed by East and West in A.D.381” once there is sufficient agreement among the Western churches.16 Lambeth in 1988 judged it possible for the Western churches “to adopt the original form of the Creed without any betrayal of their theological heritage.”15 On this reading the text of 381 stays the shared confession, and the Western churches keep the teaching the clause expresses as their own tradition of explaining it.
