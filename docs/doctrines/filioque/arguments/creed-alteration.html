Was the West entitled to add the Filioque to the creed unilaterally? (the canonical question)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — this argument concedes the doctrine and contests only the procedure: could a run of local Western synods, and finally Rome, insert two words into the ecumenical creed without an ecumenical council and without the consent of the Eastern patriarchates? The descriptive facts favour the East — the addition was unilateral, no council authorized it, and the West’s own Pope Leo III refused the creedal insertion on exactly this ground while agreeing with the teaching. But the strongest Latin reply has real bite: the canon most often cited (Ephesus, 431, canon 7) barred a rival “other faith” offered to converts, not an explanatory expansion, and the East itself recites a creed the Second Council expanded at Constantinople (381). What is left unresolved is the ecclesiological hinge the canon cannot settle by itself — whether Rome could regularize the addition by its own authority — which is precisely the papal-vs.-conciliar question that divides the churches · advanced by Photios of Constantinople, Mark of Ephesus, and the Eastern tradition; conceded in part by Pope Leo III and by modern Roman practice.

The claim

“The holy and universal Synod forbids to bring forward any other faith; or to write or believe or to teach other, or be otherwise minded. But whoso shall dare either to expound or produce or deliver any other faith to those who wish to be converted… these… the holy and ecumenical Synod decrees… to be deposed.”

— Canon 7 of the Council of Ephesus (431).1 This is the canonical or procedural objection to the Filioque, and it is deliberately separate from the two doctrinal arguments (the exegesis of John 15:26 and the “two causes” objection). It grants, for argument’s sake, that double procession might be true, and presses a different question: whose creed is it, and who may alter it? The East holds that the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed is the common property of the whole church, fixed by an ecumenical council and received by every subsequent one; that Ephesus (and after it Chalcedon) forbade producing any other creed; and that therefore no local synod, no Frankish king, and not even Rome could add words to the recited ecumenical text without a new ecumenical council. The West did exactly that — the words entered the sung creed at Toledo, spread through the Frankish realm, and were finally chanted at Rome in 1014 — so the addition, whatever its doctrinal merit, was an illegitimate unilateral alteration.

Who advances it

The charge was pressed with full force by Photios, whose encyclical of 867 denounced the addition to the creed as grounds for anathema, and it became the standing Eastern position — restated by Mark of Ephesus at Florence and by the Orthodox tradition ever since. Its most telling early witness, though, is a Western one: Pope Leo III, who agreed with the doctrine of double procession yet refused to let the words be inserted into the creed “on the express ground that the General Councils had forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary,” and hung the un-interpolated creed — in Latin and in Greek — on two silver shields in St. Peter’s.2 So the procedural objection is not merely a partisan Eastern weapon: the very see that later became the Filioque’s champion first judged the creedal insertion canonically improper.

The evidence for

The procedural case is strong on three counts. First, the text of the prohibition. Ephesus canon 7 forbids producing “any other faith” than the creed of the fathers, and the Council of Chalcedon (451), receiving the expanded Constantinopolitan creed as the faith of Nicaea, reaffirmed that “no one shall be suffered… to bring forward a different faith,” deposing clergy who dared.3 On the East’s reading, the recited creed is fenced by conciliar authority precisely so that no local power can retouch it. Second, the unilateral manner of the change is undisputed as a matter of fact: no ecumenical council ordered the insertion, and the four Eastern patriarchates were neither consulted nor consenting; the addition grew from a Spanish anti-Arian liturgical usage into a Frankish imposition and only then a Roman one, across centuries, entirely within the Latin sphere. Third, and decisively, the West’s own highest authority conceded the point for two hundred years: Leo III’s refusal, the silver shields, and Rome’s withholding of the interpolated creed until 1014 under Benedict VIII show that the insertion was recognized as an insertion, and an improper one, by Rome itself — long before it was adopted there.2 That an opponent’s star witness is the accused party’s own pope is what raises this above the ordinary polemic.

Adversarial assessment

What the Latin replies establish. Three replies genuinely blunt the objection’s letter. (1) The scope of canon 7. Its immediate object was the Nestorianizing creed that a certain Charisius had produced for converts; the phrase is “another faith” (hetera pistis) — a rival, contradictory symbol offered as an alternative — not an explanatory clarification within the same faith. Read strictly, the canon forbids composing a competing creed, which the Latins say the Filioque is not: it draws out, rather than contradicts, what the creed already professes. (2) The tu quoque. The creed the East recites is not the creed of Nicaea (325) at all; the Second Ecumenical Council expanded it at Constantinople (381), writing the entire third article on the Holy Spirit that the Nicene text lacked.4 If any expansion of the fathers’ creed is barred by the prohibition, the East’s own liturgical creed is caught first — so the canon cannot be an absolute bar on all additions. (3) Reception. Rome eventually received the addition (1014), and the Council of Florence (1439) defined that the two languages taught one faith and that the addition was a lawful explanation lawfully made — a conciliar regularization, on the Latin account, by the authority competent to give it.5

What survives them. Each reply meets the objection’s letter but not its core. The East’s charge is not “the creed may never be touched” but “the ecumenical creed may be touched only by ecumenical authority” — and on that framing the tu quoque actually cuts the other way: the Constantinopolitan expansion was made by an ecumenical council and ratified by another (Chalcedon), whereas the Filioque was inserted by local synods and one patriarchate acting alone. The disanalogy — conciliar versus unilateral — is the whole point, and the strongest Latin card leaves it standing.6 The narrow-scope reading of canon 7 is fair as far as it goes, but Photios and the East also invoke the broader principle the councils enacted (that the symbol is the common rule of faith), not the canon’s letter alone. And “reception” is the very thing in dispute: Florence was repudiated in the East within months, so it settles the question only for those who already grant that a council of the Western obedience, or the Roman see by itself, can bind the whole church — which is the papal-vs.-conciliar issue, not a neutral fact. Tellingly, modern Rome has moved a long way toward the procedural complaint even while defending the doctrine: the 1995 clarification affirms the “normative and irrevocable” value of the creed as professed in Greek at Constantinople in 381, and Greek-speaking Catholics recite the creed without the Filioque — a practical concession that the words have no place in the ecumenical text, however legitimate the Latin liturgical use.7

Assessment

Contested. On the bare facts the East is right: the words were added to the ecumenical creed unilaterally — by Western synods and finally one patriarchate — with no ecumenical council and no Eastern consent, and the West’s own Pope Leo III judged the insertion canonically improper and resisted it for two centuries. The strongest Latin defenses answer the letter of the objection without dislodging its core: the narrow scope of Ephesus canon 7 (a rival creed, not a clarification) and the reminder that Constantinople itself expanded Nicaea are both sound, but the expansion they invoke was conciliar, which is exactly the difference the East is pressing. What keeps the verdict from tipping fully to the East is that the decisive question underneath — whether the Roman see could regularize the addition by its own authority — is the papal-vs.-conciliar dispute itself, which the canonical argument presupposes rather than resolves: strong by conciliar and canonical lights, answerable by a high papal ecclesiology, with no neutral arbiter. Genuinely disputed, then, and rightly so — though it is worth recording that this is the corner of the whole Filioque quarrel where cross-confessional agreement runs deepest, with even Rome now reciting the creed in its un-interpolated Greek form alongside the Orthodox. Flag as overreach any Latin claim that the addition was simply regular (Leo III thought otherwise); and flag as overreach any Eastern claim that canon 7’s letter by itself forbids all creedal expansion (it barred a rival faith, and the East recites an expanded creed).
