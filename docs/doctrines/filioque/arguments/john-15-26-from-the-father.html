Does John 15:26’s “who proceedeth from the Father” exclude procession from the Son?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the verse genuinely teaches the Spirit’s origin “from the Father” and never says “from the Son,” but it does not contain the word “alone,” so it under-determines the dispute: strong as showing the text does not teach the Filioque, weak as showing it positively excludes it · advanced by Photios of Constantinople and the Eastern tradition.

Related arguments: is double procession sound? — see also Does the Filioque introduce “two causes” and destroy the Father’s monarchy?

The claim

“the second of the seven Holy and Ecumenical Synods directly dogmatised that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father.”

— Photios I, On the Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit §5, Myriobiblos text. The scriptural anchor is Jesus’ own word in John 15:26: “But when the Comforter is come, whom I will send unto you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, which proceedeth from the Father, he shall testify of me.”1 The Eastern reading: the one place where Christ states the Spirit’s origin, he names the Father and only the Father — therefore the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, and the Latin “and the Son” adds to Christ’s own words.

Who advances it

The argument is as old as the controversy and was given its classic form by Photios in the ninth century; it remains the standard Eastern Orthodox proof-text. Its deep roots are in the Greek fathers’ own habit of stating the Spirit’s origin as “from the Father” and defining it against the Son’s begetting — Gregory of Nazianzus (“coming forth from the Father … by Procession”) and John of Damascus (“the Father alone is cause”). The Latin side answers from the same Gospel discourse and from Augustine’s double procession.

The evidence for

Two things genuinely favour the reading. First, the lexical point: the Greek verb here, ἐκπορεύεται (ekporeuetai), in the fathers’ technical usage names the Spirit’s eternal origin or ultimate cause — a different thing from the temporal “sending” (πέμπω) and “giving” that the Son plainly does share in the same verse (“whom I will send”). Scripture applies this origin-verb to the Spirit’s relation to the Father and never, in so many words, to the Son.2 Second, the Cappadocian grammar: the East consistently holds that there is one Cause in the Godhead, the Father, lest a second source fracture the divine monarchy — Photios’s “two causes” objection. On this reading John 15:26 is not an isolated verse but the scriptural head of a whole coherent account of the Father as sole ἀρχή (origin).3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. John 15:26 does teach that the Spirit’s procession is from the Father, and the origin-verb ekporeuetai is not predicated of the Son anywhere in the New Testament. So the Eastern claim that the Bible nowhere says “the Spirit proceeds from the Son” is simply correct, and the burden the Latin tradition carries — to show the doctrine as a legitimate inference rather than a stated text — is real. This is why even the modern Roman clarification concedes that the Father is the sole cause (ἐκπορευσις) of the Spirit, and that the Latin processio is a broader word covering the Son’s mediating role, not a rival origin.4

What is overstated or imprecise. The verse does not contain the word “alone.” To assert procession “from the Father” is not, by the ordinary rules of language, to deny it of anyone else — just as “I came out from God” (John 16:27–28) does not deny the Son’s eternal generation. The exclusivity is imported, not read off the text. And the same Farewell Discourse pulls the other way: two verses on, Jesus says of the Spirit, “He shall receive of mine … All things that the Father hath are mine: therefore said I, that he shall take of mine” (John 16:14–15) — the Son’s possession of “all things” the Father has is exactly the hinge Augustine used to argue that whatever the Father gives the Spirit, he gives through the begotten Son.5 (John 20:22, the risen Christ breathing the Spirit on the disciples, is weaker for either side: it describes the mission of the Spirit in time, not the eternal relation, and both traditions grant the Son sends the Spirit temporally.) The honest result is that the single verse settles less than either polemic wants: it is decisive against reading the Filioque out of John 15:26, and inconclusive as a bar against inferring it from the wider witness.

Assessment

Contested. Weighed as an exegetical claim about this verse, the interpretation is strong in its negative form (John 15:26 does not teach double procession, and the origin-verb is proper to the Father) and weak in its positive form (it does not exclude the Son, because “alone” is absent and John 16:14–15 gives the Son a real share in what the Spirit has). The dispute is genuine and turns on prior commitments — the Greek insistence on a single Cause versus the Latin distinction between origin and procession — that the text under-determines. Trust the Eastern point that the Filioque is an inference rather than a quoted text; flag as overreach any claim that the verse by itself refutes double procession. The modern recognition that ekporeusis (from the Father alone) and processio (through the Son) need not be rivals is the most that the exegesis, on its own, will bear.
