The Third Council of Toledo (589) on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: 589 · Wikipedia
Summary: At Toledo the procession clause left the theologians’ pages and entered the recited creed — the first clear creedal trace of the Filioque. Made in Visigothic Spain to seal the Son’s co-equal divinity against Arianism, the council brought the Visigothic kingdom into Catholic orthodoxy and professed the Spirit’s procession from the Father and the Son.

Claims & evidence

Convened by King Reccared I to renounce Arianism (May 589)

The council met at Toledo in May 589 under King Reccared I, its sessions presided over by Leander of Seville. Some sixty-odd bishops attended, among them eight formerly Arian bishops who abjured their error; the assembly brought the Visigoths out of Arianism and into the Catholic faith (council overview).

The recited creed professes the Spirit “from the Father and the Son”

Credo in Spiritum Sanctum qui ex Patre Filioque procedit.

— received Latin text of the creed as professed at the Third Council of Toledo, source. In the received form the Nicene-Constantinopolitan article on the Spirit carries the added word Filioque (“and the Son”) — the doctrine set to music in a creed the whole assembly confessed aloud, not merely a theologian’s formula.

“The first clear trace of the Filioque in the Nicene Creed”

“The first clear trace of the Filioque in the Nicene Creed we find at the third Council of Toledo in Spain, A.D. 589, to seal the triumph of orthodoxy over Arianism.”

— Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, vol. 1, source.

The motive was anti-Arian: asserting the Son’s full equality with the Father

The insertion carried a polemical point. To confess that the Spirit proceeds from the Son too was to assert the Son’s full equality with the Father against Arian subordinationism — if the Son shares with the Father in being a source of the Spirit’s procession, he is no lesser, created being. The Spanish setting and this anti-Arian purpose are what fixed the clause in the West (Schaff, source).

Disputed: whether the words stood in the authentic acts of 589

Whether the Filioque actually stood in the authentic acts of 589, or was inserted into the creed-text later, is genuinely debated. Several scholars — A. E. Burn, Knut Schäferdiek, and Shawn S. Smith — regard the creed-words as a likely later interpolation, placing the secure first creedal appearance at the Eighth Council of Toledo (653); Smith argues the case in “The Insertion of the Filioque into the Nicene Creed and a Letter of Isidore of Seville,” Journal of Early Christian Studies 22.2 (2014), 261–286 (source). Even on this reading the doctrine itself is older in Spain than any creedal recitation: the Fourth Council of Toledo (633) already carries a double-procession profession — “sed procedentem ex Patre et Filio profitemur” (“but we profess him proceeding from the Father and the Son”). Henry R. Percival notes that the early users of the addition “very likely did not know they were adding anything” to the creed (source). In short: the doctrine and its Spanish, anti-Arian origin are secure; it is the exact creedal debut — 589 as received, or 653 — that is contested.

Notes

  • Toledo enacts in a creed the Latin doctrine systematized by Augustine — this is where his double procession is professed aloud rather than argued on the page.
  • The East never accepted the addition; the fixed Eastern reading (“from the Father alone / through the Son”) is set out by John of Damascus.
  • The Spanish clause later fed the imperial push at the Frankish court and toward Rome; see Charlemagne, Aachen (809), and Pope Leo III.
