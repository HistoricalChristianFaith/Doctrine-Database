Photios I of Constantinople on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: c. 867–886 · Wikipedia
Summary: Photios, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote the refutation that framed the Eastern case for a millennium: the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone (John 15:26); the Filioque introduces two causes into the Godhead and destroys the Father’s “Monarchy”; and the addition to the creed is itself illegitimate. His Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit is usually dated to composition c. 885/886, though he had already publicly condemned the Filioque in his encyclical of 867 — the “c. 867–886” span covers both.

Claims & evidence

Two causes destroy the Monarchy of the Father

“if two causes are discerned in the divine, sovereign, and transcendent Trinity, and if the Spirit thus flows from two hypostases, then where is the much-hymned, divine majesty of the Monarchy?”

— On the Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit §11, Myriobiblos text. Photios’s central objection: to make the Spirit proceed from the Father and the Son is to posit two causes (aitiai) in the Godhead, dividing the single source and dissolving the Father’s “Monarchy” — the doctrine that the Father alone is the origin of both Son and Spirit. (The standard scholarly translation is Joseph P. Farrell, The Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit, Holy Cross Orthodox Press, 1987.)

The Second Ecumenical Council dogmatised procession from the Father

“the second of the seven Holy and Ecumenical Synods directly dogmatised that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father.”

— On the Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit §5, Myriobiblos text. Photios grounds the Eastern position in the creed of the First Council of Constantinople (381), which confessed the Spirit “who proceedeth from the Father” — with no “and the Son.” The Latin insertion, on this reckoning, is not merely bad theology but an unauthorized addition to a conciliar creed.

Notes

  • Photios’s charge of “two causes / two principles” is the exact objection the Council of Florence (1439) tried to answer by defining “one principle and a single spiration,” and which Augustine had pre-empted with “one Beginning, not two.” See the doctrine summary.
  • His encyclical of 867 (to the Eastern patriarchs) had already denounced the Filioque, calling it grounds for “a thousand anathemas”; see the encyclical text.
  • On dating: the Mystagogy is generally dated c. 885/886, the encyclical to 867 — the two are commonly conflated, and the “c. 867–886” span here brackets both.
