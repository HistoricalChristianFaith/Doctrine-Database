Augustine of Hippo on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: c. 419 · Wikipedia
Summary: In On the Trinity Augustine gives Latin double procession its classic form — the Spirit proceeds “principally” (principaliter) from the Father, yet also from the Son, as the mutual love and bond of the two. The single word “principally” is the hinge on which the whole later dispute turns.

Claims & evidence

The Spirit proceeds from the Father “principally,” and from the Son also

“And yet it is not to no purpose that in this Trinity the Son and none other is called the Word of God, and the Holy Spirit and none other the Gift of God, and God the Father alone is He from whom the Word is born, and from whom the Holy Spirit principally proceeds. And therefore I have added the word principally, because we find that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son also. But the Father gave Him this too, not as to one already existing, and not yet having it; but whatever He gave to the only-begotten Word, He gave by begetting Him. Therefore He so begat Him as that the common Gift should proceed from Him also, and the Holy Spirit should be the Spirit of both.”

— On the Trinity 15.17.29, trans. Arthur West Haddan (NPNF Series 1, vol. 3), source. This is the locus classicus: the Spirit proceeds from the Father principaliter and “from the Son also,” yet the Son has this only because the Father, in begetting Him, gave it — so the Father remains the primal source.

The Spirit is of both Father and Son, the mutual love of the two

“And the Holy Spirit, according to the Holy Scriptures, is neither of the Father alone, nor of the Son alone, but of both; and so intimates to us a mutual love, wherewith the Father and the Son reciprocally love one another.”

— On the Trinity 15.17.27, trans. Arthur West Haddan (NPNF Series 1, vol. 3), source. The Latin “bond of love” model: the Spirit is what Father and Son have in common, the love uniting them — which is why for Augustine the Spirit must be “of both,” not of the Father alone.

The Spirit proceeds from both, yet from one Beginning, not two

“…it must be admitted that the Father and the Son are a Beginning of the Holy Spirit, not two Beginnings; but as the Father and Son are one God, and one Creator, and one Lord relatively to the creature, so are they one Beginning relatively to the Holy Spirit.”

— On the Trinity 5.14.15, trans. Arthur West Haddan (NPNF Series 1, vol. 3), source. This answers, centuries in advance, the Eastern objection that double procession posits “two causes”: Father and Son spirate the Spirit as one single Beginning, just as they are one God.

The Spirit is the consubstantial communion, most aptly called love

“Therefore the Holy Spirit, whatever it is, is something common both to the Father and Son. But that communion itself is consubstantial and co-eternal; and if it may fitly be called friendship, let it be so called; but it is more aptly called love.”

— On the Trinity 6.5.7, trans. Arthur West Haddan (NPNF Series 1, vol. 3), source. The Spirit as the consubstantial “communion” of Father and Son grounds why He proceeds from both: He just is the love and bond that the two share.

Notes

  • The word principaliter (“principally”) is the hinge of the whole later dispute: it lets the Latins hold both that the Spirit is from the Son and that the Father remains the primal source. This is exactly what the Council of Florence (1439) would later formalize as the Spirit proceeding from Father and Son “as from one principle” — see the Filioque timeline.
  • Against Augustine’s reading, the Greek “from the Father alone” position is fixed by John of Damascus and defended by Photios I of Constantinople.
  • Augustine’s “one Beginning, not two” (5.14.15) already meets, centuries early, Photios’s central charge that the Filioque introduces “two causes” into the Godhead.
