Maximus the Confessor on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: c. 645 (the Letter to Marinus; lifespan c. 580–662) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Greek monk of the still-undivided church, and the author of the one patristic text both East and West would later invoke as the key to reconciliation. When Constantinople faulted a Roman synodal letter for saying the Spirit “proceeds also from the Son,” Maximus defended the Latins by drawing the distinction the whole controversy would turn on: the Romans do not make the Son the cause (aitia) of the Spirit — the Father alone is cause — but use “and the Son” to name the Spirit’s coming-forth through the Son. The Latin Filioque and the Greek dia tou Huiou, he argued, need not conflict.

Claims & evidence

The Romans do not make the Son the cause of the Spirit

“Those of the Queen of cities have attacked the synodal letter of the present very holy Pope [Martin I], not in the case of all the chapters that he has written in it, but only in the case of two of them. One relates to theology, because… he says that ‘the Holy Spirit proceeds (ekporeuesthai) also from the Son.’… With regard to the first matter, they [the Romans] have produced the unanimous documentary evidence of the Latin fathers, and also of Cyril of Alexandria, from the sacred commentary he composed on the gospel of St. John. On the basis of these texts, they have shown that they have not made the Son the cause of the Spirit — they know in fact that the Father is the only cause of the Son and the Spirit, the one by begetting and the other by procession; but [they use this expression] in order to manifest the Spirit’s coming-forth (proienai) through him and, in this way, to make clear the unity and identity of the essence.”

— Letter to Marinus of Cyprus (Opusculum 10; PG 91:134D–136C), trans. Peter Gilbert, De unione ecclesiarum. This is the hinge of the whole letter. Maximus reports that when the Greeks objected to the Latin “and the Son,” the Romans answered by producing the Latin fathers and the Greek Cyril of Alexandria — and Maximus judges the answer sound: the Latins are not positing a second source of the Spirit’s being, only expressing that the Spirit comes forth through the Son. The Father alone is aitia.

Two Greek verbs, one Latin word: ekporeusis vs. proienai

The distinction Maximus presses is lexical as well as theological. Greek keeps two verbs apart: ekporeuesthai (ekporeusis) names the Spirit’s hypostatic origin — the coming-into-being of the person, which is from the Father alone (the verb of John 15:26); proienai (proodos, Latin procedere) names the broader “coming-forth” or procession that can rightly be said to be through the Son. Latin has only the one word procedere for both, so a Latin “proceeds from the Father and the Son” does not carry the Greek claim that the Spirit’s very origin is doubled. Maximus even asks the Romans to translate their phrase more carefully to avoid the confusion, and adds that they “cannot express their meaning in a language and idiom that are foreign to them as precisely as they can in their own mother-tongue, any more than we can do.”

“But, in accordance with your request, I have asked the Romans to translate what is peculiar to them in such a way that any obscurities that may result from it will be avoided… One should also keep in mind that they cannot express their meaning in a language and idiom that are foreign to them as precisely as they can in their own mother-tongue, any more than we can do.”

— Letter to Marinus (PG 91:136), trans. Peter Gilbert, De unione ecclesiarum. This is exactly the move that John of Damascus would systematize a century later (“of the Son” and “through the Son” — yes; “from the Son” as source — no) and that the two-causes objection would later sharpen against the crude Latin form.

The Spirit belongs to the Son and proceeds through Him from the Father

“Just as the Holy Spirit exists, by nature according to substance, as belonging to the Father, so also does he, by nature according to substance, belong to the Son, in that, in an ineffable way, he proceeds substantially from the Father through the begotten Son.”

— Questions to Thalassius 63 (PG 90:672), as translated in the Pontifical Council’s The Greek and Latin Traditions Regarding the Procession of the Holy Spirit (1995). The parallel outside the Letter to Marinus: the Spirit is genuinely “of the Son,” yet the Son is the one through whom — not from whom as a distinct cause — the Spirit proceeds from the Father. This is the same grammar the Cappadocians had used (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa) and the language Cyril supplied to the Latins.

Notes

  • The great bridge text. The Letter to Marinus is the single most-cited patristic passage in the reconciliation literature: it was quarried at the Council of Florence (1438–39) — whose formula “from the Father through the Son… according to the Greeks indeed as cause, and according to the Latins as principle” is essentially Maximus’s distinction — and again in the 1995 Roman clarification and the modern Catholic–Orthodox dialogue. Maximus’s point is the answer to Photios: the Son is not a second cause. See the argument on whether the Filioque introduces “two causes”.
  • A shared witness. Maximus also appears on this wiki as the great defender of Christ’s two wills against Monothelitism — see Maximus the Confessor on the person of Christ.
  • Authenticity and scope, honestly noted. A minority of scholars have questioned whether the procession section of the letter is authentic Maximus or a later Latin addition; more weighty is the caution of A. Edward Siecienski (The Filioque: History of a Doctrinal Controversy, OUP 2010) that the letter shows Maximus reconciling “from the Son” only in the sense of proienai/manifestation — it does not underwrite a double ekporeusis of the Spirit’s very origin from Father and Son as from one principle. So the text is a genuine bridge on the East’s own terms (the Father is sole cause), not a seventh-century Greek endorsement of the developed Latin dogma. Read that way it fits the Eastern spine (John of Damascus) rather than breaking with it.
