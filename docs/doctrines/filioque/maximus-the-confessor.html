Maximus the Confessor on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: c. 645 (the Letter to Marinus; lifespan c. 580–662) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Greek monk writing c. 645–646. When Constantinople faulted a Roman synodal letter for saying the Spirit “proceeds also from the Son,” Maximus reported the Roman answer: the Romans do not make the Son the cause (aitia) of the Spirit — the Father is the only cause — but use the expression to manifest the Spirit’s coming-forth (proienai) through the Son. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity cited the letter in 1995.

Claims & evidence

Maximus’s report of the Roman answer

“Those of the Queen of cities have attacked the synodal letter of the present very holy Pope [Martin I], not in the case of all the chapters that he has written in it, but only in the case of two of them. One relates to theology, because… he says that ‘the Holy Spirit proceeds (ekporeuesthai) also from the Son.’… With regard to the first matter, they [the Romans] have produced the unanimous documentary evidence of the Latin fathers, and also of Cyril of Alexandria, from the sacred commentary he composed on the gospel of St. John. On the basis of these texts, they have shown that they have not made the Son the cause of the Spirit — they know in fact that the Father is the only cause of the Son and the Spirit, the one by begetting and the other by procession; but [they use this expression] in order to manifest the Spirit’s coming-forth (proienai) through him and, in this way, to make clear the unity and identity of the essence.”

— Letter to Marinus of Cyprus (Opusculum 10; PG 91:134D–136C), anonymous translation (first published at praiseofglory.alabanza.com), revised by Peter Gilbert, De unione ecclesiarum, who notes that he does not know “the identity of the translator.” The bracketed “Martin I” is the translator’s; in the comments there Gilbert writes of Maximus “defending Pope Martin I (or, perhaps, Pope Theodore I).” Maximus reports that when the Greeks objected to the Latin “and the Son,” the Romans answered by producing the Latin fathers and the Greek Cyril of Alexandria, and he accepts their explanation: on his account the Romans do not make the Son the cause of the Spirit, but express that the Spirit comes forth through the Son, the Father being the only cause.

Two Greek verbs, one Latin word: ekporeusis vs. proienai

The letter turns on two Greek verbs: ekporeuesthai (the verb of John 15:26, used in the Greek objection) and proienai (“to come forth”), while Latin has the one word procedere. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity’s 1995 clarification reads the letter this way: “According to St. Maximus, echoing Rome, the Filioque does not concern the ekporeusis of the Spirit issued from the Father as source of the Trinity, but manifests his proienai (processio) in the consubstantial communion of the Father and the Son” (source). Maximus also asks the Romans to translate their phrase carefully, and adds that they “cannot express their meaning in a language and idiom that are foreign to them as precisely as they can in their own mother-tongue, any more than we can do.”

“But, in accordance with your request, I have asked the Romans to translate what is peculiar to them in such a way that any obscurities that may result from it will be avoided… One should also keep in mind that they cannot express their meaning in a language and idiom that are foreign to them as precisely as they can in their own mother-tongue, any more than we can do.”

— Letter to Marinus (PG 91:136), anonymous translation revised by Peter Gilbert, De unione ecclesiarum.

Maximus on the Spirit as belonging to the Son and proceeding “through the begotten Son”

“Just as the Holy Spirit exists, by nature according to substance, as belonging to the Father, so also does he, by nature according to substance, belong to the Son, in that, in an ineffable way, he proceeds substantially from the Father through the begotten Son.”

— Questions to Thalassius 63 (PG 90:672), as translated in the Pontifical Council’s The Greek and Latin Traditions Regarding the Procession of the Holy Spirit (1995). A parallel outside the Letter to Marinus: Maximus writes that the Spirit belongs “to the Son” and proceeds “from the Father through the begotten Son.” Compare the “through the Son” language of Basil, Gregory of Nyssa and Cyril.

A. Edward Siecienski, in his dissertation (Fordham University, 2006), reads the letter as “a clear explication of Maximus’s own trinitarian thinking and the consensus patrum as it existed in the seventh century (i.e., the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father and his eternal flowing forth through the Son).” ⚠ unverified: his later book, The Filioque: History of a Doctrinal Controversy (OUP 2010), not opened here, is reported to read the letter as reconciling “from the Son” only in the sense of proienai, not as a double ekporeusis of the Spirit’s origin from Father and Son as from one principle.
