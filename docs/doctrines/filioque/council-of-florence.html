The Council of Florence (1439) on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: 1439 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Council of Florence, meeting with Greek representatives, defined in Laetentur Caeli (1439) that the Spirit proceeds from Father and Son “as from one principle and a single spiration,” read the Greek “through the Son” and the Latin “from the Son” as one faith, and held the addition to the creed lawful. Mark of Ephesus refused to sign, and the union was not received in the East.

Claims & evidence

Double procession “as from one principle and a single spiration”

“the holy Spirit is eternally from the Father and the Son … proceeds from both eternally as from one principle and a single spiration.”

— Laetentur Caeli, the Bull of Union with the Greeks (6 July 1439), Pope Eugenius IV, trans. Norman Tanner, source (cf. Papal Encyclicals text). Percival (1899) quotes the council’s definition that the Latins “assert that there is one only principle, one only spiration,” in answer to the Greek concern that they held procession “from the Father and the Son, as from two principles and by two spirations,” and takes the definition as “a true and full expression of the teaching of the West” (source). Theodore Stylianopoulos (Greek Orthodox, 1986) holds that “in the context of the Creed the filioque formula inescapably confesses a joint cause (Father ‘and’ Son) in the Spirit’s origin,” that the que “posits the Son, along with the Father, as the source or cause of the Spirit,” and that ascribing joint cause to the Son blurs Father and Son “into a single, unthinkable Father-Son person, which was Mark of Ephesos’ sharp criticism of the filioque at the Council of Florence” (“The Filioque: Dogma, Theologoumenon or Error?” Greek Orthodox Theological Review 31 (1986), citing Markos A. Orphanos; online transcription).

“Through the Son” and “from the Son” read as one faith

“when holy doctors and fathers say that the holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son, this bears the sense that thereby also the Son should be signified, according to the Greeks indeed as cause, and according to the Latins as principle of the subsistence of the holy Spirit.”

— Laetentur Caeli (6 July 1439), trans. Norman Tanner, source. The bull reads the two idioms — the Greek dia tou Huiou and the Latin Filioque — as expressing one faith in different words. It also defines that “the explanation of those words ‘and from the Son’ was licitly and reasonably added to the creed for the sake of declaring the truth and from imminent need” (source).

Mark of Ephesus refused to sign; the union was not received in the East

Mark of Ephesus (Mark Eugenikos) was the one bishop present who refused to sign the decree of union (source). The New Catholic Encyclopedia reports that he declared the addition to the creed “the cause of the schism and also illegal because it was forbidden by the Council of Ephesus” (source). ⚠ unverified: Emperor John VIII Palaiologos is widely reported to have remarked that the union had accomplished nothing since Mark had not signed; this line has not been checked against a primary source here. The North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation (2003) records that the decisions of Florence “were never received by a representative body of bishops or faithful in the East, and were formally rejected in Constantinople in 1484” (source).
