Charlemagne, the Council of Aachen (809), and Pope Leo III on the procession of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque)

Dates: 809 · Wikipedia
Summary: The controversy became a public quarrel under Charlemagne. His court pressed the creedal addition on a reluctant Rome, and Pope Leo III — who agreed with the doctrine — refused to add the words to the creed, hanging the creed without them on two silver shields in St. Peter’s. The West thus split its own mind: doctrine received, creedal interpolation resisted by the very see that later championed it.

Claims & evidence

c. 808: Greek monks in Jerusalem charge the Franks with heresy for chanting the Filioque

Around 808 Greek monks in Jerusalem accused Frankish monks at the monastery on the Mount of Olives of heresy for chanting the creed with the Filioque, the dispute reaching both the pope and the Frankish court (source).

809: the Council of Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) affirms the doctrine and sends envoys to Rome

“In 809 a council was held at Aix-la-Chapelle by Charlemagne, and from it three divines were sent to confer with the Pope, Leo III, upon the subject. The Pope opposed the insertion of the Filioque on the express ground that the General Councils had forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary.”

— Henry R. Percival, “Historical Excursus on the introduction into the Creed of the words ‘and the Son,’” in The Seven Ecumenical Councils (NPNF2 vol. 14), source. Leo objected not to the doctrine but to altering the conciliar creed — the ecumenical councils had forbidden additions to the formulary.

The two silver shields in St. Peter’s: the creed hung without the addition

“he presented two silver shields to the Confessio in St. Peter’s at Rome, on one of which was engraved the creed in Latin and on the other in Greek, without the addition.”

— Percival, The Seven Ecumenical Councils (NPNF2 vol. 14), source. Leo’s gesture displayed the uninterpolated creed in both languages — a public act of restraint by a pope who held the doctrine yet would not touch the words.

Reported: the shields are said to have carried a personal inscription by Leo

⚠ unverified: reference works widely report that the shields bore the line “Haec Leo posui amore et cautela orthodoxae fidei” (“These I, Leo, set up for love and safeguard of the orthodox faith”). This wording is repeated in secondary literature but is not primary-verified here and should be treated as reported, not as a confirmed inscription.

1014: the interpolated creed is first sung at Mass in Rome

“It was not till 1014 that for the first time the interpolated creed was used at mass with the sanction of the Pope. In that year Benedict VIII. acceded to the urgent request of Henry II. of Germany and so the papal authority was forced to yield.”

— Percival, The Seven Ecumenical Councils (NPNF2 vol. 14), source. Two centuries after Leo’s refusal, Rome itself sang the interpolated creed — at the urging of Emperor Henry II, and again on imperial pressure rather than papal initiative.

Notes

  • The episode shows the West divided against itself: the doctrine was received, but the creedal addition was resisted by Rome — the very see that would later champion it. Leo’s objection was procedural (no additions to the conciliar creed), not doctrinal.
  • The words had already entered a recited creed in Visigothic Spain; see the Third Council of Toledo (589).
  • The Eastern answer came a generation later with Photios I of Constantinople.
