The Byzantine and Latin diffusion, down to the Golden Legend

Dates: Paul the Deacon (late 8th c.); Symeon Metaphrastes (10th c.); Golden Legend (c. 1260) · Golden Legend text
Summary: From the Syriac, Greek, and Latin roots the legend spread through both halves of Christendom. In the East it was retold by Symeon Metaphrastes and kept in the synaxaria; in the West it descended through Paul the Deacon and the medieval collections to Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend, the book that fixed the story for late-medieval Europe.

Claims & evidence

The Greek chronicle notices: Theophanes and George the Monk (9th c.)

The earliest surviving Greek attestation of the legend is not a narrative at all but a bare chronicle notice. Theophanes the Confessor, in his Chronographia (c. 810–814), enters the awakening in the same year he treats Nestorius and the run-up to the Council of Ephesus, and gives only the fact and a figure — assuming his readers already know the story:

“In the same year in Ephesos there occurred the remarkable miracle of the seven holy youths who arose after 184 years.”

Two things are worth noting. First, Theophanes has his own arithmetic — “184 years,” not the 195 years or “38th year of Theodosius II” of the Greek and Syriac Passion — another instance of the figures that never quite settle. Second, that a ninth-century Constantinopolitan chronicle can dispatch the miracle in a single sentence shows how thoroughly the tale was by then common property; the editors note it was “known in both East and West by the 6th cent., being recorded by Jacob of Sarug (d. 521) and Gregory of Tours.” The same notice recurs, expanded, in George the Monk (Hamartolos), whose ninth-century Chronicon also records “the awakening in Ephesus of those persecuted under Decius” — drawing, like Theophanes, on the earlier Greek chronicle tradition (and, some argue, on the lost epitome of Theodore Lector).

— Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, AM 5931; tr. Cyril Mango & Roger Scott, The Chronicle of Theophanes Confessor (Oxford, 1997), 137 (commentary note, with the editors’ parallels to George the Monk 498.18 and Michael the Syrian).

The Byzantine line: Symeon Metaphrastes (10th c.)

The polished Byzantine narrative form comes a century later. In the Greek East the tale was retold by Symeon Metaphrastes in his tenth-century menologion — his version is the standard Byzantine Passion — and kept in the synaxaria of the church, which commemorate the sleepers on 4 August (and, with the tradition of their “second sleep,” on 22 October). The Metaphrastic recension gives the Greek names: Maximillian, Jamblichos, Martin, John, Dionysios, Exakostodianos, and Antoninos.

— Symeon Metaphrastes, in Patrologia Graeca 115, 427–448 (Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca 1594); the synaxarion commemorations are ed. H. Delehaye, Synaxarium Ecclesiae Constantinopolitanae (Brussels, 1902).

The Latin line to the Golden Legend

In the West, Gregory’s Passio and the Greek fed Paul the Deacon’s History of the Lombards, an Anglo-Norman poem (Chardry’s Li set dormanz), an Old Norse fragment, and the great thirteenth-century collections. Jacobus de Voragine gave the tale its most influential Western form in the Golden Legend (Legenda aurea, c. 1260), read across Europe and later Englished by Caxton. Voragine records that two Christians, Theodorus and Rufinus, wrote the martyrs’ story and laid it among the stones that sealed the cave, and that the sleepers, once recognised, asked to be left lying in the earth until the general resurrection:

“And in the same night they appeared to the emperor, and said to him that he should suffer them to lie on the earth like as they had lain before till that time that our Lord had raised them, unto the time that they should rise again.”

An arithmetic that will not close

Voragine fixes their waking at “the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight” and reports the tradition that they slept 362 years — then, unusually for a hagiographer, checks the sum against the reign-dates and openly admits it does not work:

“It is in doubt of that which is said that they slept three hundred and sixty-two years, for they were raised the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight, and Decius reigned but one year and three months, and that was in the year of our Lord two hundred and seventy, and so they slept but two hundred and eight years.”

The candor is worth noting: the medieval West’s standard telling carries within it an acknowledgment that the figures do not add up — one strand of the wider doubt that the legend’s historicity attracted.

— Jacobus de Voragine, Golden Legend, tr. Caxton, “The Seven Sleepers.”

Notes

  • Two name-sets, one story. The Greek and Latin lines carry different rosters of names — a visible fingerprint of independent transmission from a common source, and part of why the count and identities were never quite stable.
  • The Greek witnesses are all late. Though the legend’s Greek-speaking Ephesian setting has led some scholars to posit a lost Greek original, every Greek text we actually possess — Theophanes (c. 810), George the Monk, then Metaphrastes and the synaxaria — is centuries younger than the Syriac. That asymmetry bears on the Syriac-vs-Greek priority question.
