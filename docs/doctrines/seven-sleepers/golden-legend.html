The Byzantine and Latin diffusion, down to the Golden Legend

Dates: Paul the Deacon (late 8th c.); Symeon Metaphrastes (10th c.); Golden Legend (c. 1260) · Golden Legend text
Summary: From the Syriac, Greek, and Latin roots the legend spread through both halves of Christendom. In the East it was retold by Symeon Metaphrastes and kept in the synaxaria; in the West it descended through Paul the Deacon and the medieval collections to Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend, the book that fixed the story for late-medieval Europe.

Claims & evidence

The Byzantine line

In the Greek East the tale was retold by Symeon Metaphrastes in his tenth-century menologion — his version (under 4 August) is the polished Byzantine form — and kept in the synaxaria of the church. The Metaphrastic recension gives the Greek names: Maximillian, Jamblichos, Martin, John, Dionysios, Exakostodianos, and Antoninos.

— Symeon Metaphrastes, in Patrologia Graeca 115, 427–448.

The Latin line to the Golden Legend

In the West, Gregory’s Passio and the Greek fed Paul the Deacon’s History of the Lombards, an Anglo-Norman poem (Chardry’s Li set dormanz), an Old Norse fragment, and the great thirteenth-century collections. Jacobus de Voragine gave the tale its most influential Western form in the Golden Legend (Legenda aurea, c. 1260), read across Europe and later Englished by Caxton. Voragine records that two Christians, Theodorus and Rufinus, wrote the martyrs’ story and laid it among the stones that sealed the cave, and that the sleepers, once recognised, asked to be left lying in the earth until the general resurrection:

“And in the same night they appeared to the emperor, and said to him that he should suffer them to lie on the earth like as they had lain before till that time that our Lord had raised them, unto the time that they should rise again.”

An arithmetic that will not close

Voragine fixes their waking at “the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight” and reports the tradition that they slept 362 years — then, unusually for a hagiographer, checks the sum against the reign-dates and openly admits it does not work:

“It is in doubt of that which is said that they slept three hundred and sixty-two years, for they were raised the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight, and Decius reigned but one year and three months, and that was in the year of our Lord two hundred and seventy, and so they slept but two hundred and eight years.”

The candor is worth noting: the medieval West’s standard telling carries within it an acknowledgment that the figures do not add up — one strand of the wider doubt that the legend’s historicity attracted.

— Jacobus de Voragine, Golden Legend, tr. Caxton, “The Seven Sleepers.”
