The Syriac tradition: the Story of the Youths of Ephesus and Jacob of Serugh

Dates: Syriac Story by the later 5th c.; Jacob of Serugh’s homily before 521 · Cult of Saints record E02525
Summary: The earliest extant witnesses to the legend are in the Christian East. A Syriac prose Story of the Youths of Ephesus may have been circulating by the later fifth century (Witold Witakowski, GEDSH: "perhaps first attested by the ms. Saint-Petersburg no. 4 of the 5th cent., if M. van Esbroeck’s dating is accepted"), and the West-Syrian poet Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521) devoted to the legend a verse homily — the earliest extant treatment dated by a named author. In the Syriac telling the sleepers are presented as living proof of the resurrection of the body.

Claims & evidence

The Syriac Story of the Youths of Ephesus

The narrative core is fixed early. During the persecution of the emperor Decius (r. 249–251) a group of young Christians of Ephesus refuse to sacrifice, withdraw to a cave in the mountainside, and fall asleep; the emperor orders the cave sealed. Generations later — under a Christian empire, in the reign of Theodosius II — the cave is opened, the youths wake supposing they have slept a single night, one goes down to the city to buy bread with coin two centuries out of date, and the astonished city (and the emperor) recognize the miracle before the sleepers lie down and die. The Syriac tradition carries the whole shape of the story as the later Greek, Latin, and Western versions also tell it.

Jacob of Serugh’s verse homily (mēmrā)

Jacob of Serugh (Ya‘qub of Serugh), a Syriac poet, composed a metrical homily on the youths — the earliest extant literary treatment attached to a named author, who died in 521; Witakowski (GEDSH) dates it to "the beginning of the 6th cent." and notes that it "includes all the elements known from the later prose texts." Jacob does not dwell on miracles worked by the saints or on appeals for their intercession; his interest is doctrinal. The sleep is God’s own act — he “sealed their senses” and preserved their bodies whole — performed so that, waking, the youths would be a standing refutation of those who deny that the dead rise. In the homily the wonder serves as the argument: a bodily resurrection shown in advance, in the flesh.

— Jacob of Serugh, Homily on the Youths of Ephesus; Syriac text ed. I. Guidi, “Testi orientali inediti sopra i Sette Dormienti di Efeso” (1884), 358–369; English tr. Sebastian P. Brock, “Jacob of Serugh’s Poem on the Sleepers of Ephesus,” in “I Sowed Fruits into Hearts”: Festschrift for Michael Lattke (2007), 13–30.

Syriac or Greek first?

Whether the very first written form of the legend was this Syriac Story or a now-lost Greek account written at Ephesus itself is a long-running scholarly dispute. Witold Witakowski (GEDSH) reports Theodor Nöldeke (1886) and A. Allgeier (1916–18) as holding that "the original was composed in Syriac," and Paul Peeters (1923) as arguing "that the original must have been Greek"; Witakowski himself writes that the legend "was originally, as it seems, composed in Greek." His bibliography also lists Michael Huber, Die Wanderlegende von den Siebenschläfern (1910), and Ernest Honigmann, “Stephen of Ephesus (April 15, 448 – Oct. 29, 451) and the Legend of the Seven Sleepers” (1953), whose positions the entry does not state. On either view the legend’s first written form belongs to the Christian East, before it reached the Latin West.

— Witold Witakowski, “Sleepers of Ephesus, Legend of the,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage.

Reception and debate
