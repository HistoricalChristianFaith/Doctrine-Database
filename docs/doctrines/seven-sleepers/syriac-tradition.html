The Syriac tradition: the Story of the Youths of Ephesus and Jacob of Serugh

Dates: Syriac Story by the later 5th c.; Jacob of Serugh’s homily before 521 · Cult of Saints record E02525
Summary: The oldest witnesses to the legend stand in the Christian East. A Syriac prose Story of the Youths of Ephesus was circulating by the later fifth century, and the West-Syrian poet Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521) turned it into a verse homily — the earliest treatment we can date by a named author. In the Syriac telling the sleepers are the church’s living proof of the resurrection of the body.

Claims & evidence

The Syriac Story of the Youths of Ephesus

The narrative core is fixed early and stays remarkably stable. During the persecution of the emperor Decius (r. 249–251) a group of young Christians of Ephesus refuse to sacrifice, withdraw to a cave in the mountainside, and fall asleep; the emperor orders the cave sealed. Generations later — under a Christian empire, in the reign of Theodosius II — the cave is opened, the youths wake supposing they have slept a single night, one goes down to the city to buy bread with coin two centuries out of date, and the astonished city (and the emperor) recognize the miracle before the sleepers lie down and die. The Syriac tradition already carries the whole shape of the story that every later version — Greek, Latin, Qur’anic, and Western — would inherit.

Jacob of Serugh’s verse homily (mēmrā)

Jacob of Serugh (Ya‘qub of Serugh), one of the great Syriac poets, composed a metrical homily on the youths — the only surviving specimen of the legend’s liturgical commemoration from late antiquity, and the earliest datable literary treatment we can attach to a named author who died in 521. Jacob does not dwell on miracles worked by the saints or on appeals for their intercession; his interest is doctrinal. The sleep is God’s own act — he “sealed their senses” and preserved their bodies whole — performed so that, waking, the youths would be a standing refutation of those who deny that the dead rise. The wonder is the argument: a bodily resurrection shown in advance, in the flesh.

— Jacob of Serugh, Homily on the Youths of Ephesus; Syriac text ed. I. Guidi, “Testi orientali inediti sopra i Sette Dormienti di Efeso” (1884), 358–369; English tr. Sebastian P. Brock, “Jacob of Serugh’s Poem on the Sleepers of Ephesus,” in “I Sowed Fruits into Hearts”: Festschrift for Michael Lattke (2007), 13–30.

Syriac or Greek first?

Whether the very first written form of the legend was this Syriac Story or a now-lost Greek account written at Ephesus itself is a long-running scholarly dispute. Ignazio Guidi, Theodor Nöldeke and others held for Syriac priority; Michael Huber, Paul Peeters and Ernest Honigmann argued that the origin was Greek — Honigmann tying the first history to Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451). What is not in doubt is that the tale was fully formed in the Christian East a century before it reached the Latin West.

— S. P. Brock, “Sleepers of Ephesus, Legend of the,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage.

Notes

  • Why the resurrection frame matters. The fifth century saw sharp disputes over the resurrection of the body; the sleepers, waking bodily after two hundred years, were a purpose-built icon for that debate. The early function of the legend was frankly didactic — a point the historicity argument turns on.
