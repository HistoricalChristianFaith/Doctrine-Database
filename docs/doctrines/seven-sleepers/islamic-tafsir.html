The Islamic elaboration: the commentators, the dog Qiṭmīr, and the number

Dates: classical tafsīr, 8th–10th c. onward · background
Summary: Because Sūrat al-Kahf tells the story allusively, the Muslim commentators (tafsīr) — al-Ṭabarī chief among them — filled it out from the surrounding lore, supplying names, a king, a named dog, and a resolution of the number and the years. The sleepers became figures of Muslim devotion and protection.

Claims & evidence

Filling in the gaps

The Qur’anic account leaves the persecutor, the place, and the number unstated, and the commentators supplied them from the wider tradition. The persecuting king is named Daqyānūs (Decianus — the emperor Decius of the Christian legend); the city is identified with Ephesus (Afsūs); and the Qur’anic caution against quarrelling over the count (18:22) is generally resolved, in the commentaries, in favour of seven sleepers — the same total the Christian tradition had settled on.

— See al-Ṭabarī, Jāmiʿ al-bayān (Tafsīr) on Sūrat al-Kahf, and the summary in the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus.”

The dog Qiṭmīr

The sleepers’ dog, mentioned twice in the sura (18:18, 22), is honoured in the tradition with the name Qiṭmīr and is counted among the very few animals admitted to Paradise. Its name became a byword and a blessing: written on letters and documents, or worn as an amulet, “Qiṭmīr” was held to bring protection — a part of popular Muslim piety.

Three hundred years, and nine

The Qur’anic term of “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine” (18:25) was harmonized by the commentators as three hundred solar years, equivalent to three hundred and nine lunar years, reconciling the two figures. (The Christian Golden Legend also discusses the span, arriving at a different total.)

Cult and caves

As in Christendom, the legend attached itself to places. A cave of the Companions is shown at Ephesus, another at ar-Raqīm near Amman in Jordan (often linked to the Qur’anic al-raqīm of 18:9, read by some as a place-name), and still others were claimed across the Islamic world. The sleepers were invoked for protection and their story read for blessing — a devotion Louis Massignon documented at length. What al-raqīm means — “the inscription” or a place-name — is disputed: see the question of al-raqīm.

— Louis Massignon, Les sept dormants d’Éphèse (Ahl al-Kahf) en Islam et en chrétienté (Paris, 1955–1963).

Reception and debate

  • Elaboration on both sides. The commentators name the unnamed, date the undated, and resolve the open number in the terse Qur’anic text, as the Christian Passio-writers did with the legend. Whether the Qur’anic account draws on the Christian legend is disputed: see the question of the Qur’anic account’s source.
