Modern scholarship: the wandering folktale traced, the origins argued

Dates: Baronius (16th c.) → Koch (1883), Huber (1910), Honigmann (1953), Massignon (1955–63) · Catholic Encyclopedia survey
Summary: Historical doubt about the story is itself old — Baronius questioned it in the sixteenth century — and modern scholarship set the Ephesus legend among the world’s wandering “sleeper” tales, traced its diffusion, and argued out where the literary version began.

Claims & evidence

Baronius’s doubt

When Cardinal Cesare Baronius edited the Roman Martyrology in the sixteenth century, he was already sceptical of the legend’s historicity — the first, but not the last, to say so. The tale’s popularity in East and West had never depended on its being taken as sober history; its power was as a sign.

— 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus.”

A wandering folktale

Comparative study placed the Seven Sleepers within a broad international tale-type: the hero (or holy man) who sleeps for generations and wakes into a changed world. Its kin include the Jewish Ḥoni ha-Ma‘agel and the Baruch/Abimelech legend, the Greek Epimenides, and, in the modern West, Rip Van Winkle. John Koch’s Die Siebenschläferlegende (1883) and Michael Huber’s Die Wanderlegende von den Siebenschläfern (1910) — the very title means “the wandering legend” — charted the story’s spread across languages and centuries.

— J. Koch, Die Siebenschläferlegende, ihr Ursprung und ihre Verbreitung (Leipzig, 1883); M. Huber, Die Wanderlegende von den Siebenschläfern (Leipzig, 1910).

Where did it begin?

Scholars divided over the literary origin. Ignazio Guidi, Theodor Nöldeke and others argued that the earliest form was Syriac; Michael Huber, Paul Peeters, and Ernest Honigmann held for a Greek original written near Ephesus. Honigmann’s influential study (1953) tied the first history to a specific figure and moment — Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451) — placing the legend’s composition amid a live fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body. The question is weighed on its own page.

— E. Honigmann, “Stephen of Ephesus (April 15, 448 – Oct. 29, 451) and the Legend of the Seven Sleepers,” in Patristic Studies (Studi e Testi 173; Vatican City, 1953), 125–168.

A hyphen between two faiths

Louis Massignon devoted a series of studies (1955–1963) to the cult of the sleepers across both Christianity and Islam — from Ephesus to Brittany’s Vieux-Marché pilgrimage to North Africa — reading them as “anticipatory witnesses of the Resurrection” and as a rare living trait d’union, a hyphen, joining Christendom and Islam in a single devotion.

— Louis Massignon, Les sept dormants d’Éphèse (Ahl al-Kahf) en Islam et en chrétienté (Paris, 1955–1963).
