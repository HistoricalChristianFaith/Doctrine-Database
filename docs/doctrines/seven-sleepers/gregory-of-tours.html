Gregory of Tours brings the legend to the Latin West

Dates: Passio c. 573–587; Glory of the Martyrs 94, c. 580–594 · Cult of Saints record E05364
Summary: Gregory of Tours (538–594) wrote the earliest extant Latin version of the legend — a short notice in his Glory of the Martyrs and a full Passio of twelve chapters — and credits an Eastern source, a Syrian who interpreted the text for him to render into Latin. With Gregory the Western names and outline of the story are set.

Claims & evidence

The two accounts

Gregory tells the story twice. In the Glory of the Martyrs (ch. 94) he gives a compressed notice; separately there survives under his name a Passio sanctorum septem dormientium apud Ephesum in twelve short chapters, the fuller narrative to which the shorter notice refers. Both tell how seven young men of prominent Ephesian families are examined under Decius, refuse to sacrifice, hide in a cave, and are sealed in; they wake under Theodosius, bear witness, and die. The Oxford Cult of Saints summary of the Passio names them Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, John, and Serapion; Adrian Fortescue’s 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia article gives for Gregory a different set, “Achillides, Diomedes, Diogenus, Probatus, Stephanus, Sambatus, and Quiriacus” (see the divergent rosters).

— Latin texts ed. B. Krusch, MGH Scriptores rerum Merovingicarum I.2 (1969): the Passio at 398–403, Glory of the Martyrs 94 at 100–101. Names of the Passio as summarized at the Oxford Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity, record E05364; A. Fortescue, 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus.”

“Interpreted by a certain Syrian”

Gregory states how he, a Gaulish bishop, came to know an Ephesian legend. The fuller account, he says, is the one “which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin”:

Quod passio eorum, quam Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus, plenius pandit.

(“The record of their suffering, which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin, gives a fuller account.”)

The colophon of the Passio itself names the collaborator: …translata in Latinum per Gregorium episcopum, interpretante Iohanne Syro — “rendered into Latin by Bishop Gregory, interpreted by John the Syrian.” Gregory thus records that his Latin came from the Christian East through a Syriac-speaking intermediary (see the Syriac tradition, and the question of the earliest written form).

— Gregory of Tours, Glory of the Martyrs 94, tr. Raymond Van Dam, Gregory of Tours: Glory of the Martyrs (Liverpool University Press, 1988); cf. the Oxford Cult of Saints records E00647 and E05364.

Onward into Western hagiography

From Gregory the legend entered the Latin mainstream. Paul the Deacon retold it in his History of the Lombards (late 8th c.), and from there and from the Greek it descended through the medieval collections to the Golden Legend and the vernacular retellings.

Reception and debate

  • An acknowledged translation. Gregory names his Syrian interpreter, recording the route by which an Eastern Christian legend passed into Latin through a bilingual intermediary. His colophon figures in the question of the divergent name-rosters.
