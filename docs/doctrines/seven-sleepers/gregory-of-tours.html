Gregory of Tours brings the legend to the Latin West

Dates: Passio c. 573–587; Glory of the Martyrs 94, c. 580–594 · Cult of Saints record E05364
Summary: Gregory of Tours (538–594) gave the Latin West its first version of the legend — a short notice in his Glory of the Martyrs and a full Passio of twelve chapters — and openly credits an Eastern source, a Syrian who interpreted the text for him to render into Latin. With Gregory the Western names and outline of the story are set.

Claims & evidence

The two accounts

Gregory tells the story twice. In the Glory of the Martyrs (ch. 94) he gives a compressed notice; separately there survives under his name a Passio sanctorum septem dormientium apud Ephesum in twelve short chapters, the fuller narrative to which the shorter notice refers. In both, seven young men of prominent Ephesian families — Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, John, and Serapion — are examined under Decius, refuse to sacrifice, hide in a cave, and are sealed in; they wake under Theodosius, bear witness, and die.

— Latin texts ed. B. Krusch, MGH Scriptores rerum Merovingicarum I.2 (1969): the Passio at 398–403, Glory of the Martyrs 94 at 100–101.

“Interpreted by a certain Syrian”

Gregory is unusually frank about how a Gaulish bishop came to know an Ephesian legend. The fuller account, he says, is the one “which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin”:

Quod passio eorum, quam Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus, plenius pandit.

(“The record of their suffering, which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin, gives a fuller account.”)

The colophon of the Passio itself names the collaborator: …translata in Latinum per Gregorium episcopum, interpretante Iohanne Syro — “rendered into Latin by Bishop Gregory, interpreted by John the Syrian.” The debt is thus explicit: the Latin West received the legend from the Christian East, through a Syriac-speaking intermediary, exactly as the Syriac evidence would lead one to expect.

— Gregory of Tours, Glory of the Martyrs 94, tr. Raymond Van Dam, Gregory of Tours: Glory of the Martyrs (Liverpool University Press, 1988); cf. the Oxford Cult of Saints records E00647 and E05364.

Onward into Western hagiography

From Gregory the legend entered the Latin mainstream. Paul the Deacon retold it in his History of the Lombards (late 8th c.), and from there and from the Greek it descended through the medieval collections to the Golden Legend and the vernacular retellings.
