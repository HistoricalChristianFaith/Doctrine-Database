The Qur’an: the Companions of the Cave (Aṣḥāb al-Kahf)

Dates: early 7th c. (Sūrat al-Kahf) · Qur’an 18:9–26
Summary: Within roughly a century of Gregory of Tours, Sūrat al-Kahf (“The Cave”), the eighteenth sura, tells of sleeping youths, the Aṣḥāb al-Kahf, the Companions of the Cave. Its account shares its core with the Christian legend; whether it draws on that legend is disputed.

Claims & evidence

The passage (18:9–26)

The Qur’an tells the tale allusively, as a sign already known to its hearers. The essential verses (tr. Sahih International):

“Or have you thought that the companions of the cave and the inscription were, among Our signs, a wonder?” (v. 9)

“[Mention] when the youths retreated to the cave and said, ‘Our Lord, grant us from Yourself mercy and prepare for us from our affair right guidance.’” (v. 10)

“So We cast [a cover of sleep] over their ears within the cave for a number of years.” (v. 11)

“Then We awakened them that We might show which of the two factions was most precise in calculating what [extent] they had remained in time.” (v. 12)

“It is We who relate to you, [O Muḥammad], their story in truth. Indeed, they were youths who believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance.” (v. 13)

“And you would think them awake, while they were asleep. And We turned them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretched his forelegs at the entrance.” (v. 18)

“And similarly, We caused them to be found that they [who found them] would know that the promise of Allah is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt.” (v. 21)

“They will say there were three, the fourth of them being their dog; and they will say there were five, the sixth of them being their dog — guessing at the unseen; and they will say there were seven, and the eighth of them was their dog.” (v. 22)

“And they remained in their cave for three hundred years and exceeded by nine.” (v. 25)

Comparison with the Christian legend

The account shares the core of the Christian legend: faithful young men, the sheltering cave, a God-sent sleep of “a number of years,” and the waking as a sign — here made explicit, “that they would know that the promise of Allah is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt” (v. 21), the resurrection-witness function the Syriac tradition also gives the story. It names neither the persecutor Decius nor Ephesus, and tells the tale as a sign of God’s power to preserve and to raise.

Three distinctive features

The Qur’anic account includes the sleepers’ dog, stretched across the threshold (v. 18); a caution against quarrelling over their number (“three… five… seven,” v. 22); and a term, “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine” (v. 25). The commentators took up all three in their fuller retelling.

Reception and debate

  • A shared holy story. The Seven Sleepers are venerated by both Christians and Muslims; Massignon called the tradition a “hyphen” between the two faiths.
  • Two narratives in one sura. A few verses later (18:83–101) the same sura tells of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, whose relation to the Syriac Alexander Legend is a parallel source question: see the Dhū’l-Qarnayn source question.
  • Whether the Qur’anic account draws on the Christian legend is disputed: see Does the Qur’an’s account of the Companions of the Cave draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend?
  • The word al-raqīm, paired with the cave in v. 9 (“the companions of the cave and the inscription”), has been read as “the inscription” and as a place-name, and a place-name reading underlies the identification of particular caves: see What does al-raqīm in Qur’an 18:9 mean?
