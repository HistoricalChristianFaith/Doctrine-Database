The Qur’an: the Companions of the Cave (Aṣḥāb al-Kahf)

Dates: early 7th c. (Sūrat al-Kahf) · Qur’an 18:9–26
Summary: The clearest measure of how far the legend travelled: within roughly a century of Gregory of Tours it appears in Islamic scripture. Sūrat al-Kahf (“The Cave”), the eighteenth sura, retells the story of the sleeping youths as the Aṣḥāb al-Kahf, the Companions of the Cave — keeping the Christian core while recasting it to the Qur’an’s own theology.

Claims & evidence

The passage (18:9–26)

The Qur’an tells the tale allusively, as a sign already known to its hearers. The essential verses (tr. Sahih International):

“Or have you thought that the companions of the cave and the inscription were, among Our signs, a wonder?” (v. 9)

“[Mention] when the youths retreated to the cave and said, ‘Our Lord, grant us from Yourself mercy and prepare for us from our affair right guidance.’” (v. 10)

“So We cast [a cover of sleep] over their ears within the cave for a number of years.” (v. 11)

“Then We awakened them that We might show which of the two factions was most precise in calculating what [extent] they had remained in time.” (v. 12)

“It is We who relate to you, [O Muḥammad], their story in truth. Indeed, they were youths who believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance.” (v. 13)

“And you would think them awake, while they were asleep. And We turned them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretched his forelegs at the entrance.” (v. 18)

“And similarly, We caused them to be found that they [who found them] would know that the promise of Allah is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt.” (v. 21)

“They will say there were three, the fourth of them being their dog; and they will say there were five, the sixth of them being their dog — guessing at the unseen; and they will say there were seven, and the eighth of them was their dog.” (v. 22)

“And they remained in their cave for three hundred years and exceeded by nine.” (v. 25)

What is kept, and what is changed

The recognisable core of the Christian legend is all present: faithful young men, the sheltering cave, a God-sent sleep of “a number of years,” and above all the waking as a sign — here made explicit, “that they would know that the promise of Allah is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt” (v. 21), which is precisely the resurrection-witness function the legend carried from its Syriac beginnings. What drops away is the specific Christian furniture: the persecutor Decius is unnamed, Ephesus is not identified, and the tale is abstracted into a parable of God’s power to preserve and to raise.

Three distinctive features

The Qur’anic version foregrounds elements that the later Muslim tradition would develop: the sleepers’ dog, stretched across the threshold (v. 18); a pointed caution against quarrelling over their number (“three… five… seven,” v. 22) — a reflex, it seems, of exactly the variation the Christian recensions show; and a precise term, “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine” (v. 25). These become the hooks on which the commentators hang their fuller retelling.

Notes

  • A shared holy story. With Sūrat al-Kahf the Seven Sleepers become the rare legend venerated by both Christians and Muslims — the feature that makes the whole tradition, in Massignon’s phrase, a “hyphen” between the two faiths.
  • Twin legends in one sura. The Companions of the Cave are not the only Syriac-Christian legend in this chapter: a few verses later (18:83–101) the same sura retells the Alexander tale of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, and modern scholarship reaches the same source-critical verdict for both — a prior Syriac-Christian tale received and re-told as a Qur’anic sign.
