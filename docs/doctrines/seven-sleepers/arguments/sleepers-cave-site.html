Does any site — Ephesus, ar-Raqīm near Amman, or another — have a real claim to be the cave of the sleepers?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — no surviving cave can be shown to be the sleepers’ own. The Ephesus complex is securely excavated and fixes the cult in the mid-fifth century, but it sits on an older necropolis and proves veneration, not a miracle; the modern Jordanian identification (ar-Raqīm/al-Rajīb, excavated by al-Dajani) rests on a place-name pun over a contested word plus a common Byzantine tomb-type; and the sheer number of rival caves is itself the tell · advanced for the Jordan site by Rafīq Wafā al-Dajānī, and by the local traditions of a dozen shrines from Anatolia to China.

Related arguments: al-Raqīm and the sleepers’ cave — see also What is al-raqīm — the inscription or the place-name?

The claim

The claim is that a particular physical cave is the very one in which the sleepers lay — that the legend can be pinned to a spot on the ground. Its strongest modern form is the Jordanian one: that the cave at the village of al-Rajīb, about eight kilometres south-east of Amman, is “the” cave, ar-Raqīm of Qur’an 18:9. But the same claim is made, on the strength of local tradition, for the long-shown grotto at Ephesus and for caves at Tarsus, Afşin, Loja in Spain, Chenini in Tunisia, Tuyoq in western China, and elsewhere — by one local reckoning some thirty sites in all.1

Who advances it

The Jordanian identification was pressed by the archaeologist Rafīq Wafā al-Dajānī, who excavated the al-Rajīb cave (the work dated to 1963) after a journalist brought it to notice in 1951, and published it as Iktishāf Kahf Ahl al-Kahf (“The Discovery of the Cave of the People of the Cave,” Beirut, 1964). It had classical warrant of a kind: al-Ṭabarī and al-Wāqidī had pointed to a location in the Jordan/Balqāʾ region, near ruins associated with “Daqyānūs” (Decius). The Ephesus claim, far older, is carried by the whole legend-tradition and by the church and pilgrim-cult built at the grotto; the other sites rest on regional devotion and, in several cases, later state or dynastic patronage.2

The evidence for

Ephesus — the one securely-excavated site. On the eastern slope of Mount Pion (Panayır Dağı), just outside ancient Ephesus, the Austrian Archaeological Institute excavated in 1927–28 a large two-level cemetery-church complex — burial halls, a crypt, catacombs, and hundreds of rock-cut graves — published as Das Coemeterium der Sieben Schläfer (Forschungen in Ephesos IV.2, 1937). The excavators dated the church over the grotto to about the mid-fifth century and found walls carrying frescoes and Greek and Latin pilgrim graffiti invoking the Seven Sleepers, together with a great votive deposit of terracotta lamps. The dating dovetails with the literary record — the waking is set under Theodosius II (408–450), and the first written account is placed at Ephesus around 448 — so the archaeology anchors the cult at Ephesus in the fifth century, the oldest and best-attested of all the sites.3

ar-Raqīm/al-Rajīb — a real Byzantine shrine. Al-Dajānī’s cave is a partly natural, partly rock-cut Byzantine-era burial cave holding eight sealed tombs with bone remains, its entrance framed by Byzantine-style pilasters and niches; above it stand the remains of a small Byzantine church later converted to a mosque (associated with the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Malik, d. 705), with a second, Tulunid-era mosque attested by a Kufic inscription, and a Byzantine cemetery some 500 metres to the west. Al-Dajānī’s identification was chiefly onomastic — al-Rajīb read as a dialect corruption of Qur’anic al-raqīm — reinforced by the Jordan-region tafsīr tradition and by dog remains said to answer to the Qur’an’s dog.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Two things are solid. The Ephesus excavation is real and well-dated: it establishes, beyond reasonable doubt, that a Seven-Sleepers cult existed at a specific Ephesian grotto by the mid-fifth century, which is exactly where and when the legend’s literary trail also begins. And the al-Rajīb site is a genuine Byzantine burial-cave that became a Christian and then a Muslim shrine — a real monument of shared veneration, not a fabrication.

What is overstated. Neither fact reaches the claim. (1) At Ephesus the grotto was a burial ground before the legend matured: beneath and among the Christian material lie first- and second-century pagan tombstones and Jewish lamps, so the excavation shows a pre-existing necropolis that a fifth-century cult adopted — it fixes the veneration, not a third-century sleeping. (2) The al-Rajīb identification rests on a place-name pun on a word whose meaning is itself disputed — and the strongest scholarly place-name reading of al-raqīm points not to Amman but to Petra — over a tomb-type that is extremely common (Byzantine rock-cut burial caves with multiple sarcophagi number in the hundreds across the region), while Jordan’s official renaming of the village to al-Raqīm has made the toponymic “match” partly self-fulfilling; the dog remains and solstice-alignment are corroborations sought after the identification, not evidence that led to it. (3) The decisive point is the multiplicity itself: because the Qur’an gives no location and warns against quarrelling over the sleepers’ number (18:22), each region grew its own grotto — Ephesus, Tarsus, Afşin (whose selling-point is that its mouth faces the way 18:17 describes), Lice, Loja, Chenini, Tuyoq, and more. The medieval geographers already disagreed: al-Idrīsī, who knew the Andalusian claim at Loja, held the true cave to be at Ephesus. A relic claimed in a dozen incompatible places is a mark of a wandering cult, not of a locatable event.5

Assessment

Weak. No surviving cave can be shown to be the sleepers’ own. Ephesus has the strongest and oldest claim — its cult is securely excavated and dated to the mid-fifth century — but the archaeology fixes where the veneration took root, on an older necropolis, and cannot reach the miracle it commemorates; al-Dajānī’s ar-Raqīm rests on a contested place-name and a common tomb-type; and the dozen rival caves are competing localizations bred by the Qur’an’s deliberate silence about place. This is not a verdict against the cult, which is real and old, nor a re-litigation of whether the event happened (rated weak on its own grounds): it is the narrower finding that even the site is unfixable — the shrine can be located, the sleepers cannot.
