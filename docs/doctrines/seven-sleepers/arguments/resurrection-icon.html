Was the Seven Sleepers legend composed for the fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus · witness: the Syriac tradition and Jacob of Serugh

Related readings: Was the earliest written form of the Seven Sleepers legend Syriac or Greek?

The passage (in summary)

The earliest datable treatment of the legend by a named author is Jacob of Serugh’s Syriac verse homily on the youths (before 521). In it the sleep is God’s own act: he “sealed their senses” and kept their bodies whole in the cave, so that, waking, the youths would refute those who deny that the dead rise.1

The question

Jacob of Serugh’s homily presents the sleepers as a demonstration of the bodily resurrection, and Witold Witakowski (GEDSH) foregrounds this didactic purpose in the Syriac tradition. What is disputed is the legend’s origin: whether the story was composed for a fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body, or is an older tale of a long sleep that was taken up and given that meaning.2

Composed for the fifth-century controversy

On this reading the legend was written at Ephesus in the mid-fifth century as a demonstration of the resurrection of the body, and the controversy is what produced it.

Held by: Ernest Honigmann (Patristic Studies, 1953).3

Honigmann dated what he took to be the original Greek account to Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451), and read it as a local bishop’s resurrection-miracle composed at the very moment the legend’s waking is set, supplying author, place, date, and doctrinal motive together. The later fourth and fifth centuries saw sharp controversy over the resurrection of the flesh: the Origenist disputes over whether the risen body is the same material flesh or a transformed one (see the question whether Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh), and the broader apologetic against those who denied bodily resurrection outright. A story that stages a bodily preservation and waking speaks directly to that debate. Its architecture, preservation, bodily waking, witness, and death, carries the argument, and the earliest voice makes the apologetic structural: in Jacob’s homily the miracle exists so that the youths will refute the resurrection-deniers. The setting points the same way. The waking is placed under Theodosius II, the reign in which, on this reading, the legend was being composed, so the proof is staged in living memory, as fits a tale written to speak to a present controversy.4

An older long-sleeper tale, taken up as a resurrection sign

On this reading the plot of the long sleep is older than the dispute, and the legend is that tale adopted and framed as a witness to the resurrection.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The narrative core, the miraculous long sleep, the waking, the out-of-date coins, and the recognition, belongs to an international tale-type of the hero or holy man who sleeps for generations and wakes into a changed world. Its kin include the Greek Epimenides, the Jewish Ḥoni ha-Ma‘agel and the Baruch/Abimelech legend, and later Rip Van Winkle; John Koch (1883) and Michael Huber (1910) set the Seven Sleepers within this family and charted its spread. Such a tale exists independently of any resurrection doctrine. On this reading Jacob of Serugh’s homily shows the story being used for the argument: a folktale plot fitted with a doctrinal homily. The resurrection of the flesh, moreover, was disputed across many centuries, from Athenagoras’ On the Resurrection and Tertullian’s De resurrectione carnis from the second century through the Origenist controversy and beyond, so the story could serve as a resurrection sign wherever and whenever it circulated. On this reading the legend is a long-sleeper tale that Christian writers made into a witness to the resurrection of the body.5
