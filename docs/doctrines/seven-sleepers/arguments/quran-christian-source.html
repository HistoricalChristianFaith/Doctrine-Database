Does the Qur’an’s account of the Companions of the Cave (18:9–26) draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus · witness: the Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf

Related readings: What does al-raqīm in Qur’an 18:9 mean? · on the same sura, the Dhū’l-Qarnayn account and the Syriac Alexander Legend

The passage

“It is We who relate to you, [O Muḥammad], their story in truth. Indeed, they were youths who believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance.” (v. 13)

“And similarly, We caused them to be found that they [who found them] would know that the promise of Allāh is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt.” (v. 21)

“They [i.e., people] will say there were three, the fourth of them being their dog; and they will say there were five, the sixth of them being their dog — guessing at the unseen; and they will say there were seven, and the eighth of them was their dog.” (v. 22)

— Qur’an 18:13, 21, 22 (tr. Sahih International), quran.com.1

The question

Sūrat al-Kahf tells of youths who took refuge in a cave, slept by God’s act “for a number of years,” and were woken as a sign of the resurrection; the Christian legend of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, attested in Syriac and Latin in the fifth and sixth centuries, tells of young men who fled persecution to a cave, slept, and woke as a witness to the resurrection of the body. What is disputed is the relation between the two accounts: whether the Qur’anic narrative is a retelling of the Christian legend, or an independent account of the youths given as revelation. The academic literature for the second reading was not located among the sources used here.

A retelling of the Christian legend

On this reading the two accounts are one story, received into the Qur’an from the Christian tradition and reshaped to the Qur’an’s own purposes.

Held by: John Koch (1883); Michael Huber (1910).2

The case begins from chronology. The Christian legend is attested well before Islam: in the Syriac Story and Jacob of Serugh’s homily (before 521), and in Gregory of Tours’ Latin by the 580s; the sura is roughly a century later. The legend circulated most widely in the Syriac-speaking Christianity of the Near East, the milieu from which, on this reading, the Qur’an drew much of its biblical and para-biblical narrative material. The overlap is a cluster of particulars: faithful young men who flee persecution, take refuge in a cave, are placed by God into a sleep of many years, and are woken as a sign confirming the resurrection and the Last Day. The sura makes that function explicit, “that they would know that the promise of Allah is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt” (18:21), which is the doctrinal purpose Jacob of Serugh’s homily had already given the story. On this reading the Qur’an engages a living oral legend rather than copying a written recension: it names no persecutor and no Ephesus, foregrounds the dog and the disputed number, and gives its own figure of “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine,” marks of a story received and retold. A few verses later the same sura retells the Alexander tale of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, which Theodor Nöldeke (1890) and Kevin van Bladel (2008) trace to a Syriac-Christian legend, so that the sura carries two Christian legends, each told as a Qur’anic sign. On this reading the agreement of the two accounts settles that they are one story; it leaves aside whether that story is historical.3

An account given by revelation

On this reading the Qur’an relates the youths’ story as God’s own account of it, not as a version of the Christian text.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The sura presents itself as the teller of the story: “It is We who relate to you … their story in truth” (18:13). It addresses hearers who already know of the youths and disagree about them, setting the rival counts of three, five, and seven side by side as “guessing at the unseen” (18:22), and it fixes their term in the cave at “three hundred years and exceeded by nine” (18:25). On this reading the agreement between the Qur’an and the Christian legend is the agreement of two accounts of the same youths, and the differences of detail, the absence of the persecutor’s name and of the city, and the open question of the number are the Qur’an speaking to a story in circulation and giving its own account of it.4
