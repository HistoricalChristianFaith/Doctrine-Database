Did the Qur’an’s Companions of the Cave (18:9–26) draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the Christian legend is attested in Syriac and Greek more than a century before the Qur’an and circulated in exactly the Near-Eastern Christian milieu from which the Qur’an drew much of its narrative material; the shared, specific core makes a common origin certain, with the dependence running from the older Christian tale to the Qur’anic retelling · advanced by mainstream scholarship on the Qur’an’s narrative sources · see also the sura’s twin source-argument, Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend? (sound).

The claim

The account of the Aṣḥāb al-Kahf in Sūrat al-Kahf 18:9–26 is a retelling of the Christian legend of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus. The two are not independent stories that happen to resemble each other; they are one story, received into the Qur’an from the Christian tradition and reshaped to the Qur’an’s own purposes. The pattern is not isolated: a few verses later the same sura retells a second Syriac-Christian legend — the Alexander tale of Dhū’l-Qarnayn — and reaches the identical source-critical verdict, so Sūrat al-Kahf carries two back-to-back Christian legends each retold as a Qur’anic sign. This is the standard scholarly view and the plain reading of the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia’s note that “the Ephesus story is told in the Koran (Sura xviii).”

— 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus.”

Who advances it

This is not the thesis of a single writer but the settled position of scholarship on the Qur’an’s narrative sources, going back to the nineteenth-century Wanderlegende studies (Koch, Huber) and carried by the comparative work of Louis Massignon, who treated the Christian and Islamic forms as one cult with a shared story. It follows directly from the chronology: the Christian legend is fixed in writing in the fifth and sixth centuries; the sura is early seventh.

The evidence for

Priority in time. The Christian legend is securely attested well before Islam: in the Syriac tradition and Jacob of Serugh’s homily (before 521), and in Gregory of Tours in the Latin West by the 580s. The Qur’anic sura is roughly a century later. The direction of borrowing is therefore not in question: the older text cannot depend on the younger.1

The right milieu. The legend circulated most vigorously in the Syriac-speaking Christianity of the Near East — the very environment from which the Qur’an demonstrably drew a great deal of its biblical and para-biblical narrative material. A story this popular in that milieu is exactly the kind of thing the Qur’an elsewhere engages.

A shared, specific core. The overlap is not a vague “long sleep” motif but a cluster of particulars: faithful young men who flee persecution, take refuge in a cave, are placed by God into a miraculous sleep of many years, and are woken as a sign confirming the resurrection and the reality of the Last Day. That last point is decisive: the resurrection-witness function the sura makes explicit (“that they would know that the promise of God is truth and that of the Hour there is no doubt,” 18:21) is precisely the doctrinal purpose the Syriac homily had already given the story.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Both the fact of a common origin and the direction of dependence are secure. The chronology rules out Christian borrowing from Islam; the shared specific core rules out coincidence; the milieu supplies the channel. On the central claim there is little room for reasonable doubt.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two cautions. First, “drew on” should not be flattened into “copied a text”: the Qur’an engages a living oral legend, not necessarily any one written recension, which is why it carries no persecutor’s name and no Ephesus, foregrounds the dog and the disputed number, and gives its own figure of “three hundred years, and nine.” These are the marks of a story received and re-told, not transcribed. Second, this argument establishes only that the two accounts are one story; it says nothing about whether that story is historically true — a separate question weighed on the historicity page. To argue from shared origin to shared falsehood (or shared truth) would be a category error.2

Assessment

Sound. The Qur’anic Companions of the Cave and the Christian Seven Sleepers are the same legend, with the dependence running from the earlier Christian tradition to the later Qur’anic retelling — a conclusion secured by priority in time, the shared Syriac-Christian milieu, and a specific common core culminating in the identical resurrection-sign function. The one qualification is about mechanism, not fact: the Qur’an reworks a living legend rather than copying a manuscript, which accounts for its distinctive shape. Whether the events occurred is a different question entirely.
