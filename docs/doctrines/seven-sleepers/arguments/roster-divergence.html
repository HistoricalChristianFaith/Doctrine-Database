Do the divergent Greek and Latin name-rosters point to independent transmission from a common source?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus · witness: Gregory of Tours and the Byzantine Passion

Related readings: Was the earliest written form of the Seven Sleepers legend Syriac or Greek?

The passage

The two best-known rosters of the sleepers’ names. The Byzantine Passion of Symeon Metaphrastes: Maximillian, Jamblichos, Martin, John, Dionysios, Exakostodianos, and Antoninos. The Latin of Gregory of Tours, followed by the Roman Martyrology and the Golden Legend: Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, Johannes, and Serapion.1

The question

Four of the seven names in the two lists match or are cognate (Maximian(us)/Maximillian, Martin(ianus)/Martin, John/Johannes, Dionysios/Dionysius), and three differ (Jamblichos, Exakostodianos, and Antoninos in the Greek; Malchus, Constantinus, and Serapion in the Latin). What is disputed is what the divergence shows about the legend’s transmission: whether the rosters record two lines descending independently from one source, or whether the names are a later and variable layer that cannot by themselves trace the branches.

A fingerprint of independent descent from one source

On this reading the shared core and divergent tails of the two lists mark two lines that each inherited one Eastern archetype and then drifted apart.

Held by: John Koch (1883); Michael Huber (1910).2

Koch and Huber sorted the more than two hundred surviving manuscripts (some 104 Latin, 40 Greek, 33 Arabic, 17 Syriac, and others in Ethiopic, Coptic, Armenian, Old French, Old English, and Middle Irish) into their language-families, treating a shared but imperfectly shared stock of names as a sign of common descent with drift in each branch. A single copying-chain would tend to keep the whole roster; independent invention would share no names; a shared core with divergent tails fits two scribal traditions that each inherited one list and, over centuries and across a language boundary, dropped and replaced the harder names in their own ways. Other evidence shows the same branching. Gregory states that he worked from an account “which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin” (Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus), and the colophon of his Passio names the interpreter John the Syrian (interpretante Iohanne Syro): the Latin line reached the West through the Syriac, on a track separate from the Greek. A third branch shows the same shape. The Arabic names in al-Ṭabarī and the later commentators, Yamlīkhā, Maksalmīnā, Marṭūs and the rest, are Arabized from the Greek set (Yamlīkhā from Iamblichos, Marṭūs from Martinos), not the Latin, so the Islamic tradition hangs from the Eastern-Greek line. On this reading the rosters, with the colophon and the Arabic names, trace a tree with separate Greek and Latin branches from a common Eastern root.3

The names as a later, variable layer

On this reading the names were fixed after the tradition had already divided and vary within each line, so the rosters date each branch’s naming rather than the branching itself.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The two earliest witnesses barely fix a roster: Jacob of Serugh’s homily and the Qur’an tell the story without the seven names, and the Qur’an sets out rival counts of three, five, and seven (18:22). Names are the most changeable element of a saint’s life, coined, latinized, graecized, and re-spelled, and they move within a single tradition as well as between traditions. Adrian Fortescue’s 1913 article on the legend records that “the names vary considerably,” and gives for Gregory of Tours a set wholly unlike the one above: “Achillides, Diomedes, Diogenus, Probatus, Stephanus, Sambatus, and Quiriacus.” On this reading, if the names crystallized after the archetype had split, divergent rosters record when and how each branch named the sleepers, and the branching of the tree rests on other evidence, such as Gregory’s colophon and the geography of the witnesses.4
