Which came first — the Syriac Story or a lost Greek account of Ephesus?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — a genuinely open question of literary history with strong scholars on both sides: Syriac priority (Guidi, Nöldeke) versus a lost Greek original written near Ephesus (Huber, Peeters, Honigmann, who ties it to Stephen of Ephesus). Neither camp has closed the case · debated within modern scholarship on the legend.

The claim

Two rival theses about where the written legend began. On one side, the earliest form was the Syriac Story of the Youths of Ephesus, the source behind Jacob of Serugh’s homily and, ultimately, behind the Greek and Latin. On the other, the original was a Greek account composed at or near Ephesus itself — now lost — from which both the Syriac and the surviving Greek descend. The stake is not whether the story is Eastern (it plainly is) but which language carried its first written form.

Who advances it

For Syriac priority: Ignazio Guidi (who first edited the Syriac and Oriental texts, 1884), Theodor Nöldeke, and others (Ryssel, Krymski, Allgeier, Heller). For a Greek original: Michael Huber, Paul Peeters, and above all Ernest Honigmann, whose 1953 study argued that the first history was written in Greek by Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451) — on the spot and at the very moment the legend’s waking is set.

The evidence for

For Greek priority. The decisive point is place: nearly all the early authors who report the legend, except Jacob of Serugh, gathered their information in or near Ephesus, which is Greek-speaking territory. A Greek original composed there fits the geography, and Honigmann’s attribution to Stephen of Ephesus supplies a plausible author, date, and motive — a local bishop framing a resurrection-miracle during a live fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body.1

For Syriac priority. The oldest datable literary treatments we actually possess are Syriac — the prose Story and Jacob of Serugh’s homily (before 521) — and the Syriac manuscript tradition is early and rich. Guidi and Nöldeke read the Syriac as the fountainhead rather than a translation, and Gregory of Tours’ explicit debt to a Syrian interpreter shows the Syriac line actively feeding the West.

Adversarial assessment

What holds on each side. The Greek case is strong on argument from setting (the Ephesian, Greek-speaking locale; a named local author with a motive) but weak on surviving evidence, since its postulated Greek original is lost and must be reconstructed. The Syriac case is strong on surviving evidence (the oldest datable texts in hand are Syriac) but weaker on setting (Ephesus is not Syriac ground, so a Syriac first-form implies rapid eastward travel of a western-Anatolian story). Each discipline’s strength is the other’s gap.

Why it stays open. The dispute cannot be settled from what survives: a lost Greek exemplar can be neither produced nor excluded, and “oldest surviving” is not the same as “oldest.” Both reconstructions are coherent and defended by serious philologists, and no new decisive witness has emerged to break the tie.2

One asymmetry worth stating plainly. On surviving evidence the Syriac is unambiguously earlier: no extant Greek witness predates the Syriac line at all. The earliest datable Greek attestation we actually possess is a one-line chronicle notice in Theophanes the Confessor (Chronographia, c. 810–814) — some three centuries younger than the Syriac prose Story and Jacob of Serugh’s homily (before 521) — and the oldest Greek manuscripts of the Passion itself belong to the ninth–tenth century. The Greek-priority case therefore rests entirely on the reconstructed lost Ephesian original (Honigmann’s Stephen of Ephesus) and on the argument from setting; it can point to no surviving early-Greek document. That does not refute it — a lost exemplar is exactly what the thesis posits — but it does locate the whole weight of the Greek case on a text no one has seen, which is why the crux is “contested” rather than settled either way.3

Assessment

Contested. The question is a real, unresolved crux of the legend’s literary history: the Greek thesis wins on setting and named authorship, the Syriac on surviving early texts, and neither can dispose of the other’s best point. What is not in doubt — and what the transmission timeline rests on — is that the legend was fully formed in the Christian East a century before it reached the Latin West, whichever language first wrote it down.
