Was the earliest written form of the Seven Sleepers legend Syriac or Greek?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus · witness: the Syriac tradition

Related readings: Do the divergent Greek and Latin name-rosters point to independent transmission from a common source? · Was the legend composed for the fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body?

The passage

Two pieces of evidence that both sides of the dispute address: Gregory of Tours’ account of his source, and the earliest surviving Greek notice of the legend.

Quod passio eorum, quam Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus, plenius pandit.

(“The record of their suffering, which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin, gives a fuller account.”)

— Gregory of Tours, Glory of the Martyrs 94, tr. Raymond Van Dam (1988).1

“In the same year in Ephesos there occurred the remarkable miracle of the seven holy youths who arose after 184 years.”

— Theophanes the Confessor, Chronographia, AM 5931 (c. 810–814), tr. Mango & Scott (1997).2

The question

The legend is set in Greek-speaking Ephesus, but the oldest datable texts that tell it are Syriac: a prose Story of the Youths of Ephesus and Jacob of Serugh’s verse homily (before 521). What is disputed is the language of its first written form: whether the Syriac Story is the fountainhead of the Greek and Latin, or whether a Greek account composed at or near Ephesus, now lost, stands behind both the Syriac and the surviving Greek.

The Syriac Story is the original

On this reading the legend was first written down in Syriac, and the Greek and Latin forms descend from that line.

Held by: Ignazio Guidi (editor of the Syriac and Oriental texts, 1884); Theodor Nöldeke.3

The reading starts from the surviving record. The oldest datable literary treatments of the legend are Syriac: the prose Story, circulating by the later fifth century, and Jacob of Serugh’s homily, written before his death in 521. The Syriac manuscript tradition is early and rich, and Guidi, who first edited these texts, and Nöldeke read the Syriac as the source rather than a translation. The earliest surviving Greek witness is Theophanes’ one-line chronicle notice of c. 810–814, some three centuries after the Syriac Story and Jacob’s homily, and the oldest Greek manuscripts of the Passion itself belong to the ninth and tenth centuries. The Syriac line is also seen feeding the West: Gregory of Tours says the fuller account he translated into Latin was “interpreted by a certain Syrian,” and the colophon of his Passio names the interpreter John the Syrian. On this reading the Syriac Story is the fountainhead from which the Greek and Latin traditions flowed.4

A lost Greek original written at Ephesus

On this reading the first written account was Greek, composed at or near Ephesus, and both the Syriac and the surviving Greek descend from it.

Held by: Michael Huber (1910); Paul Peeters; Ernest Honigmann (Patristic Studies, 1953).5

The reading starts from place. The legend is set in Ephesus, and nearly all the early authors who report it, Jacob of Serugh apart, gathered their information in or near Ephesus, which is Greek-speaking territory. A Greek original composed there fits that geography. Honigmann gave the thesis an author, a date, and a motive: the first history, he argued, was written in Greek by Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451), on the spot and at the very moment at which the legend’s waking is set, a local bishop framing a resurrection-miracle during a fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body. On this reading the original is a text that has not survived, and the Greek Passion manuscripts of the ninth to thirteenth centuries, the chronicle notices of Theophanes and George the Monk, and the Syriac Story all descend from that fifth-century Ephesian account.6
