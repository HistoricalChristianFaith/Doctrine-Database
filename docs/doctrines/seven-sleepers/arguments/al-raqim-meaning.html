What does al-raqīm in Qur’an 18:9 mean: “the inscription” or a place-name?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus · witness: the Qur’an, Sūrat al-Kahf

Related readings: Does the Qur’an’s account of the Companions of the Cave draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend?

The passage

“Or have you thought that the companions of the cave and the inscription were, among Our signs, a wonder?”

— Qur’an 18:9 (tr. Sahih International, which renders al-raqīm as “the inscription”), quran.com.1

The question

Sūrat al-Kahf pairs “the companions of the cave (al-kahf)” with al-raqīm (الرقيم), a word found nowhere else in the Qur’an; Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have listed it among the Qur’anic words he could not explain. What is disputed is whether it is a common noun (“the inscription,” “the tablet”), a proper name (a valley, mountain, town, or city), or a copyist’s alteration of another word. The answer bears on the legend’s history because a place-name reading is the textual basis on which particular caves, among them the site at ar-Raqīm/al-Rajīb near Amman, have been identified as the sleepers’ own. Al-Ṭabarī also records further glosses: that it was the name of the dog, and ʿIkrima’s report that it means “inkwell” in the Byzantine tongue.2

“The inscription”: a written tablet recording the sleepers

On this reading al-raqīm is the inscribed record of the youths, so that they are “the People of the Inscription.”

Held by: Saʿīd b. Jubayr; al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), Jāmiʿ al-bayān; Sidney H. Griffith (2008).3

Saʿīd b. Jubayr, as al-Ṭabarī transmits him, describes “a tablet of stone on which they wrote the story of the People of the Cave, then set it at the entrance,” and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār is cited for a lead tablet bearing the sleepers’ names, genealogies, and religion. Al-Ṭabarī, after setting out the views, chose this one, glossing al-raqīm as “a tablet, a stone, or something with writing inscribed on it.” The case rests on the morphology: the root r-q-m (ر-ق-م) means to write, mark, or inscribe, and raqīm is a faʿīl form with passive sense, “that which is written,” on the pattern of marqūm. The Qur’an uses the same root of the heavenly ledger in 83:9 and 20, a kitāb marqūm, “an inscribed book.” The sense also answers to a detail of the Christian legend: in the Syriac and Latin recensions two Christians enclose a sealed leaden tablet inscribed with the sleepers’ names and story in the wall that seals the cave, so that they can be identified when found. Jacob of Serugh’s Syriac homily has it: “they made tablets of lead and placed them beside them; on them they wrote down the names of the children of light,” and the sealed tablet passes into Gregory of Tours’ Latin. Griffith, reading the sura against the living Syriac tradition, takes the word within its ordinary lexical range, so that al-raqīm names the tablet the source-tale carried. Al-Jalālayn and Maududi gloss it the same way.4

A place-name: a valley, a town, or the city of Petra

On this reading al-raqīm is a proper name, “ar-Raqīm,” naming a real place beside the cave.

Held by: Qatāda; Mujāhid; al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī; Mehdy Shaddel (Journal of Semitic Studies, 2017).5

The geographic reading is carried by some of the oldest transmitters in al-Ṭabarī’s collection, who read the word as a valley, a mountain, or a town; one report calls it “a town in Byzantine territory.” A word that occurs only once can be a proper name the first audience knew, and the grammar allows “the companions of the cave and [of] ar-Raqīm” as two coordinated names. Shaddel gives the reading its fullest modern form, identifying al-raqīm with Petra, the Nabataean capital, whose indigenous Aramaic name is attested as Raqmū. Josephus says Petra is “called by the whole Arabian nation Rekeme,” and Eusebius’ Onomasticon that Petra “is also called Rekem.” On this reading the verse sets beside the cave a real, monument-filled city fit to stand among God’s “signs,” and the word is no obscure common noun but a name the sura’s hearers would have recognised. Abū l-Kalām Āzād had earlier read it as the biblical Rekem/Reqem of Joshua 18:27. The place-name reading has also anchored the localising of the cave: the Jordanian site at ar-Raqīm/al-Rajīb near Amman takes its claim from it.6

A copyist’s alteration of al-ruqūd, “those asleep”

On this reading the received word arose in copying, and the phrase first read “the companions of the cave and of sleep.”

Held by: James A. Bellamy (Journal of the American Oriental Society, 1991).7

Bellamy proposed that al-raqīm is a copyist’s error (taṣḥīf) for al-ruqūd, “those asleep.” That word occurs later in the same story, at 18:18, wa-hum ruqūd, “while they were asleep.” Restored, the opening verse reads “the companions of the cave and of sleep,” naming the youths by the miraculous sleep that the story goes on to tell (18:11), and the word that the earliest commentators found opaque becomes an ordinary one. Bellamy offered it as a conjectural emendation, one of a series he proposed for difficult words in the Qur’an, so that the dispute over al-raqīm becomes a question about its letters as well as its sense.8
