Origen of Alexandria on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 248 (Against Celsus, at Caesarea) · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering Celsus and the Gnostic sorcerers, Origen reports the Hebrew forms of the Name — Iao, Jah, Sabaoth, Adonai — and insists that these are "names of one and the same God," not the several deities the magicians took them for. He is a key witness to the Name in Greek transcription and to its circulation in the onomastic and magical traditions of late antiquity.

Claims & evidence

The Hebrew divine names are names of one God, borrowed and confused by the sorcerers

It must be noticed, too, that those who have drawn up this array of fictions, have, from neither understanding magic, nor discriminating the meaning of holy Scripture, thrown everything into confusion; seeing that they have borrowed from magic the names of Ialdabaoth, and Astaphaeus, and Horaeus, and from the Hebrew Scriptures him who is termed in Hebrew Iao or Jah, and Sabaoth, and Adonaeus, and Eloaeus. Now the names taken from the Scriptures are names of one and the same God; which, not being understood by the enemies of God, as even themselves acknowledge, led to their imagining that Iao was a different God, and Sabaoth another, and Adonaeus, whom the Scriptures term Adonai, a third besides.

— Against Celsus, Book VI, ch. 32, source. Iao (Ἰαώ) is the Greek transliteration of a short form of the Name that recurs on magical amulets and papyri of the period; Jah (Ἰά) is the shorter form preserved in "Hallelu-jah." Origen's point is polemical and orthodox — that the Gnostics have plundered the Scriptures for potent-sounding names while missing that they all name the one God — but the passage doubles as evidence of how the Name was written and spoken in the Greek-speaking church and its magical fringe.

Notes

  • Origen elsewhere argues (in the same work) that the divine names are not arbitrary sounds translatable at will but carry power in their native form — a view that bears on why the Name circulated untranslated in incantations. The use of the Name in magic and onomastic lore is weighed on the Origen & the divine names in magic argument.
  • Origen's Hexapla (his six-column edition of the Old Testament) is reported to have left the Tetragrammaton in archaic Hebrew letters in the Greek columns — the very practice that produced the ΠΙΠΙ confusion Jerome later records. That claim is a lead for its own witness block.
  • See also Clement of Alexandria, his predecessor at Alexandria, on the transcription Jave.
