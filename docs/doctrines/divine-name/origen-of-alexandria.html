Origen of Alexandria on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 248 (Against Celsus, at Caesarea) · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering Celsus and the Gnostic sorcerers, Origen reports the Hebrew forms of the Name — Iao, Jah, Sabaoth, Adonai — and insists that these are "names of one and the same God," not the several deities the magicians took them for. In his notes on Psalm 2 he adds a textual observation that anchors the whole story: in the most accurate manuscripts the Name stands in archaic Hebrew letters, not translated into Greek. He is a key witness to the Name in Greek transcription, to its untranslated survival in the biblical text, and to its circulation in the onomastic and magical traditions of late antiquity.

Claims & evidence

The Hebrew divine names are names of one God, borrowed and confused by the sorcerers

It must be noticed, too, that those who have drawn up this array of fictions, have, from neither understanding magic, nor discriminating the meaning of holy Scripture, thrown everything into confusion; seeing that they have borrowed from magic the names of Ialdabaoth, and Astaphaeus, and Horaeus, and from the Hebrew Scriptures him who is termed in Hebrew Iao or Jah, and Sabaoth, and Adonaeus, and Eloaeus. Now the names taken from the Scriptures are names of one and the same God; which, not being understood by the enemies of God, as even themselves acknowledge, led to their imagining that Iao was a different God, and Sabaoth another, and Adonaeus, whom the Scriptures term Adonai, a third besides.

— Against Celsus, Book VI, ch. 32, source. Iao (Ἰαώ) is the Greek transliteration of a short form of the Name that recurs on magical amulets and papyri of the period; Jah (Ἰά) is the shorter form preserved in "Hallelu-jah." Origen's point is polemical and orthodox — that the Gnostics have plundered the Scriptures for potent-sounding names while missing that they all name the one God — but the passage doubles as evidence of how the Name was written and spoken in the Greek-speaking church and its magical fringe.

In the most accurate manuscripts the Name is left in ancient Hebrew letters

In the more accurate exemplars the (divine) name is written in Hebrew characters; not, however, in the current script, but in the most ancient.

— Selecta in Psalmos (Notes on the Psalms), on Psalm 2:2, Greek in Migne, Patrologia Graeca 12. Origen — whose Hexapla set the Hebrew and the Greek versions of the Old Testament in parallel columns — here records that the best copies do not render the Tetragrammaton into Greek at all but leave it standing in Hebrew, and moreover in the older, paleo-Hebrew script (τοῖς ἀρχαιοτάτοις) rather than the square "Assyrian" letters of the copyists' own day. This is the practice at the root of the whole later muddle: the four Hebrew characters יהוה, set inside a Greek book, so resembled the Greek capitals Π-Ι-Π-Ι that readers who knew no Hebrew read them aloud as ΠΙΠΙ ("Pipi") — the very confusion Jerome records a century and a half later. Surviving Greek Old-Testament fragments confirm the report: some leave a blank for the Name, some write it in paleo-Hebrew, some substitute Kyrios. (The Selecta reach us as excerpts compiled from Origen's psalm-work, and their transmission is tangled, but this note squares exactly with Origen's known editorial practice in the Hexapla.)

Notes

  • Origen elsewhere argues (in the same work) that the divine names are not arbitrary sounds translatable at will but carry power in their native form — a view that bears on why the Name circulated untranslated in incantations. The use of the Name in magic and onomastic lore is weighed on the Origen & the divine names in magic argument.
  • The note on Psalm 2 above documents, from Origen's own textual work, the practice of leaving the Tetragrammaton in archaic Hebrew letters inside the Greek text — the very practice that produced the ΠΙΠΙ confusion Jerome later records. Together the two witnesses give the mechanism end to end: Origen (c. 248) the untranslated Name, Jerome (c. 384) the misreading it bred.
  • See also Clement of Alexandria, his predecessor at Alexandria, on the transcription Jave.
