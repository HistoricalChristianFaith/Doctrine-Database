Jerome on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 384 (Letter 25, to Marcella, at Rome) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cataloguing the ten Hebrew names of God for his correspondent Marcella, Jerome singles out the Tetragrammaton as "unspeakable" and records the celebrated blunder by which Greek readers, mistaking the Hebrew letters for similar-looking Greek ones, read the Name aloud as ΠΙΠΙ ("Pipi") — the classic proof that the Name's true sound was already being lost.

Claims & evidence

The Tetragrammaton is "unspeakable"; ignorant readers pronounce it ΠΙΠΙ

The ninth τετράγραμμον, which is ἀνεκφώνητον, that is unspeakable, is written with these letters, Jod, He, Vav, He. Which some, not understanding because of the similarity of the letters, have been accustomed to read as ΠΙΠΙ when they found it in Greek books.

— Letter 25, to Marcella, source. Jerome is running through the ten names by which the Hebrews call God — El, Elohim, Sabaoth, Elion, Ehyeh asher ehyeh, Adonai, Jah, this Tetragrammaton, and Shaddai. When the four Hebrew letters יהוה (Jod–He–Vav–He) were copied into Greek manuscripts in their original Hebrew script, readers who did not know Hebrew took them for the visually similar Greek capitals Π-Ι-Π-Ι and duly read "Pipi." Jerome's report is the standard patristic evidence that (a) the Name was treated as unpronounceable, its true vowels guarded, and (b) the practice of leaving it in archaic Hebrew letters inside a Greek text — attested in fragments of the Hexapla and of the Greek Minor Prophets scroll — actively produced misreadings.

Notes

  • The word Jerome calls "unspeakable" (ἀνεκφώνητον) reflects the settled Jewish practice, by his day centuries old, of not pronouncing the Name but reading Adonai ("Lord") in its place — the substitution that stands behind the Septuagint's Kyrios and every "the LORD" in later Bibles. That practice, and the vowel-pointing the Masoretes would later attach to the consonants, is the machinery from which the hybrid "Jehovah" was eventually (mis)assembled.
  • Jerome's translation of the Name and his general handling of the Hebrew of the Old Testament belongs with the wider question of the Hebrew-vs.-Greek text traced on the rabbinic-corruption timeline, where Jerome is himself a witness.
  • See also Clement of Alexandria (Jave) and Origen (Iao/Jah) on the earlier Greek transcriptions.
