Did the New Testament autographs contain the Tetragrammaton (the Howard thesis)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reconstruction that the New Testament authors wrote the Name יהוה in their Old-Testament quotations, later erased by scribes who substituted Kyrios, outruns the manuscript evidence entirely: every one of the thousands of surviving Greek New Testament witnesses reads Kyrios / Theos, not a single one preserves the Name, and the New Testament's own theology deliberately trades on the Kyrios rendering. The genuinely interesting half of the case — that some pre-Christian Greek Old-Testament copies did not yet use Kyrios — is real, but it does not reach the New Testament autographs.

The claim

It is that the divine name, יהוה (and possibly abbreviations of it), was originally written in the NT quotations of and allusions to the OT and that in the course of time it was replaced mainly with the surrogate ΚΣ [Kyrios].

— George Howard, "The Tetragram and the New Testament," Journal of Biblical Literature 96 (1977), p. 63.1 Howard adds that this later removal "created a confusion in the minds of early Gentile Christians about the relationship between the 'Lord God' and the 'Lord Christ.'"1 The interpretation to be weighed: the New Testament autographs contained the Tetragrammaton in their Old-Testament citations, and the universal Kyrios of the surviving manuscripts is a secondary scribal substitution.

Who advances it

The scholarly form is George Howard's 1977 JBL article, which frames it expressly as a "theory."1 Its best-known application is the New World Translation of the Jehovah's Witnesses, which restores "Jehovah" 237 times in its New Testament — the only modern version to do so — resting the decision on Howard's reconstruction and on later Hebrew New-Testament versions (the so-called "J documents," 14th century onward) rather than on any Greek witness.2 The thesis is therefore load-bearing well beyond the seminar room: it is the scholarly prop under a translation used by millions.

The evidence for

The surrogate Kyrios was not yet universal in pre-Christian Greek Bibles. This half of Howard's case is solid and genuinely interesting. Several Jewish copies of the Greek Old Testament from before and around the New-Testament period render the Name not with Kyrios but by writing it out:

  • Papyrus Fouad 266 (a Deuteronomy roll, 1st century BC) leaves a space and writes the Tetragrammaton in square Hebrew/Aramaic letters inside the Greek text.3
  • The Naḥal Ḥever Greek Minor Prophets scroll (8ḤevXIIgr, late 1st century BC / early 1st century AD) writes it in paleo-Hebrew script within the Greek.3
  • 4QLXXLevb (1st century BC) uses the Greek transliteration ΙΑΩ (Iao) — a pronounceable Greek form of the Name, not Kyrios.3

So some Greek Old-Testament copies in circulation avoided Kyrios altogether. From this Howard infers a literary analogy: New-Testament authors reading and quoting such copies would, he supposes, have carried the written Name across into their own Old-Testament citations; the nomina sacra system by which later Christian scribes contracted ΚΣ (Kyrios) and ΘΣ (Theos) could then be read as marking the very slots where the Name once stood.

Adversarial assessment

Zero New-Testament manuscripts support it. This is the decisive gap. There are thousands of Greek New-Testament witnesses, reaching back to the second and third centuries (P46, P66, P75), across every text-type and every book — and not one of them writes the Tetragrammaton, leaves a Hebrew-letter slot, or preserves Iao. Every extant witness reads Kyrios or Theos. The reconstruction is therefore entirely inferential about lost autographs: Howard offers, in his critics' words, no textual evidence for the Tetragrammaton in any ancient Christian Greek manuscript, and his own prose is frankly conjectural ("we can imagine," "if we permit our mind's eye to compare").4 Possibility is not proof; a theory about what the originals must have contained, contradicted by every copy that survives, carries the burden and does not discharge it.

The silence of controversy tells against a wholesale erasure. For the thesis to hold, scribes across the whole Greek-speaking church would have had to excise the Name from every book of the New Testament, in every text-type, so completely as to leave no manuscript trace and provoke no recorded debate. Yet the Fathers fought bitterly over far smaller variants, and when they quote the New Testament they use Kyrios, never the Name — Irenaeus cites Matthew and Romans with "Lord," not "Jehovah."5 A silent, universal, uncontested edit of the sacred text is not the likeliest explanation of a uniform reading; it is a conjecture invented to preserve one.

The New Testament's own theology cuts against restoration. The New Testament repeatedly takes Old-Testament texts that in Hebrew read YHWH and applies them to Jesus, trading precisely on the ambiguity of Kyrios — "Lord" of God and "Lord" of Christ at once. Paul quotes Joel 2:32 ("everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved") at Romans 10:13 and refers it to Jesus; the Christ-hymn of Philippians 2:9–11 bends Isaiah 45:23's "to me every knee shall bow" onto "Jesus Christ is Lord." Restoring the Tetragrammaton in these places would sever the very identification the authors are making. The New World Translation feels this pressure and answers it selectively — inserting "Jehovah" only where it judges the Father is meant and leaving Kyrios where the Son is — which imports a Father/Son distinction the undivided Greek word refuses to make. That selectivity is itself a tell that doctrine, not the manuscripts, is driving the restoration; Bruce Metzger flagged exactly this inconsistency.6

Even the Old-Testament premise is contested — and points the other way. Howard's first step assumes the "original" Greek Old Testament wrote the Name. That is itself disputed. Albert Pietersma argued that the Old Greek used Kyrios from the outset and that the Hebrew-letter Tetragrams in copies like Fouad 266 are a later Hebraizing revision, not the autograph.7 Emanuel Tov, who rejects Pietersma's Kyrios-original view, nonetheless concludes that the original translators used a pronounceable form (Iao) and that "all the texts transcribing the Tetragrammaton in Hebrew characters reflect early revisions."8 On either reconstruction the tidy picture Howard needs — one stable "YHWH in the Greek Bible" inherited straight into the New Testament — dissolves into a plural, unstable, and partly secondary set of practices (square Hebrew, paleo-Hebrew, Iao, Kyrios). The Old-Testament data is real; it will not bear the single inference the thesis rests on.

Assessment

Weak. Keep the two halves apart, and rate the interpretation on its best case. The Old-Testament half is sound and worth knowing: pre-Christian Greek copies did not uniformly use Kyrios, and some wrote the Name out — so the surrogate was a development, not a given. But the load-bearing New-Testament half — that the autographs contained the Tetragrammaton and that Kyrios is a scribal substitution — is a conjecture about lost originals with no manuscript support whatever, contradicted by every surviving witness, made improbable by the absence of any patristic trace of so vast an edit, and cutting against the New Testament's own deliberate use of Kyrios to identify Jesus with the LORD of the Old Testament. The New World Translation's selective "Jehovah" is the overreach to name plainly: it restores a word the Greek text nowhere contains, and does so only where a prior doctrine wants the distinction. The interesting evidence is genuine; the reconstruction built on it does not hold.
