Did the New Testament autographs write the Tetragrammaton in their Old Testament quotations (the Howard thesis)?

Disputed reading · on The Divine Name (the Tetragrammaton) · witness: the Greek New Testament's quotations of the Old Testament

Related readings: Is the form "Jehovah" the Name's consonants read with the vowels of Adonai, or a form of the Name in its own right? · How was the Name anciently pronounced: the two-syllable "Yahweh," or a longer form?

The passage

And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered: for in mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance, as the LORD hath said, and in the remnant whom the LORD shall call.

— Joel 2:32 (King James Version), source, where "the LORD" renders the Name יהוה.

For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.

— Romans 10:13 (King James Version), source, quoting Joel; the surviving Greek manuscripts read Kyrios, "Lord."1

The question

Where the New Testament quotes an Old Testament text containing the Name, its surviving Greek manuscripts read Kyrios or Theos. What is disputed is whether the authors themselves wrote יהוה in those places, later replaced by scribes, or wrote Kyrios from the first; the answer bears on how the Name is printed in modern New Testaments. The making of the Greek Old Testament the authors quoted is traced on the Septuagint's origin.

The Name stood in the autographs and was later replaced by Kyrios

On this reading the New Testament authors wrote the Name in their Old Testament quotations, and copyists of the following generations replaced it with the surrogate Kyrios.

Held by: the New World Translation committee (Jehovah's Witnesses, New Testament 1950) ⚠ unverified; George Howard ("The Tetragram and the New Testament," Journal of Biblical Literature, 1977).2

The New World Translation introduced "Jehovah" into 237 New Testament passages, its footnotes citing translations of the New Testament into Hebrew from the sixteenth century on, which, in Metzger's description, "understandably use the tetragram (yhwh) in rendering certain passages." Howard gave the thesis its scholarly form:

It is that the divine name, יהוה (and possibly abbreviations of it), was originally written in the NT quotations of and allusions to the OT and that in the course of time it was replaced mainly with the surrogate ΚΣ [Kyrios].

His starting point is the Greek Old Testament of the period. Several Jewish copies from before and around the New Testament era write the Name out rather than using Kyrios: Papyrus Fouad 266 (Deuteronomy, 1st century BC) in square Hebrew letters inside the Greek; the Naḥal Ḥever Greek Minor Prophets scroll (8ḤevXIIgr) in paleo-Hebrew script; 4QLXXLevb as the Greek ΙΑΩ (Iao). "On the analogy of pre-Christian Jewish practice," Howard writes, "we can imagine that the NT text incorporated the Tetragram into its OT quotations," with Kyrios and Theos reserved for the authors' own secondary references to God. He proposes the Name for passages such as Romans 10:16–17 and 14:10–11, 1 Corinthians 2:16 and 10:9, 1 Peter 3:14–15 and Jude 5, and reads the nomina sacra contractions ΚΣ and ΘΣ of later Christian copies as marking the slots where the Name once stood. Its removal, on his account, "created a confusion in the minds of early Gentile Christians about the relationship between the 'Lord God' and the 'Lord Christ.'"3,4,5

The authors wrote Kyrios from the first

On this reading the New Testament authors themselves rendered the Name Kyrios, as every surviving copy does.

Held by: Bruce M. Metzger (Theology Today, 1953); Albert Pietersma ("Kyrios or Tetragram," 1984); the author of "Appendix D: The George Howard Study" (The Tetragrammaton and the Christian Greek Scriptures); Michael Marlowe (bible-researcher.com).6

Its holders start from the manuscripts. The Greek New Testament survives in thousands of witnesses reaching back to the second and third centuries (P46, P66, P75), across every book and text-type, and they read Kyrios or Theos where the Old Testament had the Name; the reviewer of Howard's article states that "no known Christian Greek Scripture manuscripts contain the Tetragrammaton." The Fathers quote the same passages with "Lord": Irenaeus cites Matthew 1:20 and 4:10 and Romans 11:34 so, and no controversy over removing the Name from the New Testament is recorded in the patristic sources surveyed here. Metzger describes the Hebrew translations cited in the New World Translation's footnotes as "totally irrelevant material" for the Greek text. Pietersma holds that the Old Greek translators used Kyrios from the outset and that the Hebrew-letter forms in copies like Fouad 266 belong to a later Hebraizing revision. On this reading the single word Kyrios is itself the vehicle of the authors' meaning: it can be read of the Father or of Christ, a choice a rendering with the Name has to make passage by passage (Marlowe records the New World Translation giving "Jehovah" for kurios "whenever the Father is meant, but not when it refers to Christ, the Son"). Paul quotes Joel's "whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord" of Jesus (Romans 10:9–13), which Metzger reads as Paul applying to Jesus Joel's promise about Jehovah, and the hymn of Philippians 2:9–11 carries Isaiah 45:23 over to "Jesus Christ is Lord." In its holders' account the authors wrote Kyrios so that Old Testament texts about the LORD could be read of Jesus.7,8,9
