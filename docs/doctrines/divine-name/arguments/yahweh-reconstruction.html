How was the Name anciently pronounced: the two-syllable "Yahweh," or a longer form?

Disputed reading · on The Divine Name (the Tetragrammaton) · witness: Clement of Alexandria, Epiphanius of Salamis, Theodoret of Cyrus

Related readings: Is the form "Jehovah" the Name's consonants read with the vowels of Adonai, or a form of the Name in its own right? · Did the New Testament autographs write the Tetragrammaton?

The passage

Further, the mystic name of four letters which was affixed to those alone to whom the adytum was accessible, is called Jave, which is interpreted, "Who is and shall be." The name of God, too, among the Greeks contains four letters.

— Clement of Alexandria, The Stromata, Book V, ch. 6 (Ante-Nicene Fathers translation), source.1 "Jave" is the translator's spelling of Clement's Greek; Epiphanius and Theodoret later give the Name as Ἰαβέ (Iabe), Theodoret assigning that form to the Samaritans.

The question

No fully vowelled Hebrew text records the Name from a time when it was freely spoken, so its ancient sound is reconstructed from Greek transcriptions, Israelite personal names, the grammar of the verb "to be" and the Masoretic pointing. What is disputed is whether that evidence points to a two-syllable Yahweh or to a longer, three-syllable form.

A two-syllable Yahwe(h)

On this reading the Name was spoken in two syllables, Yah-weh, with a fixed first syllable Yah-.

Held by: Wilhelm Gesenius and H. G. A. Ewald (both ⚠ unverified); Anthony J. Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); John Drummond ("What Is God's Name?", Biblical Archaeology Society).2

Drummond reports that "today the form 'yahweh' is most widely accepted." Its holders draw on four lines of evidence. The Greek transcriptions give two-syllable forms: Clement's Ἰαού(έ), and the Ἰαβέ of Epiphanius and of Theodoret's Samaritans. Maas judges that "the Samaritan pronunciation Jabe probably approaches the real sound of the Divine name closest," and continues: "Inserting the vowels of Jabe into the original Hebrew consonant text, we obtain the form Jahveh (Yahweh)." The Greek β is read for Hebrew waw. The theophoric names clip the Name to Yəhô- at the front of a compound (Yəhôshuaʻ, Yəhônathan) and to -yāhû / -yāh at the back (Isaiah, Hezekiah, Josiah; Hallelu-Yah), the shapes an underlying yahw- stem would yield. The morphology reads the Name as an imperfect of the root "to be," fitting the burning-bush ehyeh asher ehyeh (Exodus 3:14); Drummond compares the place-name Yavneh, from pre-Hebrew yabniyu, for the contraction of a pre-Hebrew *Yahwiyu to Yahweh. The short forms Yah (Psalm 68:4, "JAH") and Yahu fix the first syllable. A form yhw also stands in an Egyptian topographical list at Soleb (14th century BC), "the land of the Shasu of yhw" ⚠ unverified.3,4

Holders of this reading differ over the verb's stem. Maas takes it as the simple (qal) "He Who is," citing Exodus 3:14 and noting that there is no "trace in Hebrew of a hiphil form of the verb meaning 'to be'"; W. F. Albright and F. M. Cross (⚠ unverified) read a causative (hiphil), "he causes to be." On either stem, the two-syllable shape stands on the convergence of transcription, onomastics, morphology and the short forms.5

A longer, three-syllable form

On this reading the Name had three syllables, and the form preserved in the pointed Hebrew text, read as "Jehovah," is its own.

Held by: Michaelis (Supplementa ad lexica hebraica, 1792), Drach (Harmonie entre l'Église et la Synagogue, 1844) and Stier (Lehrgebäude der hebräischen Sprache), each ⚠ unverified, whom Maas names among the scholars who regarded "Jehovah" as the true pronunciation.6

Its holders begin from the full form that the theophoric names presuppose. The prefix Jeho- of Jehoshua, Jehonathan and the other compounds is three letters of the Name with its vowels intact, and in Maas's report of the argument, "the abbreviated form Jeho supposes the full form Jehovah." The Name's meaning points the same way. It is explained as built from the verb "to be" in three tenses at once, "the imperfect, the participle, and the perfect" (ye = yehi; ho = howeh; wa = hawah), so that it means "he who will be, is, and has been." That threefold sense is what the Apocalypse gives when it names God as the one "which is, and which was, and which is to come" (Revelation 1:4; also 4:8). The pointed Masoretic text supplies the vowels of that full form, and Christian scholars read it so from the thirteenth century on, beginning with Raymund Martin's Pugio fidei (c. 1270). On this reading the Hebrew text, the names built on the Name, and the New Testament's paraphrase of it point together to a three-syllable Name.7
