Is "Jehovah" a late hybrid vocalization (the qere perpetuum), not the historic pronunciation?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the hybrid-origin thesis is near-consensus philology: "Jehovah" splices the consonants of YHWH to the vowels of the spoken substitute Adonai, and it surfaces only among medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists, not in antiquity. (The further claim that the form is therefore an error to be discarded is a usage opinion, not a philological result — kept separate below.)

The claim

A mispronunciation (introduced by Christian theologians, but almost entirely disregarded by the Jews) of the Hebrew "Yhwh," the (ineffable) name of God … This pronunciation is grammatically impossible; it arose through pronouncing the vowels of the "ḳere" (marginal reading of the Masorites: = "Adonay") with the consonants of the "ketib" (text-reading: = "Yhwh").

— The Jewish Encyclopedia (1901–06), s.v. "Jehovah," jewishencyclopedia.com.1 The interpretation to be weighed: "Jehovah" is a philological artifact of the qere perpetuum — the written consonants read with the borrowed vowels of a different word — not a recovered ancient pronunciation of the Name.

Who advances it

This is the standard reading of the Hebraists, not the thesis of a single modern proponent. The mechanism is stated in Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar (the classic nineteenth-century grammar, §17 "Of the Qerê and Kethîbh"), which gives the Tetragrammaton as the standing textbook example of a qere perpetuum;2 it is spelled out in the reference literature — the Jewish Encyclopedia's "Names of God" and "Jehovah,"3 the Catholic Encyclopedia,4 and the Encyclopædia Britannica5 — and it is repeated in nearly every modern treatment of the Name. The reader-facing version of the argument today is most associated with the Sacred-Name movement and with critics of Jehovah's-Witness usage, but the philology behind it is older and uncontroversial.

The evidence for

The pointing on YHWH is not its own. When Second-Temple reverence stopped pronouncing the Name, readers said Adonai ("Lord") in its place (whence the Septuagint's Kyrios and every "the LORD" of later Bibles). When the Masoretes fixed the vowels of the consonantal text centuries later, they pointed the four consonants יהוה not with the Name's own vowels — which reverence had let fall out of use — but with the vowels of the word actually to be said, Adonai, as a permanent marginal direction, the qere perpetuum: the eye sees יְהֹוָה but the mouth says Adonai. (Where Adonai already stands next to the Name, it is instead pointed with the vowels of Elohim, "God," to signal that substitute — a tell that the vowels track the spoken surrogate, not the Name.)4 Read the consonants of YHWH straight off the page with those borrowed vowels — as though the pointing belonged to them — and you get the hybrid Ye-hō-wāh, "Jehovah": a form no one ever spoke, made of one word's letters and another word's vowels.

The form is late, and it is Christian. "Jehovah" is absent from antiquity — the Greek Fathers who transcribed what they had heard give two-syllable forms (Clement's Jave, the Iabe of Epiphanius and Theodoret), never the three-syllable "Jehovah." It surfaces first among medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists reading the pointed Masoretic text: the Dominican Raymund Martini's Pugio Fidei (c. 1270) already has the form, and it was broadcast to the Latin West by Petrus Galatinus's De Arcanis Catholicae Veritatis (1518), after which it became the standard learned spelling and passed into English through Tyndale and the King James Version (at Exodus 6:3, Psalm 83:18, Isaiah 12:2, 26:4, and the compound place-names) and, wall-to-wall, through the American Standard Version of 1901.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core is as close to settled as philology gets. That the pointing on YHWH is the qere perpetuum for Adonai (and Elohim), that combining those vowels with the consonants yields a form that is "grammatically impossible" as a genuine word, and that "Jehovah" is medieval/early-modern rather than ancient — all of this is stated flatly in the standard grammars and reference works and is not seriously disputed.1

Two common overstatements to trim. First, the vowels of the pointed יְהֹוָה are not a clean transplant of Adonai's: Adonai opens with a composite (ḥaṭaf) shewa under its guttural א, which under the non-guttural י of YHWH becomes a simple shewa — so the borrowed vowels are the surrogate's, adapted by ordinary Hebrew pointing-rules, not copied letter-for-letter.4 A minority have tried to read that imperfect fit as a faint memory of the real vocalization rather than a pure Adonai-transplant; the point is worth noting but not overstating — the mainstream reads the pointing as the surrogate's, and the two-syllable patristic evidence (Yahweh/Iabe) tells against a genuine three-syllable "Jehovah." Second, the popular claim that Galatinus invented the form in 1518 is itself an overstatement: the spelling predates him — it stands in Martini c. 1270, in Porchetus in the fourteenth century, and in manuscripts of the same era — so Galatinus popularized "Jehovah," he did not coin it.5

Origin is not usage — keep them apart. That "Jehovah" is a hybrid by origin does not make four centuries of English liturgy, hymnody, and Bible translation that use it "wrong." A word's genealogy and its legitimacy in use are different questions: "Jehovah" is a real English word for the God of Israel with a long and honorable pedigree, whatever the tangled route by which its vowels arrived. The philological verdict here is strictly about how the form came to be; whether one should prefer "Yahweh," retain "Jehovah," or (with the Septuagint and the KJV's dominant practice) render "the LORD" is a separate matter of translation and piety, weighed on the timeline itself and in the companion arguments on pronouncing the Name.

Assessment

Sound. "Jehovah" is a philological artifact of the qere perpetuum — the consonants of the Name read with the vowels of its spoken substitute — and an artifact of the second Christian millennium at that, not a recovered ancient pronunciation; on this the grammars and reference works speak with one voice. Trim two edges: the borrowed vowels are the surrogate's adapted, not copied exactly, and Galatinus popularized rather than invented the form. And keep the finding where it belongs — this rates the origin of the vocalization, not the propriety of continuing to use it, which is a usage judgment the philology does not settle.
