Is the form "Jehovah" the Name's consonants read with the vowels of Adonai, or a form of the Name in its own right?

Disputed reading · on The Divine Name (the Tetragrammaton) · witness: the Masoretic pointing of יהוה

Related readings: How was the Name anciently pronounced: the two-syllable "Yahweh," or a longer form? · Did the New Testament autographs write the Tetragrammaton?

The passage

And I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the name of God Almighty, but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them.

— Exodus 6:3 (King James Version), source.1 The English "JEHOVAH" here renders the four consonants יהוה as they stand in the Masoretic text, pointed יְהֹוָה.

The question

The Masoretic text points the four consonants of the Name with vowels that resemble those of Adonai ("Lord"), the word read aloud in its place. What is disputed is whether the form "Jehovah," made by reading those consonants with those vowels, is a form of the Name in its own right or a reading of one word's letters with another word's vowels; the answer bears on how the English renderings "Jehovah" and "Yahweh" are each understood.

The Name's own form: "he who will be, is, and has been"

On this reading the vowels printed with the Name belong to it, and "Jehovah" is a true pronunciation of the Name.

Held by: Michaelis (Supplementa ad lexica hebraica, 1792), Drach (Harmonie entre l'Église et la Synagogue, 1844) and Stier (Lehrgebäude der hebräischen Sprache), each ⚠ unverified, whom Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) names among the scholars who regarded "Jehovah" as the true pronunciation.2

Maas reports the explanation this reading gives of the form: "Jehovah is composed of the abbreviated forms of the imperfect, the participle, and the perfect of the Hebrew verb 'to be' (ye=yehi; ho=howeh; wa=hawah)," so that it means "he who will be, is, and has been." The same threefold sense stands in the Apocalypse's paraphrase of the divine name, "from him which is, and which was, and which is to come" (Revelation 1:4; also 4:8). The theophoric names supply a second support: "the abbreviated form Jeho supposes the full form Jehovah," as in Jehoshua and Jehonathan. The form also has a long Christian use. It stands in the Pugio fidei of the Dominican Raymund Martin, written about 1270, and in Porchetus in the fourteenth century; Galatinus, writing in the early sixteenth, presents it "as known and received in his time," and Catholic and Protestant writers of that century (Cajetan, Théodore de Bèze) use it freely. Maas himself judges that "probably the introduction of the name Jehovah antedates even R. Martin." On this reading the pointed text and the medieval usage together hand down the Name as it was meant to be read.3

A reading of the Name's consonants with the vowels of Adonai (the qere perpetuum)

On this reading the vowels printed with the Name are the vowels of the word spoken in its place, and "Jehovah" arose when later readers pronounced the two together.

Held by: Wilhelm Gesenius (Hebrew Grammar, §17); Emil G. Hirsch (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1901–06); Anthony J. Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); George Foot Moore (Encyclopædia Britannica, 1911).4

Its holders start from the reading practice. Once the Name was no longer pronounced, readers said Adonai in its place, and when the Masoretes pointed the consonantal text they gave יהוה the vowels of the word to be said, as a standing direction to the reader: the qere perpetuum, which Gesenius gives as the textbook example of the device. Where Adonai already stands beside the Name, the four consonants carry the vowels of Elohim instead, signalling that substitute. The simple shewa under the first letter, where Adonai has a compound one, Maas explains as "required by the rules of Hebrew grammar governing the use of shewa." Read as a word, the combination gives Ye-hō-wāh. Hirsch puts the result in these terms:

A mispronunciation (introduced by Christian theologians, but almost entirely disregarded by the Jews) of the Hebrew "Yhwh," the (ineffable) name of God … This pronunciation is grammatically impossible; it arose through pronouncing the vowels of the "ḳere" (marginal reading of the Masorites: = "Adonay") with the consonants of the "ketib" (text-reading: = "Yhwh").

The ancient transcriptions Maas lists give Jaou (Clement), Jao (Origen) and, for the Samaritans as reported by Theodoret, Jabe. The form appears among Christian Hebraists reading the pointed text, and was carried to the Latin West by Petrus Galatinus's De arcanis catholicae veritatis (1518); Moore notes that it "occurs in manuscripts at least as early as the 14th century," so that Galatinus spread rather than coined it. On this reading "Jehovah" is the record of a reading convention, one word's consonants joined to another word's vowels.5,6,7

The Name's long-established English rendering

On this reading the history of the vowels does not decide the matter: "Jehovah" is the English form by which the Name has been known and printed for centuries.

Held by: the American Revisers (American Standard Version, 1901); the Jehovah's Witnesses ("What Is God's Name?", jw.org).8

Its holders treat "Jehovah" as the Name in English use. The King James Version prints it at Exodus 6:3, Psalm 83:18, Isaiah 12:2 and Isaiah 26:4, and the American Standard Version of 1901 printed it in place of "LORD" and "GOD" throughout the Old Testament. The American Revisers gave their reason in the Name's meaning: "This Memorial Name, explained in Ex. iii. 14, 15, and emphasized as such over and over in the original text of the Old Testament, designates God as the personal God, as the covenant God, the God of revelation, the Deliverer, the Friend of his people," and "is now restored to the place in the sacred text to which it has an unquestionable claim." The Jehovah's Witnesses state the point about spelling directly: "While many scholars prefer the spelling 'Yahweh,' Jehovah is the form of the name that is most widely recognized," and "Jehovah is a rendering of God's name in English that has been used for centuries." On this reading a personal name keeps its standing in a language through that language's use of it, and the English Bible's usage gives "Jehovah" that standing.9,10
