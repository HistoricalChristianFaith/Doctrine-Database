Epiphanius of Salamis on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 377 (the Panarion, or "Medicine Chest," against the Archontics) · lived c. 315–403 · Wikipedia
Summary: Running through the Hebrew names of God in his great heresy-catalogue, Epiphanius renders the Tetragrammaton in Greek as Ἰαβέ (Iabe) — a two-syllable ya-be, which his modern translator gives as "Jahveh" — and glosses it "He who was and is, He who forever is," tying it to God's self-naming at the burning bush. With Theodoret's near-contemporary report, this is the backbone of the patristic case that the Name's living sound was two-syllable "Yahweh," not the later hybrid "Jehovah."

Claims & evidence

The Tetragrammaton is Ἰαβέ, glossed "He who was and is"

Refuting the Gnostic Archontics — who treated the Hebrew divine names as the proper names of separate archons — Epiphanius insists they are titles of praise for the one God, and lists them with their meanings. In Frank Williams's translation:

Already in the previous Sects I have dealt at length with the translation of Sabaoth and other names—Eli and Elohim, El and Shaddai, Elyon, Rabboni, Jah, Adonai and Jahveh—since they are all to be translated as terms of praise, and are not as it were given names for the Godhead. Here too I hasten to give them in translation. … "Adonai," "He who is existent Lord." "Jahveh" means, "He who was and is, He who forever is," as he translates for Moses, "'He who is' hath sent me, shalt thou say unto them." "Elyon" is "highest."

— Panarion (Against Heresies) 40 (Against the Archontics), 5.8–10, tr. Frank Williams (Brill). What Williams renders "Jahveh" is Epiphanius's Greek Ἰαβέ (Iabe; some copies read Ἰαβαί, and one codex Ἰαυέ) — a two-syllable transcription that scholars pair with Clement's Iaoue and Theodoret's Iabe as the basis for the modern vocalization "Yahweh" (the Greek forms and the scholarly convention: K. van der Toorn, s.v. "Yahweh," Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible, 2nd ed., 1999, citing Epiphanius, Adv. Haer. 1.3.40.5; collected patristic transcriptions in "Tetragrammaton," Wikipedia). The gloss "He who was and is, He who forever is" ties the Name to the burning-bush self-disclosure, "I AM THAT I AM … 'He who is' hath sent me" (Exodus 3:14) — the same reading of the Name as a form of the verb "to be" that stands behind Clement's "Who is and shall be."

Notes

  • Epiphanius's name-list is the close cousin of Jerome's roughly contemporary catalogue of the ten Hebrew names for Marcella (El, Elohim, Sabaoth, Elion, Adonai, Jah, the Tetragrammaton, Shaddai…). Where Jerome dwells on the Name's unpronounceability and the "Pipi" misreading, Epiphanius simply hands over a spoken form of it — Ἰαβέ — without treating it as unsayable, a difference of practice worth noting between the two.
  • Whether this two-syllable Iabe really reconstructs the original vocalization of יהוה — against the three-syllable "Jehovah," a medieval fusion of the consonants with the vowels of the spoken substitute Adonai — is weighed in the Arguments section of the divine-name summary, not here; this page only records what Epiphanius reports.
  • See also Clement of Alexandria (Jave/Iaoue), Origen (Iao/Jah), and Theodoret of Cyrus (Samaritan Iabe vs. Jewish Aïa) on the other Greek transcriptions.
