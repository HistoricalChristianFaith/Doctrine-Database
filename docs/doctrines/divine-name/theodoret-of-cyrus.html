Theodoret of Cyrus on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 453 (Questions on the Octateuch — Questions on Exodus 15) · lived c. 393–457 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering a question about the Name revealed to Moses, Theodoret gives the single most-cited patristic report of how it was actually said in his day: the Samaritans pronounce it Ἰαβέ (Iabe), the Jews Ἀϊά (Aïa). The two-syllable ya-be he records from the Samaritans — who never adopted the Jewish taboo on speaking the Name — is the chief external evidence behind the modern reconstruction "Yahweh," as against the three-syllable "Jehovah."

Claims & evidence

"The Samaritans call it Ἰαβέ, the Jews Ἀϊά"

In his Questions on the Octateuch, discussing the Name disclosed to Moses (Exodus 6:2–3, "I am the LORD… by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them"), Theodoret reports the divergent pronunciations current in the two communities that still handled the Hebrew: the Samaritans say the Name Ἰαβέ (Iabe), the Jews Ἀϊά (Aïa) — Questions on Exodus 15 (Greek ed. J. F. Petruccione, tr. Robert C. Hill, CUA Press, 2007; Greek also in Migne, Patrologia Graeca 80). He repeats the point in his heresy-compendium, where he spells the Samaritan form Ἰαβαί and again distinguishes it from the Jewish Ἀϊά (Haereticarum Fabularum Compendium 5.3, PG 83).

The two forms are not two readings of the same thing. Ἀϊά transcribes not the four-letter Name יהוה but the neighbouring אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh, "I am / I will be") of Exodus 3:14, which the Jews counted among the divine names and would utter where they would not say the Tetragrammaton itself. The Samaritan Ἰαβέ, by contrast, is a two-syllable vocalization of the Name proper — and because Greek β was by Theodoret's day sounded as v, it renders something close to ya-ve. This is the datum that later Hebraists — Génébrard in 1567, then decisively Gesenius in the 1830s — used to argue the original vocalization was "Yahweh."

Notes

  • Weight and limits of the witness. Theodoret is the most-quoted patristic evidence for a two-syllable Name, but he is not an unqualified one: he wrote around 700 years after the Name had left common Jewish speech, and his exegesis works from the Greek and Syriac, not the Hebrew. Some scholars judge that he did not independently overhear the Samaritan form but took it from Epiphanius's earlier Panarion — which matters for how many independent witnesses the "Iabe → Yahweh" reconstruction really has (on the dating, his reliance on the Greek and Syriac, and the dependence question, see the collected patristic transcriptions and cautions in "Tetragrammaton," Wikipedia). Whether the reconstruction nonetheless holds is weighed in the Arguments section of the divine-name summary; this page only records the testimony.
  • The Samaritans' willingness to say the Name is itself the point: the Jewish reverential silence that reads Adonai in its place (and so gave the Septuagint's Kyrios and every "the LORD" of later Bibles) never took hold among them, so their tradition preserved a spoken form the Jews had let fall out of use.
  • See also Clement of Alexandria (Jave/Iaoue), Origen (Iao/Jah), Epiphanius of Salamis (Iabe/"Jahveh"), and Jerome (the "unspeakable" Name and the "Pipi" misreading) on the other Greek transcriptions.
