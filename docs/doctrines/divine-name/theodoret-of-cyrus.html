Theodoret of Cyrus on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 453 (Questions on the Octateuch — Questions on Exodus 15) · lived c. 393–457 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering a question about the Name revealed to Moses, Theodoret reports how it was said in his day: the Samaritans pronounce it Ἰαβέ (Iabe), the Jews Ἀϊά (Aïa). Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) took the Samaritan two-syllable form as the one that "probably approaches the real sound of the Divine name closest"; how it bears on the Name's ancient pronunciation is a disputed reading.

Claims & evidence

"The Samaritans call it Ἰαβέ, the Jews Ἀϊά"

In his Questions on the Octateuch, discussing the Name disclosed to Moses (Exodus 6:2–3, "I am the LORD… by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them"), Theodoret reports the divergent pronunciations current in the two communities that still handled the Hebrew: the Samaritans say the Name Ἰαβέ (Iabe), the Jews Ἀϊά (Aïa) — Questions on Exodus 15 (Greek ed. J. F. Petruccione, tr. Robert C. Hill, CUA Press, 2007; Greek also in Migne, Patrologia Graeca 80). He repeats the point in his heresy-compendium, where he spells the Samaritan form Ἰαβαί and again distinguishes it from the Jewish Ἀϊά (Haereticarum Fabularum Compendium 5.3, PG 83).

The two forms are not two readings of the same thing. Ἀϊά transcribes not the four-letter Name יהוה but the neighbouring אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh, "I am / I will be") of Exodus 3:14, which the Jews counted among the divine names and would utter where they would not say the Tetragrammaton itself. The Samaritan Ἰαβέ, by contrast, is a two-syllable vocalization of the Name proper — and because Greek β was by Theodoret's day sounded as v, it renders something close to ya-ve. Later Hebraists — Génébrard in 1567, then Gesenius in the 1830s — used it to argue the original vocalization was "Yahweh" (⚠ unverified).

Context and cross-references

  • Date and transmission. Theodoret wrote around 700 years after the Name had left common Jewish speech, and his exegesis works from the Greek and Syriac, not the Hebrew (⚠ unverified — no named source located). A dependence of his Samaritan form on Epiphanius's earlier Panarion, rather than independent hearing, has been proposed (⚠ unverified — no named proponent located). His and Epiphanius's transcriptions are listed side by side among the patristic witnesses in "Tetragrammaton," Wikipedia. The bearing of his report on the Name's pronunciation is set out under Disputed readings on the divine-name timeline; this page records the testimony.
  • Theodoret's report places a spoken form of the Name among the Samaritans, who did not adopt the Jewish practice of reading Adonai in its place (the practice reflected in the Kyrios of the surviving Septuagint copies and the "the LORD" of later Bibles).
  • See also Clement of Alexandria (Jave/Iaoue), Origen (Iao/Jah), Epiphanius of Salamis (Iabe/"Jahveh"), and Jerome (the "unspeakable" Name and the "Pipi" misreading) on the other Greek transcriptions.
