Clement of Alexandria on the Divine Name

Dates: c. 215 (the Stromata, at Alexandria) · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement gives one of the earliest Christian transcriptions of the four-letter Name, reporting that the "mystic name of four letters" worn into the holy of holies was pronounced Jave — a Greek rendering close to the modern "Yahweh," and evidence that the true vowels had not yet been wholly lost when a learned Greek Father wrote of them.

Claims & evidence

The four-letter Name is pronounced "Jave," meaning "Who is and shall be"

Further, the mystic name of four letters which was affixed to those alone to whom the adytum was accessible, is called Jave, which is interpreted, "Who is and shall be." The name of God, too, among the Greeks contains four letters.

— The Stromata, Book V, ch. 6, source. Clement is describing the golden plate on the high priest's mitre and the innermost sanctuary of the temple; the "mystic name of four letters" is the Tetragrammaton, YHWH. His transcription Jave (Greek Ἰαού / Ἰαουέ in the manuscript tradition) is close to the reconstruction "Yahweh" that modern scholarship would recover, and his gloss "Who is and shall be" ties the Name to the self-naming of God at Exodus 3:14 ("I AM"). It stands with Theodoret's and Epiphanius' later Iabe as a patristic witness to a two-syllable pronunciation — telling against the later medieval "Jehovah."

Notes

  • Clement writes in a passage on the symbolism of the temple and of sacred names generally; his interest is mystical and etymological, not liturgical, so he transcribes the Name freely rather than treating it as unutterable — unlike the Jewish avoidance he elsewhere knows.
  • See also Origen, his Alexandrian successor, who reports the Name as Iao/Jah in a discussion of its use in magic, and Jerome, who two centuries later calls the Tetragrammaton "unspeakable" and records the famous ΠΙΠΙ misreading.
