Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19)

a parallel-passage crux: David kills Goliath in 1 Samuel 17, while 2 Samuel 21:19 (Masoretic Hebrew) says Elhanan the Bethlehemite "struck down Goliath the Gittite," and 1 Chronicles 20:5 has Elhanan kill "Lahmi the brother of Goliath." The verse also figures in the textual questions traced on the rabbinic-corruption and Septuagint-origin pages. Smith holding Samuel prior and Chronicles a harmonization; McCarter (1984) holding Elhanan the original slayer; Heiser (2014) correcting Samuel from Chronicles; and Ozoliņš (2022) reconstructing a shared original in which Elhanan killed Goliath's unnamed brother.

How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits)

a manuscript variant rather than a contradiction between two books: the Masoretic Hebrew of 1 Samuel 17:4 makes Goliath "six cubits and a span" (~9′9″), while the Dead Sea Samuel scroll 4QSama, the Septuagint, and Josephus read "four cubits and a span" (~6′9″), as the ESV notes. One instance of the wider Septuagint–Masoretic divergence traced on the rabbinic-corruption and Septuagint-origin pages. Traced from the "four" of the Septuagint (2nd c. BC), 4QSama (mid-1st c. The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3)

Matthew and Luke both trace Jesus's descent through Joseph, and between David and Joseph their lists share only two names: Matthew runs the royal line David→Solomon→…→Jacob, "the father of Joseph"; Luke runs David→Nathan→…→Heli, and calls Joseph "the son of Heli." The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1)

the one apostle whose death the New Testament records twice: Matthew 27:3–10 has Judas fling back the silver and "hang himself," the priests then buying the potter's field; Acts 1:16–19 has Judas himself buy a field and, "falling headlong," burst asunder. Three differences are discussed — the manner of death (hanging, or falling and bursting), who bought the field (the priests, or Judas), and why it is the "field of blood" (the blood-money, or Judas's blood) — in the same family of passages as the two slayers of Goliath. Traced through its readers, earliest first: Papias (c. 110), whose account gives a third manner of death (a monstrous swelling); the fourth-century witnesses whose Latin text of Acts carries the hanging inside the verse (the Apostolic Constitutions, Augustine, Jerome's Vulgate); the Greek commentators who explain the notices by a sequence of events — the rope broke (Oecumenius, c. 550 or late tenth century), or the hanging failed (Theophylact, c. 1107; Euthymius Zigabenus, c. 1118), Theophylact adding Papias's account; and modern commentators divided between those who read the notices as independent accounts not to be combined (Barrett, Zwiep; Lake tracing all three to one loose traitor-death tradition) and those who read them as reconcilable (Bock, Keener, Marshall). The Godhead and the person of Christ

The Trinity (the co-eternal, consubstantial Son and Spirit)

how Christian writers of the second to fourth centuries spoke of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and how the Son’s relation to the Father came to be confessed as co-eternal and of the same essence (homoousios), the term of the Council of Nicaea (325). The fourth-century controversy turned on whether that confession states the apostolic faith, as its defenders held, or adds to Scripture a term and a doctrine it does not contain, as its fourth-century opponents charged. The human soul

The Origin of the Soul (traducianism, creationism, and pre-existence)

Where does an individual human soul come from: from the parents' souls as the body comes from their bodies (traducianism), created by God for each new human being (creationism), or made long before and joined to a body later (pre-existence)? Writers tied the question to how original sin reaches a newborn, to the fate of infants who die unbaptised, and to the inequality of human lots. Grace, faith, and justification

Original sin & the Fall

what Adam’s first transgression did to his descendants, and whether, and how, it reaches a newborn. The communions and their theologians describe the inheritance in their own terms: the Augsburg Confession (1530) and the Westminster Confession (1646) speak of an inherited sin that condemns and, in Westminster, of Adam’s guilt imputed; the Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (1974) of an inheritance “essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness,” without inherited guilt; the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) of a sin “contracted” and not “committed,” without “the character of a personal fault”; and Pelagius denied any transmission. Traced from Irenaeus on the race that “offended in the first Adam” (c. 180) and Tertullian on the soul propagated from Adam (c. 210), through Origen and Cyprian on the “filth” and “contagion” of birth, Ambrosiaster’s Latin “in whom all sinned” and Chrysostom’s reading of the same verse, Pelagius and Augustine (c. 411–430), and the councils of Carthage (418) and Orange (529), to John of Damascus (c. 730), the Council of Trent (1546), and John Calvin’s 1559 Institutes. Justification (how a sinner is made right with God)

Predestination, grace & free will

The communions state their teaching in their own terms: the Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) of an election that “extends only over the godly”; the Reformed Westminster Confession (1646) of some “predestinated unto everlasting life” and others “foreordained to everlasting death”; the Orthodox Confession of Dositheus (1672) of a predestination according to God’s foreknowledge of the use of free will; and the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) of a plan that “includes… each person’s free response to his grace.” Traced from Pelagius and Augustine (c. 405–429), the Council of Carthage (418), John Cassian (c. 426) and Prosper of Aquitaine (c. 429–434), Fulgentius of Ruspe (c. 520) and the Second Council of Orange (529), through Gottschalk of Orbais and the synods of Quiersy (853) and Valence (855), to Erasmus and Luther (1524–25), the De Auxiliis controversy between Molina and Báñez (1588–1607), the Remonstrance and the Synod of Dort (1610–19), and the condemnation of five propositions from Jansen’s Augustinus (1653). The problem of evil

The Problem of Evil (theodicy)

the oldest objection to belief in God, and the Christian answers to it across time: if God is perfectly good and all-powerful, why does He permit evil and suffering? Lactantius (c. 313) records the problem as a fourfold dilemma he ascribes to Epicurus, an ascription Reinhold Glei and Mark Larrimore question. Four replies recur among the witnesses: moral evil as the price of a genuinely free will (Irenaeus, Origen, Basil, Augustine); a world with evil in it schooled toward a greater good than a world without (Irenaeus, Lactantius, Augustine, Aquinas); evil as no substance God made but the privation of a good (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Aquinas); and a disorder that began in a free fall of angels (Origen, Pseudo-Dionysius, John of Damascus). Traced from Irenaeus (c. 180) through Origen, the Cappadocians, Chrysostom, Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Boethius and John of Damascus, to Aquinas, Calvin and the Westminster Confession; then Bayle's skepticism (1697), Leibniz's Théodicée (1710), Voltaire's Candide (1759), Hume's Dialogues (1779), and the modern debate (Mackie, Plantinga, Hick, Rowe, the skeptical theists, Adams, Stump, and the protest writers). Throughout, the timeline distinguishes moral evil (what wills do) from natural evil (what befalls), and a defense (that the triad is not contradictory) from a theodicy (a positive reason God permits evil). Adjacent are the predestination and original-sin timelines, the teaching of universal restoration, and the divine council reading of a fallen cosmos.

Indulgences (the remission of the temporal punishment of sin)

The Treasury of Merit (and works of supererogation)

In Catholic teaching the treasury of merit (the treasury of the Church) is the inexhaustible value of Christ's merits, joined by the prayers and good works of the Virgin Mary and the saints, held in common in the communion of saints and drawn on in granting indulgences; bound up with it are the questions whether works of supererogation, beyond what God commands, are possible, and whether merit or satisfaction can be applied to others. Its dated entries are Aquinas's scholastic account in his commentary on the Sentences (1252–56, printed as the Supplement to the Summa), Clement VI's jubilee bull Unigenitus Dei Filius (1343), and Article XIV of the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571), which rejects works of supererogation. Paul VI's Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967) locates the treasury's satisfactions and merits in "Christ the Redeemer himself", and the Catholic Catechism (1992) describes it as the infinite value of Christ's merits, with the prayers and good works of Mary and the saints. Luther (1517) called the Gospel the Church's true treasure, Calvin (1559) called indulgences a profanation of Christ's blood, and Charles Hodge (1872) argued that supererogation supposes "that a rational creature can be better than he ought to be". Peter Mogila's Orthodox Confession (1638–1642) says the saints help "by their prayers"; Alexei Khomiakov (1804–1860) asks who "can continue to speak of the merits of his own works, or of a treasury of merits and prayers"; Michael Pomazansky's Orthodox Dogmatic Theology (Russian, 1963) holds that penances called "satisfactions" by Western teachers of the ancient Church were meant "only in the moral sense", not "as a legal justification"; and John Breck (2010) writes that Orthodoxy does not hold that a person can build up a "treasury of merits".

Prayer to the Saints (invocation of the saints)

May the living address the saints in heaven, "Holy Mary, pray for us", asking them to bring their needs before God? Writers on both sides distinguish the belief that the saints intercede for the living from invocation, the second-person address to a saint, and dispute whether the two are different kinds of act. Traced from Irenaeus (c. 202), who in the passages surveyed does not address a departed saint, and Hippolytus's apostrophe to the three youths of Daniel (c. 204); through Origen (c. 233), who allows "request to saints" while reserving prayer "in its full meaning" to the Father, Cyprian (c. 252) on the departed praying for the living, the Roman graffiti to Peter and Paul (c. 250–320), the Sub Tuum Praesidium (papyrus dated from the third century to the seventh), the Cappadocians, Ambrose, Chrysostom and Jerome, the objector Vigilantius (c. 406), Augustine, and Gregory the Great; to the Second Council of Nicaea (787) and Aquinas; the Lutheran confessions (1530–31) and Calvin (1559), which reject invocation; and the Council of Trent (1563), which teaches it. Whether invocation is a post-apostolic addition (Melanchthon, 1531; Gavin Ortlund), a practice received from the primitive church (Trent; the Orthodox Church in America's manual, 2023), or a legitimate development of the doctrine of mediation (Newman, 1845) is disputed.

The resurrection of the body

The Ministerial Priesthood — who may offer the Eucharist

Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

Jesus’ teaching is recorded in four places: the Sermon on the Mount and the Pharisees’ question in Matthew, both with the “except for fornication” clause (Matthew 5:31–32; Matthew 19:3–9); the same saying without the exception in Mark and Luke (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18); and Paul’s case of the deserted believer (1 Corinthians 7:10–16). The Veneration of Images and Icons

May Christians make images of Christ and the saints and honour them, bowing before them, kissing them, and burning lights and incense to them, or does the Decalogue's ban on images forbid it? The Apostles (their lives and the traditions about them)

A footnoted timeline of the sermons, letters, anathemas, synods, and imperial edicts of the dispute between Cyril of Alexandria (patriarch 412–444) and Nestorius of Constantinople, earliest first — from the consecration of 428 and the sermons against Theotokos, through the Twelve Anathemas and the rival councils at Ephesus (431) that deposed each leader in turn, the count Candidian and the Antiochene charge of bribery, to the gifts Cyril's side sent to the imperial court during the reunion negotiations (read as customary gifts by some historians and as bribery by others), the Formula of Reunion (433), Nestorius's exile to the Egyptian desert, and the modern debate, after his own defence became known in the West, over whether the council condemned him for a Christology he held. It records how the Catholic, Orthodox and Coptic churches venerate Cyril, how the Church of the East honours Nestorius, and the Catholic–Assyrian Common Christological Declaration of 1994. The doctrinal substance is treated on the christology and Theotokos timelines; this page records the dispute itself.

The apostasy-and-restoration thesis

The Great Apostasy

did the church founded by the apostles wholly fall away after their death — losing valid authority, ordinances, and true doctrine — so that no legitimate church survived until it was restored in a later age? Latter-day Saint teaching holds that it did and that the Restoration answers it (Joseph Smith is told in his First Vision account that the churches “were all wrong”); Catholic and Orthodox teaching holds that the church has continued without a break since the apostles, and the Lutheran and Reformed confessions that a church always remained on earth, though corrupted. Traced from Paul's warning of “a falling away” beside Christ's promise that “the gates of hell shall not prevail” (c. 50–95), through Irenaeus's succession “from the apostles until now” (c. 180), Vincent of Lérins's canon (c. 434), Luther's papal “Antichrist” alongside his affirmation of a church surviving under the papacy (1528–37), Roger Williams's Seeker position awaiting new apostles (c. 1639), and Joseph Smith's Restoration (1820–30), to James E. Talmage's systematic case (1909) and the modern historians of doctrine Newman, Harnack, Kelly and Pelikan (1845–1971).
